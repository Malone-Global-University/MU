Islamization and the Malay-Indonesian World

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Islamization was not a single conversion event, not the replacement of one religion by another on a fixed date, and not simply the spread of Muslim kingdoms. It was a long, uneven historical process in which Southeast Asian communities helped shape the religious, intellectual, legal, and cultural institutions they adopted.

Near the village of Blang Me in the Aceh region of northern Sumatra stands a royal woman's tomb that carries two inscriptions, in two languages and two scripts, bearing two different dates. One text is in Malay, composed in verse and written in an Indic-derived script of the kind used in Sumatra before Arabic letters became standard for Malay. The other is in Arabic. The Malay text gives a date corresponding to 1380; the Arabic text gives one corresponding to 1389. The years probably diverge because of an error in one of the inscribed numerals, although the precise reading remains a matter of scholarly interpretation. The monument is known as Minye Tujuh, after a nearby site whose name refers to seven springs or pools, and it appears again in Section 65.3 as the first of this chapter's primary-source windows.

Minye Tujuh is a useful place to begin because it refuses a simple story. It proves that a Muslim funerary tradition existed in northern Sumatra by the later fourteenth century, and it shows that Malay speakers could express Islamic ideas in a writing system that came from an older, Indic-derived tradition. It does not tell us how many people in the surrounding villages performed the daily prayers, what the farmers of the Aceh hinterland believed, or when their grandparents or grandchildren first described themselves as Muslims. A stone records the commemoration of one person by those with the resources and the religious knowledge to commission it. Historians must reason carefully from such monuments to the wider society.

This chapter studies that wider society. Chapter 62 traced the Indian Ocean networks through which Muslim merchants, scholars, and religious travelers reached Southeast Asia. Chapter 63 examined the Muslim courts of northern Sumatra, especially Samudra-Pasai, and Chapter 64 explained how Melaka's rulers governed an Islamic maritime state. Those chapters concentrated on commerce, courts, and political institutions. Chapter 65 asks a different question: how did Islam develop from a religion practiced by particular merchants, rulers, and port communities into an increasingly influential religious and cultural tradition across the Malay-Indonesian world, and why did that transformation differ from one society to another?

The answer developed here is explicitly multi-causal. Trade, royal patronage, local religious teaching, marriage and family life, scholarship, and wider Islamic connections all mattered, but their relative importance varied by place and period. In some ports a Muslim ruler's patronage may have been decisive; in others, long-resident families, itinerant teachers, or local merchants may have mattered more. The chapter therefore avoids two familiar simplifications: the view that Islam spread chiefly because merchants carried it along trade routes, and the view that it spread through a uniformly gentle, mystical accommodation of local custom. Both contain partial truths, and neither adequately explains the evidence.

The evidence itself is a central subject of the chapter. For the period before 1500 it consists mainly of dated gravestones and inscriptions, a small number of foreign travelers' accounts, archaeological remains of burial grounds and settlements, and a larger body of later Malay and Javanese texts that preserve how communities remembered their own conversion. Many of the best-known narratives, including the stories of the Nine Saints of Java, were written down long after the events they describe. They are valuable evidence of memory, identity, and religious imagination, but they cannot be treated as contemporary reports. The chapter distinguishes carefully among what inscriptions establish, what travelers observed, and what later tradition claims.

The chapter's chronology runs from about 1200 to 1500, though some sixteenth-century sources are used to illuminate developments already under way, with explicit caution about projecting them backward. Its geography centers on northern Sumatra, the Malay Peninsula, and the north coast of Java, with briefer attention to other maritime regions whose political histories belong to later chapters. The Portuguese conquest of Melaka in 1511 (Chapter 69), the later Muslim sultanates of Aceh (Chapter 80) and Makassar (Chapter 82), and the transformation of Javanese politics (Chapters 52 and 83) lie beyond its principal endpoint; where they bear on the story, they are only previewed.

Finally, the chapter treats Southeast Asians as the principal agents of this history. Rulers, teachers, merchants, mothers, translators, scribes, and ordinary villagers selected which Islamic practices to adopt, translated Islamic ideas into Malay and Javanese, and decided how the new religion would relate to established obligations of kinship, kingship, and custom. In this respect the chapter continues the argument of Chapter 1 and Chapter 19: outside traditions became historically significant in the region because Southeast Asian societies made them so, selecting, adapting, and transforming what reached them.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define Islamization as a set of overlapping historical processes rather than a single event, and distinguish Muslim presence, elite conversion, and the formation of predominantly Muslim societies.
  2. Evaluate the kinds of evidence available for early Islamization, including inscriptions, gravestones, archaeology, travelers' accounts, and later chronicles, and explain the limits of each.
  3. Explain why the date of the earliest Muslim tombstone in a region cannot by itself establish when that region became Muslim.
  4. Compare the documented patterns of Islamization in northern Sumatra, the Malay Peninsula, and north-coastal Java, and identify why the evidence differs.
  5. Analyze the roles of rulers, merchants, teachers, families, and local communities in conversion, avoiding one-directional explanations.
  6. Describe the development of Islamic ritual, legal learning, and religious institutions, including the significance of the Shafi‘i legal tradition where documented.
  7. Assess the historical role of Sufi teachers and the scholarly debate over whether Sufism explains Southeast Asian Islamization.
  8. Trace the development of Arabic, Jawi, and Malay as languages of Islamic learning, and explain why earlier scripts did not disappear at once.
  9. Interpret the Wali Sanga traditions and the Admonitions of Seh Bari with appropriate source criticism, distinguishing later memory from contemporary evidence.
  10. Evaluate competing historical explanations for the spread of Islam and identify the problems with narratives of peaceful, uniform, or inevitable conversion.
  11. Distinguish continuity from change in religious life, without treating surviving earlier practices as proof of superficial Islamic identity.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What do historians mean by "Islamization," and why is the arrival of Muslims a different historical question from the conversion of societies?
  2. How can historians reason about religious change when the surviving evidence mostly concerns rulers, elites, and burials?
  3. Why did the pace, institutions, and outcomes of Islamization differ between northern Sumatra, the Malay Peninsula, and Java?
  4. In what ways did Southeast Asian rulers, merchants, teachers, and families act as agents rather than recipients of religious change?
  5. How did Islamic learning, legal thought, and ritual take root in local languages and social institutions?
  6. What are the strengths and limits of explanations that emphasize trade, royal patronage, marriage, or Sufism?
  7. How should historians use later chronicles, hagiographies, and religious texts that preserve memories of conversion but were written after the events?
  8. What persisted in religious and social life after Muslim communities were established, and what changed?
  9. Why does a history of Islamization require attention to variation within regions, not only between them?

Historical Context

By about 1200 the societies of maritime Southeast Asia already possessed long-established religious and political traditions. Courts in Sumatra, Java, and the Malay Peninsula had for many centuries drawn on Sanskritic ideas of kingship, Buddhist and Hindu ritual institutions, and local ancestral and spirit traditions, selecting and transforming them in the processes examined in Chapter 19. Srivijaya's scholarly and commercial prominence (Chapters 28, 29, and 47) and the temple-building courts of Central and East Java (Chapters 30–32 and 48–51) formed the religious landscape into which Muslim communities gradually inserted themselves. Beneath the courts lay village and household religion: rites of birth, marriage, and death; offerings to ancestors and place spirits; and agricultural and seafaring ritual that rarely appears in royal inscriptions.

Muslims were not entirely new to the region in 1200. Arab, Persian, South Asian, and Chinese Muslim traders had visited and sometimes resided in Southeast Asian and southern Chinese ports for centuries, and Chapter 62 examined the commercial and scholarly networks that sustained them. What changed between the thirteenth and fifteenth centuries was the formation of communities and courts in which Islam became publicly institutionalized: dated Muslim burials in northern Sumatra from the late thirteenth century, rulers who adopted Islamic titles and law, and, by the fifteenth century, a major trading port at Melaka governed by Muslim rulers (Chapter 64). Meanwhile Majapahit remained a Hindu-Buddhist court in East Java (Chapters 50–52), even as Muslim traders and families settled in its coastal ports.

Historians have long debated where Islam in Southeast Asia chiefly came from, with proposals pointing to Arabia and the Hadramawt, Gujarat, the Coromandel and Malabar coasts, Persia, and Chinese Muslim communities. These origin debates matter less than they once seemed, because the evidence points to multiple, overlapping sources rather than a single point of transmission. They also matter less than the question this chapter pursues: not where Islam came from, but how and why it became rooted in particular Southeast Asian societies.

A final piece of context is historiographical. Much early scholarship, particularly in the colonial era, explained Islamization in broad, confident narratives, sometimes suggesting that Islam spread peacefully and superficially over an earlier Hindu-Buddhist base. Later scholarship has tested these narratives against the sources and found many of them to depend on later traditions. This chapter draws on that more cautious scholarship, especially the work of Michael Laffan, M. C. Ricklefs, Peter Riddell, and G. W. J. Drewes, while recognizing that important questions remain unresolved.

Section 65.1

Reconstructing the Islamization of the Malay-Indonesian World

"Islamization" names not one process but several that overlapped and sometimes proceeded independently. It includes the conversion of individuals, which may be sincere, strategic, gradual, or imposed in varying degrees. It includes the adoption of Islam by a ruling household, which brought new titles, rituals, and alliances. It includes the formation of communities with mosques, burial grounds, teachers, and shared obligations. It includes the growth of religious learning, in which texts were read, translated, copied, and debated. And it includes cultural institutionalization, as Islamic categories entered law, language, literature, calendar, and the rhythms of family life. A court could be Muslim while its hinterland remained largely non-Muslim; a village could practice Islamic burial customs generations before any local ruler adopted the religion.

This complexity makes the central question, "when did a place become Muslim?", difficult to answer. Religious belief is not directly visible in the historical record, and the sources that survive were produced by particular people for particular purposes. Gravestones were commissioned by families with means. Royal inscriptions record the concerns of rulers. Foreign travelers noted what struck them, usually in ports and courts. Local chronicles, such as the Malay Hikayat Raja-raja Pasai and Sejarah Melayu and a range of Javanese chronicles, narrate conversion through stories of dreams, miracles, and royal baptisms, but the surviving versions were composed or edited well after the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. Such stories tell us much about how later communities explained their religious identity and little that can be taken as a contemporary record.

Foreign observers provide an important check but not a complete one. Marco Polo's brief report on Perlak in 1292 suggests that Muslim merchants had influenced the townspeople there, while he described neighboring areas differently. Ibn Battuta's visit to Samudra in the 1340s provides an eyewitness account of a court in which Islamic legal learning was prominent. Ma Huan, who accompanied Zheng He's expeditions in the early fifteenth century, described religiously differentiated populations at Javanese ports in 1433. These accounts are precious because they are contemporary, yet each records a visitor's selective impressions of coastal centers, not the beliefs of whole societies.

Modern scholarship has also changed how historians read these sources. Earlier generations often sought a single origin and date for the arrival of Islam, producing competing theories that located the principal source in Arabia, Gujarat, South India, or Persia. More recent work, notably Michael Laffan's The Makings of Indonesian Islam (2011), examines how narratives about Islam's coming were themselves shaped by later scholarly networks, colonial scholarship, and nationalist and religious concerns. The implication for students is methodological: any statement about the Islamization of a particular place should be accompanied by questions about what kind of evidence supports it, who produced that evidence, and what exactly it shows.

The chapter's governing distinction follows from this. Muslim presence, the conversion of a ruler, and the emergence of a predominantly Muslim society are different historical facts, established by different evidence and often separated by generations. Keeping them apart is the first discipline of the subject.

Historical Source and Method Study

Five Questions to Ask of Any Claim about Conversion

When a textbook, chronicle, or museum label states that a place "became Muslim" at a particular time, historians ask the following questions.

  1. What kind of fact is claimed? Is it the presence of a Muslim, the conversion of a ruler, the founding of a community, or the religious affiliation of the general population?
  2. What is the evidence? A dated stone, a traveler's report, an archaeological burial, or a later chronicle each supports very different conclusions.
  3. When was the evidence produced? A sixteenth-century or later text describing the fourteenth century is evidence of later memory unless corroborated.
  4. Whose perspective does it represent? Rulers, merchants, visitors, or later religious communities each had purposes shaping what they recorded.
  5. Who and what is missing? Villagers, women, enslaved people, and upland communities rarely appear directly in such sources.

Applying the method: A Muslim gravestone dated 1380 establishes a commemoration practice; it does not establish the religion of the people who lived nearby. A chronicle recounting a king's dream of the Prophet shows how a later community narrated its origins; it does not date the king's conversion.

Section 65.2

The Geography of Religious Change in Maritime Southeast Asia

Geography shaped where contact with Muslim traders and teachers was likely, and it shaped who could participate in the religious life that followed. It did not determine religious outcomes. The monsoon wind system (Chapter 4) governed the rhythm of voyages across the Bay of Bengal and the South China Sea, creating seasonal pauses that brought crews and merchants ashore for weeks or months and encouraged the formation of resident foreign communities. Ports at the northern entrance of the Strait of Malacca were especially well placed to receive Indian Ocean visitors, and the northern tip of Sumatra accordingly figures prominently in the earliest dated Muslim evidence.

Northern Sumatra, with Perlak, Samudra-Pasai, Lambri, and Pedir, combined access to Indian Ocean shipping with a pepper-producing hinterland and a close relationship to the Strait. The Malay Peninsula offered multiple coastal and cross-peninsular connections, with river valleys leading from the east coast toward the interior; Terengganu, Pahang, Kelantan, Kedah, and, in the fifteenth century, Melaka lay along these routes. On Java the north coast, known in Javanese and later scholarly usage as the pasisir, held a string of ports including Gresik, Tuban, Surabaya, and later Demak, Jepara, and Cirebon. These ports exported rice and other goods from the fertile interior and served as the points where Javanese, Chinese, South Asian, and other merchants met.

Ports, however, were not entire societies. In Sumatra, river corridors such as the Batang Hari and Musi linked coastal markets with upland producers, and the highland societies of the Minangkabau and Batak regions followed their own religious and social trajectories. In Java, the agrarian heartland behind the coast, organized around the great temple-building courts and village communities studied in Chapters 50 and 51, was not simply an extension of the ports. Bali, close to Java, remained a Hindu-Buddhist society. Uplands everywhere, often dependent on lowland markets yet politically and socially distinct, had less sustained contact with Muslim teachers, though contact was never absent.

Coastal geography also created a pattern that has misled historians: because early evidence clusters in ports, it can appear that Islam was a "coastal religion" that only later moved inland. The pattern is partly real, since ports concentrated visitors, patrons, and durable monuments, but it is also an artifact of the evidence. Wooden mosques and village prayer houses rarely survive, and the religious life of rural communities left few written records. The absence of evidence in the interior is not evidence that Muslims were absent from it.

Geography thus offers opportunities and constraints. Exposure to maritime commerce increased the likelihood of early contact; river routes and cross-peninsular paths extended that contact inland; mountains, forests, and distance slowed it. But whether contact led to the formation of Muslim communities depended on decisions by rulers, merchants, families, and teachers, which the following sections examine.

Section 65.3

Inscriptions, Tombstones, and the Earliest Evidence of Islamic Communities

Dated Islamic funerary inscriptions provide important chronological anchors for the study of early Islamization, provided the inscriptional date is not confused with the manufacture date of the object. Gravestones in Arabic and other scripts survive in northern Sumatra, Java, and the Malay Peninsula. The surviving markers associated with al-Malik al-Salih in Samudra record his death in 696 AH (1297 CE), but material analysis has suggested that these stones were produced much later, possibly around the late fifteenth or early sixteenth century (Chapter 63). At Pasai, the elaborate funerary memorial of the royal woman Nahrasyiyah bears a date of 831 AH (1428 CE), documenting high-status Muslim commemoration without establishing that its occupant personally ruled. The origin and carving of individual monuments must be examined separately from their inscriptions.

Evidence from Java requires a firm distinction between Trowulan and Troloyo. At Trowulan, a stone bearing a Śaka date corresponding to 1368–1369 has sometimes been treated as evidence of an early Muslim presence, but its identity as an Islamic gravestone remains disputed. At the nearby Troloyo cemetery, the only unambiguous Muslim epitaph identified in Hadi Sidomulyo's reassessment belonged to Zain ud Din and was dated 874 AH (1469–1470); the stone itself has since disappeared. On the north coast, Gresik preserves a burial inscription dated 822 AH (1419 CE), later associated with the teacher Malik Ibrahim. The contested Leran inscription, conventionally dated 475 AH (1082 CE), does not by itself demonstrate a local Muslim community. On the Malay Peninsula the Pengkalan Kempas monument offers two dates: 1385 Śaka (1463–1464) in Palaeosumatran script and 872 AH (1467–1468) in Jawi. These differences illustrate the need to assess each object's provenance, calendar, and interpretation.

What such stones establish is specific. They show that someone was commemorated in a form recognizable as Islamic, with Arabic religious formulas, at a particular time and place. They imply that people with Islamic religious knowledge were available to compose or at least copy the texts. They suggest that the commemorated person's family or community had the resources and motivation to produce a durable monument. They do not establish how many others in the area were Muslim, how recently the commemorated person or their family had adopted Islam, or what the population in the surrounding countryside believed.

Language and script matter here. The Arabic language of most stones links the local Muslim community with wider Islamic literate culture, but the Minye Tujuh monument shows that this was not the only option: the Malay text on the same tomb uses an Indic-derived script. The practice of adding a second inscription in a different script and language to the same commemorative monument shows people drawing on more than one repertoire of religious and political expression.

Dating itself is a historical problem. Hijri dates must be converted to the Common Era; stones may be damaged or reused; carvers made errors; and dates may refer to the death of the commemorated person, the completion of the monument, or a later commemoration. Wherever a date is cited in this chapter, students should treat it as the reading of a particular surviving text, not as a fixed fact beyond reconsideration.

Primary Source Window I

The Minye Tujuh Inscriptions, Aceh, Fourteenth Century

Attribution: Commemorative monument for a high-ranking woman; individual commissioner and writer unknown; the woman's name is not securely established by the Malay reading · Languages and scripts: a Malay text in verse in an Old Sumatran (Indic-derived) script, and a separate Arabic text in Arabic script · Date and provenance: the Malay text gives 781 AH (1380 CE); the Arabic text gives 791 AH (1389 CE), probably through an error in one of the recorded years; from the Minye Tujuh site near Blang Me, North Aceh · Type: Funerary and commemorative inscriptions

Context: The monument lies in the Aceh region, close to the northern Sumatran courts discussed in Chapter 63. Its two inscriptions have been studied extensively, notably by Willem van der Molen, who has examined the Malay text's literary form, its script, and the problem of the discrepancy between the two dates. Readings of individual words and names vary among scholars.

Editorial paraphrase (not a verbatim translation):

The Malay inscription, composed in verse, commemorates the death of a royal woman and expresses Islamic religious ideas, including reference to God and to the transience of worldly life. The Arabic inscription, with its different date, contains Islamic religious formulas and commemorative language appropriate to a Muslim tomb.

Analysis: The monument shows that a high-ranking woman in northern Sumatra was commemorated with Islamic religious language in two forms: Arabic, the scriptural language, and Malay, the language of the local community. The Malay verse in an Indic-derived script shows that Islamic religious expression could be conveyed in a writing system that predated Islam in the region. Islamic identity did not require immediate abandonment of earlier literate traditions.

Limitations: The inscriptions cannot tell us how widely Islam was practiced in the surrounding region. The two inscriptions appear to commemorate the same death. As van der Molen explains, a mistake in one year numeral is generally considered the most economical explanation for the nine-year difference; separate commemorative events are not established. Earlier reconstructions of the Malay woman's name differ from van der Molen's re-reading, which identifies a royal daughter without securely establishing her proper name. Perret and colleagues use the identification al-malika Wabīsa from the Arabic epigraphic tradition; other secondary accounts propose different names. These attributions must not be presented as a uniform scholarly reading.

Perspective: The monument reflects the perspective of an elite household with access to scribes and stone-carvers literate in both traditions. The perspectives of villagers, non-Muslim neighbors, and artisans who produced the stone are not represented.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the source say, show, or record, and in which languages and scripts?
  2. Who created it and under what circumstances? What can be said about the identity of the commissioners?
  3. What possible purposes shaped the decision to use two languages and two scripts?
  4. What can historians learn about the relationship between Islamic expression and earlier Southeast Asian writing traditions?
  5. What can historians not establish from this monument alone, including the extent of conversion in the surrounding population?
Section 65.4

Archaeology, Material Culture, and the Limits of Conversion Chronology

Archaeology supplements inscriptions by recovering what texts omit: the layout of burial grounds, the forms of grave markers, the arrangement of settlements, and the movement of goods. Muslim burial practice leaves distinctive traces, particularly the orientation of the body and grave relative to Mecca, simple burial without elaborate grave goods, and the use of inscribed or shaped markers. Cemeteries therefore provide some of the clearest material evidence for Muslim communities. Burial grounds at Pasai and in the Aceh region, at Troloyo and Gresik on Java, and at Barus on Sumatra's west coast reveal where Muslims were buried, and sometimes how their communities were organized, though dating many of the Barus graves is debated.

Grave markers show interaction of styles. Some Pasai stones reflect South Asian carving traditions, including elaborate calligraphic and floral decoration. Later markers in Aceh and on the Malay Peninsula developed local forms. On Java, the gravestones at Troloyo and the tomb complexes associated with later saints incorporated carved motifs and architectural styles that echo Javanese traditions while serving Islamic commemorative purposes. The mixture is not evidence of insincerity: it shows that Muslim communities used the artistic and craft resources available to them.

Mosques are harder to recover. Early mosques in the region were generally built of timber and other perishable materials, and later rebuilding has obscured most original structures. Traditional accounts, such as those attached to the Great Mosque of Demak, assign early dates to some buildings, but the surviving fabric is frequently later, and the traditions are retrospective. Architectural historians often note that Javanese mosques adopted tiered roofs rather than the domes familiar in other Islamic regions, a form that may reflect local building traditions, though the pathway of influence remains discussed. Early structures at Kudus and elsewhere belong largely to the sixteenth century and later.

Settlement archaeology and trade goods provide another line of evidence, but a limited one. Chinese ceramics, Indian textiles, and other imported goods at port sites document commerce, not religion. A merchant's presence in a port can be inferred from goods but not his or her faith. Burial evidence, inscriptions, and later textual references must be combined with material remains to infer religious affiliation.

For these reasons, archaeology makes the chronology of conversion more complex, not simpler. It can show when a Muslim cemetery was in use and sometimes how it related to earlier settlement. It cannot show, by itself, the beliefs of the living, the pace of conversion across a population, or the reasons for it. Historians therefore speak of a "conversion chronology" only with strong qualifications: the best-documented dates mark the visibility of Islamic institutions, not the moment when a society's religion changed.

Section 65.5

The Expansion of Islamic Communities, c. 1200–1500

The documented spread of Muslim communities can be arranged in three broad phases, with the caution that each phase reflects the evidence that survives and that the phases overlapped. In the first, roughly the thirteenth century, evidence concentrates in northern Sumatra. Marco Polo's 1292 account of Perlak, the death date of 1297 retrospectively recorded on al-Malik al-Salih's surviving memorials, and later Pasai traditions indicate Muslim communities and, at Samudra and Pasai, a ruling household with Islamic commemorative practice. Elsewhere evidence is slight, which is not the same as absence.

In the fourteenth century, documentary evidence widens, but the kind of evidence matters. Ibn Battuta's visit to Samudra in the mid-1340s describes an Islamic court in which the sultan attended prayer and engaged with jurists. The Terengganu inscription on the Malay Peninsula, conventionally dated 1303 but alternatively read as 1308, attests Islamic legal vocabulary in Malay. The two Minye Tujuh texts, dated 1380 and 1389, provide distinct records of Muslim elite commemoration in Malay and Arabic. On Java, a 1368–1369 Trowulan stone has been regarded as early Muslim evidence, although that identification is contested; it must not be confused with the more securely identified Muslim epitaph at nearby Troloyo, dated 1469–1470. This is not proof of a uniform fourteenth-century conversion wave.

In the fifteenth century, Melaka became a major Muslim-ruled port (Chapter 64), while Ma Huan described Muslim and non-Muslim populations at Javanese ports in the early fifteenth century. A 1419 Gresik epitaph and the securely identified 1469–1470 Troloyo inscription attest Muslim commemoration in different East Javanese settings, although neither allows population-wide estimates. Northern Sumatran monuments, including Nahrasyiyah's 1428 memorial, show sustained elite Islamic practices. The Pengkalan Kempas pillar, with its distinct dates of 1385 Śaka (1463–1464) and 872 AH (1467–1468), points to the continued coexistence of Islamic and Indic-derived inscriptional practices in peninsular communities. A growing documentary record is not the same as an evenly expanding Muslim population.

Three cautions apply. First, the sequence from Sumatra to the peninsula to Java should not be read as a wave moving smoothly in one direction. Merchants and teachers moved among all these regions, and Javanese ports had direct links to the Indian Ocean and China as well as to Melaka. Second, early Muslim presence did not guarantee growth: communities could remain small and dependent on a single patron or commercial connection, and the evidence does not allow us to rule out declines as well as expansions. Third, regional differences, examined in Chapters 63 and 64 for politics and in Section 65.13 for religion, are as important as the overall trend.

The word "expansion" itself needs unpacking. A Muslim community could grow through births within existing households, through the arrival of new merchants and teachers, through the conversion of neighbors, employees, dependents, or enslaved people, and through the movement of families from one port to another. Each mechanism has different implications for how rapidly a community grew and how deeply its members shared a religious identity, and the surviving evidence rarely allows historians to distinguish them. A cemetery that expands over several generations might reflect natural growth, continuing immigration, or conversion, and the stones alone cannot say which.

Section 65.6

Rulers, Elite Conversion, and the Patronage of Islam

Rulers and aristocratic families played a significant role in the institutionalization of Islam, but their role should be neither minimized nor exaggerated. Adopting Islam gave a ruler access to new forms of legitimacy: titles such as sultan, ritual roles such as presiding at communal prayer, and recognition within a network of Muslim rulers, scholars, and merchants extending across the Indian Ocean. It also facilitated commercial and diplomatic relations with Muslim traders, who might prefer ports where their own law and worship were respected. Chapters 63 and 64 examined these political dimensions in northern Sumatra and Melaka; here the focus is on how elite adoption influenced religious institutions and wider society.

Rulers provided material support: land for mosques and burial grounds, patronage for teachers and jurists, and the authority to issue decrees. The Terengganu Inscription, discussed in Section 65.11, is a ruler's record of Islamic legal provisions. Ibn Battuta's description of the sultan of Samudra attending religious discussions with jurists shows royal engagement with scholarship. Elite marriage alliances also served as channels of religious change, as courts married into Muslim merchant or scholarly families, or arranged marriages with other Muslim rulers.

Yet elite conversion had limits. A ruler's adoption of Islam did not necessarily transform his subjects' religion, especially where rule was personal and tributary rather than administrative. The countryside, upland communities, and ritual specialists associated with earlier traditions could remain outside direct royal influence. On Java, the pattern was particularly complex. Majapahit remained a Hindu-Buddhist court, while coastal lords and communities along the pasisir adopted Islam in varying degrees over the fifteenth century. Tomé Pires, writing in the early sixteenth century, reported that some Javanese coastal rulers belonged to families that had adopted Islam within recent generations, a retrospective observation that must be used cautiously but that is consistent with a gradual process.

Elite adoption also involved choices about continuity. Muslim rulers did not abandon earlier court vocabulary, ceremonial forms, or genealogical claims; they often combined Islamic titles and rituals with established conceptions of kingship, a pattern visible in later Malay and Javanese chronicles. The point is not that rulers were insincere but that Islamic kingship, like earlier Indic-influenced kingship, was locally shaped. Rulers also faced competing audiences: Muslim merchants and scholars on one side, and non-Muslim subjects, ritual specialists, and neighboring courts on the other.

Rulers were not the only elites whose choices mattered. Aristocratic families, port officials, and wealthy merchants managed commerce, hosted visitors, and arbitrated disputes, and their religious affiliations shaped how foreign Muslim traders experienced a port. An official who handled dealings with Muslim merchants had practical reasons to learn their customs and, in some cases, to adopt their religion. The sources seldom identify such individuals by name before 1500, but the general pattern is consistent with the gradual, household-by-household formation of elite Muslim networks alongside royal patronage.

Long-term causes

Centuries of Indian Ocean commerce had brought Muslim merchants and scholars to Southeast Asian ports; local rulers had long used external religious traditions for legitimacy.

Short-term causes

Competition among ports for trade, diplomatic advantage with Muslim-ruled commercial partners, marriage opportunities, and relationships with particular teachers.

Immediate effects

New titles, patronage of mosques and teachers, Islamic burial of royal family members, and inclusion in Muslim diplomatic and commercial networks.

Long-term consequences

Growth of religious institutions at courts and ports; foundations for later Muslim sultanates; wider but uneven spread beyond court circles.

Historical complexity: Motives varied and rarely survive in sources. Commercial advantage, religious conviction, kinship, and political calculation could operate together, and no single motive explains every case.

Section 65.7

Merchants, Teachers, Religious Specialists, and Local Agency

The first Muslim communities in many ports consisted of merchants and their households. These included traders from Gujarat and other South Asian regions, Arabs and Persians, Muslim traders from the Coromandel and Malabar coasts, and Chinese Muslims, whose presence in Javanese ports is noted by Ma Huan. Merchants brought commercial law, religious obligations, and personal networks, and they often settled for long periods. But merchants were not all missionaries, and trade does not explain the formation of local religious learning. Indeed, many long-distance traders were primarily concerned with commerce, and Muslim merchants coexisted with non-Muslim rulers and neighbors in numerous ports.

Teachers, jurists, and other religious specialists were central to the establishment of lasting institutions. They taught the Qur'an, prayer, and legal rules; conducted funerals and marriages; and advised rulers. Some came from the Indian Ocean world, including Arabia and South Asia; others were trained locally or traveled for study. Later Malay chronicles, such as the Sejarah Melayu, tell of religious questions exchanged between Melaka and Pasai scholars, suggesting a tradition in which Pasai was recognized as a center of learning, but these accounts were written down later and should be treated as retrospective. Sufi practitioners, discussed in Section 65.10, formed one important category of religious specialist, though not the only one.

Southeast Asians acted at every level. Local merchants, including Javanese and Malay traders who sailed to Indian Ocean and Chinese ports, encountered Islam abroad and brought it home. Local scribes and translators rendered Arabic religious terms into Malay and Javanese. Local rulers and elites chose whether and how to patronize teachers. Local communities decided whether to attend a new mosque, observe a new fast, or alter burial practices. The history of conversion is therefore a history of negotiation and translation, not simply of transmission from outside.

Interests could diverge. Merchants valued predictable commercial conditions. Teachers sought students, patrons, and the means to establish institutions. Rulers valued legitimacy and alliances. Villagers had to weigh religious claims against existing obligations to kin, ancestors, and local ritual specialists. These interests sometimes aligned and sometimes did not, which helps explain why Islamization proceeded at different speeds in different settings.

Intermediaries deserve particular attention. Interpreters, scribes, brokers, and bilingual families linked visiting merchants with local rulers and communities, and they often translated not only words but customs and expectations. Ma Huan's account indicates that Chinese Muslims lived in Javanese ports, and later Javanese traditions attribute a prominent role to figures of Chinese or Champa association in the coming of Islam. The reliability and extent of these claims are debated; what the evidence supports is a multi-ethnic Muslim presence in which no single group can be credited with transmitting the religion.

Section 65.8

Families, Intermarriage, Generations, and Community Conversion

Religious change takes hold through households. A mosque requires a congregation, and a congregation is made of families who bring children to prayer, arrange marriages, bury their dead, and pass on obligations. The most durable social mechanism of Islamization was therefore generational: a family that adopted Islamic practices raised children within them, and over several generations the practices became unremarkable and expected. Evidence for this process is scarce because ordinary family life rarely appears in surviving sources, so historians reconstruct it cautiously from legal rules, later traditions, and the structure of port societies.

Marriage between visiting or resident Muslim men and local women is widely cited as a channel of religious change, and it is plausible. In many ports foreign merchants stayed for months or years, and their households were formed through local marriages. Islamic law expects children of a Muslim father to be raised as Muslims, and where this expectation was followed, a family could become a Muslim household within a generation. But intermarriage did not guarantee conversion: some foreign merchants may have adopted local practices, some wives may have retained earlier ritual obligations, and families may have combined practices. A ruler's marriage into a Muslim family likewise did not necessarily alter his subjects' religion.

Women's roles were significant even though the sources rarely record them directly. Royal women are commemorated at Minye Tujuh and in the tomb of Nahrasyiyah at Pasai, showing that women of high rank were commemorated within Islamic elite culture, although their precise political roles cannot be established from these tombs alone. In households, women commonly carried responsibility for early child-rearing, which in a Muslim household would include early religious instruction. Later Javanese traditions describe royal women as intermediaries in conversion narratives, including the story of a princess associated with Champa who married a Majapahit ruler. Such stories belong to later tradition, not contemporary evidence, but they reflect a recognition that marriage alliances mattered.

Communities also formed through settlement and shared institutions. Muslim merchants and their families often lived in quarters of port cities, with a mosque, a cemetery, and a recognized leader. These neighborhoods could draw in neighbors through commerce, education, and mutual obligation. Property rules, especially Islamic inheritance rules, created further reasons for families to align with Muslim legal institutions, though in many places local custom continued to shape inheritance in practice (Section 65.12).

Intergenerational identity could also shift in the opposite direction. Families might adopt Islam for convenience and drift away; children might practice differently from parents; and migration might separate households from religious communities. The documentary evidence rarely reveals such trajectories. Historians should therefore avoid inventing biographies for unrecorded individuals and instead describe plausible mechanisms while acknowledging that their relative importance cannot be measured precisely.

Division III — Islamic Learning, Sufi Traditions, Language, and Religious Practice
Section 65.9

Islamic Belief, Ritual, Legal Learning, and Religious Institutions

Islam as a lived religion consists of belief and practice organized through institutions. For Southeast Asian Muslims, as elsewhere, core obligations included the profession of faith, the five daily prayers, fasting during Ramadan, almsgiving, and, for those able, pilgrimage to Mecca. Communal worship centered on the Friday congregational prayer at a mosque. Burial rites, marriage contracts, and the observance of festivals gave Islam a visible presence in family and community life. Each of these practices required knowledge, whether through instruction from a teacher, observation of other Muslims, or recitation of the Qur'an, and each required some degree of institutional support, such as a place to pray and a person to lead.

Legal learning formed a second pillar. Islamic jurisprudence, fiqh, developed several schools, and in Southeast Asia the Shafi‘i school became dominant in the centuries that followed. Ibn Battuta's account of Samudra in 1345–1346 is the key contemporary evidence for Shafi‘i learning at a Southeast Asian court: he described the ruler as a follower of that school, and as someone who engaged with jurists in religious discussion. This is evidence for one court at one moment; it does not prove that every Muslim community in the region followed the same school or that all communities possessed trained jurists. Still, the Shafi‘i school's prevalence along Indian Ocean coasts, including the Malabar coast and southern Arabia, makes it a plausible shared inheritance for early Southeast Asian Muslim communities.

Religious institutions varied. Mosques, ranging from simple prayer houses to royal congregational mosques, formed the basic communal institution. Teachers gathered students in informal settings, giving rise to teaching circles and, in later centuries, the boarding schools known in Java as pesantren and in the Malay world as pondok. The earliest evidence for such institutions is later than the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, and some scholars have suggested that they drew upon earlier traditions of religious retreat and instruction in the region, a hypothesis that remains debated. Judges, qadi, appear in the sources for some courts and ports, and rulers' decrees such as the Terengganu Inscription show royal involvement in legal regulation.

Practice differed across communities and periods. A court mosque in a major port might follow textual norms closely, while a small village prayer house might blend Islamic prayer with local ritual obligations. Burial customs could follow Islamic requirements while memorial feasts reflected earlier patterns. Fasting and prayer could be observed with varying rigor. Historians must therefore avoid treating the legal ideals described in texts as a record of actual conduct, and they must also avoid assuming that departures from textual norms indicate lack of commitment.

Pilgrimage and study abroad connected local communities to the centers of Islamic learning. The pilgrimage to Mecca required a long and costly voyage, and the evidence for Southeast Asian pilgrims before 1500 is limited, with much firmer documentation from the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Even so, travel to and from the Indian Ocean world, whether for pilgrimage, trade, or study, offered Southeast Asian Muslims access to teachers and texts, and returning travelers could bring new knowledge and prestige. The Islamic calendar and its festivals likewise linked local communities to a shared rhythm of worship that extended well beyond the region.

Section 65.10

Sufi Teachers, Devotional Traditions, and Religious Authority

Sufism, the mystical and devotional dimension of Islam, has long occupied a prominent place in explanations of Southeast Asian Islamization. Sufi teachers emphasized inner knowledge of God, spiritual discipline, and devotional practices such as remembrance of God (dhikr). Some belonged to organized brotherhoods, tariqa, that traced spiritual lineages from teacher to student. A. H. Johns, in an influential 1961 article, argued that Sufism should be recognized as a major category for understanding Indonesian literature and history, and the idea that itinerant Sufi teachers brought Islam to Southeast Asia in a form receptive to local religious sensibilities became widely influential.

Later scholarship has examined this thesis critically. The evidence for specific Sufi teachers active in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries is limited and often comes from later chronicles. Malay and Javanese traditions name individual teachers, such as scholars associated with Pasai and Melaka, and tell of texts exchanged and questions answered. But many of these narratives were composed centuries afterward, and some of the major Sufi-influenced writers of the Malay world, such as Hamzah Fansuri, belong to the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Michael Laffan has argued that the image of an essentially Sufi Indonesian Islamic past was itself constructed through later scholarship and retrospective narratives, and he cautions against treating that image as a direct description of the early period.

A balanced assessment recognizes Sufi influence without making it the sole explanation. Sufi teachers likely contributed to the formation of religious communities, particularly where devotional practice, teacher-student relationships, and claims of spiritual authority resonated with existing expectations about holy persons and ascetics. But Islamic law, trade connections, courtly patronage, and family networks also mattered, and juristic and devotional currents were not opposed in the way later polemics sometimes suggest; many teachers combined legal and mystical learning. The label "Sufi" can also obscure differences among teachers who emphasized different traditions and texts.

Religious authority took several forms. Jurists and judges claimed authority through legal knowledge; Sufi teachers through spiritual lineage and experience; rulers through patronage and political power; and descendants of venerated figures through genealogy. Titles and claims of descent from the Prophet, known to scholars as sayyid or sharif claims, appear in some traditions, and their historical status varies case by case. Where evidence is sparse, historians should describe the types of authority that were available without asserting detailed biographies for individuals.

Southeast Asian societies already had traditions of holy persons, ascetics, and sacred sites, and some scholars have suggested that Muslim teachers and their tombs were understood partly in relation to these. The veneration of tombs, sometimes referred to in Malay as keramat, became an important feature of later religious life. Whether such veneration in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries reflected continuity with older practice, Sufi influence, or both cannot be settled from the evidence, and different regions may have followed different paths.

Everyday Religious Life

What Could Muslim Religious Life Include in a Port Community?

For fourteenth- and fifteenth-century port communities, historians can describe the range of practices that were available, using legal norms, inscriptions, and traveler accounts, while acknowledging that the surviving evidence rarely documents any single household. These practices may have included daily prayer, observance of Friday congregational worship where a mosque existed, fasting in Ramadan, Qur'anic recitation, burial according to Islamic custom, and visits to the graves of respected persons. Devotional gatherings and teacher-student instruction could supplement these practices where teachers were present.

Muslim households coexisted with neighbors who followed other traditions. Everyday life therefore included negotiation: sharing markets and festivals, managing mixed households, and deciding how to combine inherited obligations with new ones. The evidence indicates diversity rather than a single standard of practice.

Section 65.11

Arabic, Jawi, Malay, and the Development of Islamic Literacy

Language is one of the clearest windows on how Islam took root. Arabic, the language of the Qur'an and of formal worship, provided the religious core: recitation, prayer formulas, and many technical terms were learned in Arabic and often retained in that language. Persian and South Asian languages contributed additional vocabulary and texts in many Indian Ocean communities. Yet the formation of a durable local tradition required religious ideas to be expressed in Malay and Javanese, the languages of everyday life, and this entailed both translation and adaptation.

Jawi is the name given to the Arabic-derived script adapted for writing Malay, with additional letters to represent sounds not found in Arabic. It became a principal vehicle of Malay Islamic writing, and the Terengganu Inscription, discussed in the source window below, is the earliest known substantial text in Jawi. The adaptation of Arabic letters for Malay was an act of local intellectual work, producing a writing system that connected Malay speakers with the wider Islamic literate world while preserving their own language. Vocabulary shows similar adaptation: Malay and Javanese absorbed many Arabic and Persian religious terms, while retaining and re-purposing earlier words, including Sanskrit-derived terms that had long served religious and courtly purposes.

Jawi's later prominence should not obscure its early limits. Jawi did not immediately displace earlier scripts. The Minye Tujuh Malay inscription of 1380, written in an Indic-derived Old Sumatran script, shows that Islamic religious expression in Malay could be written in a pre-existing writing system. In Java, Islamic texts continued to be written in Javanese script, and a form of Arabic-derived writing for Javanese developed later. Muslims in the region also wrote in Arabic, and some wrote in other scripts. Literacy remained restricted to a minority, so most Muslims encountered religious texts through recitation and oral instruction.

Qur'anic learning relied heavily on memorization and recitation. Teachers taught the Arabic alphabet and recitation as the foundation of religious education, and students might learn to recite without understanding the Arabic. Translation and commentary in Malay and Javanese developed gradually, and surviving manuscripts of such works are largely later than 1500. Historians therefore infer the earlier development of Islamic literacy from inscriptions and from the practical requirements of religious life, not from a rich manuscript record.

Everyday vocabulary shows the same layering. Malay words for prayer (sembahyang), fasting (puasa), heaven (syurga), hell (neraka), sin (dosa), and religious reward (pahala) derive from Indic or earlier local sources, and speakers continued to use them for Islamic concepts even as Arabic terms such as salat and iman entered the language. The persistence of these words is not evidence of an incomplete conversion. It shows that Muslim speakers drew on the whole of their linguistic inheritance to express a new religious life, in a process that linguists and historians treat as characteristic of religious translation generally.

Section 65.12

Islamic Law, Malay Custom, and the Negotiation of Religious Practice

Southeast Asian societies had their own legal and customary traditions before Islam, usually referred to in Malay and Indonesian as adat. Adat encompassed norms regulating marriage, inheritance, property, dispute settlement, and relations among kin groups and between rulers and subjects. When Islamic legal ideas arrived, they met these established systems, and the relationship between them was negotiated over centuries rather than settled at a single moment.

Islamic law offered a comprehensive framework for matters including marriage contracts, divorce, inheritance, commercial transactions, and criminal penalties. For merchants, its commercial rules provided a recognized basis for contracts across ports. For courts, it supplied ideas of just rule and a vocabulary of judgment. The Terengganu Inscription shows a ruler issuing provisions in Islamic terms, and Ibn Battuta's account of Samudra shows legal discussion at court. Later Malay legal compilations, such as the digests associated with Melaka and other Malay courts, blend Islamic legal concepts with Malay customary rules and royal authority.

Those compilations require caution. The surviving manuscripts of the Melaka laws and related texts date from later centuries, and they were copied, expanded, and edited over time. They are valuable evidence of how Malay legal traditions combined Islamic and customary elements, but they cannot be treated as complete records of fifteenth-century legal enforcement. The ideals stated in a legal text, the decisions of a court, and the practice of a village could differ substantially, and the sources rarely allow historians to trace the relationships among them.

The negotiation took different forms in different places. In some societies Islamic inheritance rules coexisted with older practices of property transmission, as in the later history of the matrilineal Minangkabau, where adat and Islamic law interacted over centuries and produced long-running debates. In other settings, family law shifted more readily toward Islamic norms. Slavery and debt bondage, which were widespread in the region (see Chapter 91), existed under both customary and Islamic frameworks, and Islamic rules regulated but did not abolish them; the relationship between the two systems varied.

Accommodation did not mean that Islamic identity was shallow. The coexistence of adat and Islamic law reflects the ordinary situation of religious communities everywhere: normative texts are applied through local institutions, and the resulting practice reflects both. In Southeast Asia, the negotiation produced distinctive legal cultures, with rulers, judges, and communities each contributing to how Islamic norms were understood and applied.

Questions of who decided disputes and how are especially hard to answer. Village elders, kin-group heads, local chiefs, and rulers all handled conflicts in earlier societies, and Muslim judges and jurists are documented chiefly at courts and ports. For most rural communities, evidence about whether and when Islamic legal institutions became part of dispute settlement is thin. It is safer to describe a spectrum of arrangements, from court settings where Islamic legal reasoning was prominent to village settings where customary mediation continued, than to assume a single legal order across a region.

Division IV — Regional Adaptation: Java, Malay Literature, and Religious Culture
Section 65.13

Mapping the Regional Patterns of Islamization

Comparing regions clarifies what the evidence does and does not show. Northern Sumatra offers the earliest well-dated Islamic commemorative evidence, the best-documented early Muslim court, and a long record of Malay and Arabic inscription. Its Islamization is relatively visible, but the evidence concentrates on ruling households and the coastal centers. The relation of these centers to the Sumatran uplands remains much less clear.

The Malay Peninsula presents a different pattern. Evidence includes the Terengganu Inscription on the east coast, the Pengkalan Kempas stones inland, and the rise of Melaka. The political history belongs to Chapter 64; for religious history the significant point is the diversity of settings: a royal decree on the east coast, an inscription on a river route, and the cosmopolitan port of Melaka. The peninsula's Islamization appears to have involved multiple centers rather than the diffusion from a single capital.

North-coastal Java presents a third pattern. Here Muslim merchants, teachers, and local communities developed within the political world of Hindu-Buddhist Majapahit. Ma Huan's account and the 1419 Gresik epitaph provide different kinds of evidence for early-fifteenth-century Muslim activity. Near the court center, the religious identity of a 1368–1369 Trowulan stone is disputed, whereas an explicit 1469–1470 Muslim epitaph is associated with Troloyo. These points illuminate a mixed and changing social landscape, but not a single datable transformation of Java as a whole. By the early sixteenth century some north-coast polities had become important Muslim centers; inland conversion followed different trajectories.

Other regions complicate the picture further. Brunei, Sulu, and Mindanao are treated in Chapter 66, the Maluku sultanates in Chapter 71, and Makassar and the wider eastern archipelago in Chapter 82. For these areas, the dating of Muslim communities and courts is generally later or poorly documented before 1500, and the chapter makes no claim about them beyond what Chapter 66 and the later chapters examine. Bali remained Hindu-Buddhist, and uplands across Sumatra, Borneo, and elsewhere continued to follow diverse religious traditions.

The comparison supports three conclusions. First, the earliest dated evidence is not always the best guide to the importance of Islam in a given society. Second, the pattern of Islamization depended on the interplay of local politics, commerce, and learning, so no single model fits all regions. Third, large parts of the archipelago cannot be classified as Muslim or non-Muslim around 1500 because the evidence is inadequate.

Comparison also requires attention to who produced the evidence and why. The prominence of northern Sumatra in the record reflects not only early Muslim activity but also the survival of stones and the later interest of Malay and Acehnese traditions in celebrating Pasai. The prominence of Java's saints reflects the strength of Javanese historical memory and shrine culture. Regions without such traditions or durable monuments appear thin in the record, and historians must guard against treating the visibility of evidence as a measure of historical importance.

Section 65.14

Islam and the Cultural World of Java

Java offers the most elaborate case study of how Islam entered a society with deep Hindu-Buddhist traditions and a powerful court culture. By the fourteenth century Java was home to a sophisticated agrarian and commercial society with centers of political authority, temple complexes, courtly literature, and ritual specialists (Chapters 50 and 51). Muslim communities formed within this world, not outside it, and their history cannot be separated from its social and religious setting.

Ma Huan's account of Java in 1433 is a key contemporary source. He described Javanese ports, including Gresik, Tuban, and Surabaya, as places where several populations lived. As he reported, these included Muslim merchants who had come from the west, Chinese settlers some of whom were Muslim, and indigenous Javanese whose practices he viewed unfavorably. His account shows religious and ethnic variety at the ports and suggests that Muslim communities were established there, but it also shows Ma Huan's own perspective as a Chinese Muslim visitor. It says less about the religious life of Javanese inland.

The funerary evidence near Majapahit's center requires unusually careful reading. A Trowulan stone dated 1368–1369 has often been identified as an early Muslim marker, but its original function and religious attribution remain uncertain. At Troloyo, the explicitly Muslim epitaph naming Zain ud Din and recording 874 AH (1469–1470) is the most securely interpretable case; Hadi Sidomulyo argues that earlier stones in the complex cannot simply be assigned to Muslim burials. Other inscriptions may date monuments or structures rather than provide identifiable epitaphs. The broader presence of Muslim people in the Majapahit era remains plausible and is supported by other testimony, but neither a disputed stone nor a later saint tradition provides a census of the court's subjects.

Sacred space is another source of evidence. Tombs of venerated figures became sites of pilgrimage and veneration, a practice with parallels in earlier Javanese traditions of honoring ancestors and holy persons. Mosque architecture used tiered roofs, and some religious complexes incorporated gateways and carved decoration that resemble earlier Javanese forms. The Kudus mosque and minaret, a sixteenth-century complex, are frequently cited examples. These features suggest that Muslim communities drew on the resources of Javanese craft and ritual culture. They should not be read as proof that Islam was merely a veneer over earlier religion: builders use familiar forms to create new meanings.

The political context was changing. As Chapter 52 explains, Majapahit fragmented over the fifteenth century, and coastal polities gained greater autonomy. Muslim coastal rulers and communities benefited from commerce and from relationships with Melaka and the wider Indian Ocean. But the transition of inland Java to predominantly Muslim identity occurred over a much longer period, and later scholars such as M. C. Ricklefs have emphasized that Javanese religious identities continued to evolve for centuries, extending well beyond the fifteenth century.

Section 65.15

The Wali Sanga, Islamic Teaching, and Javanese Religious Literature

Javanese tradition remembers the spread of Islam through the Wali Sanga, the "Nine Saints" or "Nine Guardians." Accounts vary as to which figures belong in the group, but names frequently associated with it include Maulana Malik Ibrahim, Sunan Ampel, Sunan Bonang, Sunan Giri, Sunan Drajat, Sunan Kalijaga, Sunan Kudus, Sunan Muria, and Sunan Gunung Jati. Their tombs are sites of veneration and pilgrimage, and the stories of their lives, miracles, and teachings form a rich body of tradition about the coming of Islam to Java.

Historians treat these traditions with careful source criticism. The principal narratives, found in Javanese chronicles and hagiographical texts, were composed or compiled long after the fifteenth century, and they reflect the concerns of later communities, including the legitimation of dynasties, shrines, and religious lineages. Individual saints' dates, deeds, and relationships are not uniformly established by contemporary evidence. The group of nine may itself be a later schematization. Some figures are associated with dated tombs, such as Malik Ibrahim's gravestone at Gresik, but the connection between a dated stone and the saint of later tradition is an interpretation, not a demonstrated fact. Research on Java's religious history also cautions against projecting later categories of religious identity backward into the fifteenth century.

This does not make the traditions historically worthless. They show how Javanese communities explained their own religious origins, which saints were honored, and how Islam was integrated into Javanese cultural memory: the saints are commonly depicted as teachers who worked through persuasion, example, and adaptation of local arts. Whether any particular saint used a particular art form, such as shadow-puppet performance, is a matter of later tradition, and the claims should be treated as evidence of how later Javanese remembered the relationship between Islam and local culture.

More secure evidence for Javanese Islamic intellectual life comes from texts. One is the Admonitions of Seh Bari, a Javanese Muslim religious work attributed to the figure remembered as Sunan Bonang, edited and translated by G. W. J. Drewes in 1969. The surviving text belongs to the sixteenth century, and the attribution to Bonang is traditional. Its significance for this chapter lies in what it shows: a mature Javanese-language Islamic teaching that uses Arabic terms alongside Javanese vocabulary to discuss God, the self, and the path of spiritual instruction.

Later sources associate certain centers with particular religious influence. Giri, near Gresik, appears in later Javanese and external sources as a religious center whose teachers and rulers had wide connections in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, and Demak is remembered as a leading Muslim court of the early sixteenth century. The extent of their influence, and its relationship to earlier fifteenth-century developments, belong largely to the chapters on early modern Java (Chapter 83), and this chapter notes them only as evidence that the Javanese north coast was becoming a center of Muslim religious and political life by the end of the period.

Section 65.16

Malay and Javanese Religious Literature, Storytelling, and Cultural Expression

Literature carried Islam into imagination and memory. Malay and Javanese writers adapted Islamic narratives, ethical teaching, and legendary histories, producing works that circulated at courts and, through recitation, among wider audiences. Malay tales of the Prophet and early Muslim heroes, stories of earlier prophets, and romances of figures such as Alexander the Great (Iskandar Zulkarnain) in Islamic form became part of Malay literary culture. In Java, Islamic stories such as those of Amir Hamzah were adapted into Javanese, and religious poetry and teaching texts developed alongside older Javanese literary traditions.

Court chronicles also narrated religious history. The Malay Hikayat Raja-raja Pasai tells of the conversion of Pasai's first ruler through a dream, and the Sejarah Melayu relates the conversion of Melaka's ruler and the exchange of questions with Pasai scholars. These accounts are examples of how courts explained their Islamic origins. Their composition and surviving manuscripts are generally later than the events described: the Sejarah Melayu in its best-known form belongs to the seventeenth century, while the Hikayat Raja-raja Pasai is dated variously and survives in later manuscripts. They are evidence of literary and political memory, not eyewitness accounts of conversion.

Historians distinguish among several stages in the life of a text. Composition is the creation of the work; copying is its transmission through manuscripts, which could add, omit, or alter material; attribution is the naming of an author, often a retrospective act that links a text to a venerated figure; and editorial reconstruction is the modern scholarly effort to establish a text from variant manuscripts. A work attributed to a fifteenth-century figure but preserved in a later manuscript may preserve older material, reflect later revision, or both, and only careful textual study can assess which.

Performance mattered as much as writing. Much religious storytelling, recitation, and teaching was oral, and performance traditions such as wayang puppetry in Java adapted stories to new religious settings. The sources for early performance are limited, and later traditions about saints' use of such arts must be treated with caution. What the evidence does show is that Islamic themes entered the narrative repertoire of Malay and Javanese societies and were expressed through local literary forms. These developments were not superficial adaptations; the translation of religious ideas into local idioms was itself a creative intellectual achievement, and it made Islam intelligible and meaningful to audiences beyond those who read Arabic.

The survival of literature is itself a historical problem. Palm-leaf, bark, and paper manuscripts decay quickly in tropical climates, and many works were recopied only when communities valued them. The Malay and Javanese manuscripts that survive were mostly copied in later centuries, often in courts, religious schools, or colonial-era collections. The literature of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries is therefore largely lost or preserved only through later copies, and any account of early Islamic literary culture must be reconstructed from fragments, inscriptions, and later works that may preserve earlier material.

Division V — Continuity, Religious Diversity, Historical Interpretation, and Synthesis
Section 65.17

Continuity and Change in Malay-Indonesian Religious Life

The arrival of Muslim communities changed religious life in important ways, but it did not erase what came before. Understanding the relationship requires attention to both transformation and persistence, and to the ways in which new Islamic identities could alter the meaning of older practices without eliminating them.

Change was most visible in institutions and public ritual. Mosques and Muslim cemeteries appeared in ports and at courts. Rulers adopted Islamic titles and issued decrees in Islamic terms. Legal and ritual norms, including prayer, fasting, and Islamic burial, were introduced into communities. New written languages and scripts, especially Jawi, developed. Religious learning acquired new content, and texts, teaching, and stories were produced in Malay and Javanese with Islamic subject matter. Communal membership began to be defined, for some, by participation in Islamic worship and law.

Continuity was equally important. Kinship structures, village organization, agricultural cycles, and many customary obligations persisted. Courts retained much of their ceremonial vocabulary and conceptions of royal power. Rituals surrounding birth, marriage, death, and harvest continued, often reinterpreted in Islamic terms or combined with new practices. Languages retained a rich stock of Sanskrit-derived religious vocabulary that Muslims continued to use, including words that became standard in Malay and Indonesian for religious concepts. Beliefs in spirits and ancestral power persisted in many communities alongside Islamic belief, and those who held them did not necessarily see a contradiction.

A crucial interpretive point concerns sincerity. The persistence of earlier practices has sometimes been presented as proof that Islamic identity was shallow. This reasoning is flawed. Religious communities everywhere combine inherited practices and new teachings, and the persistence of earlier customs does not by itself reveal the depth of belief. Moreover, new Islamic identities could reshape the meaning of old practices: a meal for the dead might be understood as an Islamic act of remembrance and charity, and a ruler's ceremonial might be understood as the dignity of a Muslim sovereign. At the same time, some Muslim teachers criticized certain practices, and reformist debates over proper practice recur in later centuries. Contestation, not simple accommodation, characterized religious life.

Finally, historians should avoid projecting later categories onto the fifteenth century. Distinctions such as santri and abangan, used in twentieth-century studies of Java to contrast more strictly observant Muslims with those who combined Islam with other practices, did not exist in the same form then, and M. C. Ricklefs's work shows that Javanese religious distinctions changed substantially over time. The fifteenth-century situation was more fluid.

Section 65.18

Why Did Islam Spread? Historical Explanations and Debates

Historians have proposed several explanations for the spread of Islam in the Malay-Indonesian world. Each captures part of the evidence, and each has limitations.

The trade explanation emphasizes the substantial role of Muslim merchants in many Indian Ocean routes and the potential advantages of joining their commercial and social networks. Their influence varied by port and period, and the trading world also included substantial Hindu, Buddhist, Chinese, and other non-Muslim participation; no single religious community dominated every route. Trade helps explain the concentration of early Islamic evidence in ports and why some rulers cultivated Muslim partners. But commercial contact alone does not explain why Islamic institutions took root in particular families, coastal neighborhoods, and courts while others maintained different religious identities. A merchant network supplied opportunities for relationships; conversion required choices and social processes that cannot be reduced to the movement of goods.

The marriage and family explanation points to intermarriage and household formation as mechanisms of community growth, as discussed in Section 65.8. It is plausible and consistent with Islamic law but difficult to measure. The Sufi explanation, associated with the influential work of A. H. Johns, emphasizes the appeal of mystical teachers, and later critics, notably Michael Laffan, show how this image was constructed from later sources. The scholarship explanation points to the role of Islamic learning, teachers, and texts in providing a durable religious framework. The local political choice explanation stresses that rulers and communities may have adopted Islam to gain advantage against rivals or to claim a distinct identity, and the cultural adaptation explanation stresses that Islam became established because it was translated into local languages and institutions.

A separate debate concerns origins. Proposals have placed the principal source of Southeast Asian Islam in Arabia and the Hadramawt, in Gujarat, in the Coromandel and Malabar coasts, in Persia, or in Chinese Muslim communities. Scholars such as G. W. J. Drewes questioned some of the older confident claims, and Syed Muhammad Naquib al-Attas argued for an earlier and more directly Arabian contribution. Most current scholarship accepts multiple sources and long-term contact, and emphasizes that debates over a single origin matter less than understanding how communities adopted and adapted Islam.

Narratives of peaceful, uniform, or inevitable Islamization are also problematic. Peaceful conversion by example and persuasion is attested in many traditions, but the sources for fifteenth-century violence or coercion are limited, and later periods did see warfare between Muslim and non-Muslim polities, discussed in later chapters. Describing the process as uniform ignores regional variation; describing it as inevitable ignores the contingency of rulers' choices, the survival of Hindu-Buddhist Bali, and the long delay in many regions. A responsible account recognizes multiple causes operating together.

Section 65.19

The Malay-Indonesian Religious World around 1500

By about 1500, the religious landscape of maritime Southeast Asia included societies at very different stages. A useful way to organize the evidence is to distinguish several categories, with the understanding that boundaries are fuzzy and the evidence incomplete. The categories also relate to the regional context surveyed in Chapter 67.

The first category consists of established Muslim courts with institutional support for Islam. Samudra-Pasai in northern Sumatra and Melaka on the peninsula belong here, with documented rulers, mosques, scholars, and legal learning (Chapters 63 and 64). Along Java's north coast, the ports of Gresik, Tuban, and others had Muslim communities and, increasingly, Muslim lords, though political and religious authority was in flux. The Muslim courts at Brunei, treated in Chapter 66, may also be placed in this broad category by the end of the period, subject to the evidence discussed there.

The second category consists of substantial Muslim communities in religiously mixed settings. Many port towns fit this description: Muslim merchant quarters coexisted with non-Muslim populations under rulers whose own religious commitments varied. The Javanese ports described by Ma Huan and Pires illustrate this pattern, as do some peninsular and Sumatran ports, though evidence differs by place.

The third category comprises regions whose documented courts and much of their populations remained outside Islam. Bali, the Sundanese interior of western Java, and many upland societies of Sumatra and Borneo followed different religious trajectories from those of some coastal ports. Bali is particularly useful as a comparison: its rulers, temples, ritual specialists, and local communities maintained and reshaped Hindu-Buddhist and ancestral practices through changing relations with Java. Its geographical proximity to Javanese maritime routes did not predetermine a Muslim political outcome. Evidence for the belief of every village remains uneven, and continuity must not be mistaken for an absence of historical change or contact.

The fourth category is regions where evidence is inadequate. The eastern archipelago and parts of Sulawesi, Borneo, and the Philippines fit here for the period before 1500, as discussed in Chapters 66, 71, 72, 76, and 82. Scholars should resist both assuming early Islamization and assuming its absence. Where later sources describe Muslim rulers, those sources are to be used cautiously for earlier periods.

Two further points set context for later chapters. First, the Portuguese capture of Melaka in 1511 (Chapter 69) did not end Islamization; the dispersal of Muslim traders and scholars helped sustain and in some respects accelerate it, as later chapters on Aceh, Johor, Java, and Makassar examine. Second, many of the most visible transformations of Islamic institutions, scholarship, and mass conversion took place after 1500. The period this chapter studies is better understood as the formative phase of a longer process than as its conclusion.

Section 65.20

Islamization and the Malay-Indonesian World: A Historical Synthesis

The chapter has argued that Islamization was a set of overlapping processes: the arrival of Muslim merchants and teachers, the adoption of Islam by rulers, the formation of Muslim communities, the development of religious learning, and the embedding of Islamic categories in language, law, and culture. These processes did not proceed in lockstep. A port might have a Muslim ruler and a small Muslim community; a village might practice Islamic burial customs generations before its ruler converted; a region might host Muslim traders for centuries without becoming Muslim.

Chronology resists simple summary. The earliest documented Muslim presence in northern Sumatra dates to the late thirteenth century, but dated stones, inscriptions, and travelers' accounts widen the evidence during the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries across Sumatra, the peninsula, and Java. These dates mark visibility, not completion. No universal conversion date exists, and the study of each region requires its own evidence.

Geography shaped opportunities without dictating outcomes. Ports near the Strait of Malacca and along Java's north coast were exposed to Indian Ocean contacts, while many inland and upland communities followed different trajectories. Bali offers a contrasting pattern of sustained and evolving Hindu-Buddhist and local ritual institutions under rulers who made their own political choices; geographical proximity to Islamizing ports is insufficient to explain its history. These varied results, rather than an inevitable movement in one direction, show how social institutions, patronage, and local decisions shaped religious change.

Southeast Asians were the central agents. Rulers chose titles and patrons; merchants moved between religious worlds; families raised children and arranged marriages; teachers adapted instruction to local languages; scribes created Jawi and adapted Javanese writing; and communities negotiated the relationship between Islamic law and adat. Wider Islamic connections mattered, but they became historically significant because local societies engaged with them.

The result was a body of institutions and cultural forms that were Islamic and Southeast Asian at once: mosques with local architecture, legal cultures combining Islamic and customary elements, a script adapted for Malay, devotional and literary traditions in Malay and Javanese, and historical memories such as the Wali Sanga. To describe these as superficial adaptations misunderstands how religions develop in new settings.

Finally, the process was neither completed by 1500 nor uniform. Many regions changed after the chapter's endpoint, and the following chapters, from Brunei and the northern networks (Chapter 66) to Portuguese Melaka, Aceh, Makassar, and Mataram, show later stages. The synthesis for students is that Islamization is best understood as an extended, multi-causal historical process shaped by local choices as much as outside influence.

Historical Synthesis

Five Principles for Understanding Islamization

  1. Presence, patronage, and society are different. Muslim merchants, Muslim rulers, and predominantly Muslim societies are distinct historical facts.
  2. Evidence determines the claim. Stones, travelers' accounts, archaeology, and later texts each support different conclusions.
  3. Causes operated together. Trade, patronage, family, teaching, and local choice all mattered, in varying proportions.
  4. Adaptation was creative. Language, law, architecture, and literature show local agency, not superficiality.
  5. The process was uneven and incomplete by 1500. Regional diversity and later developments are essential to the story.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 65 has examined how Islam developed from a religion associated with particular merchants, rulers, and port communities into an influential religious and cultural tradition in the Malay-Indonesian world between about 1200 and 1500. Its central argument is that Islamization was an extended, uneven, multi-causal process in which Southeast Asian communities helped shape the institutions they adopted.

The first division addressed evidence and chronology. Islamization was defined as several overlapping processes: individual conversion, court adoption, community formation, religious learning, and cultural institutionalization. Dated inscriptions and gravestones, such as the Samudra stone and the Minye Tujuh monument, show Muslim commemorative practice but cannot reveal the beliefs of whole populations. Travelers' accounts by Marco Polo, Ibn Battuta, and Ma Huan, and archaeological evidence from cemeteries, supply further information, while later chronicles preserve memory rather than contemporary testimony.

Geography shaped exposure to Muslim traders and teachers. Ports at the northern entrance of the Strait of Malacca and on Java's north coast were especially open to Indian Ocean contact, while interiors and uplands often experienced slower or different change. Because early evidence clusters in ports, the absence of evidence in the interior should not be read as the absence of Muslims.

The second division examined conversion and community formation. Rulers adopted Islamic titles, law, and patronage, gaining legitimacy and commercial connections, but their conversion did not automatically convert their subjects. Merchants, teachers, and local communities interacted in ways that involved negotiation and translation. Families and generations were the most durable channel of religious continuity, with intermarriage and household formation contributing, though not uniformly.

The third division explored learning, law, and language. Islamic ritual and legal learning, including the Shafi‘i tradition documented at Samudra by Ibn Battuta, provided institutions for communal life. Sufi teachers were significant, but scholars now caution against treating Sufism as the sole explanation. Arabic provided the religious core, Jawi adapted Arabic letters for Malay as seen in the Terengganu Inscription, and earlier scripts persisted, as at Minye Tujuh. Islamic law interacted with adat in ways that varied by region.

The fourth division examined regional adaptation. Northern Sumatra, the Malay Peninsula, and north-coastal Java each present distinct patterns of evidence. In Java, Muslim communities developed in ports and near the Majapahit core, expressing Islam through local craft, sacred space, and language. The Wali Sanga traditions preserve Javanese memory but are later and require source criticism; texts such as the Admonitions of Seh Bari show the intellectual culture that developed in Javanese. Malay and Javanese literature embedded Islamic narratives in local forms.

The fifth division addressed continuity, interpretation, and synthesis. Religious life changed in institutions, law, language, and public ritual, while kinship, village life, court ceremony, and many customs persisted, often reinterpreted. Surviving earlier practices do not prove superficial Islam. Explanations for the spread of Islam, whether trade, royal conversion, marriage, Sufism, scholarship, local political choice, or cultural adaptation, each capture part of the evidence, and narratives of peaceful, uniform, or inevitable conversion are inadequate.

By about 1500, established Muslim courts, substantial Muslim communities in mixed settings, predominantly non-Muslim societies, and regions with inadequate evidence coexisted in maritime Southeast Asia. Many major transformations occurred after the chapter's endpoint, as later chapters on Aceh, Java, Makassar, and the northern maritime networks show.

The chapter's significance lies in its demonstration that outside traditions became historically significant in Southeast Asia because local societies adopted, translated, and transformed them. This conclusion continues the argument of Chapters 1 and 19 and prepares the ground for the study of Brunei, Sulu, and Mindanao in Chapter 66 and the early modern developments of Volume V.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it changes the question historians ask. Chapters 62 through 64 explained how Muslim merchants, courts, and ports entered the political and commercial history of maritime Southeast Asia. Chapter 65 asks what it meant for ordinary and elite communities to become Muslim, how religious institutions took root, and how Islam became a tradition expressed in Malay and Javanese. The answer shows that religious change is a social and intellectual history, not only a political or commercial one.

The chapter also demonstrates a method. The same stone, text, or tradition can support very different conclusions depending on how it is read. A gravestone shows commemoration, not the beliefs of a population; a chronicle shows memory, not eyewitness testimony; a sixteenth-century treatise shows intellectual culture, not the careers of earlier teachers. Learning to match claims to evidence is one of the most transferable skills of historical study.

The developments studied here shaped later centuries in important ways. The Malay and Javanese Islamic literatures, the legal cultures that combined Islamic and customary norms, and the networks of teachers and students established in this period provided foundations for the sultanates of Aceh, Johor, Banten, Makassar, and Mataram, and for debates over reform and identity that continued into the modern era. Yet the chapter's central caution is that none of these outcomes was inevitable by 1500.

Above all, the chapter reinforces the textbook's larger argument. Southeast Asia has never been a passive crossroads. Rulers, merchants, teachers, families, scribes, and villagers selected, translated, and transformed what reached them, and in doing so made Islam a Southeast Asian tradition as well as a world religion. Chapter 66 turns to the northern maritime networks of Brunei, Sulu, and Mindanao, where similar questions arise in a different political and geographic setting.
