Chapter Opening

The Dutch East India Company did not simply fade into a colonial government. Its charter lapsed, its debts and territories passed to a European state, and over the next quarter-century that state, Napoleonic, British, and Dutch in turn, tried to turn a commercial empire of ports, monopolies, and treaties into a territorial administration, while still depending on the same Javanese regents, village heads, laborers, soldiers, and taxes on which the Company had relied.

On 31 December 1799 the charter under which the Verenigde Oostindische Compagnie (VOC, the United East India Company) had operated since 1602 reached its end and was not renewed. For almost two centuries the Company had combined the functions of a trading firm and a government: it signed treaties, fortified harbors, garrisoned soldiers, collected tolls and taxes, administered justice in Batavia and other settlements, and enforced monopolies on spices and other commodities. When the charter expired, no single dramatic transfer of power was visible on the streets of Batavia. Company officials continued to hold their posts, Company soldiers remained in their barracks, and Javanese regents continued to deliver rice, coffee, and labor. What had changed was the legal source of authority: a European state, the Batavian Republic, now claimed the Company's possessions, obligations, and responsibilities.

This chapter examines what that change meant, and what it did not. It begins with the crisis of the late-eighteenth-century VOC and its financial and political weaknesses. It then follows four overlapping developments between about 1780 and 1825: the institutional and financial collapse of the Company; the wars of the French Revolution and Napoleon, which reshaped Dutch sovereignty and exposed Java to attack; the British conquest of Java in 1811 and the administration of Thomas Stamford Raffles; and the Dutch restoration after 1816. Running through all four is the question of how a state claiming sovereignty over the Indonesian archipelago converted that claim into functioning authority.

The chapter's governing argument is that formal state sovereignty expanded faster than effective colonial control. By 1825 the Dutch government in Batavia possessed more formal institutions, a larger claim to territory, and broader ambitions than the VOC had ever articulated. Yet it still governed largely through Indonesian intermediaries, depended on coerced labor and extractive taxation, and faced rulers, communities, and individuals who contested its authority, from the courts of Central Java to the villages of the Moluccas. The "colonial state" that emerged was therefore a prolonged and contested reorganization rather than a clean break.

Evidence for this history is abundant but uneven. The VOC and the Dutch state left extensive archival records in Batavia and the Netherlands, including resolutions, letters, accounts, and decrees. British administrators produced reports, minutes, surveys, and Raffles's published scholarship. Javanese perspectives survive in court chronicles (babad), letters, and later memoirs, including the Javanese account of the 1812 attack on Yogyakarta analyzed by Peter Carey. Moluccan voices are harder to recover, because much evidence on the 1817 revolt comes through Dutch investigations and trial records, and must be read critically. Throughout, this chapter asks whose perspective each source represents and whose is missing.

Indonesian societies were not passive recipients of European decisions. Javanese rulers negotiated, manipulated, and resisted; regents and village heads decided how far to cooperate; farmers and laborers evaded, fled, or bargained; merchants, including the Chinese communities of Java's ports, adapted to shifting fiscal regimes; and in the Moluccas, soldiers and village communities rose against a restored authority they had not agreed to. The chapter emphasizes these responses alongside European policy.

The chapter fits between the Vietnamese dynastic upheavals of Chapter 99 and the great conflict of Chapter 101. It builds on the VOC's rise and violence treated in Chapters 77 through 79 and on the contest for Java examined in Chapter 83, and it stops at the threshold of the Java War, whose full history belongs to Chapter 101. The compulsory export-crop system that would follow is the subject of Chapter 102, and the later territorial expansion beyond Java is examined in Chapter 103. Its British dimension also links to Chapter 105 on Penang, Singapore, and the Straits Settlements.

  5. Assess Herman Willem Daendels's reforms, distinguishing declared objectives from policies actually implemented.

Historical Context

The VOC was chartered in 1602 by the States General of the Dutch Republic, which granted it a monopoly over Dutch trade east of the Cape of Good Hope together with powers to make war and peace, build fortresses, and conclude treaties in Asia. Its supreme governing body in the Netherlands was the board of the Heren XVII (Seventeen Gentlemen), drawn from six regional chambers: Amsterdam, Zeeland, Delft, Rotterdam, Hoorn, and Enkhuizen. In Asia, the Governor-General and the Council of the Indies governed from Batavia, which the Company had founded in 1619 on the site of the Javanese-Banten port of Jayakarta (see Chapter 79). Subordinate governors and commanders ran establishments elsewhere, including Ambon, Ternate, Banda, Makassar, Melaka, Padang, Ceylon, and the north coast of Java.

By the mid-eighteenth century the Company's position in Java was more territorial than its commercial origins suggested. After the wars of succession in Mataram, the VOC had gained control over much of the north coast (the Pasisir) and became the arbiter among Javanese rulers. The Treaty of Giyanti in 1755 divided the old Mataram realm between Surakarta and Yogyakarta, and a further division created the Mangkunegaran principality in 1757 (examined in Chapter 83). Meanwhile the Company drew revenue and produce from the Priangan highlands of West Java through compulsory coffee deliveries organized through Sundanese regents, and from the north coast through forced deliveries of rice and other goods, leases of tolls and markets, and trade monopolies. Beyond Java, spice monopolies in the Moluccas relied on treaties with local rulers and on the violence documented in Chapter 78.

Company government rested on a distinctive blend of European and Indonesian institutions. European officials known as Residents represented the Company at courts and in districts; Javanese and Sundanese regents (bupati) supervised local populations, mobilized labor, and delivered produce; village heads managed household obligations; and Chinese merchants and tax-farmers operated tolls, markets, and credit. The Company's armies combined European, Asian, and local troops. Authority depended on negotiation with Indonesian elites as much as on the Company's own firepower. Students should remember this pattern, since the successor governments of 1800–1825 inherited both its practices and its limits.

The wider European context was one of revolution and war. The Dutch Republic, once a major commercial and naval power, had lost relative strength to Britain in the eighteenth century. The Fourth Anglo-Dutch War (1780–1784) delivered a severe blow to the Company's shipping and finances. Then, in 1795, French revolutionary armies and Dutch Patriot allies overturned the old Republic and created the Batavian Republic, ending the stadtholder regime. These European upheavals were the immediate setting in which the VOC's crisis became its dissolution, and they supplied the pressures, French and British, that shaped the Indies for the next two decades.

Division I

The Crisis and Dissolution of the VOC

Central question: Why did the VOC collapse, and what did its disappearance change about colonial government?

Section 100.1

The VOC in the Late Eighteenth Century: Commercial Empire under Strain

By the 1780s the VOC held a patchwork of possessions rather than a continuous empire. Batavia and the north coast of Java were the core of its Indonesian holdings, supplemented by coffee-producing districts in the Priangan, trading posts and fortresses on Sumatra's coasts including Padang, Makassar in Sulawesi, and a chain of positions in the Moluccas. Further afield it held Melaka, Ceylon, the Cape, and establishments in India. Each place had been acquired in different circumstances: by conquest, by treaty with a local ruler, by lease, or by agreements that gave the Company rights over trade rather than land. Understanding the VOC as a unified territorial state would therefore be misleading.

The Company's income depended on monopolies and on compulsory arrangements. It bought pepper, spices, coffee, and other goods at fixed prices through contracts with Indonesian rulers and regents, and it sold them in Europe and Asia at a profit when markets permitted. In the Moluccas, as Chapter 78 showed, the monopoly rested on violent enforcement, restrictions on cultivation, and forced deliveries. In Java, the Company relied on obligations owed through regents, who organized deliveries from villages and in turn retained privileges and income. This was not the "free trade" of a merchant firm; it was a system of political bargains and coercions that gave a commercial company the characteristics of a regional power.

This is the essential distinction for the chapter: a commercial corporation exists to trade for profit under legal rules, whereas a company exercising governmental authority also administers territory, collects taxes, maintains armies, and makes agreements binding on populations. The VOC performed both functions at once, and its commercial accounts were encumbered by the costs of governing. When it fell, the question was therefore not only who would pay its debts, but who would inherit the sovereignty, treaties, garrisons, and dependent communities that came with them.

A map of the company's establishments makes their diversity clearer than the word empire does. In Batavia the governor-general directed a dense urban bureaucracy and a port connected to the commerce of the Java Sea. In the Priangan highlands, cultivation and coffee deliveries depended upon Sundanese regents who commanded obligations from villages. At the Javanese courts, Dutch residents acted within a complicated inheritance of agreements, succession disputes and ritual relationships. In islands where cloves or nutmeg grew, monopoly enforcement rested on locally specific arrangements and repeated coercive interventions. Elsewhere a fortified factory might claim privileges without commanding the countryside beyond its guns. Such differences shaped the future government's problems: it inherited legal papers and officers but no single uniform procedure for ruling all these places. The distance between Amsterdam and Batavia, and between Batavia and its dependent districts, magnified reliance upon intermediaries.

Company sovereignty also had a social geography. European merchants, soldiers and administrators were a minority, while Asian soldiers, boatmen, clerks, artisans, enslaved workers and merchants sustained the settlements. Chinese merchants and tax-farmers played major roles in credit, retail trade and the collection of tolls; Javanese and Sundanese notables supplied political authority and rural manpower; Makassarese, Malay and other seafarers connected the ports. The company could impose violence, but its day-to-day operations depended upon people whose interests did not simply mirror the shareholders'. Contracts that looked like fixed commercial arrangements on paper could become contested obligations in villages. The later state would confront the same difficulty when it claimed that its revenue rules were more regular and impartial: the practical work of extraction still passed through local hierarchies. Therefore a history of the VOC's end must reconstruct not only a European balance sheet but also the government and society incorporated into that balance sheet.

Historical Vocabulary
Chartered corporation

Definition. A chartered corporation was a body created and empowered by a sovereign authority through a charter, which defined its monopolies, privileges, and obligations for a stated period.

Historical context. The VOC's charter of 1602 from the States General granted exclusive Dutch trading rights east of the Cape and powers that ordinary firms did not possess, including making war and peace, building fortresses, and appointing governors and judges. Charters were renewed periodically; the one that lapsed on 31 December 1799 was the last.

Why it matters. The charter explains both the Company's power and its vulnerability. Because its authority was delegated, the state could withdraw it, as the Batavian Republic did.

Potential misunderstanding. The modern term "company" suggests a private business. The VOC was quasi-public, owned by shareholders yet endowed with governmental powers, and should not be mistaken for either a purely private firm or a fully developed state.

Section 100.2

War, Debt, Corruption, and Company Finance

Historians have long debated why the VOC declined, and the best explanations combine several causes. A first cause was the cost of governing. Fortresses, garrisons, ships, and the administrative apparatus of a territorial presence required continuous expenditure, and the Company's military commitments in Java, the Moluccas, Sumatra, and elsewhere grew over the eighteenth century. A second cause was competition: British and other European traders gained ground in Asian markets, and the Company's monopoly positions in some commodities, notably pepper and textiles, eroded. A third was a changing product mix: the profitable spice trade of earlier centuries gave way to lower-margin goods such as coffee, tea, and textiles, for which price competition was stronger.

The Fourth Anglo-Dutch War (1780–1784) acted as a major shock rather than a sole cause. British naval pressure disrupted the Company's shipping between Asia and Europe, captured or blocked vessels, and cut off markets and remittances. The Treaty of Paris of 1784 ended the war on terms that gave British traders improved access to waters the Dutch had tried to restrict, and the Company emerged with weakened finances and fewer ships. In the years that followed, the VOC borrowed heavily and continued to pay dividends to shareholders even when its trading profits did not justify them, a practice that deepened its dependence on loans and, eventually, on state support.

Corruption was real, but it was not the whole story. Company servants earned modest official salaries and supplemented them through private trade, gifts, and the exploitation of office, practices that contemporaries often tolerated or even assumed, and that criticism by reformers later magnified. Private enrichment reduced the revenue reaching the Company and encouraged weak accounting, yet the scale of the debt, the wartime losses, and the cost of garrisons had structural origins that honest administration alone could not have solved. Environmental and health pressures in Batavia, including malaria and the silting of canals, added to mortality among Europeans and to the expense of maintaining the capital. By the 1790s the Company's accounts could be sustained only by borrowing, and the total liabilities, by most scholarly accounts, came to well over one hundred million guilders. Explaining the collapse therefore requires attention to the interaction of war, debt, competition, the costs of governance, and imperfect administration.

The language of decline conceals an important question about how loss was measured. A monopoly purchase could yield a handsome gross return in Europe while requiring expensive garrisons, transport and credit in Asia. Sales recorded at a favorable price did not automatically generate liquid money in Batavia, where troops and officials needed provisions and salaries immediately. Ships waited for winds and convoys, wartime blockades interrupted remittances, and a loss in one trading circuit might leave obligations in another unpaid. Private traders could also redirect valuable cargoes outside authorized channels, though their conduct did not explain every shortfall. Historians consequently examine cash flow, borrowing and the costs of political power alongside profits on individual products. A debt total is meaningful only if its date, accounting categories and conversion into later currency are specified. The familiar figure above one hundred million guilders should be treated as an approximate late-company liability, not an audited amount valid for every year in the 1790s.

War sharpened these vulnerabilities while altering the political choices available in the Netherlands. The British navy could seize isolated stations and choke maritime communications; creditors and investors simultaneously demanded action from the Dutch authorities. Reform was difficult because reducing expensive defenses could expose overseas holdings to rival powers, while abandoning trading privileges might surrender the revenue used to finance government. Even a successful attempt to dismiss corrupt officials would not solve the need for an expensive Asian administration. The state's assumption of company obligations was therefore not merely a rescue prompted by mismanagement. It was also an assertion that Dutch overseas commerce and colonial claims had become matters of public finance and international rivalry. The old corporation's disappearance reflected an increasingly uncomfortable union of shareholder interest, military empire and sovereign responsibility.

Section 100.3

The Batavian Revolution and the Dissolution of the VOC, 1795–1799

In January 1795, French revolutionary forces entered the Netherlands and supporters of the Patriot movement proclaimed the Batavian Republic, ending the stadtholdership of William V, who fled to England. The change at the Dutch center had immediate consequences for the Indies. The stadtholder, from exile, issued instructions (the so-called Kew letters) calling on colonial governors to cooperate with Britain against France, and British forces took over several Dutch possessions in 1795 and 1796, including Melaka, Padang, and positions in the Moluccas such as Ambon and Banda. Dutch authority in the archipelago was thus fractured by European politics within months of the Revolution.

The new Republic initially treated the VOC as a matter of national interest rather than a private firm. In 1795 it dismissed the old board of directors and created a committee to supervise Company affairs, and over the next several years it moved toward assuming control outright. The state's reasons were fiscal and political: the Company was insolvent in practical terms, its creditors included many prominent Dutch investors, and the revolutionary government saw colonial wealth and trade as national assets that should be answerable to the nation, not to a chartered oligarchy. Reform commissioners were sent to the East, but their efforts to reorganize Company government, reduce costs, and restore revenue had limited success.

The formal end came when the charter expired on 31 December 1799 and the state declined to renew it. The Company's possessions in Asia, its debts, its garrisons, and its administrative responsibilities passed to the Republic, administered after 1800 through a Council for the Asian Possessions and Establishments. The legal consequences were substantial. The state became answerable for the Company's creditors, inherited the treaties and obligations the Company had made with Indonesian rulers, and assumed the burden of governing communities whose relationship with the Company had been defined by contract, tribute, and coercion. What the state did not acquire was a ready-made colonial bureaucracy: it inherited a Company staff, Company habits, and Company debts.

The takeover was not completed by one decree issued in isolation. Revolutionary authorities investigated the company, established supervisory bodies and debated the responsibilities of its creditors and shareholders while war made the Asian possessions less accessible. The VOC's governing chambers and the Heren XVII had tied commercial privileges to a particular corporate organization; removing that organization raised questions about which prior decisions still applied. Officials in Batavia had to determine whether instructions transmitted under the old charter continued to have force, which treaties and revenue obligations the new sovereign would honor, and how to remit accounts to a distant and changing government. At the local level, the continuity of officeholders could make a dramatic legal change nearly invisible. This discrepancy between legal transformation and everyday continuity should be treated as evidence, not as proof that dissolution was insignificant.

An equally important transformation took place in the language used to justify rule. State committees increasingly characterized the Asian establishments as national possessions and sources of public prosperity. That language differed from the chartered company's formal orientation toward shareholder returns, but it did not entail political rights for the peoples subject to colonial government. Indonesian rulers were not parties to the Dutch constitutional changes; their understandings of older alliances and promises could conflict with metropolitan assertions that all company rights had automatically passed to the state. The Kew letters and British seizures demonstrated a further problem: different European governments asserted competing authority over the same ports during the revolutionary wars. A useful primary-source comparison would place Dutch state measures next to an actual agreement with an Indonesian ruler and ask whether the two documents describe sovereignty in the same terms. Often they did not.

Section 100.4

From Chartered Company to State Administration, 1800–1807

The first years of state rule were marked by continuity. Officials who had served the Company largely kept their posts, with new titles and a different legal master. Governors-General followed one another in rapid succession, with Pieter Gerardus van Overstraten, Johannes Siberg, and Albertus Wiese holding the office across the early 1800s, but the structure of government in Batavia and the districts changed little. The army, the taxation arrangements, the Company's leases of tolls and markets, and the forced-delivery systems remained in operation. The Council for the Asian Possessions in the Netherlands and the Batavian Republic's Charter for the Government and Administration of its East Indian Possessions, adopted in 1804 after a report in 1803, expressed a state-directed program, yet, as historians note, the substance of day-to-day government continued Company practice.

Several reasons explain this continuity. The state lacked the money to build a new bureaucracy, particularly during war, and the officials who knew local conditions were Company men. British sea power disrupted communication and trade, so that Batavia was effectively cut off from effective supervision, and the revenue on which any reform depended did not arrive. Meanwhile, Indonesian rulers and regents, who had made agreements with the Company, continued to deal with the same European officials, and had little reason to believe that their obligations or privileges had changed in principle. Peasants likewise experienced the transfer chiefly through the same demands for deliveries, services, and taxes.

The transition therefore did not produce a centralized territorial government. It produced a state formally sovereign in law but operating through a Company-era administrative inheritance, constrained by wartime isolation, and uncertain about what to do with the territories, treaties, and debts it had acquired. This gap between legal claim and administrative capacity is the central theme of the chapter, and it set the stage for the more forceful attempt at reconstruction that Daendels would make after 1808.

The charter prepared for the Batavian Republic's Asian possessions in 1804 is a revealing document because it gives the state takeover a programmatic rationale. A commission considered how administration should be constituted after the VOC; its report preceded a charter that defined the overseas territories as possessions of the state and linked their government to commerce and metropolitan finance. Reformers disagreed over how much existing company privilege and official discretion should be retained. The final design represented a compromise between more ambitious criticism of colonial abuses and the arguments of established officials who feared financial disruption. Yet a metropolitan charter was not a new census, court staff or revenue apparatus in the Indies. Instructions crossed an ocean dominated by Britain, and administrators on Java were left to make decisions with limited resources. The historian should distinguish the formulation of a constitutional ideal from its implementation in a local district.

Early state administration retained much of the VOC's social infrastructure. A local regent had obligations arising from political agreements and access to labor through kinship, office and village authority. A tax-farmer's claim to collect tolls might be backed by a contract, a guard and lenders who had advanced the purchase price of the farm. A village was affected when officials or creditors changed demands, not simply when a new European constitution was promulgated. These arrangements could reproduce exploitation while keeping essential government services functioning. Continuity also created conflict: state officials who claimed a more regular administrative order could question privileges their predecessors had guaranteed. The 1804 charter thus matters less as evidence that the old regime disappeared than as a statement of the new state's ambition to govern and profit from an inherited structure it had not yet mastered.

Central question: How did European warfare encourage administrative centralization and military reconstruction in Java?

Section 100.5

The Napoleonic Wars and the Dutch East Indies

The Dutch state itself changed several times during these years. The Batavian Republic gave way in 1806 to the Kingdom of Holland under Napoleon's brother Louis Bonaparte, and in 1810 the Netherlands were incorporated directly into the French Empire. Each change altered the legal chain of command above the Indies, but the more serious problem was strategic: Britain controlled the seas and could threaten or seize colonial possessions at will, as it had done in 1795–1796 and again in the early nineteenth century. The Dutch Indies were, in effect, a French-aligned colony that the French could not reach.

Java's strategic vulnerability thus became the central problem of colonial government. The island was productive, populous, and commercially valuable, and its loss to the British would deprive both the Dutch and the French of a major asset and give Britain a commanding position on the sea routes between India and China. British interest in Java was shaped by this logic and by the East India Company's concern for the China trade. Meanwhile, the wars disrupted the colony's commerce: exports could not reach European markets reliably, revenue shrank, and the colonial government faced mounting military and administrative costs without a secure supply of funds.

These conditions encouraged centralization. A government that could not rely on metropolitan help had to draw on local resources, and one expecting invasion had to build defenses, discipline its officials, and secure the cooperation or submission of Javanese rulers. This is the context in which Napoleon, through Louis Bonaparte, appointed Herman Willem Daendels to govern Java with extensive powers and a mandate to prepare for defense.

The political terminology of the period needs careful handling. Java passed from the rule of the Batavian Republic to the Kingdom of Holland and then into the orbit of the French Empire, but its institutions did not instantaneously become French departments each time power changed in Europe. Orders from the metropole took months to arrive, if they arrived at all. Administrators could invoke European law while deciding locally how to provision troops, defend harbors and keep taxes flowing. The British had secured important Dutch positions in the previous wars and seized Moluccan holdings again in 1810. This made the possibility of a full-scale assault on Java a practical planning problem, not a remote diplomatic abstraction. Military construction and tighter administrative direction flowed from that predicament.

Strategic isolation also altered the balance between colonial populations and their European governors. Armies required locally recruited soldiers and carriers; fortifications consumed timber, masonry and food; emergency fiscal measures could antagonize rulers whose cooperation defense depended upon. European decision-makers sometimes treated Javanese courts as obstacles to a rational defense system even though those courts commanded substantial resources and political legitimacy. Meanwhile traders assessed the risks of blockade and seizure, and communities supplying ships or road labor confronted immediate demands imposed in the name of an overseas war. A colonial government's determination to preserve an island for its sovereign could therefore weaken its relationships with the people on whom preservation depended. The Napoleonic period is best understood through this contradiction between an expanding military agenda and fragile means of enforcement, rather than as a simple prelude to the British conquest.

Section 100.6

Herman Willem Daendels and Administrative Reconstruction, 1808–1811

Daendels, born in 1762, had been a Dutch Patriot, fled to France after the Patriot defeat of the 1780s, and served in the French revolutionary and Napoleonic military system before being appointed Governor-General. He reached Batavia in January 1808 with orders to defend Java against Britain and with an outlook shaped by revolutionary France: hostile to what he regarded as the corruption and inefficiency of the Company era, and inclined toward centralized, hierarchical authority. His government was authoritarian in style and aggressive in its implementation of decisions.

His program had several parts. He reorganized the military, increasing the size and discipline of the army, in which Javanese and other Indonesian soldiers served alongside Europeans, and built or strengthened fortifications and arsenals, notably in Surabaya and Semarang. He restructured territorial administration, creating prefectures led by European officials and reducing the room for local discretion that Company-era governors and Residents had enjoyed. He attempted to discipline European officials, curbing private enrichment and subjecting appointments to tighter control, though in practice he also sold government lands to private buyers to raise money. He moved the administrative center from the unhealthy old port toward Weltevreden, a shift that reshaped the capital's geography. He also issued paper money and copper coinage to meet the government's cash needs, with unhappy economic effects.

Historians must distinguish between Daendels's declared objectives and his achievements. The stated aims were efficiency, defense, and the elimination of Company-era abuse. The measures taken were often costly and coercive, relied on the labor and resources of the Javanese population, and produced new forms of abuse, including the dismissal or humiliation of officials who resisted him. His relations with the Javanese courts and regents, treated in Section 100.8, were especially contentious.

Daendels's civil administration sought to make authority traceable through a clearer chain of command. The restructuring of Dutch-controlled Java into nine prefectural commands placed new officials between the governor-general and diverse local offices. European prefects were expected to account for revenue, security and the execution of orders, while the earlier autonomy of the governor of the northeast coast was curbed. An audit apparatus and reforms to salaries and promotion were intended to address abuses associated with poorly paid company servants. These changes were uneven in practice: districts varied greatly in political status, and the courts of Surakarta and Yogyakarta could not be treated simply as identical to areas already under direct European administration. The word prefecture also requires explanation. It signaled centralization under an appointed official, not an instantaneous conversion of all villages into units governed directly by European clerks.

The apparent rationality of the new hierarchy coexisted with emergency improvisation. Daendels had to raise troops, sustain coastal defenses, improve transport and find cash amid blockade. He drew on existing Javanese command networks to recruit workers and soldiers, making local elites indispensable even as he restricted their formal autonomy. Changes in Batavia's administrative geography toward Weltevreden reflected disease, military security and the search for a more functional governmental center. Paper money and sales of land offered short-term expedients but could destabilize prices and shift costs to rural households. Daendels's reputation as a reformer therefore rests on two different records: documents articulating centralized official duties, and accounts of how people were compelled to meet the government's urgent demands. Both are necessary to evaluate his government accurately.

Section 100.7

The Great Post Road: Infrastructure, Defense, and Forced Labor

The most famous project of the Daendels period was the Great Post Road (Grote Postweg), a route running the length of northern Java from the western end near Anyer to Panarukan in the east, commonly estimated at roughly a thousand kilometers. Earlier roads and tracks existed across much of the route, so the work was largely one of improvement, connection, and standardization rather than the creation of an entirely new line. The strategic purpose was military: a road that allowed troops, messages, and supplies to move quickly along the north coast could help defend the island against a British landing. It also served administrative and commercial purposes, speeding communication and the movement of produce.

The labor required was provided through compulsory mobilization organized by regents and local officials, who called out villagers to build, widen, and repair the road. Contemporary and later accounts describe heavy burdens, dislocation of agricultural work, hardship in difficult terrain such as the mountainous section near Sumedang and in malarial areas, and anger among those who were forced to labor. There is also a widely repeated tradition of very high mortality, but the figures given in popular accounts are poorly documented and should not be repeated as established fact. What can be said with confidence is that the construction involved coercion on a large scale, that it drew on the regents' authority over their populations, and that civilians bore the costs.

The road's longer-term significance was considerable. It became part of the infrastructure of the colonial state, shaping later settlement, trade, and military movement, and it symbolized a new capacity for rapid communication and control. At the same time, its construction illustrates a pattern repeated throughout the chapter: new ambitions of the state were realized through older mechanisms of obligation, mediated by local elites.

Students should distinguish the route drawn on a later map from the historical construction process. Daendels connected existing roads and paths, widened sections, improved gradients and bridges, and pressed local officials to supply labor where a continuous military route did not exist. The geography varied sharply: low-lying coastal stretches required embankments and drainage, while passes through upland terrain required cutting, earthmoving and difficult approaches. The line connected garrisons, government stations and market centers but did not eliminate river crossings, seasonal damage or the cost of moving carts over long distances. It was particularly valuable for official correspondence and rapid military movement. Benefits to merchants and travelers were real but distributed unequally, since access to the route and ability to use it depended upon money, animals and exemption from the heaviest labor duties.

The road also exposes problems in the evidence. Later retellings sometimes attach a single enormous death toll to its construction; comprehensive registers identifying all deaths attributable to roadwork have not been established for the entire route. Contemporary and subsequent reports nevertheless demonstrate that labor demands were severe, and coercive recruitment itself does not require an exact fatality total to be historically significant. Workers could lose planting time or be required to leave households for extended periods; villages might have to supply food and transport for labor parties. Chiefs and officials allocating work could protect their own followers or redirect the burden downward. A good geography exercise should therefore ask students not only to trace Anyer, Batavia, Cirebon, Semarang, Surabaya and Panarukan but also to examine where marshes, rivers and mountain passes increased the cost of construction. The road was simultaneously a technological achievement and a system for transferring colonial military costs to colonized populations.

Section 100.8

Fiscal Reform, Javanese Courts, and the Limits of Daendels's Authority

Daendels needed money. His military and construction programs were expensive, trade was blockaded, and the government's revenues were falling. He responded by increasing forced deliveries of coffee and other produce, selling government lands and, in some cases, whole districts to private buyers (including Chinese and European purchasers), issuing paper currency, and leasing or selling offices and privileges. These measures raised cash in the short term but strained the rural population, enriched some private individuals, and left the government's finances fragile. They also indicate the limits of centralization: a governor who sells public land to fund his government is relying on expedients, not on a settled fiscal structure.

His relations with Javanese rulers were equally fraught. Daendels rejected the ceremonial arrangements that had given the Company's Residents a subordinate or deferential place at the courts. He insisted that the Dutch representative be treated as the representative of a sovereign, not as a petitioner or an ally of lesser rank, and he reduced the courts' autonomy in matters such as appointments and territorial jurisdiction. In Banten, Daendels imposed direct annexation and severely restricted the sultanate in 1808 amid disputes over labor and strategic works, but the remaining sultanate was formally abolished under British rule in 1813; in Cirebon the rulers' authority was curtailed; and in Central Java, tensions with Sultan Hamengkubuwana II of Yogyakarta escalated into confrontation. In 1810 the Regent of Madiun, Raden Ronggo, rebelled and was killed, and in December of that year Daendels marched troops to Yogyakarta, forced the Sultan to abdicate in favor of his son (Hamengkubuwana III), and imposed new conditions.

These interventions had two consequences. They demonstrated that the colonial state was willing to depose a ruler, and they left unresolved resentments among members of the Yogyakarta elite. By 1811 Java's government was strong on paper but vulnerable in practice: its officials and Javanese rulers were estranged, its finances were improvised, its army was stretched, and the British were preparing to invade. Daendels was recalled in 1811 and replaced by Jan Willem Janssens, who arrived shortly before the invasion.

Court protocol mattered because it expressed political rank. Under previous arrangements a resident might approach a Javanese monarch through a carefully ordered audience, with seating, costume and forms of address marking the diplomatic relationship. Daendels sought to place the European representative in a more dominant position, regarding earlier deference as evidence of weak colonial authority. For Javanese rulers this was not a trivial dispute about etiquette; ceremony was one of the mechanisms through which sovereignty and the distribution of legitimate offices were publicly defined. Changes in ceremonial practice were followed by disputes over who could appoint ministers, command soldiers, collect taxes and exercise jurisdiction. Raden Ronggo's resistance in 1810 and the intervention at Yogyakarta demonstrate how challenges to court authority could turn a reform agenda into military confrontation. They also established precedents invoked by British administrators.

The case of Banten needs a two-stage chronology. Daendels imposed direct annexation and severe restrictions upon the sultanate in 1808 amid disputes connected with labor and strategic works, but the final formal abolition of the remaining sultanate occurred under Raffles in 1813. Describing the entire abolition as complete in 1808 obscures the intervening political arrangements and changes of ruler. More broadly, selling public lands to finance defense might provide immediate revenue while undermining the state's ability to regulate owners' demands on cultivators. Court interventions could similarly obtain formal signatures at the cost of mistrust and instability. Daendels did not simply replace a failing commercial bureaucracy with a stable modern administration. He created stronger instruments of command while intensifying conflicts that his successor would inherit. The record thus links administrative ambition, fiscal emergency and the political costs of coercion.

Historical Debate

Reform or Coercion? Did Daendels and Raffles Change the Colonial State?

The question: Were the governments of Daendels (1808–1811) and Raffles (1811–1816) genuine reformers who began a modern territorial state, or did they chiefly reorganize older methods of extraction under new names?

Interpretation A, institutional change: Some historians stress real innovations: Daendels's centralized chain of command, military construction, and assertion of sovereign precedence over Javanese rulers; Raffles's attempt to substitute a land-based tax paid by cultivators for forced deliveries and to reduce the independent income of regents. On this view these governments mark the start of a more bureaucratic, state-centered colonialism.
Interpretation B, continuity of coercion and mediation: Others emphasize that both governments relied on regents, village heads, and compulsory labor; that fiscal need drove policy; that implementation was patchy; and that older methods of extraction survived under different labels. On this view the changes were real but limited, and the underlying dependence on coercion and intermediaries persisted.

Evidence: Both sides draw on official decrees, administrative correspondence, financial accounts, and Javanese and European narratives. They differ in how much weight to give declared policy versus observed practice.

Points of disagreement: Interpretation and scale: how much change counts as "fundamental," and whether to judge by intention, design, or results.

Why it matters: The answer affects how the later Cultivation System (Chapter 102) is understood: as a break with the reform era or as a continuation of persistent practices.

Central question: How different was British rule from the Dutch systems it replaced?

Section 100.9

The British Invasion and Conquest of Java, 1811

The British expedition against Java was organized from British India under the authority of Governor-General Lord Minto, who accompanied it. The strategic motives combined the protection of British trade routes to China, the denial of a potential French base in the Indian Ocean and the Indies, and the commercial and fiscal attractions of Java. Thomas Stamford Raffles, who had served in Penang and Melaka and had urged action against Java, played a significant advisory role and was subsequently appointed Lieutenant-Governor.

The British expedition landed at Cilincing east of Batavia on 4 August 1811, occupied Batavia by 8 August, and then attacked the principal Dutch-French defensive concentration at Meester Cornelis on 26 August. Governor-General Janssens commanded the defending government, whose soldiers included Europeans and Indonesians. Janssens retreated inland and, after further fighting, capitulated on 18 September 1811 at Tuntang, near Salatiga. The capitulation transferred the Dutch-French colonial government and its military positions into British hands; the extent of rights that conquest conferred over Javanese courts remained disputed. The campaign was short, a result of the Dutch-French side's isolation, limited resources, and the British command of the sea, and of the shallow loyalty of many in the colonial army and population to a government that had demanded much from them.

British conquest changed the legal position of Java without ending its commercial and strategic importance. Java passed from the Dutch-French colonial government to administration through the British East India Company, distinct from the dissolved Dutch East India Company. British policy would be shaped by the competing aims of profit, reform, and the security of India. The occupation also reopened the question of Java's relations with its courts, which Raffles would handle, as Section 100.11 shows, with considerable force.

The military chronology is especially important. British troops landed at Cilincing east of Batavia on 4 August 1811 and occupied Batavia by 8 August; the decisive assault on the fortified position of Meester Cornelis followed on 26 August. The withdrawal of Governor-General Jan Willem Janssens toward central Java and the subsequent capitulation at Tuntang on 18 September marked the end of organized resistance. Treating Batavia as falling after Meester Cornelis reverses the actual sequence and conceals the difference between taking the administrative capital and breaking the main defensive concentration. The British expedition drew upon British and Indian soldiers, naval transport, artillery and local intelligence, so an account centered entirely on British European officers would misrepresent its composition. The campaign also unfolded during the monsoon and disease environment that shaped both armies' operational choices.

The legal consequences were more complicated than the battlefield result. The capitulation transferred Dutch-French government positions and asserted British control, but Javanese rulers did not necessarily interpret this as proof that their sovereign rights had passed wholesale to the British East India Company. Lord Minto's instructions and Raffles's subsequent claims about conquest and treaties became part of a wider argument over whether the British could replace the European government alone or demand political subordination from the courts. Some Javanese leaders hoped a change of European ruler would remove the obligations Daendels had imposed; British administrators often argued that prior European rights remained binding. That clash in expectations helps explain the violence of 1812 more effectively than a sequence of victories alone. It also provides an immediate example of the chapter's central distinction between an empire claiming formal sovereignty and the rulers who continued to contest that claim.

Section 100.10

Thomas Stamford Raffles and the British Reform Program

Raffles arrived with strong views on how Java should be governed. Influenced by liberal commercial ideas and by British experience in India, he criticized the Dutch system of forced deliveries and the extractive role of regents as oppressive and economically inefficient. His alternative was a system in which cultivators would pay a rent to the government for the land they used, in money or kind, and then be free to sell their produce on the market. He also intended to reduce the regents' power by converting them from semi-autonomous rulers living off local obligations into salaried officials, and he expected greater freedom of commerce and some restriction of the slave trade.

Historical Vocabulary
Land rent (landrente)

Definition. A system in which the government treated cultivators as tenants of the state and charged them rent, assessed on the land they used, in place of compulsory deliveries and personal services owed to regents and officials.

Historical context. Raffles drew the idea from British land-revenue systems in India. From 1813 he introduced it in parts of Java, with assessment often made through village heads.

Why it matters. It sought to replace a system of obligation through intermediaries with one of direct fiscal relations between state and villages, and it became a reference point in later Dutch debate.

Potential misunderstanding. It did not mean free peasant proprietorship. Villagers remained subject to taxation and in practice often to the village headman's allocation, and implementation varied widely by region.

In practice the reforms were uneven. The British administration lacked the survey data, trained personnel, and time needed to assess land and population accurately; the money economy in many districts was too thin to support cash rents; and village heads and regents often retained or adapted their intermediary roles. In some areas the new arrangements were introduced and worked partially; in others the older systems persisted. Forced coffee deliveries in the Priangan, for example, remained a source of revenue. The government was also constantly short of funds, which led it, like Daendels, to sell land and farm out monopolies and tolls. Raffles's reforms were thus ambitious in design and limited in effect.

Land rent was difficult to translate from principle into assessment. Officials had to decide whether a field was irrigated or dry, how much crop it could reasonably yield, which households possessed use rights, and what fraction of estimated production should be collected. Holdings and rights were often intertwined with village custom, noble appanages, seasonal cultivation and obligations to local officeholders. A statement that the government stood as ultimate landlord did not answer who had authority to measure plots or resolve disputes. Raffles's administrators experimented with different forms of district and village assessment, sometimes relying on headmen for information and allocation. In practice the headmen who allegedly lost arbitrary authority could acquire considerable influence over the distribution of new assessments. A household facing an unrealistic cash demand might sell grain at an unfavorable moment, borrow from a merchant, or fall into arrears. These consequences complicated the reformers' claim that paying rent would necessarily free cultivators to participate in open markets.

The reform program also faced a chronic mismatch between policy and fiscal need. The British occupation was expected to contribute revenue, yet maintaining troops and administration was expensive, and implementation took time. Established sources of income, including coffee deliveries in the Priangan, monopoly practices and sales of public land, remained attractive. Some officials treated regents as future salaried civil servants; villagers might still encounter them as men able to compel labor and mediate disputes. Administrative language about rights and improvement cannot by itself demonstrate changed household experience. Historians must compare orders with surviving accounts, reports on collection, petitions and regional variation. Raffles's program mattered as a precedent for later Dutch debates over land revenue, even where its actual operation remained partial. Its importance was therefore institutional and intellectual as well as fiscal, without requiring the claim that it inaugurated a uniform system of private peasant property.

Javanese Court Politics and the British Assault on Yogyakarta, 1812

When the British arrived, the Central Javanese courts were already in turmoil. Hamengkubuwana II, forced to abdicate by Daendels in December 1810, regained the throne at the end of 1811, in the confusion following the Dutch collapse, and sought to reassert his position. Raffles, who wanted a stable and subordinate court system, disapproved. Relations deteriorated over ceremonial precedence, the status of the Resident, territorial and fiscal questions, and the Sultan's refusal to accept the reduced status the British demanded. There were also tensions with Surakarta, where Susuhunan Pakubuwana IV had his own grievances and, according to British claims, had exchanged messages with the Yogyakarta court that British authorities regarded as hostile. Whether and how far a genuine plot existed is disputed; Javanese court politics were complicated, and British interpretations suited British purposes.

In June 1812 a British-led force, including large numbers of Indian soldiers (the "sepoys" that gave the Javanese name Geger Sepehi, "the Sepoy Disturbance"), assaulted the Yogyakarta kraton (palace-city). The fortified palace was taken in a short but violent engagement, after which the British deposed Hamengkubuwana II, exiled him to Penang, and restored his son Hamengkubuwana III. The kraton was plundered: treasure, weapons, manuscripts, and court regalia were seized, and many of the manuscripts entered British collections. Raffles imposed a treaty that stripped Yogyakarta of territory and revenues and reduced the court's autonomy, and the following year the British created the Pakualaman, a small principality under Prince Natakusuma (Pakualam I), further dividing the court. Surakarta also lost territory and power through pressure on Pakubuwana IV.

Javanese actors were not merely victims. Members of the Yogyakarta elite, including Natakusuma and others, calculated their interests and in some cases collaborated with the British against the Sultan; others resisted or sought accommodation later. The Javanese chronicle tradition, including a court account studied by Peter Carey, records the attack from the point of view of those who suffered it and registers both shock and fine political distinctions. The assault had lasting effects: it humiliated the court, shifted power among rival factions, and contributed to a political atmosphere in which, a decade later, Prince Diponegoro and others would see European authority as illegitimate.

The contest over Yogyakarta was also a contest over how treaties should be read. Raffles and his officers maintained that the rights previously asserted by the Dutch could be inherited by conquest and invoked to require the sultan's compliance. Hamengkubuwana II argued, according to surviving correspondence, that obligations established under Daendels were no longer valid following the change of government. The 1811 arrangements and subsequent communications gave the two parties reasons to draw different conclusions about authority. Historians should not equate a claim written in an English official dispatch with a legal concession accepted by a Javanese court. An interpretation emphasizing these incompatible expectations makes the assault appear not simply as a response to an uncontrollable monarch but as a coercive resolution of a dispute about sovereignty. Javanese rulers also acted strategically: divisions within Yogyakarta and competing ambitions at Surakarta shaped both the British intervention and its local allies.

The storming of the palace on 19–20 June 1812 involved British and Indian troops, including sepoys, and resulted in extensive loss of court property and manuscripts. The imposed settlement went beyond changing the ruler: subsequent agreements placed important outlying territories, including Kedu and eastern districts, under British control and shifted rights over tolls, markets and aspects of judicial authority. These were not merely geographic adjustments. Appanage revenues supported court households, aristocrats and retainers; losing districts altered the material basis of political loyalty. The separate creation of Pakualaman under Natakusuma in 1813 intensified the internal division of Yogyakarta's aristocracy. Court chronicles and British reports remember the campaign differently, including on violence, plunder and the legitimacy of intervention. Placing those accounts beside each other allows students to see how an imperial government transformed military victory into lasting political and fiscal claims, and how the damaged court remembered the event as a rupture.

Section 100.12

Administration, Scholarship, and the Limits of British Reform

British administration in Java extended beyond land rent. The government reorganized judicial arrangements, introducing British-style courts and procedures alongside existing systems; sought to restrict the importation of slaves, though slavery itself continued; and expanded surveying, statistical inquiry, and the collection of information on land, population, and agriculture. Colin Mackenzie and other officers carried out surveys, and the government supported scientific and antiquarian projects. One example was the clearing and documentation of the Borobudur monument in 1814 under a commission from Raffles, which brought the Buddhist monument examined in Chapter 31 to wider scholarly notice.

The period's knowledge production reached its best-known form in Raffles's The History of Java, published in London in 1817. The work draws on Javanese texts, oral accounts, and informants, as well as British observation, and it is an important source for Javanese society, religion, and antiquities in the early nineteenth century. It also expresses the assumptions of its time: it uses a comparative framework favoring British institutions, presents the Javanese as in need of reform, and is partly an argument for British retention of Java. Historians therefore treat it both as invaluable evidence and as a colonial text requiring critical reading.

The limits of British reform were practical and political. The administration was short-lived, under-resourced, and dependent on existing local officials. Its financial returns were disappointing, which weakened the case for retaining Java in London. Raffles himself was criticized and eventually recalled, and in March 1816 he was succeeded by John Fendall, who served until the handover to the Dutch. The interlude left a legacy of experimentation, of knowledge accumulated, and of a sharpened regional political landscape, but it did not end the dependence on coercion, intermediaries, and extraction.

British knowledge production had an administrative purpose. Maps, censuses, botanical observations and collections of manuscripts could help officers determine which land to tax, which routes troops might use, and which rulers possessed influential genealogical claims. Such information also gave Raffles the materials from which to present a comprehensive picture of Javanese civilization to European audiences. His <em>History of Java</em>, published in 1817 after the restoration of Dutch rule, brought architecture, religion, political history and social practices into an influential English-language account. Yet it did not offer a neutral window onto an unchanged society. Officials selected informants and arranged facts according to their own ideas about antiquity, law, civilization and improvement. The removal of manuscripts during the assault on Yogyakarta demonstrates the uncomfortable connection between scholarship and coercive possession. A text now held in a European collection may preserve valuable Javanese evidence while having reached that collection through an unequal political event.

Judicial reforms were similarly complex. Colonial courts could claim more regular procedures, but access to them varied according to status, language, local authority and the identity of the parties. British measures concerning the slave trade did not abolish slavery in Java; the labor system had multiple legal and customary forms that cannot be collapsed into a single reform proclamation. In the countryside, appraisals of Raffles's success depended heavily on whether an official evaluated the elegance of a regulation or its effect upon revenue and cultivators. The administration's short duration makes durable outcomes particularly difficult to measure. Nevertheless, the British period left important precedents in record-making, relations with the courts and arguments about land rent. Its legacy lies in those unresolved institutional questions and their appropriation by later rulers as much as in any single policy carried out consistently across the island.

Section 100.13

The Anglo-Dutch Settlement and the Return to Java, 1814–1816

Napoleon's defeat reshaped the European order. The Netherlands emerged as an independent monarchy under William I, and the great powers wished to create a strong Dutch state as a barrier against France and a counterweight in the Low Countries. The Anglo-Dutch Convention signed in London on 13 August 1814 provided for the return of most Dutch colonies seized by Britain, with specified exceptions such as the Cape and certain South American territories. Java was among the territories to be restored, though the timing and conditions took two further years to settle, and the final handover occurred in August 1816.

The restoration was not a simple reversion. The Dutch state that returned was different from the Company or the Batavian Republic: it was a monarchy with a constitution, a colonial administration under royal authority, and a commitment to making the colonies economically productive for the Netherlands. Meanwhile, the Indies had changed. The British interlude had altered the courts, the regents' positions, and the fiscal system; the villages and towns had experienced three or four governments in a decade; and Indonesian leaders had learned that European authority was neither permanent nor monolithic.

Expectations diverged. European officials wanted to restore order, revenue, and Dutch authority and, in some cases, to adopt British ideas selectively. Javanese rulers and regents hoped for restored privileges or at least stability; some feared reprisals for earlier collaboration; and many ordinary people hoped for lighter burdens. The transfer of institutions was therefore an act of negotiation between expectations as well as a legal exchange.

A diplomatic agreement made in London could not settle all the questions on Java's coast. Colonial officials had to transfer archives, treasury balances, buildings, ships, claims against local contractors and outstanding promises to Indonesian rulers. British officers had supervised tax collection and appointments for several years, while Javanese courtiers had responded to British treaties and the violent reordering of Yogyakarta. Restored Dutch administrators could neither pretend those developments had not occurred nor automatically accept every British assertion. The restoration therefore required decisions about which land contracts, assessments and administrative practices to retain. The returning government also confronted debts, deficient records and an uncertain flow of commercial revenue after years of warfare. These practical issues made the formal handover a beginning rather than the completion of political reconstruction.

The Anglo-Dutch Convention of 1814 should be distinguished from the treaty of 1824. The earlier convention concerned the return of possessions following the Napoleonic wars and contained exceptions; the later treaty adjusted British and Dutch claims and spheres in maritime Southeast Asia. It is anachronistic to read the eventual boundaries of Indonesia and Malaysia back into the 1814 settlement. Nor did restoration mean the reappearance of the VOC: the returning Dutch government operated under a restored monarchy whose overseas possessions were treated as royal-state responsibilities. The central problem was how to combine claims of regular administration and revenue-producing colonies with a political landscape transformed by British rule. From the standpoint of Javanese courts, restoration created opportunities for renegotiation and new dangers of renewed intervention. From the standpoint of villages, the critical issue was what taxes and labor obligations would actually follow.

Section 100.14

The Commissioners-General and the Government of Van der Capellen

The restoration was supervised by three Commissioners-General: Cornelis Theodorus Elout, Godert van der Capellen, and Arnold Adriaan Buyskes. They arrived in 1816 to receive the colony from the British and formulate new governmental institutions. They promulgated a governmental regulation (Regeringsreglement) on 22 December 1818; it set out the governor-general's royal authority, higher institutions, and provisions concerning justice, cultivation and trade. The Commission transferred regular government to Van der Capellen in January 1819, and further judicial regulations were issued that year. The emerging legal framework was more explicit than the Company-era structure, but its effective reach differed between districts.

Van der Capellen, named governor-general in the restoration arrangements of 1816 and exercising the regular government independently from January 1819 until 1826, favored a program of orderly administration, public works, and the promotion of agriculture and trade. He attempted to reconcile the central government's authority with Indonesian political structures, upholding the formal positions of the courts and regents while extending European supervision. His administration established or reorganized residencies, courts, and fiscal offices and sought to curb the worst abuses of local officials. It also faced severe financial constraints, as revenues fell short of expectations and costs, including the military, remained high.

The government's difficulty lay in the gap between ambition and capacity. A formal structure of Residents, judges, and inspectors could be drawn on paper far faster than it could be staffed with competent and honest people, and a government that hoped to use Javanese regents as subordinate officials found that they retained considerable social power. The restored state's formal reach was greater than the Company's, but its actual control in many districts depended on the cooperation of those same regents and village heads.

The chronology of the Commissioners-General requires precision. Elout, Buyskes and Van der Capellen arrived in Java in 1816 and together supervised the assumption and reorganization of Dutch government. Their commission continued into the opening weeks of 1819, when its powers passed to the regular governor-general's administration; Van der Capellen, who had held the governor-general's title within the restoration arrangements, assumed his position independently after the commission ended. The regulation promulgated on 22 December 1818 set out the structure of higher government and rules concerning justice, cultivation and trade. Its first article described the governor-general as exercising supreme authority in the king's name. These provisions supplied a legal vocabulary for a royal colonial state, not evidence that all territory under Dutch claim already had identical local institutions. Subsequent regulations in 1819 developed judicial arrangements for different populations, reflecting the stratified character of colonial law.

The governmental structure was shaped by competing commitments. Commissioners spoke of limiting arbitrary practice and imposing predictable rules, but they also had to restore the colonial treasury and satisfy a Dutch monarchy that considered the Indies essential to commercial recovery. The governor-general depended upon European residents and indigenous regents, while judicial and fiscal offices required records and personnel that remained unevenly distributed. Land revenue could not be standardized simply by announcing a new legal code. The desire to regulate European land leasing in the courts and the wish to preserve a financially solvent colony produced further conflicts in the 1820s. The historian should therefore treat the 1818 regulation as a significant institutional milestone while asking how officials applied it in specific districts. Administrative reform created channels of command and appeal; it did not eliminate the power of intermediaries or the social inequalities from which revenue was extracted.

Section 100.15

Land, Taxation, Revenue, and the Emerging Colonial Bureaucracy

The restored government neither fully abandoned nor fully adopted Raffles's land-rent system. In some residencies it continued assessing and collecting land-based taxes; in others, older forms of delivery and service persisted or returned. Practical difficulties, including inadequate surveys, limited money circulation, and a shortage of European personnel, constrained the experiment. Coffee remained a compulsory crop in the Priangan, and tolls, market taxes, and monopolies, often leased to Chinese tax-farmers, remained significant sources of revenue. The government relied on these because they were administratively simple and yielded cash.

Compared with its predecessors, the restored system showed both change and continuity. The VOC had extracted through contracts, deliveries, and political bargains with regents; Daendels through centralization, forced labor, and expedients such as land sales; the British through land rent and the aspiration to a market economy; and the Dutch after 1816 through a hybrid that preserved elements of each. The regents' role was redefined as that of salaried or privileged officials within a European-supervised hierarchy, but they continued to organize labor and collection. The relationship of Javanese rulers to European officials was increasingly formal, with Residents given greater supervisory power, yet it still depended on negotiation. Institutional change thus coexisted with persistent methods of extraction.

Another important development was the growth of a more elaborate colonial bureaucracy. Appointments, ranks, salaries, and reporting requirements multiplied, and the government's need for information about land, population, and revenue increased. This bureaucratization, even where incomplete, marks a shift from the more improvised and personal administration of the Company era, and it laid foundations for the more extensive system that followed the Java War.

The diversity of the government's revenue instruments helps explain the limited reach of reform. A land-rent assessment required information about harvests, soils, cultivators and local tenure that officials rarely possessed in uniform form. A monopoly or tax farm, by contrast, could generate a payment from an intermediary who accepted the risk of collection. Coffee deliveries in Priangan were maintained through administrative arrangements that did not conform neatly to the language of agricultural freedom. The authority of regents was retained because they knew local networks and could organize deliveries, but this reliance created uncertainty about whether the colonial state or established elites controlled the burdens imposed on villagers. Documents from different residencies consequently describe very different experiences under an apparently single colonial government. Comparison across regions is essential before drawing conclusions about a general policy.

Fiscal pressure had consequences beyond accounting. Charging tolls on traffic could raise the cost of bringing rice and other goods to market; an advance paid for a lease might become a debt when colonial officials intervened to cancel it. Villages already dealing with high transport costs, uncertain harvests and military recruitment could find it difficult to distinguish new taxes from old obligations administered under new names. Some elites benefited from salaried office and access to state contracts, while others lost claims to land or courts of justice they had previously exercised. Colonial officials increasingly wrote reports in standardized categories, yet the categories themselves could obscure complex forms of tenancy, appanage and communal allocation. A serious political-economic history should ask who recorded the land, who paid the assessment, who retained a margin from collection, and who had power to contest an excessive demand. Those questions reveal the institutional continuity underneath changing fiscal doctrines.

Historical Vocabulary
Resident and Regent

Definition. A Resident was a European official who represented the colonial government at a court or administered a residency, a territorial unit. A regent (bupati in Javanese) was an Indonesian ruler-official who governed a district under the authority of a sovereign or, increasingly, a European government.

Historical context. The VOC used Residents as political agents and commercial supervisors. Regents had existed in Javanese and Sundanese polities and were retained, adapted, and sometimes reduced by successive colonial governments, which relied on them to mobilize labor and collect produce.

Why it matters. The relationship between Resident and regent was the daily point of contact between colonial and Indonesian authority, and its terms shaped both cooperation and conflict.

Potential misunderstanding. Residents were not simply "governors," and regents were not simply "employees." The balance of power varied by place and period and was repeatedly renegotiated.

Section 100.16

Resistance in the Moluccas: The Pattimura Revolt of 1817

The restoration of Dutch authority in the central Moluccas, which the British had governed from 1810 until the handover, provoked a major revolt in 1817. Its best-known leader was Thomas Matulessy, known as Pattimura, a former soldier who had served in the British-organized militia. The revolt began in May 1817 on the island of Saparua, where insurgents attacked Fort Duurstede and killed the Dutch Resident, Johannes van den Berg, and members of his family and garrison. The uprising spread to nearby islands, including Haruku and Nusa Laut, and involved communities on Ambon and Seram.

The grievances were concrete. They included labor and boat-service demands; the requirement to supply produce and clove deliveries under controlled prices; the payment of wages or purchases in devalued paper currency; plans to recruit local young men for the colonial army; the loss or reduction of positions and salaries for local teachers and officials; and a general resentment of the return of a regime whose demands were seen as heavier than those of the British. The revolt drew on village and kinship networks, Christian and Muslim communities, and local leaders including Philips Latumahina and Anthony Rhebok. Women also took part: Christina Martha Tiahahu, from Nusa Laut, became a prominent figure in the resistance and in later Indonesian historical memory.

The Dutch response was a military expedition that retook the main positions, captured the leaders, and suppressed the revolt by the end of the year. Pattimura was captured in November and executed at Ambon in December 1817; Christina Martha Tiahahu died in January 1818 aboard a ship carrying exiles. The revolt shows that European claims to sovereignty did not equal local acceptance. In a region where the VOC had long enforced monopolies through violence, the restored state faced a population that remembered, and compared, earlier regimes, and that was willing to rebel when its burdens increased. Pattimura later became a national hero in Indonesia, an example of how historical memory reshapes older events, though the original rebellion was a local movement with specific grievances rather than a nationalist movement in the modern sense.

The revolt's documentary record makes it possible to move beyond a simplified story of colonial troops against local fighters. Pattimura had served in forces organized during British occupation and acquired military experience and standing among village leaders. The return of Dutch rulers raised anxiety about renewed clove monopolies, recruitment for overseas military service and threats to locally valued religious and educational institutions. A petition dated 29 May 1817, attributed to rebel leaders, set out grievances concerning the position of Christian schoolteachers and Dutch obligations to their communities. This evidence is important because it records more than retrospective nationalism: the petitioners appealed to promises, fairness and religious relationships embedded in the history of Dutch rule itself. The role of Christian identity did not make the revolt exclusively religious, and participation across the islands was not uniform. Local alliances and the priorities of particular settlements influenced both mobilization and resistance to the insurgents.

The attack on Fort Duurstede on 15 May and the ensuing Dutch counteroffensives reveal a conflict over who had the right to command labor, troops and village resources. The death of the resident and others at the fort formed part of the violence of the uprising; the Dutch retaliation and executions formed part of the violence of restoration. The government retook strategic positions during a difficult campaign and executed Pattimura in Ambon in December. Christina Martha Tiahahu's later prominence in national memory deserves attention, but some details of individual participation survive mainly through later representations and require source-conscious presentation. The revolt demonstrates that former colonial subjects could judge successive European governments against one another and mobilize claims to just treatment using familiar political and religious language. This is historically different from assuming that all rebels shared a modern program of Indonesian nationhood. The episode belongs at the center of the chapter because it shows the boundaries of restored Dutch power outside Java.

Section 100.17

Colonial Finance, Commerce, and the Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824

King William I took a close interest in colonial commerce, believing that the Indies could contribute to Dutch recovery. In 1824 he sponsored the creation of the Netherlands Trading Society (Nederlandsche Handel-Maatschappij, NHM), a company intended to carry colonial produce, finance trade, and revive Dutch shipping. Though not itself a governing body like the VOC, the NHM became an important instrument of state-linked commerce, and its role in marketing Javanese produce grew after 1830, as Chapter 102 shows.

Anglo-Dutch tensions over the Straits of Melaka and Sumatra were meanwhile resolved by a treaty signed in London on 17 March 1824. In summary, the Dutch ceded Melaka and their Indian establishments to Britain, while Britain ceded the establishment at Bencoolen (Bengkulu) and its other British possessions on Sumatra, and undertook to make no new settlements or treaties with Sumatran rulers. The agreements restricted each party's settlements and treaties in specified areas, notably the Malay Peninsula and Sumatra, and recognized the British position at Singapore subject to restrictions farther south. The treaty regulated European claims and actions; it neither transferred sovereignty over all Sumatra nor settled indigenous political rights. Other articles addressed trade in the region and relations with local polities. Singapore, founded by Raffles in 1819 and contested by the Dutch, was in effect recognized as British (see Chapter 105).

The treaty's significance lies in the division of maritime Southeast Asia into two European spheres, a division that would shape the later boundaries of Malaysia, Singapore, and Indonesia. Yet it was an agreement between European powers, made without the participation of Malay rulers, Acehnese leaders, or other local polities whose territory and trade it affected. It asserted claims of exclusivity that on the ground would take decades to realize, as Chapters 103 and 104 show for the Dutch expansion and the Aceh War. European diplomatic lines were thus ahead of political reality.

The treaty's individual articles deserve closer attention than a generalized description of a dividing line. Article IX transferred Fort Marlborough and the other British possessions on Sumatra to the Netherlands and committed Britain to abstain from future settlements and treaties with local rulers there. Article X transferred Melaka to Britain and established reciprocal Dutch restrictions on the Malay Peninsula. Article XII recorded Dutch withdrawal of objections to Singapore and British undertakings concerning islands south of the Singapore Strait. These clauses regulated two European governments' behavior toward one another; they did not record the consent of Acehnese, Malay, Minangkabau or other political authorities. British promises not to negotiate with Sumatran rulers did not prove that those rulers were Dutch subjects. Likewise, local commercial routes continued to cross waters that European diplomats increasingly treated as distinct zones.

The Netherlands Trading Society also shows how different the new state-linked commercial order was from the VOC's corporate government. The society was created to stimulate shipping and the marketing of colonial goods under royal patronage, not to replace the governor-general as sovereign ruler. Its later prominence in the export-crop regime belongs to Chapter 102; here the critical issue is the fusion of metropolitan commercial policy with the fiscal goals of colonial administration. A territorial state could aim to secure revenues for the Dutch treasury while merchants pursued profitable cargoes and private contracts. Those interests sometimes overlapped and sometimes produced conflict. The 1824 treaty strengthened a European diplomatic framework for expansion, but Dutch officers still faced local political powers with their own trading relationships and military resources. Students should therefore compare a treaty map showing European claims with a map showing the actual territories governed by individual courts, villages and rulers in the 1820s.

Section 100.18

Everyday Life under Successive Colonial Governments

For most inhabitants of Java and the Moluccas, the sequence of governments was experienced through concrete obligations. Farmers owed rice, coffee, or other produce; labor on roads, fortifications, and public works; and cash or kind for taxes and tolls, whichever authority collected them. Village heads allocated these burdens among households and acted as buffers, enforcers, or both. Military requisitions, including boats, carts, porters, and provisions, were heavy in war years. Households could respond by working harder, shifting crops, moving to other districts, seeking protection from a patron, or, at the limit, joining a rebellion.

Merchants, artisans, and Chinese communities were also affected. Chinese traders and tax-farmers operated tollgates, markets, and credit networks, profiting from the government's need for cash but also becoming targets of resentment when tolls and fees rose. Port-town artisans and merchants faced disrupted shipping during the wars and uncertain currency under Daendels. Soldiers, including Javanese and Ambonese troops, served under changing flags. Women worked in agriculture, markets, and crafts, and some noblewomen held significant political influence at the courts; in the Moluccas women could be active participants in resistance, as the case of Christina Martha Tiahahu shows. Enslaved people and debt-bondsmen, found in Batavia and other ports, were affected by changes in the slave-trade regulations, though slavery persisted (see Chapter 91).

Environment and health shaped these years too. Crop failures and epidemics struck Java in the early 1820s, including a cholera epidemic that began in 1821 and the eruption of Mount Merapi in 1822, which added hardship to populations already bearing heavy obligations. The evidence for ordinary experience is indirect: colonial reports, court chronicles, petitions, and later memoirs record it through the lenses of officials and elites, and historians must read between the lines.

Daily experience varied sharply with household position and local environment. A cultivator with access to irrigated rice fields might owe a proportion of harvest, labor service and fees collected along the route to market; a tenant or land-poor worker could be vulnerable to wages set by harvest cycles and to the demands of patrons. Some women marketed food, textiles and household products, helped manage farms or exercised authority within aristocratic households, but their choices were constrained by obligations, marriage arrangements and unequal access to office. In port towns, enslaved workers, servants, artisans and sailors formed part of a cosmopolitan population with statuses that colonial regulations only imperfectly captured. Soldiers who changed masters after 1811 or 1816 might retain skills but lose payments, offices or pensions. The continuity of dependence is especially visible when a government proclaimed a new system while keeping much of the personnel who enforced the old one.

Ordinary life also extended beyond direct taxation. Irrigation works, village ceremonies, mosques, churches, markets and family networks gave communities ways to organize assistance and negotiate local conflicts. A road that sped an official's journey could shorten a merchant's delivery yet impose several weeks of unpaid labor on nearby households. Changes in currency might favor creditors or tax collectors and burden cultivators selling produce. Environmental shocks made those contradictions more acute: epidemic disease and Merapi's 1822 eruption had consequences independent of European policy but affected people whose reserves and institutions were already stressed. Scholars should be cautious about inferring the thoughts of unnamed villagers from reports compiled by officials worried about rebellion or arrears. Petitions, court narratives, legal disputes and occasional records of household obligations can help recover agency without inventing personal testimony. A meaningful social history places the people who supplied labor, food, trade and resistance alongside the administrators who claimed to govern them.

Everyday Life and Social History

Obligation, Mobility, and Survival, 1795–1825

Consider a cultivator in the Priangan highlands whose village was required to deliver coffee at fixed prices and supply labor for the road. Under each successive government the demand might have a different name, but its practical form was similar: the village head would assign shares, the regent would answer to the Resident, and failure invited punishment. A rice farmer on the north coast, in contrast, might have faced land-rent assessment under the British, then returned to deliveries or taxes under the Dutch, with new tolls at markets leased to a Chinese tax-farmer.

In a Moluccan village, an obligation to supply clove deliveries, rowers, and soldiers could change with each new governor; payment in devalued paper currency in 1817 provided a sudden and concrete grievance. These experiences remind students that structural change in colonial institutions mattered chiefly through their effects on household labor, food, mobility, and security, and that populations responded in varied ways, from compliance to flight to armed resistance.

Section 100.19

Political Tensions in Central Java and the Approach of the Java War

By the early 1820s the Central Javanese courts were weakened and divided. Hamengkubuwana IV, who had come to the throne as a child in 1814, died in 1822, and his successor, Hamengkubuwana V, was an infant of about two. The court was governed by a regency that included members of the royal family and was heavily influenced by the Dutch Resident and the Patih (chief minister), Danureja IV, who was closely linked to European interests. Rival factions competed for influence, and the dignity of the court had been battered by the events of 1810 and 1812.

A series of grievances converged. One concerned land: Javanese aristocrats had leased lands in the principalities to European and Chinese planters, who paid advances and ran estates using local labor. When the colonial government enacted restrictions on 6 May 1823, published later that month, specified leases to European and Chinese lessees in the princely territories were to terminate by January 1824, and disputes over advances and compensation exposed noble lessors to serious financial pressure. A second concerned taxation and tolls: tollgates leased to Chinese operators multiplied, and the burden on peasants and traders grew. A third concerned ceremonial and political status, as European Residents interfered in appointments and court ritual. A fourth concerned religion and legitimacy: many elites and religious figures saw the court's dependence on non-Muslim European authority as compromising its moral standing. Natural hardships, such as cholera and the Merapi eruption, intensified the sense of crisis.

Prince Diponegoro, the eldest son of Hamengkubuwana III by a secondary wife, was by this time a prominent figure. Born around 1785, he had declined to accept a formal position that would tie him to the Dutch-influenced court, lived at Tegalrejo near Yogyakarta, was known for religious piety and for contacts with religious teachers, and attracted the respect of many who saw him as a critic of court corruption and European influence. His particular grievances included disputes over land and the treatment of his family and the broader perception that the court had lost its independence. These tensions came to a head in 1825 when a conflict over road-building stakes near Tegalrejo helped trigger the outbreak of war. The war itself, its leadership, and its consequences are examined in Chapter 101; here the point is that the conflict had deep roots in the political and economic changes of the previous decades.

The land-lease crisis requires attention to the exact form of the 1823 regulation. An order approved on 6 May, and published later that month, restricted the leasing of land in the princely territories to Europeans and Chinese and set the termination of certain longer contracts by January 1824. It was not a general abolition of land tenure or a law that instantly returned every field to peasant owners. Aristocratic lessors had often received advances and spent them; returning those advances could force borrowing or the liquidation of other assets. Planters lost expectations based on costly investments, while cultivators might remain subject to obligations regardless of which party claimed the land. The policy's declared concern with disorderly leasing and the risks posed by private control thus had distributional effects that made court politics more volatile. Toll farming and litigation over appanage rights compounded these tensions.

Religion and kingship cannot be treated as an automatic consequence of economic distress. Diponegoro's religious education, personal networks, family position and criticisms of court conduct gave moral language to grievances whose immediate causes differed among supporters. Disagreement over court regencies and the treatment of his estate intersected with memories of 1812 and growing suspicion of European intrusion. The outbreak of 1825 was contingent: particular decisions and confrontations turned long-term tensions into war, but the eventual conflict should not be narrated here as though it had already begun. Ending this section at the threshold allows Chapter 101 to examine recruitment, Islamic leadership, strategy, rural society and military consequences on their own terms. For Chapter 100, the key conclusion is that the state that claimed to have rationalized government was entering a profound crisis in its relationship with central Javanese society. The history of reform had not eliminated the political costs of coercive sovereignty.

Section 100.20

Historical Synthesis: From Commercial Empire to Territorial Colonial Government

Comparing the corporate and the state periods clarifies what changed. Under the VOC, sovereignty was delegated and justified by commercial purpose, though in practice the Company acted as a regional power. After 1799 the state claimed sovereignty directly, subject in turn to French, British, and then restored Dutch royal authority. The army, once a Company force, became a state army with greater formal organization, and its role in disciplining Javanese rulers grew, as Daendels and Raffles demonstrated. Administration evolved from improvised Company arrangements to a more formal structure of governors, Residents, regents, and regulations. Infrastructure, notably the Great Post Road, linked the island more effectively. Relations with local rulers became more assertive, as the depositions of Sultans in 1810 and 1812 showed.

Yet continuities were equally striking. The state relied, as the Company had, on regents and village heads to mobilize labor and collect produce; on compulsory deliveries and tolls; on military force as the final argument; and on a population that bore the costs. Finances were chronically stretched, and expedients such as land sales and farming out revenue persisted. Territorial control remained uneven: Java's core districts were effectively administered, but outer regions of the archipelago were governed lightly or contested, as the Moluccan revolt of 1817 and the unsettled position of the Sumatran and Bornean polities showed. The treaty of 1824 drew lines on the map that were far ahead of effective authority.

The conclusion that follows is that the transformation from the VOC to the Dutch colonial state was a prolonged and contested reorganization of commercial power, territorial sovereignty, and government authority. Corporate dissolution, Napoleonic warfare, British administration, and Dutch restoration altered institutions but did not remove the underlying dependence on coercive labor, taxation, local political intermediaries, and military force. By 1825 a more ambitious territorial colonial state had emerged, but its authority remained geographically uneven and politically contested. The Java War would test that state severely, and the system that emerged afterward, treated in Chapters 101 and 102, built on the foundations laid in these years.

The distinction between the legal and social histories of government is the chapter's most important conclusion. The company charter ended, authority in Europe changed repeatedly, the British arrived and departed, and the restored monarchy promulgated regulations that treated the Indies as royal possessions. Each event altered the language of official power and sometimes the institutions empowered to enforce it. Yet colonized communities did not experience a simple sequence in which an old system disappeared and a new one took its place. Appointments, regents, road labor, coffee deliveries, land revenue and court treaties passed through successive administrations and were modified rather than wholly reinvented. Some projects, such as the Great Post Road or the formalization of government procedures, produced lasting changes in infrastructure and administrative reach; others, such as the attempted land-rent reorganization, remained uneven and contested.

Evaluating whether this was a fundamental break requires specifying the question. At the metropolitan level the transformation from a shareholder company to a state claiming direct sovereignty was decisive. At the level of a village ordered to supply labor, the continuity of intermediaries and compulsory obligations could be more immediate. At a Javanese court, however, the assertion that Europeans could decide dynastic succession and seize royal lands represented a grave shift in relations of power. In the Moluccas the attempted return to older monopolies prompted organized resistance, showing that continuity itself could be experienced as intolerable. These different scales should not be collapsed into a single verdict. By 1825 the colonial government possessed a stronger framework for command, but neither reliable revenue nor uncontested legitimacy. That unfinished transformation prepares the Java War of Chapter 101 and the later experiments in extraction and expansion examined in Chapters 102 and 103. The chapter should leave students with an interpretive method: compare formal sovereignty, actual enforcement and the experiences of local societies before declaring that a new era has definitively begun.

Historical Debate

Was the Transformation from the VOC a Fundamental Institutional Break?

The question: Did the dissolution of the VOC and the subsequent regimes create a qualitatively new type of colonial government, or did the state largely inherit and extend Company practices?

Interpretation A, a significant break: The transfer of sovereignty to a state, the development of formal bureaucracy, the new assertiveness toward Javanese rulers, and the shift from commercial to territorial objectives mark a genuine transformation. On this view the state of 1825 differs in kind from the VOC of 1780.
Interpretation B, evolutionary continuity: The VOC was already a territorial power with governmental functions; its personnel, methods, and dependence on local intermediaries persisted; and the state's reforms were limited by fiscal and practical constraints. On this view the change was one of degree and legal form.

Evidence: Institutional records, personnel lists, fiscal accounts, and the practical operation of regents and Residents are cited by both sides.

Points of disagreement: Chiefly interpretation and terminology: what counts as a "fundamental" break, and whether legal and institutional change or social experience should be the measure.

Why it matters: It shapes how historians understand the origins of nineteenth-century colonialism and the extent to which later policies were novel.

Student task: Select three criteria (for example, source of sovereignty, administrative structure, labor system) and assess whether each shows break or continuity between 1780 and 1825.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Final Assessment: Sovereignty and Control, 1780–1825

  • Changed: the legal basis of authority; the scale and formality of bureaucracy; the assertiveness of military force toward courts; infrastructure and information-gathering; the geopolitical division of the archipelago between Dutch and British spheres.
  • Continued: reliance on intermediary elites; compulsory labor and deliveries; fiscal improvisation; uneven control of outer regions; Indonesian contestation of European claims.
  • Pace: change was punctuated by crises and uneven geographically, with court elites and regents more affected by institutional shifts and farmers by continuing obligations.

Why the pattern matters: It explains why the formal strengthening of the state coincided with the outbreak of a major war in 1825.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the transformation of the Dutch East India Company's commercial and territorial possessions into the foundation of a nineteenth-century colonial state. Its central argument is that formal sovereignty expanded faster than effective control, and that the new state, for all its ambition, depended on the coercive labor, taxation, intermediaries, and military force that had sustained the Company.

The VOC of the late eighteenth century was a hybrid: a chartered corporation that also governed territory, collected taxes, kept armies, and made treaties. It was undermined by the costs of governance, competition, shifting commodity markets, and weak accounting, with the Fourth Anglo-Dutch War (1780–1784) delivering a severe shock. Corruption contributed but was only one of several interacting causes.

The Batavian Revolution of 1795 produced a state willing to intervene, and the Company's charter expired on 31 December 1799. The state took over its possessions, debts, and obligations, but it inherited Company personnel and practices and lacked the resources and communications needed to build a new government. Early state administration was therefore marked by continuity.

The Napoleonic Wars changed the context. As the Dutch state became a French client and then part of France, Java was isolated and exposed to British attack. Herman Willem Daendels, governing from 1808 to 1811, responded with centralization, military construction, and fiscal expedients, including the Great Post Road, built by compulsory labor, and aggressive treatment of the Javanese courts. His reforms strengthened the government on paper but strained the population and the finances.

The British conquest of 1811 brought Thomas Stamford Raffles and a program of land rent, judicial change, and scholarly inquiry. The 1812 assault on Yogyakarta deposed the Sultan, plundered the kraton, and reorganized court politics, creating lasting resentment. Raffles's reforms were uneven and limited, but his administration generated knowledge, including The History of Java, that remains an important though colonial source.

After the Anglo-Dutch Convention of 1814, the Dutch returned to Java in 1816 under Commissioners-General and then Governor-General van der Capellen. The restored government created a more formal structure, kept elements of earlier systems, and faced fiscal constraints. The Pattimura revolt of 1817 in the Moluccas showed that claims to sovereignty did not guarantee local acceptance.

By 1824 the Netherlands Trading Society had been founded and the Anglo-Dutch Treaty had divided British and Dutch spheres in maritime Southeast Asia, lines that anticipated later borders but that ran ahead of real control. Everyday life for farmers, village heads, merchants, soldiers, and laborers involved continuing obligations and burdens under each government, aggravated in the early 1820s by epidemic and natural disaster.

In Central Java, grievances over land leases, tolls, court status, and European interference converged with the standing of Prince Diponegoro to produce the crisis that led to war in 1825. The transformation from the VOC to the colonial state was thus a prolonged and contested reorganization: institutions changed, but the dependence on coercion and local intermediaries endured, and the resulting territorial state was ambitious, formalized, and uneven in its reach.

The governing regulation defining the structure and powers of colonial government after the restoration.

The 1817 rebellion in the central Moluccas led by Thomas Matulessy against restored Dutch rule.

A commercial company founded in 1824 under William I to carry colonial produce and support Dutch trade.

The London treaty of 17 March 1824 dividing British and Dutch spheres in maritime Southeast Asia.

Local rulers, regents, and village heads who mediated between colonial government and rural populations.

A government claiming territorial sovereignty over a colony, with formal institutions of administration, taxation, and force, as distinct from a trading company.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a state is made from the inheritance of a company. The VOC's dissolution did not create a colonial government at a stroke; it began a reorganization that was shaped by European war, British ambition, and the choices of Indonesian rulers, regents, soldiers, and villagers. Understanding that process helps students see the nineteenth-century colonial state as constructed, contested, and dependent, not as an inevitable or all-powerful entity.

The chapter also reveals a larger pattern in modern Southeast Asian history: the gap between claimed sovereignty and effective authority. The same gap appears in the Moluccan revolt of 1817, in the Anglo-Dutch Treaty's lines drawn over polities that were not consulted, and in the tensions of Central Java. It would recur in later chapters on colonial conquest, from the Java War to the Aceh War, and in the stories of British and French expansion elsewhere in the region.

Several continuities survive beyond 1825: reliance on intermediaries, compulsory labor and deliveries, fiscal improvisation, and Indonesian resistance. The Cultivation System of Chapter 102 would exploit and intensify these. At the same time, the era's innovations, including bureaucratic formalization, infrastructure, systematic knowledge, and the Anglo-Dutch division of the archipelago, shaped the political geography on which later colonial states and eventually modern nations would be built. In the larger textbook, this chapter marks the point where the maritime commercial world of earlier volumes gives way to territorial colonial rule, while reminding students that Southeast Asian societies remained historical actors throughout.
