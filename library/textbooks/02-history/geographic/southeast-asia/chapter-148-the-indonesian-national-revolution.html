Chapter Opening

On 27 December 1949, ceremonies in Amsterdam and Jakarta formally ended Dutch sovereignty over most of the former Netherlands East Indies. The Indonesian delegation at the Dutch ceremony was headed by Mohammad Hatta, who had been one of the signatories of the declaration of independence in August 1945. By December 1949 he was the prime minister of a new federal state, the Republic of the United States of Indonesia. The difference between these two moments is the central historical problem of this chapter. In 1945 Indonesians asserted their independence; four years later the former colonial power formally recognized the sovereignty of an Indonesian successor state. The intervening years explain how a political claim that the Netherlands initially refused to acknowledge became the foundation of an internationally recognized government.

The path between proclamation and settlement was neither straightforward nor assured. The Republic began with limited resources, a rapidly assembled administration and armed organizations that did not all answer to a single command. It faced the return of a colonial government backed by substantial military resources. The Dutch sought to restore authority through negotiation, armed force and the construction of federal political institutions. The Republic suffered military defeats and lost access to valuable economic territories. In December 1948 its capital was occupied and its leading officials detained. Nevertheless, civil and military institutions elsewhere continued to function, and the Dutch government ultimately negotiated the restoration of the very Republic it had attempted to remove from the political scene.

At the same time, Indonesians disagreed about the meaning of independence. Some understood it principally as the achievement of national sovereignty; others sought a wider transformation of landholding, labor, local authority or religious and political institutions. In parts of Java and Sumatra, established elites confronted social revolutions. Elsewhere traditional rulers joined the Republican cause or participated in federal political arrangements. The dispute between federal and unitary models was shaped both by colonial strategy and by real differences among regions. The Madiun crisis of 1948 revealed profound conflict within the nationalist movement itself. The national revolution was thus also a struggle over the kind of society and state that would emerge from decolonization.

International politics changed the conditions of the struggle. Indonesian diplomats built relationships with foreign governments before the Netherlands recognized sovereignty. India, Australia, Egypt and other countries helped challenge the characterization of Indonesia as an exclusively Dutch concern. The newly established United Nations provided institutions through which the conflict could be discussed and mediated. American policy evolved as the costs of the war, prospects for European recovery and political developments in Asia interacted. None of these external developments can explain independence without reference to the Indonesian institutions and movements that gave international diplomacy a viable political counterpart.

Historians study this transformation through diplomatic documents, United Nations resolutions, constitutional texts, government records, military archives, financial evidence, newspapers, oral histories and regional studies. Agreements are particularly useful for identifying formal commitments, but they cannot establish whether those commitments were implemented or explain how ordinary people experienced the war. Evidence from a local community may reveal changes invisible in the records of a conference. Conversely, a vivid individual account cannot necessarily establish the behavior of an entire region or political movement. Throughout this chapter, students should compare evidence at different scales and distinguish claims to authority from the actual exercise of power.

Chapter 147 examined the proclamation and the immediate creation of Republican institutions. The present chapter begins with the problem of defending those institutions and traces the national revolution through social upheaval, Linggadjati, the first Dutch offensive, Renville, Madiun, the second offensive, the emergency government, international intervention and the negotiations of 1949. It ends with the transfer of sovereignty. Chapter 149 takes up the separate problem of integrating the newly independent state. The argument of the present chapter is that independence was secured by the interaction of resistance, institutional adaptability, diplomatic strategy, popular organization, regional political accommodation and changing international conditions. The importance of each factor can be understood only by examining the ways it affected the others.

Historical Context

The territorial framework within which Indonesian nationalism developed was largely inherited from the Netherlands East Indies. Dutch colonial expansion had incorporated diverse societies into a political and economic system whose administrative center was Batavia. Yet the colonial state had not erased the differences among its territories. Javanese administrative traditions, Sumatran commercial and religious networks, the courts of eastern Indonesia, plantation labor communities and the maritime connections of Borneo, Sulawesi and Maluku retained distinctive features. The Republic's claim to succeed the colonial state therefore encompassed a vast geographical area whose inhabitants had experienced colonial rule through different institutions. The political integration of that area was a historical problem rather than a natural consequence of drawing a boundary on a map.

The nationalist movement had developed before the Second World War through political parties, schools, newspapers, labor organizations and religious associations. The promotion of Indonesian as a common national language helped activists communicate across regional and linguistic boundaries. The movement contained different political tendencies, including secular nationalism, Islamic political organization, socialism and communism. Colonial restrictions and repression shaped the careers of its leaders, some of whom spent years imprisoned or exiled. A shared opposition to colonial rule did not eliminate differences over the social and constitutional arrangements that should follow independence. These differences became more consequential when nationalist leaders acquired actual responsibility for government.

The Japanese occupation, treated in Chapter 144, transformed the immediate conditions of Indonesian politics. Wartime mobilization created new organizations and expanded some opportunities for Indonesians to acquire administrative and military experience. It also subjected the population to severe extraction, labor coercion, shortages and political restrictions. The defeat of Japan in August 1945 disrupted the authority of the occupation regime before an agreed successor administration could take its place. Nationalist leaders and local activists took advantage of that disruption to proclaim independence and establish Republican institutions. They inherited damaged infrastructure, an unstable monetary system and a population whose experiences of the occupation differed greatly. The revolution began amid the consequences of war, not under conditions favorable to orderly state-building.

Allied responsibilities after the Japanese surrender complicated the political situation. British and British Indian troops entered parts of Java and Sumatra to receive the surrender of Japanese forces, release and evacuate prisoners and maintain order. Dutch civilian authorities sought to reestablish their administration in connection with Allied operations. In other parts of the archipelago, the circumstances of reoccupation differed. These arrangements affected the relative opportunities of local nationalist organizations to establish open governmental institutions. The resulting military confrontation was not merely a dispute over whether a declaration had been issued. It concerned which authority would govern, command armed forces, control resources and represent Indonesia internationally.

The Netherlands itself had suffered German occupation and faced the demands of postwar reconstruction. Dutch policymakers regarded Indonesian economic resources and established commercial relationships as important to recovery and to the country's international position. Some recognized the necessity of constitutional change but disagreed about the scope and timing of Indonesian independence. The federal strategy developed partly within this context. Its advocates maintained that a federation would accommodate the diversity of the archipelago; Republican opponents argued that institutions established under Dutch military protection were designed to preserve influence and divide the national movement. The dispute between these perspectives would shape both warfare and diplomacy.

The international setting was also changing. The United Nations had been founded in 1945, India achieved independence in 1947 and struggles over colonial rule were unfolding elsewhere in Southeast Asia. Governments differed over whether colonial conflicts should be regarded as domestic matters or issues of international peace and security. The emergence of the Cold War affected American, Soviet and European policies, but Asian governments and Indonesian nationalists pursued interests not reducible to that rivalry. The Indonesian revolution should be understood within this wider setting while retaining the agency of Indonesians themselves. The following sections examine how local and national institutions interacted with these international opportunities and constraints.

Core Vocabulary Preview

National revolution
A struggle to establish or defend national sovereignty against an existing political order. In this chapter, the term denotes the 1945–1949 struggle to preserve the Republic and end Dutch colonial rule.
Social revolution
A fundamental transformation of social authority, property or hierarchy. Local upheavals challenged officials, aristocrats and existing patterns of economic power.
Regional revolution
A local or regional transformation shaped by particular institutions, histories and conditions of colonial reoccupation. It need not imply separatism.
Republican legitimacy
The claimed or accepted right of the Republic and its institutions to exercise governmental authority. Legitimacy can exist even when effective control is disputed.
National sovereignty
Supreme independent political authority of a state in its internal and international affairs. The Republic asserted sovereignty in 1945; the Dutch transfer recognized the RIS in 1949.
De facto recognition
Acknowledgment of authority exercised in practice, without necessarily granting full legal recognition of sovereignty. Linggadjati used this distinction.
De jure recognition
Formal legal recognition of a government or state. Its implications depend on the nature of the recognition and relevant legal arrangements.
Effective control
The practical ability of an authority to govern, administer services and enforce decisions in a territory. It is distinct from a claim to jurisdiction or political allegiance.
Federalism
A constitutional arrangement distributing powers between a common national government and constituent units. The distribution is established through the constitutional framework.
Unitary state
A state in which ultimate constitutional authority resides in one national order, although administrative powers can be decentralized.
Constituent state
A political unit forming part of a federation, with authority defined by its federal constitution. It is not normally an independent sovereign state in its own right.
Coalition government
A government supported by several political organizations or groups. Its stability depends in part on their continued cooperation.
Diplomatic mediation
Assistance from a third party in negotiating a dispute. Mediation can provide procedures, communication and proposals without necessarily imposing a settlement.
Ceasefire
An arrangement to suspend hostilities. A ceasefire need not resolve territorial claims, sovereignty or the causes of conflict.
Armistice
A formal arrangement suspending military hostilities; the precise legal and practical effects depend on the text.
Internationalization
The transformation of a dispute previously treated as domestic or bilateral into a matter involving international governments or institutions.
Colonial restoration
An attempt to reestablish the authority of a colonial power after disruption or loss of control.
Revolutionary opposition
Opposition arising within a revolutionary movement, often over strategy, authority or the social meaning of change.
Emergency government
A temporary governmental arrangement created to preserve public authority when ordinary institutions cannot operate normally.
Constitutional continuity
Preservation of the identity and legitimacy of a political order across an institutional crisis, even when its ordinary procedures are disrupted.
Demobilization
The release or dissolution of military personnel and organizations, requiring practical arrangements for their transition to other employment or service.
Military rationalization
Reorganization of military personnel, command structures and resources to create a more coherent and sustainable force.
Economic blockade
Restrictions on the movement of goods, trade and access to markets intended to constrain an opposing authority or territory.
Sovereignty settlement
A negotiated arrangement resolving or redefining competing claims to sovereign political authority, often accompanied by economic, military and constitutional instruments.
National integration
The process of incorporating territories, communities and institutions within a common national political framework.

Section 148.1

The Republic at the Beginning of the National Revolution

By the end of 1945, the Republic of Indonesia had acquired some of the outward characteristics of a state without possessing secure command of the territory it claimed. Sukarno and Mohammad Hatta had been installed as president and vice president, the Constitution of 1945 supplied an institutional framework, and Indonesian officials had begun to organize ministries, provinces and local administrations. Yet Allied troops had entered important ports, Dutch officials were returning with them, and armed organizations claiming allegiance to the Republic often operated with considerable independence. The first problem of the national revolution was consequently not to announce independence a second time, but to convert a declaration into a durable system of government capable of withstanding an attempt to restore colonial rule.

The institutional resources of the Republic had been assembled over several different historical periods. The late colonial state had trained Indonesians as clerks, teachers, technical specialists and subordinate administrators, even while restricting their participation in senior positions. Japanese occupation had widened some opportunities for Indonesian administration and mobilization under coercive wartime conditions. Nationalist parties, Islamic associations, educational organizations, trade unions and youth groups provided networks outside official institutions. When Japanese authority broke down, Indonesians could draw upon personnel who knew how to maintain records, run railways, publish newspapers, collect revenues and organize meetings. These resources did not guarantee a functioning state, but they reduced the distance between the political claim to independence and the everyday work of making it effective.

The constitutional arrangement itself changed during the first months. The Central Indonesian National Committee (Komite Nasional Indonesia Pusat, or KNIP), initially an advisory institution, acquired legislative functions pending elected representative institutions through the vice-presidential proclamation of 16 October 1945. The announcement of 3 November encouraged the formation of political parties. In November a cabinet headed by Sutan Sjahrir emerged under parliamentary practice, while the presidency remained in existence. These changes offered channels through which ideological groups could compete within the Republic and made the government less easily identifiable with a single wartime leadership. They also created difficult questions about ministerial responsibility, presidential prerogatives and the relationship between elected government, temporary representative institutions and revolutionary authority.

Administration proceeded unevenly across the archipelago. In many districts of Java and Sumatra, officials took over government offices and transferred their allegiance to the Republic. In eastern Indonesia and parts of Borneo, earlier Allied and Dutch reoccupation constrained the formation of comparable administrations. A Republican governor might be appointed for a large region without possessing the means to enforce decisions throughout it. Conversely, a community with a strong nationalist movement might fall outside any effective Republican administrative boundary. The difference between jurisdiction claimed on paper, military presence on the ground and allegiance expressed by inhabitants would recur throughout the revolution. A single map showing one uniformly Republican territory would conceal this problem.

Military organization presented a parallel challenge. The People's Security Agency (BKR) was succeeded by the People's Security Army (TKR) in October 1945; later reorganizations eventually produced the Indonesian National Army (TNI) in 1947. Former members of Japanese-trained formations, former soldiers of the colonial army and recruits without formal military experience brought different skills and expectations into these institutions. Beside regular forces stood numerous laskar, or armed associations, connected to political parties, religious groups, neighborhoods and local leaders. Their strength increased the capacity for resistance but complicated discipline, supply and the execution of agreements concluded by civilian leaders. Military institutionalization involved bargaining over authority, not simply changing the name of an army.

The Republic faced material difficulties that a constitution could not resolve. Wartime extraction had damaged rural livelihoods, transport and commercial distribution. Several currencies circulated and the availability of cash did not ensure the availability of food. Officials struggled to pay employees or maintain services. Nevertheless, the ordinary activities of civil administration had political significance: a functioning school, a recognized appointment, a reliable local court or a tax accepted as legitimate represented practical reasons for citizens to continue dealing with Republican institutions. The state was being constructed through transactions with society as well as through speeches and military commitments.

International standing remained another dimension of this unfinished process. The Netherlands denied that the proclamation had ended its legal sovereignty, and its description of Republican leaders as products of the Japanese occupation aimed to weaken their credibility. The Republic sought relations with foreign journalists, governments and international organizations to overcome that characterization. Some foreign governments recognized the Republic before December 1949; Dutch recognition through the eventual settlement was a distinct event. The national revolution therefore combined three connected but distinguishable objectives: effective domestic government, preservation of independent political authority against armed restoration, and external acknowledgment of that authority. Failure on any one of these fronts could endanger advances on the others.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From the Proclamation of 1945 to the National Revolution

Change in political authority. The proclamation asserted an independent political order; the revolution tested whether this order could maintain a cabinet, territorial administration, armed forces, public finance and foreign representation despite hostile intervention.

Continuity in institutional resources. Officials, railway workers, clerks, teachers and former military personnel brought experience acquired under colonial and Japanese rule into the new institutions. The political purpose of their work changed even where administrative procedures remained familiar.

Uneven change. The transfer of allegiance was more feasible in some inland districts than in ports subject to Allied or Dutch authority. The existence of a new national government therefore did not imply identical local transformations.

Historical Vocabulary

National Revolution

A national revolution seeks a fundamental change in the sovereign political status of a people. In Indonesia it refers here to the struggle of 1945–1949 to maintain the independence proclaimed in August 1945 and secure the termination of Dutch colonial sovereignty.

National revolution is analytically distinct from social revolution, which concerns the distribution of authority, property and status within society. Both occurred together in Indonesia, but an advocate of national independence did not necessarily favor the overthrow of local elites or the redistribution of land.

Section 148.2

The Legacy of Surabaya and the Relocation of Republican Government

The conflict at Surabaya in late 1945 marked the transition from the turbulent aftermath of the proclamation to a prolonged struggle over the survival of the new state. As Chapter 147 explains, the confrontation arose from the collision of Allied responsibilities after Japan's surrender with the activities of Indonesians determined to prevent the return of colonial government. For the national revolution, its importance lay less in the possession of a particular city than in the consequences of the confrontation for political legitimacy, military organization and diplomatic judgment. The capacity of local populations to sustain resistance showed that the Republic could not be dismissed as merely a declaration made by a small group of politicians in Jakarta.

Surabaya also revealed an imbalance in conventional military resources. British and British Indian forces possessed the equipment, communications and logistical support of an established military organization. Indonesian resistance drew on regular formations as well as less centralized revolutionary groups. Such mobilization could impose costs and deny a foreign administration the appearance of consensual rule, but it did not assure a military victory over better-equipped forces. The suffering of the civilian population made it clear that urban confrontation carried costs that extended well beyond the combatants. The commemoration of 10 November as Heroes' Day subsequently expressed the place of Surabaya in Indonesian national memory, although a national commemoration cannot reproduce the full diversity of local experiences.

The political implications were complex. To nationalists who distrusted negotiation, the resistance demonstrated that popular determination ought to be the foundation of policy. For leaders considering the international balance of power, it underscored the need to protect the central government, maintain international contact and avoid situations in which a superior conventional force could disable the Republic's principal institutions. These were not necessarily incompatible judgments. Resistance could make negotiations possible by demonstrating that restoration was costly, while diplomacy could help prevent the destruction of the social and institutional resources upon which future resistance depended. The controversy concerned the conditions and limits of negotiation rather than a simple opposition between courage and compromise.

Jakarta remained an important commercial and communications center, but its status as the old colonial capital made it an obvious center of Allied and Dutch administration. Republican ministers and officials experienced increasing insecurity there. In early January 1946, the leadership relocated the seat of Republican government to Yogyakarta. The decision relied on the support of Sultan Hamengkubuwono IX and Paku Alam VIII, who had declared their territories part of the Republic. Their commitment offered established administrative institutions and a comparatively secure setting in the interior of Java. Relocation was a political alliance as well as a practical response to the military situation.

The Yogyakarta rulers' support illustrates why the revolution should not be interpreted as a uniform repudiation of every inherited institution. The sultanate had exercised authority under the colonial system, but its leaders could redirect that authority toward the Republic. Local officials, financial resources and the prestige of the court helped sustain the new government. The provision of facilities and resources to national institutions made the commitment tangible rather than ceremonial. The Republic gained legitimacy through an alliance with a traditional political center, while the sultanate secured a recognized position within the emerging national order. This outcome differed sharply from the destruction or weakening of aristocratic authority elsewhere.

The move also imposed practical costs. Central administration was separated from the principal port, the foreign diplomatic community and major colonial financial institutions. Republican negotiators still had to communicate with representatives outside the capital, and the movement of documents, goods and personnel depended upon transport links subject to interruption. Government offices had to be established or adapted in a city not designed as the administrative center of a modern archipelagic state. The relocation thus demonstrated both the Republic's vulnerability and its capacity to improvise. Its survival did not depend on continuous possession of the former colonial capital.

In retrospect, Yogyakarta became closely associated with the Republic's endurance, but its selection should not be mistaken for a guarantee of security. Its occupation by Dutch forces in December 1948 would test whether government could survive the loss of this second capital as well. The lesson of 1946 was that a government could move while maintaining political identity and that local alliances could become national resources. The more severe test of 1948–1949 would require a further separation between the location of the leadership and the existence of the state itself. Surabaya and the relocation therefore establish an important theme for the chapter: territorial setbacks could be devastating without necessarily ending the Republic's institutional or political life.

Cause and Effect

Military Pressure and the Relocation of Republican Government

Structural conditions

The concentration of colonial government and international connections in Jakarta made the city important to both rival authorities. The Republic inherited the capital without possessing secure control over it.

Immediate pressure

Allied and Dutch presence and the consequences of the early confrontations increased the risks to Republican leaders and civil administration.

Enabling condition

The support of the Yogyakarta and Pakualaman rulers provided administrative facilities, political prestige and resources.

Consequences

A new seat of government increased institutional security but did not remove economic or diplomatic dependence on connections outside Yogyakarta.

Relocation depended on both pressure and opportunity. Military insecurity explains why leaders sought another base; local political cooperation helps explain why Yogyakarta was a feasible choice. Neither circumstance alone is a complete explanation.

Section 148.3

Social Revolution in Java and Sumatra

The national revolution raised questions about social authority that could not be answered by declaring an end to foreign rule. For much of the population, the colonial order had been experienced through local officials, landowners, plantation managers, religious authorities and hereditary rulers rather than through direct contact with European administrators. Wartime requisitions, compulsory labor and shortages had intensified grievances. When established coercive institutions weakened in 1945, demands for independence could become demands to change who controlled land, taxation, local offices and access to essential resources. The phrase social revolution refers to these struggles over the organization of society, which intersected with but were not identical to the national struggle against Dutch sovereignty.

The Three Regions Affair in the north-central Javanese districts of Tegal, Brebes and Pemalang demonstrates this intersection. Rural and urban activists challenged local administrators in late 1945, drawing upon longstanding resentment of the colonial administrative hierarchy and the experience of Japanese occupation. Officials associated with requisitions or other unpopular policies became targets of demands for their replacement. Youth activists, local political organizers and individuals with older radical affiliations participated in movements that sought to redefine legitimate authority. The resulting upheaval included coercion and violence, but it also involved experiments in local government and arguments about whether administrative office should be accountable to the population rather than inherited from the previous order.

It is important not to describe every participant as a member of a single organized movement. Economic grievances, personal rivalries, political commitments and disputes about local leadership overlapped. Some inhabitants supported the removal of officials while others feared the disorder associated with the breakdown of ordinary administration. Republican authorities had to confront a difficult dilemma: the same popular mobilization that had helped prevent colonial restoration could challenge the government's own appointments and its ability to administer a district. The eventual reassertion of Republican authority in the Three Regions shows that the revolutionary government did not automatically endorse every action performed in the name of independence. Historians including Anton Lucas and Benedict Anderson have used local political evidence to examine these tensions between state-building and popular mobilization.

East Sumatra offered a different social setting. Plantation capitalism had created a multiethnic labor force and substantial economic inequalities; Malay and other aristocratic institutions possessed particular relationships with the colonial system. In March 1946 a social revolution targeted hereditary rulers and members of aristocratic families. Political activists and armed organizations acted amid uncertainty about the rulers' attitudes toward independence and accumulated grievances concerning social privilege. The upheaval destroyed or gravely weakened a number of established political structures, and its violence affected people whose conduct or sympathies varied widely. The experiences of individual families cannot be inferred from their social classification. Anthony Reid's regional scholarship places these events within the distinctive history of northern Sumatra rather than treating them simply as a local copy of the Javanese revolution.

Comparing East Sumatra with Yogyakarta helps explain why inherited status alone did not determine a ruler's fate. The Yogyakarta court aligned itself openly with the Republic and could supply resources useful to the national leadership. In East Sumatra, different relationships between plantation society, labor organizations, local rulers and colonial authority created a more combustible political environment. Local actors had different opportunities to mobilize, and the anticipated return of Dutch power made the future position of traditional institutions especially contentious. The contrast demonstrates the importance of political choices and institutional relationships. It would be misleading to suggest either that aristocracies were uniformly committed to colonial restoration or that the Republic invariably sought to abolish them.

The disruption of local hierarchy also raised questions of gender and household survival. Political mobilization drew women into provisioning, communications, nursing, associations and other public responsibilities, while social unrest could expose women and children to displacement or violence that had little relation to their individual political actions. Households experienced changes in property, employment and access to markets that are often difficult to reconstruct from the archives of central political institutions. Local testimony and social history are essential complements to diplomatic records because the latter mainly explain negotiations among governments rather than the distribution of the revolution's consequences.

Social and national revolution thus reinforced one another in some settings and came into conflict in others. Popular actions could dismantle institutions that the Dutch might otherwise use in restoring their authority; they could also undermine public order and alienate groups the Republic hoped to incorporate. Central leaders' efforts to regulate autonomous movements were part of the construction of national government, but regulation could appear to local activists as the abandonment of social transformation. The disagreement would remain significant in the political conflicts of 1946–1948. It also explains why independence in 1949 did not settle every question about land, class, local authority or the distribution of the benefits of sovereignty.

Historical Debate

National Independence or Social Revolution?

Historians can ask whether the decisive transformation was the removal of colonial sovereignty, a change in social relations, or the interaction of both. These are questions of explanatory emphasis rather than mutually exclusive definitions.

National liberation. Diplomatic agreements, the organization of national government and the preservation of Republican authority support an account focused on the struggle against the Netherlands. This perspective explains the national framework but may understate locally initiated upheavals.
Social transformation. Local records and studies of the Three Regions and East Sumatra reveal struggles over officeholding, labor and aristocratic privilege. Such evidence demonstrates that independence acquired meanings beyond the constitutional question, though it cannot by itself explain the outcome of international negotiations.
Interaction. Regional comparisons, especially with Yogyakarta, show that the national struggle could either protect or destabilize existing institutions depending on their relationships to Republican authority. This interpretation requires evidence from both national and local scales.

Section 148.4

Regional Revolutions beyond Java

The Republic's claim encompassed the former Netherlands East Indies, but the revolution developed through sharply different regional settings. The archipelago's seas connected communities through commerce and migration while also complicating the movement of officials, information and supplies during war. The distribution of Allied occupation forces after Japan's surrender influenced how rapidly Dutch officials could return. Differences in the organization of local government, religious associations, labor and nationalist networks shaped how communities responded. It is therefore necessary to distinguish the national political framework from the regional processes through which inhabitants either supported it, negotiated alternatives or resisted particular institutions claiming to represent it.

Aceh was especially important because direct Dutch military reoccupation did not occur there during the revolution. Muslim scholars and organizations, including networks associated with the All-Aceh Association of Ulama (PUSA), exercised considerable influence. The Cumbok conflict of late 1945 and early 1946 pitted forces associated with religious leaders against elements of the hereditary territorial elite known as uleebalang. The outcome transformed local authority and helped consolidate support for the Republic. But Acehnese attachment to independence did not erase distinct regional political and religious aspirations. The survival of local institutions capable of mobilizing resources made Aceh valuable to Republican resistance, while the subsequent relationship between Aceh and the central state would remain a separate problem of national integration.

In West Sumatra, Minangkabau commercial, educational and religious networks connected local society to other parts of Indonesia. The Republican presence in the interior around Bukittinggi gave Sumatra a governmental role that became particularly important after December 1948. The coast and principal urban centers were more exposed to Dutch military pressure than many inland communities. East Sumatra, by contrast, contained important plantation districts and the city of Medan, where rival claims to political authority were closely related to access to valuable commercial assets. South Sumatra's oil, agricultural resources and river connections around Palembang made its political and economic position significant. None of these settings can be treated as representative of the island in its entirety.

In Sulawesi, earlier Allied and Dutch reoccupation limited the possibilities for establishing an open Republican administration. Nonetheless, nationalist networks organized resistance and maintained ties to the wider Republic. In southern Sulawesi, the Dutch counterinsurgency associated with Raymond Westerling in 1946–1947 involved extrajudicial killings and collective coercion against local populations. Such violence must be understood within the effort to impose colonial authority, not dismissed as an incidental consequence of an otherwise purely constitutional federal project. At the same time, the island included political actors willing to consider federal arrangements for reasons that ranged from local autonomy to dependence on restored Dutch institutions. Opposition to a centralized Republican government was not identical to support for indefinite colonial rule.

Bali provides another contrast. Nationalist resistance associated with I Gusti Ngurah Rai confronted the reestablishment of Dutch authority. His death in November 1946 became a central element of Balinese revolutionary memory. Political organization on the island was also shaped by its courts, local communities and the development of the State of East Indonesia. The existence of a federal political institution did not eliminate Republican sympathies. In the Lesser Sunda Islands, differences between local rulers, religious communities and colonial administrative arrangements likewise prevented a single pattern of political mobilization. The historian must examine the scale of each claim rather than extrapolating from a famous resistance episode to the entire eastern archipelago.

In Kalimantan and Maluku, the early return of Dutch administration meant that many nationalists lacked the protected territorial base available to the Republic in central Java. Political movements nevertheless developed in urban areas, among workers and through regional associations. Some inhabitants favored closer association with the Netherlands, others supported independence, and many confronted decisions shaped by employment, religious affiliation, security and local leadership. In Maluku, recruitment into colonial military institutions formed part of the historical background to divisions over the future state. It does not justify assuming that all Moluccans shared a single political outlook. Later conflicts over integration belong chiefly to Chapter 149 and should not be read backward as predetermined outcomes.

Regional variation had consequences for national diplomacy. The Dutch could point to institutions outside Republican territory as evidence that the Republic did not represent every Indonesian community. Republican leaders responded that restricted administrative control often reflected foreign military occupation rather than the absence of nationalist feeling. Both arguments concerned representation, but neither territorial possession nor public declarations alone could measure the preferences of an entire population. The subsequent inter-Indonesian conferences would seek to bridge some of these differences. Their possibility rested on the recognition that the archipelago's political future involved more actors than the Dutch government and the leadership in Yogyakarta, even though those two governments remained central to ending the war.

Section 148.5

Sjahrir, Republican Government, and the Strategy of Diplomacy

The appointment of Sutan Sjahrir as prime minister in November 1945 gave the Republic a leadership arrangement suited to the difficult problem of external recognition. Sjahrir had participated in the nationalist movement before the war and had resisted association with the Japanese occupation administration. His political outlook combined national independence, democratic socialism and opposition to fascism. To governments that doubted the origins or representative character of the Republic, his presence provided an alternative to the Dutch portrayal of independence as the product of Japanese wartime policy. That portrayal had already become increasingly implausible in light of popular mobilization, but overcoming it in international politics required sustained work rather than a single rebuttal.

Sjahrir's strategy rested upon a distinction between the Republic's ultimate goal and the immediate terms it could obtain. He did not regard negotiation itself as a concession of the right to independence. Rather, he sought to turn the Republic into a recognized participant in discussions that the Netherlands initially preferred to treat as matters of colonial administration. A negotiated acknowledgment of Republican authority might create a territorial and political basis for further gains. It might also allow foreign governments to develop relations with the Republic without immediately recognizing its claims over the entire former colony. Diplomacy, in this conception, could enlarge the number of actors with a stake in a peaceful transition to Indonesian independence.

The strategy had domestic opponents. Tan Malaka, a veteran revolutionary with a history of international communist activity but an increasingly independent political position, became associated with the Persatuan Perjuangan, or Struggle Union. The coalition articulated a program centered on complete independence and rejection of arrangements that subordinated the Republic to Dutch authority. Its supporters feared that incremental recognition would transform the Republic into one component of a political order controlled from outside. They also questioned whether negotiating leaders could legitimately make concessions regarding territories claimed by all Indonesians. The Struggle Union drew support from organizations with different ideological commitments, making it more useful to understand it as a coalition around a political program than as a single disciplined party.

The dispute became particularly serious in 1946. Sjahrir's cabinet faced pressure from opposition groups and underwent changes in its political support. In June, Sjahrir was abducted by elements associated with the political and military opposition; the July crisis included demands for a change in government. Sukarno refused to surrender the direction of Republican policy to these challengers, and the central leadership eventually reasserted its position. The episode is often termed the July 3 Affair. Its origins lay in overlapping disagreements about diplomacy, civilian authority, military leadership and the distribution of power within the nationalist movement. It would be inaccurate to identify all soldiers with the challengers or all civilian leaders with the negotiating program.

The relationship between civilian and military leadership was especially consequential. Officers had to consider the implications of a ceasefire for formations that had been organized under urgent local conditions. Diplomats could promise a change in military policy only to the extent that the government could secure compliance from armed groups. Conversely, military leaders needed administrative institutions to provide resources, coordinate policy and maintain the foreign relationships that might constrain Dutch action. Mutual dependence did not eliminate disagreements over authority. The Republic's capacity to negotiate required its leaders to establish a working relationship with an army whose position was grounded in the struggle itself rather than merely in orders issued from the cabinet.

Sjahrir also faced the problem of negotiating with a Dutch government whose domestic political constraints differed from his own. Dutch officials debated how far recognition of the Republic could be reconciled with the continuation of the Netherlands' relationship to Indonesia. Parliamentary and public opinion in the Netherlands placed limits on concessions; officials in the East Indies had their own priorities. Negotiations therefore operated at several levels simultaneously: between the Republican and Dutch delegations, within each government, and among the communities and political organizations whose cooperation would be needed to implement an agreement. British mediation could help bridge differences but could not eliminate the conflicting objectives that produced them.

The importance of Sjahrir's diplomacy lay partly in the precedents it established. Negotiations culminated in the Linggadjati Agreement, demonstrating that the Netherlands could enter a written political arrangement with the Republican government. Yet the agreement's domestic reception and eventual collapse would show the limitations of the approach. Sjahrir's career during the early revolution provides an example of a broader problem in wars of decolonization: leaders must sometimes negotiate while possessing less military power than their opponents, but doing so can expose them to accusations that they have compromised the objectives that sustain their own authority. Evaluating the policy requires attention to available alternatives and outcomes, not just the apparent distance between its initial terms and eventual independence.

Section 148.6

The Linggadjati Agreement

The Linggadjati negotiations emerged from the recognition that neither the Dutch attempt to reestablish authority nor the Republic's claim to immediate control of the former colony could be resolved quickly by conventional political arrangements. British representatives had an interest in ending the confrontation, both because the Allied occupation was temporary and because prolonged involvement threatened Britain's own resources and standing in Asia. Lord Killearn, the British special commissioner in Southeast Asia, helped mediate the discussions. The Dutch delegation included Lieutenant Governor-General Hubertus van Mook and representatives of the Commission-General, while Sjahrir headed the Republican delegation. Negotiations at Linggadjati in western Java produced a draft initialed on 15 November 1946 and formally signed on 25 March 1947. The interval between those dates is an essential part of the history.

At the center of the text was Dutch acknowledgment of the Republic's de facto authority in Java, Madura and Sumatra. The distinction between de facto and de jure authority was not merely technical. The Republic obtained acknowledgment of its actual political existence, but the Netherlands did not thereby immediately recognize it as the fully sovereign government of the entire archipelago. Areas within the three islands were already subject to Dutch military occupation, and their future administrative status was to be addressed during implementation. Republican leaders hoped that recognition would secure a political foundation from which the larger national claim could advance. Dutch leaders could interpret the same formula as limiting the Republic's status to an interim regional position.

The agreement envisaged a sovereign federal United States of Indonesia encompassing the territory of the former Netherlands East Indies, subject to provisions concerning the wishes of particular populations. The Republic, Borneo and the Great East were envisaged as components of the future federal state, without making these categories an immutable constitutional map. A Netherlands-Indonesian Union would connect the new federation and the Kingdom of the Netherlands, with the Dutch monarch at its head and common institutions for matters of shared concern. The timetable envisaged the establishment of the federal and union arrangements before 1 January 1949. The arrangement was therefore prospective: it described a transition that still had to be carried out rather than establishing a sovereign federation on the day of signature.

The proposed Union illustrates the ambiguity of the settlement. Indonesian negotiators could accept an association of formally independent partners if it provided a path to the termination of colonial rule. Dutch negotiators and politicians could understand it as a means of preserving influence over defense, economic relations and external affairs. The practical meaning of common institutions would depend upon their legal powers, composition and relationship to the two governments. A federal Indonesia might protect regional interests, but it could also be used to limit the Republic's participation in national institutions. Neither the word federal nor the word union, standing alone, answers whether the resulting relationship would be equitable. Those questions were precisely what the subsequent negotiations needed to settle.

The agreement provoked controversy before formal signature. In the Netherlands, parliamentary endorsement was accompanied by interpretations and explanatory statements that Republicans feared would narrow the concessions of the negotiated text. In Indonesia, critics questioned whether the government had accepted restrictions inconsistent with the proclamation of August 1945. The enlargement and reorganization of the KNIP became part of the domestic political struggle over acceptance. Sjahrir's government ultimately secured sufficient support to proceed, but signature did not resolve the disagreement about what had been authorized. An agreement can bind parties to written terms while leaving them with divergent expectations about how those terms will be translated into institutions.

After March 1947, disputes over implementation increasingly concerned practical matters of authority. The Dutch sought an interim federal administration, arrangements for economic recovery and access to plantations, and cooperation on military and security questions. Republicans were reluctant to accept institutions that might subordinate their government before sovereignty had been achieved. Control of trade and foreign relations was important because economic resources and international contact supported the Republic's existence. Disputes over the return of property, joint police arrangements and troop dispositions were therefore inseparable from disagreements about the ultimate political settlement. The mutual suspicion generated by earlier armed confrontation made apparently technical issues difficult to resolve.

Linggadjati was simultaneously a breakthrough and an unstable compromise. It recorded direct negotiation between the two governments, created a framework for future sovereignty, and supplied principles that later international mediators could invoke. It also postponed major conflicts and failed to secure the cooperation needed for implementation. Its collapse should not be interpreted to mean that its provisions were irrelevant or that renewed war was inevitable from the moment of signature. The agreement shaped the terms of later mediation, including Renville and the eventual federal settlement. Its historical significance lies in both the political recognition it achieved and the unresolved contradictions that led the parties back into conflict.

Section 148.7

Dutch Federalism and the Contest over Indonesia's Political Future

Federalism entered the Indonesian revolution as both a constitutional question and an instrument of political competition. A federation divides authority between a national government and constituent political units under a common constitutional arrangement. A unitary state maintains a single ultimate constitutional authority, even if considerable power is delegated to provinces or local governments. Neither arrangement is inherently democratic or authoritarian, colonial or nationalist. What made federalism controversial in Indonesia in the 1940s was the process by which the proposed units were organized, the power of the Dutch authorities to shape that process, and the uncertainty over whether the resulting government would possess meaningful independence.

Van Mook promoted a federal structure that would prevent the Republic from becoming the sole successor to the Netherlands East Indies. Dutch officials argued that the archipelago's diversity required regional representation and that a government dominated by Java could not adequately protect the interests of distant communities. Federal units would participate in negotiations regarding the new state alongside the Republic. The approach also offered a strategic advantage to the Netherlands: a federation assembled under Dutch supervision could diminish Republican influence, preserve administrative relationships and provide an alternative to unconditional recognition of the Republic. These different justifications need to be examined together rather than assuming that official constitutional arguments fully explain the policy.

At the Malino Conference in southern Sulawesi in July 1946, delegates from Borneo and eastern Indonesia discussed the future political structure of areas outside the Republic's main zone of control. Further discussions at Denpasar in December 1946 led to the establishment of the State of East Indonesia, Negara Indonesia Timur (NIT), with its capital at Makassar. Its first president, Tjokorda Gde Raka Soekawati, came from Bali. The state included diverse territories with different histories of colonial administration and local political organization. It represented a concrete institutional expression of Dutch federal policy before the final signature of Linggadjati. Over the following years additional states and autonomous regions were formed, including East Sumatra and Pasundan in western Java.

The political character of these entities cannot be captured adequately by describing them simply as Dutch creations or as freely constituted expressions of regional preference. Dutch military and administrative power gave officials enormous influence over the circumstances in which they were established. The capacity of opponents to organize freely was restricted in some areas, and the existence of a state administration did not demonstrate that the population endorsed it. Nevertheless, local politicians participated for different reasons. Some feared that immediate incorporation into the Republic would compromise regional autonomy, others sought a transitional route to Indonesian independence, and still others valued their existing ties to the Netherlands. The choices available to them were shaped by local circumstances as well as Dutch policy.

This diversity became apparent in the formation of the Bijeenkomst voor Federaal Overleg (BFO), commonly translated as the Federal Consultative Assembly, in 1948. It provided representatives of the federal territories with a framework for coordinating their positions. Figures including Sultan Hamid II of Pontianak and Anak Agung Gde Agung of East Indonesia became important participants in discussions about a future Indonesian state. Federal politicians did not always accept the Dutch government's preferred policies. As the costs of continued war increased and the prospects of a genuinely sovereign federation became more concrete, some sought a political accommodation with the Republic. Their ability to pursue an independent position would become particularly important after the second Dutch offensive.

Republican leaders generally distrusted the proliferation of federal units under Dutch protection because it appeared to fragment a national movement whose claim extended to the entire former colony. Yet the Republic had already accepted the principle of a federation in Linggadjati. Its objections therefore concerned who could create constituent units, whether local populations were able to express their preferences freely, the allocation of powers during transition and the timing of sovereignty. It was possible to oppose Dutch-sponsored federalism while accepting a negotiated federal settlement as a temporary or substantive solution. The tension between these positions became central to the negotiations of 1949.

The competition over constitutional design was also a competition over political representation. The Netherlands could claim to speak for territories administered outside the Republic; the Republic could appeal to a national movement that extended beyond its actual jurisdiction. Federal representatives could claim distinct regional interests without denying Indonesian nationhood. The historical challenge is to test such claims against evidence of local organization, political freedoms, electoral practices and the relationship between institutions and military authority. The eventual federal Republic of the United States of Indonesia did not simply reproduce Van Mook's original plans. It resulted from bargaining in which Indonesian federal and Republican politicians acquired increasing capacity to shape the outcome. The durability of the resulting arrangement is a separate question considered in Chapter 149.

Section 148.8

The First Dutch Military Offensive and the Internationalization of the Revolution

By the middle of 1947, disputes over the implementation of Linggadjati had reached a point at which Dutch leaders chose to use large-scale military force. The decision followed months of competing proposals for an interim government, economic administration and security arrangements. The Netherlands faced mounting expenses from maintaining forces in Indonesia and hoped to recover economically productive territories. Republican leaders were unwilling to accept arrangements they regarded as incompatible with their independent authority. The negotiations did not produce an agreed method of resolving these disputes before the Dutch government authorized the offensive. The breakdown was therefore rooted in incompatible political objectives, economic pressures and decisions taken by identifiable governments rather than in an automatic consequence of the agreement's wording.

The offensive that began on 21 July 1947 is commonly known as Operation Product and, in Indonesia, as the First Dutch Military Aggression. Dutch authorities described it as a police action, a designation intended to frame the conflict as the restoration of order within territory over which the Netherlands still claimed sovereignty. Republican leaders rejected that characterization because it denied the status of their government and treated armed intervention against it as an internal administrative matter. Terminology was consequently part of the diplomatic struggle. If the conflict were accepted as a purely domestic police matter, the Dutch argument against international intervention would be strengthened; if regarded as warfare between parties to an international dispute, the case for United Nations involvement would become much stronger.

Dutch military superiority enabled the capture of important areas in Java and Sumatra, including economically productive districts and transport centers. These gains disrupted Republican administration and reduced access to commercial revenue and export commodities. The offensive did not eliminate the central government or destroy the political networks that sustained the Republic. Control of major economic assets brought the Dutch immediate advantages but also required the maintenance of administrative and security institutions over a population whose allegiances remained contested. Capturing an area and obtaining durable political acceptance of the authority exercised there were separate problems. The difference became increasingly apparent as the war continued.

For civilians, changes in territorial control could alter access to employment, markets, food, currency and local offices. Residents of areas brought under Dutch administration might find that the documents and money recognized by one authority were no longer accepted by the other. Government personnel and members of political organizations faced decisions about remaining in place, withdrawing or continuing their activities clandestinely. Such disruptions increased the pressures on the Republican government even where it retained control. Families moving from contested areas added to the demand for housing and supplies. The economic and administrative consequences of territorial contraction would become especially severe following the next diplomatic settlement.

The international response prevented the Netherlands from treating the offensive solely as a bilateral or colonial affair. India and Australia submitted communications to the United Nations Security Council on 30 July 1947. Their approaches to the legal basis for intervention differed, but both challenged the attempt to keep the conflict outside international consideration. On 1 August the Council adopted Resolution 27, calling for the cessation of hostilities and peaceful settlement of the dispute. Further action in August led to the establishment of a Committee of Good Offices to assist negotiations. The sequence gave the Republic access to an international forum despite its unsettled legal status and created a process through which the Netherlands' conduct could be scrutinized by other governments.

Internationalization did not mean that the Security Council immediately recognized all Republican claims or possessed the means to impose a final settlement. The great powers had different interests in the conflict, and the Council generally pursued mediation and negotiated agreement. For the Netherlands, however, the ability to frame the conflict as a domestic question had been significantly weakened. A new offensive might now carry diplomatic costs alongside its military and financial costs. The Republic could use reports of events in Indonesia, communications with sympathetic governments and participation in discussions to make its continued existence more visible. The institutional environment within which both parties made decisions had changed.

The first offensive consequently produced different results depending on the criterion used to assess it. In territorial and economic terms, the Netherlands gained significant advantages. In political terms, the Republic survived and the dispute acquired an international character that the Dutch government had not sought. The failure to reconcile these outcomes created a difficult situation for the next round of negotiations. Republican diplomats would have to negotiate from a materially weakened position, while the Netherlands would attempt to convert military gains into constitutional and political concessions. The Renville Agreement was the outcome of that effort, and its terms reveal the unequal bargaining position in which the Republic found itself.

Section 148.9

United Nations Intervention and the Renville Agreement

The Committee of Good Offices established a new framework for negotiation after the first Dutch offensive. The Republic selected Australia, the Netherlands selected Belgium, and the two chosen members agreed on the United States as the third participant. This arrangement allowed both parties a voice in constituting the mediating body without placing the final choice of mediator exclusively in either government's hands. The committee was not an international government with authority to dictate Indonesia's constitution. Its function was to assist the parties in reaching agreement, observe conditions and report to the Security Council. Its influence depended partly on the credibility of its members and the wider diplomatic consequences of refusing their proposals.

Negotiations took place aboard the American naval vessel USS Renville in the harbor of Batavia, the contemporary Dutch name for Jakarta. The location provided a setting outside the direct control of either delegation, although the surrounding political and military circumstances remained unequal. The Republican delegation under Prime Minister Amir Sjarifuddin confronted a Netherlands that held substantial territory acquired during the July offensive. The ceasefire arrangements agreed on 17 January 1948 addressed that situation, while accompanying political principles envisaged a longer-term settlement. The documents should be read separately from the consequences that followed them: the truce attempted to regulate the immediate military situation, whereas the principles outlined the political process that was supposed to replace it.

One of the agreement's most consequential elements was its treatment of the line claimed by the Netherlands as the limit of its advanced positions, commonly known as the Van Mook Line. The truce recognized a demarcation for purposes of regulating the cessation of hostilities rather than establishing an internationally recognized national frontier. It required the withdrawal of forces from areas assigned to the other side under the truce arrangements. Republican formations in pockets within Dutch-held territory faced especially difficult decisions. A temporary military demarcation could have long-lasting political effects because it affected which administration operated in a district and which government could collect resources there. It is therefore misleading to equate the Renville line with either the territorial extent of the Republic's original claim or the eventual boundaries of Indonesia.

The movement of the Siliwangi Division from West Java into Republican-held territory became a prominent example of the agreement's consequences. The relocation, often remembered as the Hijrah or migration, displaced soldiers and associated populations and changed the distribution of military formations within the Republic. It also left a more complicated political environment in West Java, where not every armed organization accepted the central government's decisions. The Darul Islam movement associated with Sekarmadji Maridjan Kartosuwiryo would develop in this context, although its origins cannot be reduced to the troop withdrawal alone. For Republican leaders, compliance with the truce was intended to preserve the possibility of diplomatic progress. For opponents, it appeared to surrender positions and expose local communities to renewed colonial authority.

The accompanying political principles envisaged movement toward a sovereign United States of Indonesia and arrangements through which populations could express their preferences concerning political affiliation. They discussed political freedoms, elections or plebiscites, and the transition from Netherlands sovereignty to the future federation. The implementation of these principles depended upon conditions in territories administered by opposing authorities. Could people express their preferences freely when political organizations faced restrictions, populations had been displaced and competing military institutions exercised power? The question of how an election would be supervised became nearly as important as the promise that one would take place. The documents supplied a basis for further bargaining, not a completed constitutional process.

The domestic reaction in the Republic was severe. Amir Sjarifuddin's government lost support and fell shortly after the agreement. Mohammad Hatta formed a new cabinet, confronting a reduced territorial and economic base, a displaced military establishment and bitter political divisions. The fall of the cabinet demonstrated how a diplomatic concession made under military pressure could alter the domestic balance of power. It did not erase the agreement, and the new government still had to operate within the constraints it had imposed. Political responsibility for a settlement therefore extended beyond the individuals who had signed it.

Renville also exposed the limits of international mediation. A formally accepted truce reduced some immediate hostilities but did not eliminate disputes over sovereignty or secure political agreement. The Committee of Good Offices could investigate complaints and advance proposals, yet implementation required cooperation by governments whose expectations remained substantially different. The Republic hoped that the international framework would prevent further Dutch military action, while Dutch leaders continued to seek terms that would preserve their influence during the transition. The arrangement left both sides dissatisfied in different ways. Its failure to produce a final settlement set the conditions for the political and military crises of 1948, when the Republic would have to demonstrate not only its capacity to resist colonial restoration but its ability to withstand serious conflict within its own ranks.

Section 148.10

Territorial Contraction, Economic Pressure, and Republican Administration

Territorial contraction placed severe strains on the economy of the Republic. The first Dutch offensive and the Renville demarcation reduced access to valuable plantations, ports, industrial facilities and transport routes. The Republican territories still contained agricultural production, workshops, labor and local commercial networks, but the government had fewer opportunities to convert these resources into revenue or foreign exchange. The loss of a port could matter far beyond its immediate surroundings: it could prevent commodities from reaching buyers, interrupt the import of medicines and machinery and weaken the financial capacity of ministries elsewhere. The Republic's territorial difficulties consequently translated into a struggle over the material foundations of government.

The monetary situation illustrates the problem. At independence, several kinds of currency inherited from the colonial and Japanese occupation periods circulated in Indonesia. The return of Dutch administration introduced further complications, including the circulation of money associated with the Netherlands Indies Civil Administration. The Republican government sought to establish a currency of its own, Oeang Republik Indonesia (ORI, in contemporary spelling). ORI became legal tender on 30 October 1946. Its introduction symbolized the assertion of monetary authority, but it was also an attempt to simplify payments, sustain public expenditure and distinguish the Republican economic sphere from the territories under Dutch control. Bank Negara Indonesia, created in 1946, formed part of the developing Republican financial infrastructure.

Issuing money did not in itself make the economy stable. The government had to obtain the materials and technical facilities needed to produce notes, distribute them across insecure territory and persuade people to accept them in transactions. A farmer deciding whether to sell rice for Republican currency had to consider whether that currency could purchase goods later. A civil servant receiving wages in depreciating money might depend upon additional sources of income or help from relatives. The purchasing power of currency depended on the availability of commodities as well as confidence in its issuer. Inflation and shortages could weaken the practical relationship between the population and government even when political support for independence remained strong.

Communication and distribution difficulties led to the issue of regional Republican currencies, commonly known as ORIDA, in several areas. These arrangements helped maintain local transactions when the national currency was difficult to obtain. They also illustrate the tension between administrative unity and the need to improvise under extraordinary circumstances. A government seeking a common monetary system could not always supply the same means of payment throughout its claimed territory. The regional notes were evidence of local institutional initiative as well as of the weakness of central logistical capacity. Their existence helps explain why monetary integration remained necessary after sovereignty had been secured.

The Netherlands used restrictions on trade and shipping to constrain the Republican economy. Indonesian merchants and officials sought alternative channels for exchanging commodities and acquiring supplies, particularly through maritime connections with Singapore and the wider regional trading system. Rubber and other exportable goods could provide resources unavailable through ordinary public taxation. Such exchanges, which Dutch authorities frequently treated as illicit, were understood by Republican participants as a means of maintaining economic and political survival. They involved merchants and intermediaries with their own commercial interests; nationalist commitment and profit-making did not necessarily exclude one another. The extent and distribution of these activities varied with local conditions, and surviving official records do not always capture informal commerce accurately.

The burden of adjustment fell unevenly across society. Urban households dependent on wages were especially vulnerable when food prices rose and supplies became unreliable. Rural producers might have access to food but face coercive demands from rival armed or administrative authorities, reduced markets for their products and disruption of transport. Refugees and people separated from their ordinary employment needed accommodation and assistance. Women frequently performed the work of provisioning households and supporting community networks, while relief workers and medical personnel confronted shortages of equipment. These experiences show why the history of the Republican economy cannot be reconstructed solely from budgets, currency issues or agreements over export revenues.

Administrative policy under Hatta in 1948 therefore had to reconcile competing demands. The government needed to sustain the military, finance civil institutions, maintain supplies and demonstrate to foreign observers that the Republic possessed the capacity to govern. Reducing expenditure or reorganizing personnel might improve financial stability, yet it could also provoke resistance from groups whose income and status depended upon existing arrangements. The rationalization of the armed forces, discussed in the following section, had a substantial economic dimension. It illustrates how the external struggle against Dutch restoration created domestic conflicts over the allocation of scarce resources. The Republic's endurance depended in part on preserving enough ordinary economic activity to make political authority meaningful, even when its institutions could not provide the stability expected of a government in peacetime.

Section 148.11

Hatta's Government, Military Reorganization, and the Madiun Crisis

Mohammad Hatta became prime minister in January 1948 after the fall of Amir Sjarifuddin's cabinet. The new government confronted a combination of territorial contraction, financial weakness and political fragmentation. Hatta sought to reduce the strain of maintaining numerous armed organizations and improve the relationship between military command and the civilian government. Reorganization and rationalization, frequently described by the Indonesian abbreviation Re-Ra, attempted to create a more coherent armed force and reduce the costs of an establishment that had grown under improvised revolutionary conditions. The policy was not purely a technical reform. It concerned the distribution of authority, employment and political influence within the Republic.

The armed forces consisted of personnel with very different backgrounds. Former members of Japanese-sponsored military formations and colonial units brought varying degrees of training; laskar organizations drew strength from party, religious and local networks. Integrating formations into the national army required decisions about ranks, command relationships and eligibility for continued service. Demobilization presented additional difficulties in an economy that could not easily absorb former combatants. Officers and political organizations had reasons to resist changes that might diminish their influence. Rationalization could therefore appear to one group as necessary state-building and to another as an attempt to eliminate a rival faction under the guise of administrative reform.

The political opposition was reconstituted after Renville. Amir Sjarifuddin and other left-wing forces organized within the People's Democratic Front (Front Demokrasi Rakyat, or FDR), challenging the Hatta government's policies. The Indonesian Communist Party (PKI) was one of the organizations within the broader left. In August 1948, Musso returned from a long period abroad and promoted a reorganization of the communist movement and a more confrontational political line. Ideological disagreements were connected to labor mobilization, disputes over military rationalization and criticism of the cabinet's diplomatic strategy. The rising international tension between the United States and the Soviet Union provided a wider context, but Indonesian actors were engaged in conflicts that had their own domestic origins.

During the months preceding the Madiun crisis, clashes and political killings involving rival armed and political groups increased the insecurity of the Republic's interior. Rivalries around Surakarta were particularly significant. On 18 September 1948, left-associated forces took control of key institutions in Madiun in East Java. Sukarno and Hatta treated the events as a direct challenge to the Republican government, and Musso and the communist leadership aligned themselves with the confrontation. Republican forces suppressed the movement in the ensuing weeks. Musso was killed in October, and Amir Sjarifuddin was executed in December while the Republic faced the renewed Dutch offensive. The suppression involved arrests, executions and other forms of violence, the experiences and consequences of which were not limited to the leaders of the contending political organizations.

Historians have debated the nature of the Madiun events. One interpretation sees a communist attempt to overthrow the Republican government, drawing on the actions in Madiun, Musso's political program and the public confrontation between the leaders. Another places greater emphasis on the escalation of local military and political conflicts, asking how far national leaders planned the initial seizure of authority and how much the confrontation developed through events that escaped centralized control. These interpretations need not be mutually exclusive: local clashes can escalate into a national challenge even when the original course of events was not predetermined. The degree of coordination, intentions of particular leaders and chronology of decisions are questions that must be assessed through evidence rather than inferred from the eventual outcome.

The crisis had consequences for foreign perceptions of the Republic. Hatta's government's ability to suppress a challenge from the communist left made it more credible to some American policymakers who were increasingly concerned about communist influence in Asia. Yet it would be misleading to claim that Madiun alone caused the United States to support Indonesian sovereignty. The international mediation process was already under way, and later American decisions reflected Dutch military action, European recovery, Asian nationalism and broader geopolitical concerns. Madiun was one factor in an evolving assessment of the Republic, not a sufficient explanation of the settlement of 1949.

The domestic costs of the confrontation were substantial. A movement that presented itself internationally as a united national struggle had experienced bitter conflict among Indonesians committed to different versions of independence. The government's survival strengthened its claim to central political authority, while the defeat of the left altered the composition of the nationalist movement. The effects on local communities and political prisoners, and later competing memories of the events, cannot be understood merely as evidence of successful military consolidation. Madiun demonstrated that the Republic could confront internal challenges, but its suppression did not resolve every disagreement about economic organization, representation, the political role of the army or the relationship between ideological movements and the state. These issues would remain important after the struggle against Dutch restoration had ended.

Section 148.12

The Second Dutch Military Offensive and the Crisis of Republican Government

By the end of 1948 the Renville process had failed to produce the intended political settlement. Disagreements over the organization of a transitional government, the Republic's position in the future federation and the conditions for transferring sovereignty persisted despite international mediation. The Netherlands had acquired significant territory in 1947 but had not obtained the political outcome its leaders desired. Republican authorities continued to function within the reduced area available to them, and the suppression of Madiun had removed one of the government's most serious internal challenges. The situation led Dutch policymakers to consider a more decisive use of force against the central Republican institutions themselves.

On 19 December 1948, Dutch forces began the second major offensive, Operation Kraai, known in Indonesia as the Second Dutch Military Aggression. The attack resulted in the occupation of Yogyakarta and the detention of President Sukarno, Vice President and Prime Minister Hatta, and other Republican leaders. In military terms, the Netherlands had obtained control of the seat of government. In political terms, it hoped to demonstrate that the Republic could no longer function as a separate participant in negotiations. The operation reflected the assumption that control of the central institutions would enable the Netherlands to proceed toward a federal settlement on terms more favorable to its own policy.

The decision to remain in Yogyakarta and the continuation of resistance elsewhere illustrate the different responsibilities of political and military leaders. Sukarno and Hatta were detained but remained internationally recognizable representatives of the Republic. General Sudirman, despite serious illness, continued to exercise military leadership outside Dutch-held centers. His refusal to accept the cessation of Republican resistance made it more difficult to present the occupation of the capital as a final political outcome. The army's relationship to civilian government had been contentious, as earlier crises showed, but it continued to identify its struggle with the preservation of the Republic. The military resistance and the continued political relevance of the detained leaders both undermined the assumption that occupation had ended the state's existence.

Before their detention, Republican leaders made provision for emergency government. Communications with Sumatra were disrupted, and the circumstances in which emergency authority was established must be distinguished from a routine administrative transfer. Sjafruddin Prawiranegara and other officials in Sumatra proceeded to organize the Emergency Government of the Republic of Indonesia (PDRI), which began functioning on 22 December. Its existence gave the Republic an institutional center distinct from the detained leadership and the army. The precise relationships among these centers of authority would require coordination, but the survival of multiple institutions was itself important. The Netherlands could no longer assume that it had eliminated the government's ability to act simply by controlling its former capital.

The occupation intensified the suffering of civilians. In addition to the immediate effects of military operations, the loss or disruption of administrative centers affected the distribution of food, the payment of salaries and the availability of medical and other services. Local communities encountered the pressures associated with rival authorities and continued warfare. People who provided assistance to Republican organizations could face colonial repression, while households unable to move or protect themselves bore substantial risks. The persistence of the state should not be confused with an uninterrupted ability to provide ordinary public services. Institutional survival in this period often depended upon improvisation under conditions in which many individuals experienced severe hardship.

The renewed offensive had particularly serious diplomatic consequences because the United Nations was already engaged in efforts to resolve the dispute. The Security Council and the Committee of Good Offices had treated Renville as a framework for further negotiation. The detention of Republican leaders and disruption of their government raised questions about whether the Netherlands intended to honor that process. Other governments could assess the operation in relation to previous commitments rather than simply accept the Dutch description of an internal security measure. This increased the pressure on federal Indonesian politicians as well as on the Netherlands, because their prospective institutions risked losing legitimacy if associated with the destruction of the Republic.

The second offensive reveals the distinction between conventional military achievement and strategic political success. The Dutch secured important territory and disrupted the ordinary operation of Republican government, yet they did not obtain the internationally accepted elimination of the Republic. Instead, military resistance, emergency administration and foreign diplomatic pressure continued. The Netherlands would eventually have to negotiate the return of the very government it had sought to displace. This outcome was not certain in December 1948: the Republic could have suffered further fragmentation or the loss of external support. Understanding why those possibilities did not prevail requires examining the emergency government, international intervention and the changing relationship between Republican and federal Indonesian leaders during the first half of 1949.

The Emergency Government of the Republic of Indonesia

The Emergency Government of the Republic of Indonesia, or Pemerintahan Darurat Republik Indonesia (PDRI), was established in Sumatra on 22 December 1948. It arose because the second Dutch offensive had disrupted the normal functioning of the Republican cabinet and presidency. Its formation was not an attempt to establish an independent Sumatran state or replace the political objectives of the Republic. Its purpose was to preserve the capacity of the existing Republic to act while its principal leaders were detained. This distinction between an emergency government and a rival government is essential to understanding the PDRI's significance in the history of Indonesian state formation.

Before the Dutch occupation of Yogyakarta, the Republican leadership issued authorization for Sjafruddin Prawiranegara, a minister then in Sumatra, to form an emergency government. A further contingency contemplated the organization of a government abroad if circumstances made this necessary. The evidence concerning transmission and receipt of these instructions must be handled carefully: issuing an authorization from the capital does not establish that it reached its intended recipient at the appropriate time. Sjafruddin and his colleagues proceeded on their own initiative in the disrupted circumstances of Sumatra. Their actions were consistent with preserving the national government, whether or not the original message had arrived before the decision. The PDRI thus illustrates the importance of shared political purpose and regional initiative alongside formal delegation.

Sumatra possessed institutional resources that made emergency administration possible. Republican provincial authorities, military organizations, local officials and political networks had developed before the crisis. The inland areas of West Sumatra offered administrative and social connections that could continue functioning despite Dutch pressure on major centers. Emergency arrangements relied on these existing institutions rather than constructing an entire state from nothing. Ministers and other officials took on responsibilities under conditions of interrupted communication and limited supplies. The maintenance of governmental functions was uneven, but the ability to appoint representatives, communicate political decisions and coordinate resistance provided evidence of continued institutional life.

Communications were especially important because the Republic needed to establish that its government had not disappeared. Radio broadcasts and the transmission of official information helped maintain contact among dispersed Republican institutions and with representatives abroad. Diplomats outside Indonesia could continue to speak on behalf of a government whose central leaders were no longer free to operate normally. The value of communication was therefore constitutional and diplomatic as well as administrative. It linked actions in Sumatra, military resistance in Java and the efforts of Indonesian representatives in international forums. Disruption of ordinary correspondence did not mean that every channel of communication ceased to exist.

The PDRI's relationship with the national army demonstrates the advantages and difficulties of dispersed authority. General Sudirman continued to lead the military struggle, and Republican formations maintained resistance in Java and Sumatra. The military and emergency civil government shared a commitment to the Republic but possessed different responsibilities and sources of authority. Neither could automatically exercise detailed command over all organizations claiming allegiance to the state. This arrangement was less orderly than a conventional government operating from a secure capital; nonetheless, it made it more difficult for a single military defeat to eliminate the Republic's political and administrative existence. The government survived through a network of institutions whose activities were not wholly dependent on a single location.

In Java, the General Offensive of 1 March 1949 in Yogyakarta briefly demonstrated that the Dutch occupation had not eliminated organized Republican resistance. It became an important episode in national memory because it challenged the public impression that the Republic had ceased to possess military capacity. The role of individual leaders in its conception and the relative importance of the event have been subjects of historical and political debate. It should be placed within a broader pattern of resistance and diplomatic activity rather than credited alone with bringing about the settlement. The Security Council had already adopted a major resolution in January 1949; the military demonstration added to an existing political situation rather than creating international intervention from nothing.

When the detained leaders returned and ordinary Republican institutions were restored in mid-1949, the PDRI relinquished its emergency authority. The process was not automatic: questions of consultation and legitimacy arose because the detained leadership had participated in negotiations whose implications affected the emergency government and the army. The return of authority on 13 July 1949 nevertheless affirmed that the PDRI's purpose had been continuity rather than permanent separation. Its experience demonstrates a general historical distinction: an established state may lose a capital and still possess functioning institutions, public allegiance and diplomatic representation. The Republic's survival in 1948–1949 depended upon these forms of continuity working together, and Sumatra's contribution was central rather than peripheral to that achievement.

Section 148.14

International Pressure and the United Nations Commission for Indonesia

The second Dutch offensive produced an international response more severe than the Netherlands had encountered in 1947. The Security Council had already been considering the Indonesian question, and the occupation of Yogyakarta could be evaluated against the ceasefire and political arrangements previously negotiated with international assistance. The detention of the Republican leadership made it difficult for the Netherlands to argue that it was participating in a cooperative transition toward sovereignty. International pressure was not a single decision imposed by one great power. It developed through the actions of Asian governments, Australia, the United States and other participants in the United Nations, whose motivations overlapped without being identical.

The response of Asian countries reflected the importance of Indonesia to the wider process of decolonization. India had become independent in 1947, and Jawaharlal Nehru's government regarded attempts to restore European colonial authority in Asia as matters of regional concern. The New Delhi Conference on Indonesia in January 1949 brought together governments opposed to the renewed Dutch action. It advanced demands for the restoration of the Republic and progress toward independence, increasing the diplomatic costs of treating Indonesia as an exclusively Netherlands affair. Asian solidarity was not simply a reaction to American or Soviet policy. Independent governments were attempting to influence the international order in which their own sovereignty would have to be secured.

Indonesia had established relationships beyond Asia as well. Egypt's recognition and the friendship treaty of June 1947 were important achievements of Republican diplomacy, associated with representatives including Agus Salim. Support from Arab countries helped challenge the Netherlands' claim that the Republic lacked legitimate international standing. Diplomatic missions and contacts required personnel, resources and information at a time when ordinary communications were difficult. These relationships demonstrate that the Republic was not merely an object of decisions taken by external powers: Indonesian representatives actively sought recognition, cultivated alliances and brought the conflict before wider audiences.

Australia's contribution likewise extended beyond formal mediation. From 1945, Australian maritime workers and other unions participated in boycotts of Dutch shipping associated with the attempted restoration of colonial rule. Indonesian seafarers and political activists helped bring the issue to the attention of Australian labor organizations. The boycotts, remembered as the Black Armada, were a form of cross-border solidarity distinct from the later diplomatic policy of the Australian government. Australian representatives also participated directly in the Committee of Good Offices and its successor. Together these activities illustrate the different channels through which foreign societies could affect the Indonesian struggle: government diplomacy, labor action and public communication.

On 28 January 1949 the Security Council adopted Resolution 67. It called for the cessation of Dutch military operations and Republican guerrilla warfare, the release of Republican political prisoners detained since 17 December 1948, and the return of Republican officials to Yogyakarta. It recommended renewed negotiations toward a sovereign federal Indonesia and expanded the mediating machinery by transforming the Committee of Good Offices into the United Nations Commission for Indonesia (UNCI). The resolution did not itself create Indonesian sovereignty or restore the capital. Its importance lay in establishing an internationally supported framework for action and assigning the commission a more substantial role in assisting and supervising the process. Subsequent negotiations would be necessary to give practical effect to its recommendations.

American policy evolved in the context of both European recovery and political change in Asia. The Netherlands was an important European partner receiving assistance under the European Recovery Program. At the same time, prolonged colonial warfare threatened regional stability, placed strain on economic recovery and risked alienating Asian nationalist governments. American officials increasingly concluded that cooperation with a viable Indonesian government offered better prospects than indefinite Dutch military restoration. The Hatta government's defeat of the communist challenge at Madiun affected assessments of its political orientation, but it was not the only factor. In December 1948 American authorities suspended certain assistance-related procurement authorizations intended for use in Indonesia. The possibility of wider economic consequences gave diplomatic pressure greater weight; it is misleading, however, to describe the entire settlement as the immediate result of a blanket termination of Marshall Plan aid to the Netherlands.

International pressure became effective because it interacted with conditions inside Indonesia. The survival of the Republic gave mediators a political counterpart to restore, continued resistance raised the costs of occupation, and Indonesian federal leaders increasingly questioned a settlement dependent on eliminating the Republic. Foreign governments could encourage negotiation but could not manufacture the administrative and political structures necessary for an Indonesian state. The Dutch government faced a combination of military expense, limited political results, regional opposition and diplomatic isolation. The UNCI framework provided a means by which these pressures could be translated into renewed talks, leading to the Roem–Van Roijen Statements in May 1949.

The Roem–Van Roijen Agreement

By the spring of 1949 the central issue confronting the parties was how to resume negotiations without accepting the political consequences the Netherlands had sought to obtain through its December offensive. The Dutch government proposed a conference at The Hague to negotiate the future transfer of sovereignty. Republican leaders insisted that meaningful participation required the restoration of their government and the release of detained officials. The disagreement concerned more than the location of the next conference. If the Republic entered talks solely as a group of former officeholders under Dutch control, its status would be fundamentally different from that of a functioning government able to negotiate on behalf of its own institutions.

The United Nations Commission for Indonesia helped establish preliminary talks in Jakarta beginning in April. Mohammad Roem represented the Republic and Jan Herman van Roijen represented the Netherlands. The discussions produced the parallel statements of 7 May 1949, commonly called the Roem–Van Roijen Agreement or Statements. The terminology matters because the outcome took the form of commitments expressed by the representatives rather than a comprehensive peace treaty settling every disputed constitutional and economic question. The declarations were intended to create the conditions under which a final conference could proceed. The full sovereignty settlement remained to be negotiated.

Roem's statement committed the Republican side to pursue the issuance of an order for its armed adherents to cease guerrilla warfare, to cooperate in restoring peace and maintaining law and order, and to participate in the proposed Round Table Conference. These were important undertakings because the government needed the cooperation of military formations and regional authorities if its promises were to be effective. The statement did not establish that every armed organization had already ceased operations on 7 May. It expressed a course of action to be implemented as the political conditions for restoring the Republic were put in place. The distinction between a declaration of intent and an implemented military order is fundamental to interpreting the document.

Van Roijen's statement expressed Dutch acceptance of the return of the Republican government to Yogyakarta and its freedom to function within the agreed area. The Dutch undertook to cease military operations, release relevant political prisoners and refrain from establishing new federal states or further extending existing ones in a manner prejudicing the Republic's position during the transition. They agreed to pursue a prompt transfer of sovereignty through the proposed conference. The statements thus reversed a central political consequence of the second offensive: the Republic would not be excluded from the constitutional process as a result of the occupation of its capital. Nevertheless, the Netherlands had not yet transferred sovereignty, and negotiations remained necessary to determine the scope and terms of the eventual settlement.

Implementation required difficult conversations among the parties. Republican leaders needed to secure support from the PDRI, military commanders and other officials who had continued the struggle during their detention. Some feared that negotiations undertaken before adequate consultation might compromise the gains made through resistance. Dutch authorities had to make arrangements for the withdrawal of forces from Yogyakarta and the restoration of Republican administration. International mediators participated in working out the sequence of practical steps and reporting on progress. Each side needed confidence that its commitments would not be exploited by the other before reciprocal measures took effect. The history of Linggadjati and Renville gave both governments reasons to be concerned about this problem.

The political significance of the statements can be understood by comparing them with the earlier agreements. Linggadjati offered de facto recognition and a future constitutional framework; Renville attempted to regulate a ceasefire after Dutch territorial gains; Roem–Van Roijen addressed the restoration of a government whose normal operation had been disrupted. The 1949 commitments were therefore more directly concerned with reversing the exclusion of the Republic and enabling a final negotiation over sovereignty. They did not eliminate the federal framework accepted in previous discussions. Instead, they created an opportunity for Indonesian Republican and federal representatives to negotiate their own relationship before the international conference.

The May statements were a turning point because they connected continued resistance and international pressure to a concrete political change. The Republic recovered the prospect of operating openly from its capital and negotiating as a government rather than as a displaced political movement. The Netherlands obtained a commitment to renewed negotiations and an effort to end the continuing armed struggle. Neither side had secured all its desired outcomes, and the most difficult disputes over debt, military institutions, the Union and New Guinea remained unresolved. The achievement of the statements lay in reopening a feasible route toward settlement. Their value depended on subsequent actions, particularly the restoration of the government in Yogyakarta and the development of a common Indonesian constitutional position.

The Restoration of Republican Government and Inter-Indonesian Negotiations

The restoration of Republican government in Yogyakarta was the first major practical test of the Roem–Van Roijen Statements. Dutch forces withdrew from the city in late June 1949, and Sukarno and Hatta returned on 6 July. Their return had great political significance because it demonstrated the reversal of the effort to eliminate the Republic through occupation of its capital. Officials could resume their responsibilities, and the government regained a recognized territorial base from which to prepare for the Round Table Conference. The recovery of the capital did not immediately end hostilities across the archipelago or restore the whole area once claimed by the Republic. It restored a central institution essential to the negotiations.

The relationship between the returning leaders and the emergency authorities had to be regularized. The PDRI had maintained civil authority during the crisis, while the army had continued armed resistance. The restoration of the ordinary government could not be treated merely as a return to the situation before December 1948, because the experience of emergency rule had demonstrated the political importance of institutions outside the capital. Consultations brought the emergency arrangement to an end in July, with Sjafruddin returning his authority to the restored government on 13 July. The episode showed both the value of regional initiative and the persistence of the Republic's commitment to a common central political framework.

Meanwhile, the position of the federal territories had been changing. Some of their leaders had become increasingly critical of Dutch military policy, particularly after the second offensive. A federal state created while the Republic was excluded or destroyed risked appearing dependent on continuing colonial military support. Federal leaders who favored an independent Indonesia had reasons to seek agreement with the Republic on the structure of the prospective national government. Republican negotiators likewise had reasons to work with them: agreement among Indonesian political representatives would reduce the ability of the Netherlands to use their differences to determine the terms of sovereignty.

The Inter-Indonesian Conferences held in Yogyakarta in July and Jakarta in late July and early August 1949 brought Republican and BFO representatives together. Their discussions concerned the constitution of the future United States of Indonesia, its national symbols, the relationship of the constituent states to the federal government and the organization of its armed forces. Agreement on broad principles allowed the Indonesian delegations to approach the negotiations at The Hague with more closely coordinated positions. This did not mean that the Republic and the BFO merged into a single delegation or eliminated every difference. They retained distinct representation at the international conference, and their future constitutional relationship remained a subject of negotiation.

The question of the armed forces was particularly sensitive. The Republican army possessed legitimacy derived from its resistance to colonial restoration, while the federal territories included security institutions and personnel associated with the colonial military order. A sovereign federal government needed a common defense structure, but its creation required agreement about command, the status of existing soldiers and the future of the Royal Netherlands Indies Army (KNIL). Republican leaders favored a central role for the TNI in the new national armed forces. Federal representatives had to consider the security and interests of their own territories and populations. These discussions anticipated one of the central administrative challenges of independence: the integration of organizations that had previously served opposing authorities.

Not all political movements accepted the emerging settlement. In West Java, Kartosuwiryo and the Darul Islam movement rejected the Republic's territorial and political compromises and advanced an Islamic conception of state authority. On 7 August 1949 Kartosuwiryo proclaimed the Islamic State of Indonesia (Negara Islam Indonesia). This development occurred before the Dutch transfer of sovereignty and demonstrates that Indonesian state formation involved competing claims not reducible to a choice between Dutch rule and the Republic. The subsequent campaigns and political history of Darul Islam belong to later chapters. Its emergence here is important because the anticipated settlement with the Netherlands could not guarantee agreement among all Indonesian political and religious movements.

The inter-Indonesian negotiations made a final settlement more attainable by creating a substantial measure of agreement among participants whose earlier political relationships had been shaped by the Dutch federal strategy. This cooperation did not prove that federalism enjoyed permanent popular support, nor did it make a unitary state inevitable. It represented a political choice under particular circumstances: securing sovereignty through a constitutional framework broad enough to incorporate the Republic and the federal territories. The Round Table Conference would translate that framework into legal and institutional arrangements with the Netherlands. The consolidation of the resulting state and the resolution of the conflicts it inherited would require further decisions after the transfer itself.

Section 148.17

The Round Table Conference at The Hague

The Round Table Conference (Konferensi Meja Bundar, or KMB) met at The Hague from 23 August to 2 November 1949. It brought together three principal delegations: the Netherlands under J. H. van Maarseveen, the Republic under Mohammad Hatta and the federal representatives of the BFO under Sultan Hamid II. The United Nations Commission for Indonesia assisted the negotiations, and its American representative, Merle Cochran, played an important mediating role. The meeting was possible because the Republic had survived the Dutch offensives, its government had been restored in Yogyakarta and the Republican and federal representatives had established a substantial degree of agreement through the inter-Indonesian conferences. Its proceedings concerned the legal, constitutional, military and economic conditions under which colonial sovereignty would end.

The principal political achievement was agreement that the Netherlands would transfer sovereignty to the Republic of the United States of Indonesia (Republik Indonesia Serikat, or RIS). This would be a federal state in which the Republic established in 1945 formed one constituent entity alongside other states and autonomous territories. The new federation would possess international sovereignty, while its provisional constitution would establish the distribution of internal powers among its national and constituent institutions. The arrangement differed from a simple extension of the existing Republican government throughout the archipelago. It also differed from the Dutch attempt to establish a federation without meaningful Republican participation. The agreement was a negotiated institutional compromise whose importance must be understood in relation to the alternatives the participants faced in 1949.

The Netherlands-Indonesian Union formed a related but distinct part of the settlement. It was to associate the sovereign partners through arrangements for matters of mutual concern, with the Dutch monarch as head of the Union. The Indonesian negotiators sought to ensure that the association did not deprive their state of meaningful independence. Dutch negotiators sought to preserve institutional and commercial connections after the end of colonial authority. The Union's existence should not be confused with the continued sovereignty of the Dutch government over the federation. The critical questions concerned the obligations the two states accepted and the authority of common institutions rather than the ceremonial designation of the head of the association.

Military arrangements constituted another major issue. The settlement envisaged the withdrawal of Dutch military forces and the dissolution of the colonial KNIL, together with provisions for personnel to enter the new federal armed forces or make other transitions out of their previous service. The TNI would form the principal foundation of the new national military organization, but the integration of personnel from institutions with opposing wartime loyalties posed serious practical and political difficulties. Rules regarding the transfer of authority could be negotiated at a conference; the establishment of effective command and the acceptance of new institutions by soldiers and local communities required implementation. The problems that arose during that process belong principally to Chapter 149.

Economic negotiations were among the most difficult. Dutch officials sought the assumption by the successor state of liabilities incurred under the Netherlands Indies administration, while Indonesian delegates objected particularly to burdens associated with the effort to defeat the Republic. The settlement involved domestic and external obligations and concessions concerning the amount claimed by the Netherlands. Contemporary American diplomatic correspondence described the amount assumed as approximately 4.3 billion guilders, of which about 3 billion was internal debt; other historical accounts present somewhat different totals or classifications. The difference in figures makes it important to specify the accounting basis rather than treat a rounded figure as an exact universal total. The political point is clear: independent Indonesia inherited substantial liabilities, and the distribution of the costs of colonial rule and war was contested.

The broader financial and economic arrangements concerned the future treatment of Dutch enterprises, commercial relations and financial institutions. Political independence did not instantly replace colonial-era patterns of ownership, trade and access to capital. The Indonesian government needed to preserve economic activity, obtain foreign exchange and rebuild institutions damaged during the war, but its commitments also constrained some possible economic policies. The negotiated settlement consequently involved more than a decision about the national flag or diplomatic representation. It established conditions that would influence the economic choices available to the independent state. Evaluating these arrangements requires distinguishing the achievement of sovereignty from the subsequent transformation of an inherited colonial economy.

The most important territorial question left unresolved concerned the western part of New Guinea, then administered by the Netherlands as part of its colonial system. The Indonesian delegation claimed the territory as part of the former Netherlands East Indies, while Dutch negotiators resisted its immediate inclusion in the transfer. The compromise maintained the existing administration pending negotiations intended to determine the territory's political status within one year of the transfer of sovereignty. This formula postponed the disagreement rather than establishing a definitive settlement. The inhabitants of New Guinea had their own interests and political futures, which could not be adequately represented by treating the issue solely as a territorial bargaining problem between two governments.

On 2 November the conference concluded its negotiations. The Charter of Transfer of Sovereignty, the Union statute, financial and economic arrangements and related documents together established the framework for the transfer later that year. Their significance lies in the interaction of political achievement and negotiated obligations. The Republic had helped secure the end of Dutch sovereignty over most of the former colony, but the terms reflected a balance of bargaining positions rather than the unrestricted implementation of every Republican demand. Independence brought the capacity to make new political choices, together with institutions, debts and unresolved conflicts inherited from the struggle.

Section 148.18

December 27, 1949: The Transfer of Sovereignty

The transfer of sovereignty on 27 December 1949 represented the decisive legal termination of Dutch colonial sovereignty over most of the former Netherlands East Indies. At the ceremony in Amsterdam, Queen Juliana and Mohammad Hatta participated in the formal transfer to the Republic of the United States of Indonesia. A corresponding ceremony in Jakarta involved the Dutch representative A. H. J. Lovink and Sultan Hamengkubuwono IX on behalf of the new Indonesian government. These ceremonies gave public expression to arrangements concluded at the Round Table Conference. They were not the beginning of the Indonesian claim to independence, which had been proclaimed more than four years earlier, but the formal acknowledgment by the former colonial power of the independent sovereignty of the new federal state.

The Charter of Transfer of Sovereignty must be distinguished from a declaration of national independence. The 17 August 1945 proclamation asserted that the Indonesian people were independent without seeking the authority of the Netherlands to make that assertion. The 1949 charter expressed the termination of Dutch sovereignty and recognition of a successor state through a negotiated international settlement. The two documents concerned related but different political and legal acts. Between them lay the development of a Republican government, armed resistance, foreign recognition, international mediation and repeated attempts to reach a settlement. Treating either document as a complete substitute for the other would obscure the character of the revolution.

The identity of the recipient of sovereignty is equally important. The RIS was not simply the 1945 Republic enlarged to its claimed national borders. It was a federal state constituted through negotiations involving the Republic and federal representatives. Sukarno became president of the RIS, while Hatta served as its prime minister. Within the federation, the Republic continued as a constituent state, with its own institutions centered in Yogyakarta and Assaat serving as acting president of that constituent Republic. The federal government operated from Jakarta. This overlapping political terminology can confuse students because both the national federation and one of its constituent entities used the language of republican government. Their constitutional relationship, not just the title of a political office, determines which institution is meant.

The transition required changes in administration, diplomatic representation and the organization of the armed forces. Ministries of the federal government had to assume responsibilities previously exercised by colonial and Republican institutions. Diplomatic relationships could now be developed on the basis of recognized sovereignty rather than the more uncertain status of the wartime Republic. Public officials, employees of commercial enterprises and soldiers faced questions about their position within the new system. The ceremonies did not settle all the practical issues associated with these changes. They altered the legal framework within which the work of integration could proceed, while leaving important questions to be resolved by the Indonesian government itself.

The territorial exception in western New Guinea qualified the geographical scope of the transfer. Article 2 of the charter maintained the status quo of Dutch administration while providing for negotiations on the territory's political status within a year. The two governments had been unable to reconcile their claims at The Hague. The settlement did not establish an automatic transfer to Indonesia at the end of the stated period, nor did it definitively settle sovereignty in favor of the Netherlands. The resulting dispute persisted beyond 1949. Its subsequent development and the experiences of Papuan communities require separate examination rather than a compressed account that presents eventual outcomes as inevitable consequences of the 1949 agreement.

Recognition of Indonesian sovereignty also needs to be distinguished from admission to the United Nations. The Republic had already established relationships with several foreign governments, and Indonesian representatives had participated in United Nations discussions of the dispute before the transfer. Full United Nations membership followed on 28 September 1950, after the federal arrangement had been replaced by a unitary state. The chronological distinction illustrates that external recognition is a process involving multiple acts by different institutions and governments. The Dutch transfer was especially significant because it ended the principal colonial power's claim to rule the territories transferred; it did not create all international relationships from the beginning.

The events of 27 December 1949 therefore mark the end of the main phase of the national revolution treated in this chapter, but not the end of Indonesian political transformation. The independent federation possessed the authority to determine its future within the constitutional and international conditions established by the settlement. Federal institutions, regional demands, military integration and economic reconstruction remained unresolved or incomplete. The shift to a unitary Republic on 17 August 1950 belongs to Chapter 149. For the purposes of Chapter 148, the essential outcome is that an attempt to restore colonial government had failed and a negotiated transfer of internationally recognized sovereignty had taken place. The distinction among 1945, 1949 and 1950 provides the chronological framework through which the achievement of independence and the subsequent construction of the Indonesian state can be understood.

Section 148.19

The Human, Economic, and Institutional Costs of the Revolution

The achievement of sovereignty came at a heavy human cost. Warfare, social revolution, political conflict and the disruption of ordinary administration affected communities throughout Indonesia, although the nature and intensity of these experiences varied considerably by region. In some areas the most immediate danger came from Dutch military repression; in others, violence among rival local organizations, attacks on vulnerable civilian populations or the coercive demands of armed groups shaped daily life. The end of the revolution did not restore people who had been killed, displaced or deprived of their livelihoods. A history centered exclusively on successful diplomatic agreements and the survival of national leaders would fail to explain the consequences of the process through which sovereignty was secured.

Modern research into Dutch military conduct has substantially revised earlier official accounts. The joint research program involving the NIOD Institute for War, Holocaust and Genocide Studies, the Royal Netherlands Institute of Southeast Asian and Caribbean Studies (KITLV), and the Netherlands Institute for Military History (NIMH), published its major findings in 2022. Its synthesis, Beyond the Pale, concluded that extreme violence by Dutch forces was widespread and structural, rather than consisting only of isolated deviations from an otherwise generally acceptable military policy. Researchers examined the relationship between conduct in Indonesia, military institutions and political responsibility in the Netherlands. Extrajudicial executions, torture, abusive detention and destruction of civilian property were among the documented practices. The institutional context was the attempt to reimpose colonial control against a population in which substantial resistance persisted.

Indonesian revolutionary violence must also be examined within its particular contexts. During the early phase of the revolution, European and Eurasian civilians, Moluccans, Chinese Indonesians and other persons perceived by some revolutionary groups as associated with colonial authority or as outsiders could become targets of attacks. The term Bersiap, widely used in Dutch accounts, describes aspects of this period of extreme violence but is contested because it can reduce the early revolution to a portrayal of indiscriminate Indonesian aggression or obscure the broader setting of colonial rule and war. Social revolution and the Madiun confrontation produced further forms of repression and violence among Indonesians. The responsibility of particular organizations should not be generalized to all members of a political, religious or ethnic category. Nor should recognition of violence by several parties be used to erase the different political objectives, institutional capacities and responsibilities involved.

The experience of displacement connected the human and economic consequences of the revolution. People moving away from contested towns or areas of fighting needed food, shelter and work. Families could become separated, and ordinary arrangements for education and health care were disrupted. Transport damage and shortages made it difficult for producers to supply markets or for urban consumers to obtain necessities. In areas where rival authorities claimed the right to requisition goods or collect contributions, households faced multiple obligations. These conditions affected people differently according to income, occupation, location and access to networks of assistance. The capacity to sustain the revolutionary struggle depended in significant part on the work of civilians whose contribution was rarely recorded in formal military histories.

Women's participation extended across many activities, including medical care, food supply, communications, fundraising, political organization and, in some instances, armed service. Women's organizations connected national independence with discussions of education, political participation and social reform. Their experiences were not uniform: participation in public organizations could offer opportunities to influence the new state, while wartime conditions intensified burdens within households and exposed people to additional dangers. Recognizing the work of women and other civilians changes the explanation of Republican endurance. It shows that national institutions could survive only because local communities continued to perform essential social and economic functions under extraordinary pressure.

The new state inherited substantial institutional problems. Infrastructure required repair, government revenue was limited, the monetary system had been fragmented and the economy remained tied to commercial structures developed under colonial rule. Thousands of officials and soldiers associated with different administrations faced uncertain futures. The political authority acquired through revolutionary resistance created expectations that independence would bring social improvement and more equitable access to resources. Yet the need to stabilize government, maintain trade and fulfill negotiated obligations limited the immediate capacity to satisfy all those expectations. The economic inheritance of the revolution was not merely a list of destroyed assets; it included relationships of ownership, employment, debt and institutional organization that the independent government would have to transform or accommodate.

Historians must be cautious when estimating the total human cost. Records are incomplete, categories of deaths and displacement differ, and many affected communities left limited documentation accessible to researchers. The absence of a precise aggregate does not diminish the reality of suffering or the responsibility of institutions and individuals. Rather, it requires attention to the sources through which particular events and patterns can be established. Survivor testimony, court records, administrative reports, military archives, local histories and economic documents answer different questions. Bringing them together permits a fuller account of the revolution as both an extraordinary political achievement and a costly transformation of society. The work of building an independent Indonesia began amid the consequences of this experience rather than on an undamaged institutional or social foundation.

Section 148.20

Why the Indonesian National Revolution Succeeded

The Indonesian National Revolution succeeded in its central objective because the Netherlands was unable to restore durable colonial sovereignty and ultimately agreed to a transfer of authority. This outcome cannot be explained by the conventional military balance alone. Dutch forces were capable of capturing major towns and economically important territories, while the Republic lacked comparable resources. Nor can it be explained simply by the existence of a proclamation in 1945, since political declarations do not invariably produce independent governments. The historical problem is to explain why the Republic retained sufficient legitimacy, institutional capacity and external support to survive repeated military setbacks, and why the Netherlands eventually found a negotiated settlement more feasible than continued restoration.

The first element of the explanation is the depth of Indonesian nationalist organization before and during the revolution. Political parties, religious associations, labor groups, educational networks and local movements had developed connections that extended beyond the government formed in August 1945. These organizations supplied personnel and forms of legitimacy that could survive the occupation of an administrative center. Although they disagreed about political and social objectives, many recognized independence as an overriding national goal. Their capacity to mobilize communities helps explain why the Republic did not disappear when its leaders were detained. Nationalism was not a substitute for administration or military organization; it was a political resource that made these institutions more difficult to eliminate.

The second element was the development of military resistance and its relationship to civilian society. Republican forces could not guarantee the protection of all territory claimed by their government, but their continued existence prevented Dutch control of major centers from producing a conclusive political outcome. Maintaining resistance required recruitment, supplies, communication and cooperation with local communities. The civilian government and military leadership did not always agree, and some armed groups contested central authority. Nevertheless, the principal military institutions continued to identify their struggle with the preservation of the Republic. That connection helped sustain the national government's relevance even when its ordinary operation had been disrupted. Military resistance was politically effective because it denied the Netherlands an accepted conclusion to the war, not because it produced an uninterrupted sequence of conventional victories.

The third element was the survival and adaptability of Republican institutions. The relocation to Yogyakarta in 1946 and the formation of the PDRI in 1948 demonstrated that the government could continue operating under changing conditions. Civil administration, monetary arrangements and diplomatic representation were maintained with varying degrees of effectiveness. These activities enabled the Republic to present itself not merely as an armed movement but as a government capable of entering international agreements. The willingness of leaders in Sumatra, military commanders and the restored cabinet to return to a common political arrangement in 1949 prevented emergency structures from developing into a permanent competing government. Institutional continuity supplied the foundation upon which a negotiated restoration could be built.

Diplomacy formed the fourth element. Sjahrir, Hatta, Roem, Agus Salim and other representatives pursued different opportunities to obtain acknowledgment of the Republic's political status and ultimately secure sovereignty. The agreements of Linggadjati and Renville involved concessions and failed to prevent renewed war, yet they established precedents for direct negotiation and international mediation. Diplomatic initiatives brought the Republic into relationships with Asian and Arab states and enabled it to use the United Nations as a forum in which Dutch actions could be challenged. Negotiation and resistance reinforced each other under some conditions: the existence of resistance made the exclusion of the Republic impractical, while international recognition and mediation reduced the likelihood that military defeat alone could determine its political fate.

The fifth element was the changing international and Dutch political environment. Asian governments opposed colonial restoration; Australian diplomacy and labor solidarity contributed to wider opposition; the United States increasingly regarded continued Dutch warfare as inconsistent with its interests in European recovery and Asian stability. Security Council intervention provided a framework through which these pressures could affect negotiations. Meanwhile, the costs of military operations and the failure to establish a stable political settlement weakened the case for continuing the war. These factors were not external gifts that produced independence independently of Indonesian action. They became effective because there was an enduring Republican government and a viable negotiated alternative to indefinite military occupation.

Finally, cooperation between Republican and federal Indonesian leaders made a settlement possible on terms that included the Republic. The federal project had initially been an important instrument of Dutch policy, but some federal representatives developed positions that were not identical to the interests of the Netherlands. The inter-Indonesian conferences helped establish the constitutional basis of the future federation and reduce the political divisions the Dutch government could exploit. The resulting RIS was a compromise, not the permanent victory of one constitutional model over all others. Its later transformation does not mean that accepting it in 1949 was unnecessary or inevitable. The choice must be evaluated against the contemporary alternatives, including further war and continuing colonial sovereignty.

The success of the revolution was therefore conditional and cumulative. No single event ensured the final outcome, and each stage involved alternatives that could have produced different results. The Republic's achievement lay in transforming a national claim into a political reality through the interaction of organized resistance, government, diplomacy, popular support, regional cooperation and changes in the international balance. Yet sovereignty did not automatically resolve the social conflicts, economic obligations and institutional divisions inherited from the revolution. Chapter 149 examines how the independent Indonesian state confronted those problems, including the transition from the federal settlement to a unitary Republic. The central lesson of Chapter 148 is that independence was neither simply bestowed by a former colonial power nor secured by military action alone; it emerged from a sustained, contested process of political and institutional survival.

Historical Debate

Was Independence Secured Primarily through Resistance, Diplomacy, or International Pressure?

Resistance-centered interpretation. Continued military resistance and local support made the occupation of cities insufficient to end the war. Evidence includes the survival of Republican organizations after the December offensive. This interpretation must explain how resistance became a recognized political settlement rather than indefinite warfare.
Diplomacy and governance. The Republic maintained institutions and representatives, obtained recognition and participated in international mediation. The restoration of government and the Hague negotiations support this emphasis. It must explain why the Netherlands could not simply exclude the Republic from the process.
International pressure and Dutch constraints. UN intervention, opposition from Asian governments, American policy and the costs of continued occupation altered the Netherlands' options. This view must account for the Indonesian political institutions and organizations that made a negotiated transfer possible.

These explanations are most productive when treated as interacting mechanisms rather than competitors for exclusive credit. For example, resistance made the claim of Republican extinction implausible, diplomacy communicated that fact internationally, and international pressure helped secure a restoration of central government.

Historical Vocabulary

Sovereignty Settlement

A sovereignty settlement is a negotiated arrangement that resolves or redefines competing claims to supreme political authority. It may include constitutional, territorial, economic, military and diplomatic instruments. The parties can agree to end colonial sovereignty while leaving particular disputes or obligations unresolved.

The Hague settlement recognized the federal RIS and provided for a transition in other institutions. It did not constitute the original Indonesian declaration, incorporate western New Guinea at that time or establish the permanent form of the Indonesian state. Its significance must be understood in relation to both what it achieved and what it deferred.

Chapter Summary

The Indonesian National Revolution began with a Republic that possessed political legitimacy and emerging institutions but did not enjoy uncontested authority over the territory it claimed. The government established in 1945 drew on the experience of nationalist organizations, colonial-era administrators and people mobilized during the Japanese occupation. It developed ministries, representative institutions and military organizations while facing the return of Allied and Dutch forces. The distinction between legal and political claims, effective administrative control and international recognition remained essential throughout the struggle.

The experience of Surabaya demonstrated the strength of popular resistance and the vulnerability of the Republic to superior conventional military resources. The relocation of central government to Yogyakarta in January 1946 relied on the cooperation of the sultanate and offered a comparatively secure institutional base. It also demonstrated that the existence of the government was not dependent on possession of the former colonial capital. This capacity to adapt to territorial disruption would become especially important during the second Dutch offensive.

The revolution transformed social authority in ways that varied substantially across Indonesia. The Three Regions Affair challenged local administrative hierarchies in Java, while the East Sumatran social revolution gravely weakened established aristocratic institutions. Aceh's Cumbok conflict reflected a different relationship between religious leaders and hereditary authorities. The Yogyakarta court, by contrast, became a central supporter of the Republic. In regions where Dutch administration returned early, nationalist activity did not necessarily disappear, but its organizational possibilities differed from those in Republican-held territories. These contrasts reveal why national independence did not produce one uniform social revolution.

Sutan Sjahrir pursued diplomacy as a means of obtaining acknowledgment of the Republic and advancing its claim to independence. Opposition associated with Tan Malaka and the Persatuan Perjuangan feared that compromise would undermine the nationalist objective. The political crisis of 1946 exposed disagreements over negotiation, military authority and the direction of Republican policy. The eventual Linggadjati Agreement, initialed in November 1946 and signed in March 1947, acknowledged the Republic's de facto authority in Java, Madura and Sumatra while envisaging a sovereign federal state and a Netherlands-Indonesian Union.

Linggadjati left major questions unresolved. Dutch officials pursued a federal strategy through institutions including the State of East Indonesia and other constituent entities. Some regional representatives had their own reasons for supporting federal arrangements, although Dutch military and administrative power strongly shaped the circumstances of their creation. Disagreement over the implementation of Linggadjati and the control of economic and security institutions culminated in the first Dutch offensive of July 1947. Dutch territorial gains were accompanied by a loss of diplomatic freedom as the United Nations became involved in the conflict.

The Committee of Good Offices assisted negotiations that produced the Renville truce and political principles of January 1948. The demarcation of positions and withdrawal of forces placed the Republic under greater territorial and economic pressure. Its government had already introduced its own currency, and regional financial arrangements helped sustain economic activity under difficult conditions. Nevertheless, the loss of commercial resources and the burden of supporting a fragmented military establishment created serious challenges. The fall of Amir Sjarifuddin's cabinet and the formation of Hatta's government reflected these domestic consequences of the international settlement.

Hatta's efforts to rationalize the armed forces and stabilize administration encountered opposition from political and military organizations. The People's Democratic Front and the reorganized communist movement confronted the government amid increasing local conflict. In September 1948 the Madiun crisis escalated into an open challenge that Republican forces suppressed. Historians disagree about the relative importance of planned communist action, local factional conflict and the government's policies. The confrontation altered the political composition of the Republic and affected foreign assessments, but it did not by itself determine American policy or the eventual Dutch transfer.

The second Dutch offensive of December 1948 occupied Yogyakarta and detained the central Republican leadership. However, General Sudirman continued military resistance, and Sjafruddin Prawiranegara and other officials formed the PDRI in Sumatra on 22 December. The emergency government relied on regional institutions and communication networks to preserve the capacity of the Republic to act. Continued resistance and the existence of a functioning emergency administration undermined the Dutch claim that occupation had eliminated the Republic. The restoration of ordinary government in 1949 confirmed that emergency authority had served continuity rather than permanent separation.

International opposition to the renewed offensive intensified. Asian governments, Australian representatives and American policymakers acted for overlapping but different reasons. Security Council Resolution 67 of 28 January 1949 called for an end to military operations, the release of detained Republican political prisoners and the restoration of Republican officials to Yogyakarta. The United Nations Commission for Indonesia took on an expanded mediating role. International pressure operated alongside resistance within Indonesia and the growing costs and limited political effectiveness of colonial restoration.

The Roem–Van Roijen Statements of 7 May 1949 established commitments that made restoration of the government and further negotiations possible. Republican leaders returned to Yogyakarta in July, and the inter-Indonesian conferences brought the Republic and federal representatives into closer cooperation. They retained distinct delegations while developing a common constitutional position. Other political movements, including Darul Islam, did not accept the emerging state arrangement, demonstrating that agreement with the Netherlands would not resolve every internal claim to sovereignty.

The Round Table Conference at The Hague concluded on 2 November 1949 with arrangements for the transfer of sovereignty to the federal RIS, the Netherlands-Indonesian Union, military transition and financial and economic obligations. The agreement left western New Guinea under Dutch administration pending further negotiations. On 27 December 1949 the formal transfer took effect. The event must be distinguished both from the proclamation of 17 August 1945 and from the subsequent transition to a unitary Republic in 1950. The new government acquired sovereignty while inheriting economic liabilities and difficult problems of institutional integration.

The revolution's success resulted from the interaction of nationalist mobilization, continued resistance, institutional survival, diplomatic work, regional cooperation and international pressure. It was not inevitable, and it came at substantial human and material cost. Dutch structural military violence, revolutionary and communal violence, displacement, shortages and economic disruption shaped the lives of civilians. Independence created the political capacity to address these problems, but it did not immediately solve them. The next chapter examines the effort to consolidate and integrate an independent state whose institutions and society had been profoundly shaped by four years of revolution.

Key Terms

National revolution
A struggle to establish or defend national sovereignty against an existing political order. In this chapter, the term denotes the 1945–1949 struggle to preserve the Republic and end Dutch colonial rule. Explain how the national revolution combined military resistance, governmental continuity and diplomacy. Contrast it with social revolution in East Sumatra.
Social revolution
A fundamental transformation of social authority, property or hierarchy. Local upheavals challenged officials, aristocrats and existing patterns of economic power. Use the Three Regions Affair and East Sumatra to explain how social transformation intersected with the national struggle without necessarily following central government policy.
Regional revolution
A local or regional transformation shaped by particular institutions, histories and conditions of colonial reoccupation. It need not imply separatism. Contrast Aceh, Yogyakarta and Sulawesi. Explain why differing regional experiences do not establish differing levels of commitment to independence by themselves.
Republican legitimacy
The claimed or accepted right of the Republic and its institutions to exercise governmental authority. Legitimacy can exist even when effective control is disputed. Consider how national movements, local alliances and foreign relationships sustained legitimacy during the occupation of Yogyakarta.
National sovereignty
Supreme independent political authority of a state in its internal and international affairs. The Republic asserted sovereignty in 1945; the Dutch transfer recognized the RIS in 1949. Distinguish the proclamation, Dutch recognition, international recognition by other states, and the exercise of authority in particular districts.
De facto recognition
Acknowledgment of authority exercised in practice, without necessarily granting full legal recognition of sovereignty. Linggadjati used this distinction. Explain why Dutch acknowledgment of Republican authority in Java, Madura and Sumatra did not amount to immediate recognition of full sovereignty.
De jure recognition
Formal legal recognition of a government or state. Its implications depend on the nature of the recognition and relevant legal arrangements. Compare the political acknowledgment provided by Linggadjati with the formal transfer to the RIS. Do not treat recognition by one government as equivalent to recognition by every government.
Effective control
The practical ability of an authority to govern, administer services and enforce decisions in a territory. It is distinct from a claim to jurisdiction or political allegiance. Explain why the occupation of Yogyakarta did not necessarily end the Republic and why Dutch control did not prove universal acceptance of Dutch authority.
Federalism
A constitutional arrangement distributing powers between a common national government and constituent units. The distribution is established through the constitutional framework. Assess the difference between Dutch-sponsored federalism, regional demands and the negotiated RIS. Why is a federal system not inherently colonial?
Unitary state
A state in which ultimate constitutional authority resides in one national order, although administrative powers can be decentralized. Distinguish the federal state established in December 1949 from the unitary state formed in 1950; avoid assuming that the latter was inevitable.
Constituent state
A political unit forming part of a federation, with authority defined by its federal constitution. It is not normally an independent sovereign state in its own right. Identify the position of the 1945 Republic within the RIS and explain why the two political institutions should not be treated as interchangeable.
Coalition government
A government supported by several political organizations or groups. Its stability depends in part on their continued cooperation. Consider why opposition to Renville could undermine the Amir cabinet and how a change of cabinet affected Republican political strategy.
Diplomatic mediation
Assistance from a third party in negotiating a dispute. Mediation can provide procedures, communication and proposals without necessarily imposing a settlement. Compare British mediation at Linggadjati with the Committee of Good Offices and UNCI. Identify the limits of mediation when participants disagree.
Ceasefire
An arrangement to suspend hostilities. A ceasefire need not resolve territorial claims, sovereignty or the causes of conflict. Explain why the Renville ceasefire did not itself settle the Indonesian question and why further agreements were needed.
Armistice
A formal arrangement suspending military hostilities; the precise legal and practical effects depend on the text. Compare a military truce with a final political settlement. Identify what a suspension of hostilities cannot establish about a state’s sovereignty.
Internationalization
The transformation of a dispute previously treated as domestic or bilateral into a matter involving international governments or institutions. Trace how India and Australia brought the Indonesian question before the Security Council following the first offensive.
Colonial restoration
An attempt to reestablish the authority of a colonial power after disruption or loss of control. Assess why restoration of Dutch control over major cities and commercial assets did not yield a stable colonial political order.
Revolutionary opposition
Opposition arising within a revolutionary movement, often over strategy, authority or the social meaning of change. Contrast opposition to Sjahrir in 1946 with the conflicts surrounding Madiun in 1948. Identify similarities and differences without conflating the groups.
Emergency government
A temporary governmental arrangement created to preserve public authority when ordinary institutions cannot operate normally. Explain how the PDRI preserved Republican government while central leaders were detained and why returning emergency authority mattered.
Constitutional continuity
Preservation of the identity and legitimacy of a political order across an institutional crisis, even when its ordinary procedures are disrupted. Distinguish continuity of governmental identity from uninterrupted control of territory or services during 1948–1949.
Demobilization
The release or dissolution of military personnel and organizations, requiring practical arrangements for their transition to other employment or service. Explain why reducing an armed establishment might improve public finances while creating political or social tensions.
Military rationalization
Reorganization of military personnel, command structures and resources to create a more coherent and sustainable force. Connect Hatta’s Re-Ra policy to fiscal pressures and factional conflict. Why did it affect the distribution of political power?
Economic blockade
Restrictions on the movement of goods, trade and access to markets intended to constrain an opposing authority or territory. Trace the effects of Dutch restrictions on Republican revenue, household consumption and regional commercial connections.
Sovereignty settlement
A negotiated arrangement resolving or redefining competing claims to sovereign political authority, often accompanied by economic, military and constitutional instruments. Use the 1949 charter and related arrangements to distinguish the end of colonial sovereignty from the obligations inherited by the successor state.
National integration
The process of incorporating territories, communities and institutions within a common national political framework. Explain why achieving recognized sovereignty did not immediately create unified military, monetary and administrative systems. The subsequent process belongs to Chapter 149.
Police action
A Dutch designation for the two major offensives, intended to portray them as internal restoration of order. The term was contested by the Republic. Explain how terminology affected the argument over United Nations jurisdiction. Compare the term with the Indonesian designation of military aggression.
Bersiap
A contested term used particularly in Dutch accounts for aspects of early revolutionary violence in 1945–1946. It should be contextualized rather than applied to all Indonesian nationalist activity. Explain why a term used for a period of violence can highlight particular victims while obscuring other contexts. Evaluate institutional responsibility using specific evidence.
Malino Conference
A Dutch-sponsored conference in July 1946 involving representatives from Borneo and eastern Indonesia to discuss federal arrangements. Connect Malino to Denpasar and the development of federal entities. Distinguish the constitutional proposals from evidence of popular support.
State of East Indonesia
Negara Indonesia Timur (NIT), a federal entity established in December 1946 with its capital at Makassar. Assess how the state became part of Dutch federal strategy while providing a setting for regional politicians with their own objectives.
BFO
Bijeenkomst voor Federaal Overleg, the Federal Consultative Assembly, coordinating representatives of federal entities from 1948. Explain the BFO’s role in inter-Indonesian negotiations and its separate representation at The Hague.
Round Table Conference
The Hague negotiations of 23 August–2 November 1949 involving Dutch, Republican and federal representatives with UNCI assistance. Distinguish the charter, constitutional arrangements, Union statute and economic settlement. Why was the transfer incomplete in its territorial scope?

Chapter Reflection

The Indonesian National Revolution demonstrates that sovereignty cannot be understood solely through a single ceremony, legal document or military outcome. A proclamation established a political claim; the government's survival, the actions of its supporters and the work of its representatives made that claim increasingly difficult to disregard. The settlement of 1949 gave the new federal state recognized sovereignty, but the ability to exercise that authority depended on institutions and relationships built throughout the struggle. The distinction between the formal existence of a state and its actual capacity to govern is applicable to other processes of decolonization examined in this volume.

The chapter also raises questions about the relationship between national unity and political diversity. The Republic brought together movements with different social programs and regional interests, and their conflicts did not disappear when they shared opposition to Dutch rule. Federalism, military reorganization and emergency government all involved decisions about the allocation of authority. The experiences of the Three Regions, East Sumatra, Aceh and Yogyakarta show that participation in a national revolution could produce very different local outcomes. Students should evaluate these differences without assuming that one region's experience represents the whole archipelago or that the later unitary state was predetermined.

Finally, the achievement of sovereignty must be examined alongside the experiences of those who bore the costs of the struggle. Diplomatic documents identify the institutions and commitments recognized by negotiators, while social and regional histories reveal the work, suffering and choices of populations whose lives were transformed. Both kinds of evidence are essential. Chapter 149 turns to the problems of integrating the federal settlement, reorganizing the inherited institutions and building a unitary state. It asks how Indonesians used their newly recognized sovereignty to confront conflicts that the national revolution had not resolved.
