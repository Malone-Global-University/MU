Chapter Opening

Portuguese Melaka endured from 1511 to 1641 as a fortified center of commerce, administration, and cultural interaction, but it never became master of the Strait. Its survival rested on stone walls, ships, Asian merchants, local brokers, imported rice, and opportunistic diplomacy; its vulnerability lay in the fact that none of these could eliminate rival ports, alternative routes, or competing Malay and Sumatran rulers.

Two sieges frame this chapter. In 1568 a fleet from Aceh, the Muslim sultanate of northern Sumatra, arrived off Melaka and besieged the Portuguese fortress for several weeks in early 1568; the defenders held, reinforcements arrived, and the attackers withdrew. In 1640 and 1641 another besieging force gathered around the same fortress, this time composed of ships and soldiers of the Dutch East India Company together with forces of the Sultanate of Johor. On 14 January 1641 the Portuguese garrison surrendered. Between these episodes lay a century and a quarter in which the Portuguese held one of the most valuable ports in maritime Asia, repeatedly defended it, and repeatedly failed to turn possession into control of the waters beyond the walls.

Chapter 69 explained how Afonso de Albuquerque's expedition captured Melaka in 1511. This chapter asks a different question: how did a European-ruled fortress-city survive for 130 years in the middle of the Strait of Malacca, surrounded by rulers who regarded it as an intrusion, and why did survival never produce supremacy? The answer cannot be found in a catalogue of battles alone. It requires attention to the officials who administered the port, the merchants who paid its customs, the households that lived in its neighborhoods, the rulers of Johor and Aceh who pursued their own ambitions, and the Orang Laut, Javanese, Tamil, Gujarati, Chinese, and other communities whose labor and commerce kept the harbor functioning.

The chapter distinguishes three things that older writing often merges: military possession, commercial participation, and sovereignty. The Portuguese possessed a fortress and its immediate surroundings. They participated in commerce, sometimes profitably, as one group of traders and as a crown seeking revenue. But they never exercised sovereignty over the Strait, and they never monopolized the trade that passed through it. Asian merchants carried goods by other routes, Malay and Sumatran courts extracted their own revenues, and even Melaka's food supply depended on societies outside Portuguese rule. Keeping these three phenomena separate is the chapter's central analytical discipline.

The chapter also treats Southeast Asian polities as historical actors rather than as obstacles to European ambition. The Melakan ruling house did not vanish in 1511; it reconstituted itself, through successive relocations and dynastic branching, as the Johor successor polity. Aceh was not merely a reaction to Portugal but an expanding trading state with its own sources of wealth, its own Indian Ocean and Islamic connections, and its own strategic aims. Johor and Aceh were not permanent allies against the Portuguese; they also fought one another. The military balance, too, was not a simple contest between European firearms and unprepared Southeast Asian armies: Southeast Asian courts used artillery, galleys, fortifications, and large-scale logistics, and the outcomes of sieges depended heavily on supply, disease, reinforcement, and alliances.

Historians reconstruct this history from an uneven body of evidence. Portuguese royal correspondence, chronicles, merchant memoirs, missionary letters, early maps, and a pictorial representation of the 1568 siege survive in relative abundance. Malay court traditions, notably the Sejarah Melayu (Malay Annals) and later Acehnese chronicles, preserve other voices, though they were composed or revised later and for their own purposes. Dutch records become important from the early seventeenth century. Each of these sources reflects the interests of its authors, and Chapter 70 treats them as evidence to be interrogated rather than as neutral narration.

The chapter is organized in five divisions of four sections each. Division I examines what Portuguese Melaka was as a functioning society: its fortress, administration, commerce, and everyday life. Division II introduces the two principal competing centers, Johor and Aceh, and the commercial geography that connected them. Division III follows the wars and sieges, culminating in 1568. Division IV examines commercial policy, religion, and diplomacy, including the Johor–Dutch alliance of 1606. Division V treats the crisis of the seventeenth century, the siege of 1629, the conquest of 1640–1641, and the chapter's synthesis. The later histories of Aceh under Iskandar Muda (Chapter 80), of the Johor-Riau world (Chapter 81), and of the Dutch East India Company (Chapter 77) are reserved for their own chapters and enter here only as far as the story of Melaka requires.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish military possession, commercial participation, and sovereignty as separate historical phenomena in the study of Portuguese Melaka.
  2. Describe the institutions through which the Estado da Índia governed Melaka, including the captaincy, the factor, customs administration, and the garrison, and explain their limits.
  3. Explain how Asian merchants, brokers, laborers, and households sustained Melaka's commerce and daily life after 1511.
  4. Trace the continuation of Melakan political authority through Bintan, Kampar, and Johor, and explain why Johor was a successor polity rather than a Portuguese creation.
  5. Analyze the commercial and political foundations of Aceh's early-sixteenth-century expansion and its motives for challenging Portuguese Melaka.
  6. Compare the material resources, political traditions, and maritime capabilities of Portuguese Melaka, Johor, and Aceh without treating their relations as a stable three-sided system.
  7. Reconstruct the principal sieges and campaigns from 1511 to 1641, attributing reported figures to their sources.
  8. Evaluate a Portuguese pictorial source on the 1568 siege as historically situated evidence rather than a scaled battlefield map.
  9. Assess the limits of the cartaz system and the Portuguese claim to regulate shipping in the Strait.
  10. Interpret the 1606 Johor–VOC agreement as evidence of Johor's strategic diplomacy and distinguish its terms from their later fulfillment and dispute.
  11. Explain why the siege of 1629 failed and the siege of 1640–1641 succeeded without attributing either outcome to simple military superiority.
  12. Evaluate the historical significance of Portuguese Melaka and why the Strait remained a contested maritime world after 1641.

Historical Context

Before 1511, Melaka had been the principal entrepôt of the Strait of Malacca. Chapter 64 traced the rise of its sultanate, and Chapter 62 the wider Indian Ocean Islamic commercial world in which it flourished. Its rulers provided harbor services, regulated trade through harbor officials, and offered protection to merchants from Gujarat, the Coromandel Coast, Bengal, Java, China, and many other places. The seasonal rhythm of the monsoon brought ships from the west and east to meet at the Strait, as Chapter 4 explained, and Melaka's position allowed it to host the exchange of Indian textiles, Southeast Asian spices and aromatics, Chinese goods, and the products of the archipelago.

Chapters 68 and 69 described the arrival of the Portuguese in Asian waters and their conquest of Melaka in 1511. The Portuguese came with a clear strategic idea, articulated by Albuquerque and his successors: to command key chokepoints of Indian Ocean commerce through fortresses and naval power, to divert spice and pepper trade to the Cape route, and to collect revenue from shipping. Goa, captured in 1510, later became the chief administrative capital of the Estado da Índia around 1530, succeeding the earlier Portuguese headquarters at Cochin. The Estado was a loose collection of fortresses, factories, and jurisdictional claims through which the Portuguese crown and its officials operated in Asia. Melaka was one of its most important eastern posts, a gateway to the Spice Islands, China, and Japan.

Yet the conquest created a problem as well as an opportunity. The sultan, Mahmud Shah, and much of the Melakan elite withdrew rather than submit. Many Asian merchants, particularly Muslim traders who had no wish to operate under a Christian fortress, redirected their business to other ports. The Portuguese held a strategically valuable site but not the political community that had made it work. They had to rebuild a commercial city while defending it from the courts that had been displaced and from other powers whose interests now collided with theirs. Meanwhile the Strait itself had never belonged to one authority. Its western approaches were shaped by the pepper-producing coasts and ports of northern Sumatra, its eastern shore by Malay peninsular courts, and its southern islands by Orang Laut communities whose navigational skills and maritime mobility had long underpinned the power of Melaka's rulers.

Students should keep three analytical cautions in mind. First, the Portuguese were not the only European or even the only colonial-style power that mattered to Melaka; by the early seventeenth century Dutch ships challenged them directly. Second, neither Johor nor Aceh was a mere reaction to Portugal. Each had origins, interests, and political traditions that predated or exceeded the conflict. Third, the Portuguese "empire" in Asia should be understood as a network of fortified ports, treaties, private commerce, and negotiated arrangements, not as a territorial state comparable to the later colonial empires examined in Volume VI.

Historical Vocabulary
Casado

Definition. In the Portuguese empire, a casado ("married man") was a Portuguese or other European man who settled permanently in an Asian port, typically married to a local or Asian-born Christian woman, and was entitled to certain privileges in exchange for residence and military service.

Historical context. Albuquerque and later officials encouraged such settlement to create a loyal resident population that would defend and populate fortified ports. At Melaka, casados formed a core of the resident Portuguese community and many engaged in private commerce, sometimes in competition with the Crown's own trading interests.

Why it matters. The casado community shows that Portuguese Melaka was not simply a garrison of soldiers rotating from Lisbon but a settled, mixed society with its own commercial interests and loyalties.

Potential misunderstanding. The term does not describe a uniform group. Casados varied in wealth, origin, and relationship to the Crown, and their wives and households came from diverse Asian backgrounds.

Historical Vocabulary
Luso-Asian

Definition. "Luso-Asian" is a modern scholarly term for people and communities of mixed Portuguese and Asian descent, or culturally shaped by sustained Portuguese-Asian interaction, including Portuguese-speaking or Catholic Asian communities.

Historical context. Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century sources use a variety of contemporary labels, including terms for Christians, baptized converts, and the offspring of mixed unions. These labels carried legal, religious, and social meanings that differ from modern ethnic categories. Manuel Godinho de Eredia, a cartographer born in Melaka of mixed Portuguese and Southeast Asian parentage, is one well-known example of such a person.

Why it matters. The concept allows historians to describe a real social development, the emergence of mixed households and communities in Melaka, without assuming that a single fixed identity existed from the outset.

Potential misunderstanding. The modern Portuguese Eurasian community of Melaka has its own history and identity, and its descent from sixteenth-century households should not be assumed to be simple or uninterrupted. This chapter uses "Luso-Asian" analytically and cautiously.

Section 70.1

Reconstructing Portuguese Melaka, 1511–1641

The history of Portuguese Melaka rests on several families of evidence, each illuminating some experiences and hiding others. The most abundant material is Portuguese: letters from Albuquerque and later governors, correspondence between the Crown and its officials, reports by captains and factors, chronicles by writers such as João de Barros, Fernão Lopes de Castanheda, Gaspar Correia, and Diogo do Couto, and merchant and missionary writings. Tomé Pires's Suma Oriental, composed in the years after the conquest, offers a systematic description of the port and its trade. Letters of Jesuit missionaries, including Francis Xavier, record religious activity and travel. The Flemish jeweler Jacques de Coutre, who spent years in the region, left a memoir that Peter Borschberg has edited for the period around 1594 to 1625, and Manuel Godinho de Eredia, born in Melaka, produced a description and map of the city in the early seventeenth century.

A second family of sources comes from Malay and other Asian traditions. The Sejarah Melayu, whose surviving versions are associated with a revision made at the Johor court in the early seventeenth century, narrates the history of the Melakan dynasty and its aftermath. Acehnese works such as the Hikayat Aceh and the Bustan al-Salatin present the history of northern Sumatra from within. Chinese records from the Ming period, Dutch East India Company documents from the seventeenth century, and the occasional Indian Ocean Muslim source supplement these. Archaeology, including the standing remains of the fortress gate and church foundations, adds a material dimension, although the layers of Portuguese, Dutch, and British building have complicated the site.

These sources have predictable biases. Portuguese administrative documents were produced by officials and clergy for audiences in Goa and Lisbon, often to justify actions, request resources, or demonstrate loyalty. Reports of battles tend to magnify Portuguese courage and enemy numbers. Malay chronicles were composed within court traditions that sought to legitimate dynastic succession and to interpret misfortune through ideas of kingship and moral order. Dutch records served a company seeking commercial advantage over its Portuguese rivals. Little local administrative documentation from Melaka's own households, Asian merchant firms, or ordinary residents survives, so the daily experience of most inhabitants must be reconstructed indirectly through Portuguese descriptions of neighborhoods, fiscal records, missionary baptisms, and later Dutch censuses.

A further difficulty is chronological. Some of the most vivid narratives of events in Melaka, including the famous sieges, were written decades later, or compiled by authors in Goa or Lisbon who had never visited the Strait. Students should therefore ask, of every account, who wrote it, when, from where, for whom, and on what information. The distinction between contemporary testimony, retrospective chronicle, and modern reconstruction is central to evaluating claims about fleet sizes, casualty figures, and the motives of rulers. Where this chapter reports numbers it attributes them to their sources; where scholars disagree it says so.

Historical Source and Method Study

Whose Melaka? Matching Questions to Sources

Administration and revenue: Portuguese royal correspondence and fiscal records are strongest here, but they show what officials chose to report and what reached the Crown.
Commerce and communities: Pires, Eredia, Coutre, and later Dutch reports supply detail, yet each reflects a particular moment and a particular vantage point.

Section 70.2

The Fortress, Harbor, and Portuguese Administration

After the conquest, the Portuguese quickly began building a fortress beside the river mouth, using stone and materials from existing structures. This fortress, later called A Famosa, was the heart of Portuguese Melaka. It should not be imagined as a single completed structure of 1511. The initial works were hurried and defensive; later decades brought additional walls, bastions, gates, and a church complex on the hill overlooking the harbor. The fortified area enclosed the administrative core, a residential district for Portuguese settlers, and religious institutions, while a much larger Asian urban population lived in neighborhoods outside the walls. The distinction between the fortified center and the wider urban and regional world is essential to understanding the extent of Portuguese power.

The chief official was the captain of Melaka, appointed by the Crown, usually for a fixed term. The captain combined military command, judicial authority, and supervision of customs and trade, and the office was prized because it offered rights to profit from certain trade routes, including voyages to places such as the Maluku Islands and Timor. Beside the captain stood other officers: a factor (feitor) responsible for the Crown's commercial interests, a treasury officer, a judge, and other officials staffed from Lisbon and Goa. The garrison, which varied over time, combined Portuguese soldiers with Asian auxiliaries, and its upkeep was a constant expense.

Melaka formed part of the Estado da Índia, with its capital at Goa. Royal ambitions articulated in Lisbon and orders issued from Goa had to be implemented by a captain living in a port months away by sea, among merchants and neighbors who had their own interests. The relationship between Goa and Melaka involved reporting, appeals for reinforcements, and the periodic dispatch of fleets, but communication was slow and the captain acted with considerable practical autonomy. The gap between royal ambition and administrative capacity runs through the whole history of Portuguese Melaka. Officials could issue regulations about customs, shipping, and religion, but they depended on local brokers, Asian merchants, and neighboring rulers to make them effective.

Customs duties were a principal source of revenue, collected at the customs house by officials who worked with Asian interpreters and intermediaries. Revenue helped pay for the fortress and garrison, but costs were considerable, and contemporary and modern assessments differ about how far Melaka's income covered its expenses. Portuguese rule also drew on existing Melakan institutions. The Portuguese retained, adapted, or replaced offices familiar to merchants, including harbor officials and community leaders who mediated between the fortress and Asian residents. The result was a hybrid administration: European in its command structure, but dependent on Asian offices and skills.

Finally, the territorial reach of the Portuguese was limited. They governed the fortified city and its immediate surroundings, and exercised influence over some adjacent areas through alliances, but they did not govern the Malay Peninsula or the waters of the Strait. Johor, Perak, Pahang, and Sumatran coastal polities retained their own rulers and revenues. To describe Melaka as a Portuguese colony in the later sense of a territorial dependency would misrepresent its character: it was a fortified commercial enclave embedded in a political landscape that remained largely Malay and Sumatran.

Section 70.3

Merchants, Customs, Brokers, and the Commercial City

One of the persistent errors in older accounts is the claim that Melaka's trade collapsed after 1511. The evidence does not support so stark a conclusion. Trade changed, and some merchants left, but the port continued to function as a commercial center, and many communities remained. What the Portuguese inherited was not only a site but a commercial system with established practices: harbor officials who represented different groups of merchants, customary rules for weighing and taxing goods, brokers who spoke multiple languages, and a calendar tied to the monsoon winds.

Gujarati merchants, who had been among the most prominent pre-conquest traders, were affected unevenly. Some shifted their business to ports such as Pasai and later Aceh, or to Java, where Muslim ports remained open. Others continued to trade at Melaka under Portuguese rule, sometimes through arrangements negotiated with the captain. Tamil merchants, often called Keling in Malay sources, retained a significant presence and played important roles as financiers and shippers. Chinese traders came to Melaka, though their numbers and the terms of their access were shaped by Ming policy and by the later Portuguese establishment of a base at Macao. Javanese, Malay, and other merchants from the archipelago continued to bring rice, spices, and other goods. The Portuguese crown sought to profit through royal trading ventures and customs, while private Portuguese merchants and casados traded on their own account.

Brokers and harbor intermediaries were essential. A Portuguese official arriving from Lisbon or Goa could not simply decide how a Chinese junk, a Gujarati vessel, and a Javanese trading ship should be taxed and served. Local specialists who knew languages, prices, credit relationships, and the reputations of merchants mediated these transactions. Customs revenue depended on their cooperation, as did the stability of the harbor. At the same time, these intermediaries had their own interests and sometimes their own loyalties, and Portuguese relations with prominent Asian leaders could be tense. The execution of the Javanese merchant leader Utimutiraja, traditionally dated 1516 after accusations of conspiracy, illustrates how quickly trust could collapse and how closely commercial and political loyalty were intertwined.

Overall, Portuguese Melaka remained cosmopolitan but altered in composition. It no longer enjoyed the unqualified loyalty of a Muslim-ruled port, and its trade faced competition from ports that offered lower costs or fewer restrictions. Evidence for the scale of change is uneven. Early Portuguese descriptions emphasize the continued richness of the port, while later reports increasingly stress competition, cost, and insecurity. Historians therefore speak of transformation and redistribution of trade rather than simple collapse, an approach elaborated in the comparative work of M. A. P. Meilink-Roelofsz and later scholars.

Paraphrase / Synthesis (not a verbatim excerpt):

In summary, Pires describes Melaka as a port visited by merchants from many parts of Asia, speaking a remarkable number of languages by his reckoning, whose trade was organized through several harbor officers each responsible for merchants of particular regions. He treats customs and gift-giving practices as integral to commerce, and he stresses the city's strategic position at the meeting point of the monsoon trades. It also reveals Portuguese strategic ambitions: Pires writes as someone seeking to explain how control of the port could advantage the Crown.

Section 70.4

Everyday Life in Portuguese Melaka

Behind the walls and ordinances lay a city of households, markets, workshops, and neighborhoods. The population is difficult to quantify: Portuguese estimates vary and often count only Christians or tax-paying residents. What the sources do show is a city divided between a small fortified European and Christian core and much larger Asian suburbs. Early-seventeenth-century descriptions name outlying districts, including areas along the river and shore, where Malay, Javanese, Tamil, Chinese, and other residents lived and worked, often in communities organized by origin and occupation.

Occupations reflected the maritime economy. Sailors, pilots, shipwrights, caulkers, porters, boatmen, and fishermen worked in and around the harbor. Craftsmen produced and repaired goods, and market sellers supplied food, cloth, and household items. Moneylenders and financiers, among them Tamil merchants, provided credit. Servants and enslaved people performed domestic labor and some skilled work. Slavery was a feature of Melaka both before and after 1511, and enslaved people, often brought from elsewhere in the region, appear in Portuguese households and commercial enterprises. Their experiences, like those of other dependents, are mostly visible through the records of owners and officials.

Marriage and household formation were central to the Portuguese project. Officials encouraged casados to marry Asian women, typically Christian converts or women brought into Christian households, and mixed households became a basic unit of Portuguese Melaka. Women managed households, ran businesses, inherited property in some cases, and mediated between communities, though their legal and social positions varied with status, religion, and the circumstances of marriage. Children of these unions grew up in settings that blended languages and customs, and the gradual emergence of Portuguese-based creole speech and Catholic religious practices among these households is a long-term development rather than an immediate result of the conquest. Malay continued to function as a major language of trade and daily communication, and many Portuguese residents learned it.

Food supplies exposed the city's dependence on its neighbors. Rice, the staple, came largely from outside the immediate hinterland, arriving by sea from places such as Java and mainland ports, while fish, fruit, and other local foods were supplied by fishermen and farmers of the surrounding region. Fresh water, timber, and building materials likewise depended on regional access. Religious institutions structured community life: churches, a confraternity devoted to charity, hospitals, and schools served Christian residents, while Muslims, Hindus, and others maintained their own practices, though under conditions that differed from the pre-1511 sultanate. Disease and the periodic hardships of siege and blockade affected all groups, though not equally.

Everyday Life and Social History

Inside and Outside the Walls

The fortress housed officials, clergy, soldiers, and wealthy settlers, but the majority of Melaka's residents lived beyond its walls. A Tamil moneylender, a Javanese shipwright, a Chinese shopkeeper, a Malay fisherman's family, an enslaved domestic servant in a casado household, and a Portuguese official's wife of Asian origin each lived within a different web of obligations, language, religion, and risk. When sieges came, walled and unwalled residents experienced them very differently: the fortress offered shelter to some, while others faced displacement, loss of livelihood, or hunger.

Because most surviving records were produced by officials and clergy, historians must read them for indirect evidence of women, laborers, enslaved people, and non-Christian communities. The apparent dominance of Portuguese voices in the record should not be mistaken for a dominance of Portuguese people in the city's population.

Division II · Sections 70.5–70.8

Johor, Aceh, and the Reorganization of the Strait

The three principal centers of political competition: Portuguese Melaka, Johor, and Aceh.

Section 70.5

From Sultan Mahmud Shah to the Johor Successor Polity

The Portuguese conquest did not end the Melakan sultanate. Sultan Mahmud Shah and members of his court withdrew from the city, and over the following years they sought bases from which to continue the dynasty and contest the Portuguese. Malay and Portuguese sources agree on the broad outline, though not on every date or detail. The court moved through several locations in the peninsula and islands before settling at Bintan, in the Riau archipelago, which became its principal base. From there Mahmud Shah maintained relations with followers, maritime communities, and neighboring rulers.

Bintan was well placed for such a role. It lay among islands whose inhabitants, the Orang Laut, had long provided Melaka's rulers with maritime labor, navigation, and military service. Control of such followers was a key asset in a world where authority depended on personal allegiance and mobility as much as on territory. From Bintan the Melakan court could threaten Portuguese shipping and communicate with ports across the Strait. The Portuguese made several attempts to dislodge it during the 1510s and 1520s, with limited success until a major expedition in 1526 destroyed the Bintan settlement. Mahmud Shah withdrew to Sumatra, where he died at Kampar in 1528.

The dynasty did not end with him. His sons established separate courts: one is traditionally associated with the founding of the sultanate of Perak, and Alauddin Riayat Shah II established a new court on the Johor River, from which the Johor sultanate takes its origin. These capitals were riverine: located on rivers that provided access to the sea and to inland resources, defensible by water and by the movement of followers, and suited to a political order in which rulers governed through networks of ports and people. Johor drew legitimacy from descent from the Melakan line, from its claim to the regalia and traditions of that dynasty, and from the continued allegiance of maritime followers.

It would be a mistake to describe Johor as a Portuguese-created state, or as merely a refuge for exiles. It was a successor polity: a political formation that claimed continuity with Melaka while adapting to new circumstances. Its rulers negotiated, fought, and sometimes cooperated with the Portuguese, Aceh, and neighboring Malay polities, as the following sections show. The fuller history of the Johor-Riau world, including later dynastic change and Bugis involvement, belongs to Chapter 81.

The sources for these years show how differently the same events could be told. Portuguese reports describe the Bintan court mainly as a base for raiding and hostile intelligence, while the Sejarah Melayu, revised at the Johor court in the early seventeenth century, narrates the same decades as a story of loss, endurance, and dynastic continuity that served the interests of Johor's later rulers. Neither is a neutral register. It is also worth remembering that the Melakan population did not simply follow its ruler into exile: many Malay residents remained in or near Melaka under Portuguese rule, so that loyalty to the old dynasty and everyday coexistence with the new fortress could coexist within the same communities, and even within the same families.

Section 70.6

The Rise of Aceh and the Northern Sumatran Challenge

On the western side of the Strait, the northern tip of Sumatra became the base of a new power. The region had a long history of ports engaged in the pepper trade and in connections with Indian Ocean Muslim commerce; Chapter 63 examined Samudera Pasai and other early Muslim polities there. In the early sixteenth century a ruler associated with Aceh, usually identified as Ali Mughayat Syah, brought neighboring communities under his control, and by around 1524 Aceh had taken Pasai, where the Portuguese had briefly established a presence. Dates and details of these early conquests rest on a combination of Portuguese reports and later Acehnese tradition and should be treated with some caution.

Aceh was both a trading state and a military power. It controlled a pepper-producing coast, hosted merchants from India and further west, and became a destination for those unwilling to trade at Portuguese Melaka. Its rulers presented themselves as Muslim sovereigns, and their religious identity helped attract merchants and scholars from the wider Islamic world and legitimized resistance to Portuguese Christian power. Acehnese rulers also sought military assistance and technical expertise from Muslim partners, and later sources and Portuguese reports mention contacts with the Ottoman world, although the scale and effect of such help is debated. Aceh's expansion brought it into competition with other Sumatran and peninsular polities, not merely with the Portuguese.

Motives for challenging Melaka were therefore strategic and commercial. Melaka's Portuguese rulers attempted to control shipping through the cartaz system and to divert trade; an Acehnese ruler who wanted to attract that trade to his own ports had every reason to weaken the fortress. Attacking Melaka also promised plunder, prestige, and the removal of a rival. At the same time, Aceh's attacks were not a pure expression of religious war: Acehnese and Portuguese traders also met in commerce, and Aceh fought other Muslim powers, including Johor. The later reign of Iskandar Muda, when Aceh reached its greatest regional extent, is the subject of Chapter 80.

Aceh's internal organization helped explain its strength. Its rulers controlled access to a pepper coast and appointed harbor officials to receive foreign merchants, who came from Gujarat, the Coromandel Coast, Arabia, and elsewhere. The port connected northern Sumatra to Red Sea and western Indian commerce, giving it a role as a Muslim alternative to Melaka for merchants traveling westward. Later tradition credits a later sixteenth-century Acehnese ruler with enlarging the fleet and directing sustained campaigns against both Melaka and Johor, though the exact chronology of these campaigns is better established for some episodes than for others. Historians therefore describe Aceh's rise as the product of commercial position, political consolidation, and opportunity, rather than as the unfolding of a single religious program.

Section 70.7

Rival Ports, Alternative Routes, and Commercial Competition

The Portuguese held a port, but the Strait contained many others. Johor's riverine and island centers, Patani on the peninsula's east coast, Pahang, Perak's tin-producing region, Kedah, and Sumatran ports at Pasai, Aceh, Jambi, and Palembang all competed for shipping. In Java, northern ports such as Gresik and Jepara and, from the 1520s, Banten on the Sunda Strait offered access to pepper and rice and served merchants avoiding Melaka. Further east, ports in Borneo and Sulawesi, including the later rise of Makassar examined in Chapter 82, drew spice and other trade. Merchants chose among these ports on the basis of duties, security, availability of cargo, and political relations.

Ships could redirect trade. A vessel traveling from India to the archipelago did not have to stop at Melaka if it could reach a suitable alternative, and shipping routes through the Sunda Strait allowed some commerce to bypass the Strait of Malacca altogether, though this required different seasonal timing and navigational conditions. Acehnese ports became important for Muslim merchants traveling to and from the Red Sea and western India. Patani and other eastern ports provided access to Chinese and Japanese trade. Portuguese efforts to require ships to carry passes and call at Melaka met resistance in these circumstances, because the means of enforcement, patrolling vessels and a limited number of ships, were never enough to cover the entire commercial space.

The result was a commercial landscape of competition rather than monopoly. Melaka retained advantages: a fortified harbor, existing commercial infrastructure, access to Portuguese networks reaching Goa, China, and Japan, and its central position. But its rivals offered lower costs, greater political acceptability to Muslim merchants, or proximity to sources of pepper and other goods. Competition also fed political rivalry, because each port's ruler profited from trade and sought to protect it. The Portuguese could attack rival ports, as they did in Johor, but destruction of a port did not eliminate the commerce it served; merchants and followers moved elsewhere or returned when the threat passed.

The Portuguese tried to compete as well as coerce. Officials sometimes adjusted duties or offered protection to attract merchants, and royal and private Portuguese traders sailed to rival ports, including Patani and ports in Java, to buy cargoes that could then be carried through Melaka. But commercial competition worked in both directions: the rise of Banten from the 1520s offered pepper buyers a route that bypassed the Strait of Malacca entirely, and Chinese junks from Fujian and other coasts, together with Japanese and Siamese traders, could call at Patani or other eastern ports without passing Portuguese customs. Seasonal winds and navigational skill determined which of these options was practical in a given year, and merchants combined them flexibly.

Section 70.8

The Political Balance of the Strait: Power without Regional Unity

By the mid-sixteenth century three centers dominated the Strait's politics, though each differed in its resources. Portuguese Melaka possessed a fortress with artillery, a harbor, customs revenue, and access, through the Estado da Índia, to ships, soldiers, and commercial networks reaching from Africa to Japan. Its weaknesses were distance from reinforcement, a small European population, dependence on outside food supplies, and the cost of defense. Johor drew on dynastic legitimacy, maritime followers, riverine settlements, and trading relationships, but it lacked a single great entrepôt and relied on mobility. Aceh possessed a rich pepper coast, wealth from international trade, a large fleet, and rulers able to mobilize resources for distant campaigns.

Relations among these powers were never stable. Johor fought the Portuguese, but it also fought Aceh, and at times negotiated or cooperated with the Portuguese against Aceh. Aceh attacked Melaka and Johor alike. The Portuguese attacked Johor and defended against Aceh, while trading with both when possible. Warfare coexisted with commerce and negotiation; merchants continued to move between ports during hostilities, and rulers weighed truce against campaign according to circumstances. It would be wrong to describe the system as a permanently balanced triangle or as a coalition of Southeast Asian powers against Europeans. Tributary claims overlapped, and rulers asserted authority over each other's territories without the means to make those claims permanent.

The Orang Laut, the sea-oriented peoples of the Riau-Lingga archipelago and the Strait's coasts, were central to this balance. They provided pilots, crews, fighting men, and knowledge of channels, tides, and hidden anchorages. They were not an undifferentiated group of pirates, as hostile Portuguese and Dutch writers sometimes portrayed them, but communities with their own leaders and relationships that could shift among patrons. Malay rulers, including those of Johor, depended on their allegiance; the Portuguese and, later, the Dutch also sought their service. Their mobility and local knowledge were strategic assets in a maritime environment where naval success depended less on size than on information, seasonality, and the ability to move.

The political history of the Strait between 1511 and 1641 is therefore best understood as a competition among three centers and several satellites, in which power depended on alliances, supply, and maritime skill rather than a simple hierarchy. This framework prepares the military narrative of Division III, in which the Portuguese were able to defend Melaka without eliminating the threats around it.

Wars, Sieges, and the Defense of Portuguese Melaka

How Portuguese Melaka survived repeated threats without converting defensive success into regional supremacy.

Section 70.9

The First Decades of Military Resistance, c. 1511–1550

The first decades after the conquest were a period of improvisation. The Portuguese had taken the city with a relatively small force, and their hold on it depended on completing the fortress, retaining a garrison, and keeping open the sea lanes to Goa. The displaced Melakan court was not the only danger. Reports describe an expedition from northern Java in 1513, associated with the ruler of Jepara and Demak, that sought to dislodge the Portuguese and failed. The episode illustrates that opposition to the Portuguese was not confined to the Malay peninsula and that Javanese powers also saw Melaka as a stake in their own commercial and political aims. Chapter 69 treats the immediate aftermath of the conquest in more detail.

The most persistent early opponent was the court at Bintan. Portuguese commanders, based at Melaka, mounted several expeditions against it during the 1510s and 1520s, with mixed results, until the expedition of 1526 destroyed the settlement. The campaigns involved amphibious operations among islands and rivers, where Portuguese ships and artillery were advantageous in open water but less decisive in narrow channels and shallow creeks that favored local vessels and knowledge. Even after the destruction of Bintan, the dynasty regrouped, and Portuguese expeditions against Johor in the 1530s and later again showed both the Portuguese capacity to strike and the limits of that capacity.

On the western side, Acehnese expansion generated a new threat. The conquest of Pasai in the 1520s eliminated a Portuguese outpost on the Sumatran coast and placed a hostile and ambitious state across the Strait. Acehnese expeditions against Melaka are reported in the 1530s and 1540s, and Portuguese sources describe naval engagements and attempts to land forces. The details and dates of individual engagements are reported unevenly, and the Portuguese sources characteristically stress their own successes. What emerges clearly is that Melaka faced the possibility of attack from both the Johor side and the Acehnese side, and that its garrison and naval resources were stretched.

Defense depended on more than walls. A fortress needs a garrison that is paid and fed, ships to patrol approaches, and information about enemy movements. Maritime surveillance relied on watch posts, patrol vessels, and informants, including local brokers and Asian merchants who reported on fleets gathering in rival ports. The fortress's food supply required secure sea lanes, and a blockade or a bad monsoon could quickly create hardship. Reinforcements came from Goa, but delays meant that captains often had to rely on local resources and on Asian auxiliaries, a point developed in Section 70.16.

The Portuguese naval establishment in the Strait was always small. Captains relied on a few ships of European design, supplemented by local vessels, Asian crews, and auxiliaries recruited among residents of the city. Disease affected European newcomers heavily, and replacing losses depended on irregular arrivals from Goa. A brief Portuguese occupation of Pasai in the early 1520s, ended by the Acehnese conquest, illustrated the difficulty of holding positions at a distance from Melaka. These constraints meant that even successful expeditions, such as the destruction of Bintan, could rarely be followed by lasting occupation. The captain's practical task was to keep the fortress supplied and the sea approaches watched, and to strike when opportunity and weather allowed.

Section 70.10

Johor, Aceh, and the Changing Military Balance, c. 1550–1600

In the second half of the sixteenth century the conflict became more complex. In 1564 an Acehnese force attacked the Johor capital on the Johor River and captured Sultan Alauddin Riayat Shah II, who was taken to Aceh and died there. The episode is crucial because it shows that Aceh's military ambition was directed against Muslim rivals as well as the Portuguese. Johor, weakened and displaced, continued under successors. For the Portuguese, the Acehnese attack meant that a potential ally against Aceh was damaged, but it also removed pressure on Melaka from one source while strengthening the other.

The objectives of each contender differed. The Portuguese captains sought to hold Melaka, protect trade routes, and strike enemy bases when resources permitted. Johor's rulers sought to preserve and rebuild the dynasty's authority, recover followers and revenue, and avoid domination by either Portugal or Aceh; they therefore alternated among resistance, negotiation, and, at times, cooperation. Aceh's rulers sought to expand their sphere in northern Sumatra and the peninsula, attract trade to their ports, and weaken Melaka. Each faced constraints: the Portuguese had few ships and men, Johor lacked a central port and large fleet, and Aceh's long supply lines made distant campaigns risky.

Portuguese expeditions against Johor continued in the later sixteenth century. The best-known episode is the destruction of the Johor capital at Johor Lama by a Portuguese force in 1587, recorded in Malaysian museum documentation and consistent with Portuguese reports, though the sequence of earlier and later attacks varies in modern reconstructions. Students should distinguish the timing of a campaign, the destruction of a capital, and the later accounts of its consequences. The destruction of a capital disrupted a court's resources and forced relocation, but the dynasty moved to new sites and persisted. A Portuguese success at one point did not alter the underlying fact that Johor retained followers on the water, links to other rulers, and the capacity to rebuild.

By 1600, therefore, the military balance had changed less than the number of campaigns might suggest. Aceh had demonstrated its strength but had not taken Melaka. The Portuguese had held the fortress and struck at Johor but had not eliminated it. Johor had survived repeated attacks and retained claims to Melakan legitimacy. The arrival of a new European competitor, the Dutch, would change the calculations of all three, as Section 70.15 explains.

The Johor court's movements after 1564 and 1587 illustrate its resilience. Rather than depending on a single stronghold, it relocated along rivers and islands, drawing on followers who could re-establish settlements elsewhere. Neighboring Malay courts, including Perak and Pahang, maintained their own relationships with Johor, the Portuguese, and Aceh, so that the peninsula was a mosaic of rulers rather than a single front. The union of the Portuguese and Spanish crowns in 1580 changed the formal political setting of the Estado da Índia, but it did not give Melaka additional ships or men in the short term, and the captains continued to manage the Strait with limited means.

Section 70.11

The Acehnese Siege of Melaka in 1568

In early 1568 a large Acehnese force besieged Melaka. Portuguese accounts report a substantial fleet and an army that established positions outside the fortress. The main offensive is conventionally reconstructed over roughly five weeks, from about 20 January to 25 February, rather than as uninterrupted fighting lasting several months; the precise sequence should be checked against individual accounts. The captain, Dom Leonis Pereira, directed the defense, and Portuguese relief forces helped end the offensive. The figures for the size of the attacking fleet and army come from Portuguese reports and should be treated as claims rather than established numbers, since defenders had reason to stress both the scale of the threat and their own success.

The siege was a serious test of fortifications and naval capacity. The Acehnese brought galleys and other vessels, artillery, and siege works. Portuguese sources and later scholarship note possible Ottoman and other Muslim technical assistance, although how much help came and how decisive it was remain debated. The defenders used the fortress's walls and bastions, artillery, and ships, and relied on reinforcements and on the loyalty of those within the walls. The siege also affected the wider urban population: residents beyond the walls were exposed to the attackers, supplies were strained, and the movement of food and people was disrupted.

The siege demonstrated both strength and limits. The fortress resisted a large attack, confirming that artillery and fortifications could withstand even a determined Asian army and navy. Yet the Acehnese withdrawal did not eliminate Aceh's capacity to threaten again, and the defenders' victory did not extend Portuguese authority beyond the walls. Pierre-Yves Manguin's study of the siege emphasizes the naval dimension and the value of the contemporary pictorial source for reconstructing the city's defenses and the form of Acehnese galleys.

The siege and its aftermath were also part of a contest over narrative. Portuguese authorities presented the relief and defense as a providential deliverance, a theme repeated in later chronicles, and such accounts shaped how subsequent generations remembered the event. Acehnese and Malay traditions treated the same expedition differently, and Dutch observers later used it to assess Melaka's defenses. For historians, the practical lesson is to use the surviving image and reports to reconstruct fortifications, ship types, and sequence, while treating numbers and moral interpretations as claims to be tested.

Section 70.12

Fortifications, Naval Power, and Repeated Conflict

It is tempting to read these conflicts as a contest between European firearms and less advanced Southeast Asian forces. The evidence does not support this reading. Malay, Javanese, and Acehnese rulers used cannon, firearms, fortifications, and large ships long before and after the Portuguese arrival, and Melaka's own pre-1511 defenses had included substantial artillery. Aceh's galleys and artillery, whether or not assisted by Muslim partners from beyond the region, could threaten the fortress, and Johor used riverine fortifications and mobile forces suited to local waters. Both sides adapted techniques and sometimes recruited each other's specialists.

The Portuguese advantages were real but specific. A stone fortress with bastions and artillery, positioned at the river mouth and supported by ships, could resist assault by land and sea. A fleet of Portuguese-style vessels could outgun many opponents in open waters. These strengths made Melaka hard to take by direct attack. Yet they did not extend over the surrounding waterways, where small boats, local pilots, and knowledge of channels and tides gave opponents room to raid, harass shipping, and avoid battle. The Portuguese never had enough ships to patrol the entire Strait, and monsoon seasons constrained operations.

This explains the distinction between tactical success and strategic control. Defending a fortress is a tactical and logistical problem that the Portuguese repeatedly solved. Controlling the Strait is a strategic problem requiring permanent naval superiority, secure supply, and allied or subordinate ports, conditions the Portuguese lacked. Each Portuguese victory preserved the city without removing the sources of threat. Each enemy withdrawal reflected supply, weather, disease, or political decisions as much as battlefield defeat.

Repeated conflict also took a toll. Sieges and blockades disrupted trade, increased costs, exposed civilians, and strained supplies and morale. Fortifications required continuing maintenance, and garrisons needed pay and provisions. The cumulative effect was to raise the cost of holding Melaka while the benefits depended on commerce that rival ports could attract. This was the setting in which Dutch competition entered.

Ship types illustrate the contrast. Portuguese captains used galleys and smaller oared vessels for operations in the Strait, along with larger sailing ships for long-distance voyages, while Acehnese fleets combined large galleys with local craft. Orang Laut and Malay vessels, light and maneuverable, could use shallow channels and seasonal winds in ways heavier ships could not. The fortifications of Melaka were elaborated over time as bastions and walls were added, and the siege of 1568 showed which features worked and where improvements were needed. Fortification, in short, was a continuing project, not a finished achievement, and its cost competed with every other call on the captain's resources.

Section 70.13

Portuguese Commercial Policy and the Limits of Maritime Monopoly

Portuguese policy aimed to regulate and profit from shipping rather than to exclude Asian merchants altogether. The Crown claimed rights over certain commodities and routes, collected customs duties, and operated its own trading ventures. It also issued passes, known as cartazes, that Asian ships were expected to carry, specifying routes and requiring payment of duties at Portuguese ports. The system claimed authority over the sea lanes and presented Portuguese power as protection. In practice it functioned as a tax and licensing regime that depended on enforcement.

Enforcement was uneven. Portuguese ships could seize vessels lacking passes, but they could not be everywhere. Smuggling, bribery, and evasion were common, and officials themselves sometimes participated in private trade. The captaincy of Melaka was both a military office and a source of private profit, which encouraged the use of position for commercial advantage and created tension with the Crown's interests. Private Portuguese merchants, casados, and Asian partners often traded more flexibly than royal ventures. Meanwhile, rival ports offered alternatives, and Asian merchants understood how to use them. Sanjay Subrahmanyam's work on Portuguese Asia emphasizes that the Estado was one participant among many in a commercial world that remained largely Asian.

The Portuguese could therefore claim to control shipping without being able to enforce the claim. Legal assertion and practical capacity differed. A monopoly in law, even backed by occasional violence, did not translate into a monopoly in fact, and the continued importance of Asian merchants, shippers, and financiers meant that commerce in the Strait proceeded along multiple channels. This does not mean that Portuguese policy had no effect: the cartaz system added costs, created friction, and gave Portuguese authorities leverage in some cases. But its main outcome was to make Melaka one costly option among several rather than the unavoidable gateway.

The Crown's commercial ambitions extended to particular commodities. Spices and pepper were treated as royal interests, and officials tried to channel them toward the route around Africa to Lisbon. In practice, the volumes that reached Europe depended on prices, supply, and the willingness of producers and merchants to sell to Portuguese buyers, all of which varied. The route between Melaka and Maluku, and the trade to China and Japan that the Portuguese pursued through Macao after its establishment in the 1550s, offered profitable opportunities that were often exploited through private ventures and through rights granted to captains and others. Melaka's role was thus as a node in several routes, valuable for what it allowed the Portuguese to link rather than for any single monopoly.

Asian merchants responded pragmatically. Some obtained passes and traded under them; others avoided Portuguese ports; some cooperated with Portuguese partners when it paid. Cartaz holders could sail with a degree of protection, and the pass also served as a source of income and information for the Portuguese. But the same merchants maintained links to rival ports, so that the system never produced a closed circuit. The coexistence of licensed and unlicensed trade shows why historians regard the Portuguese as one power among several in a commercial world with many centers.

Section 70.14

Christianity, Religious Plurality, and Luso-Asian Society

Religion was central to Portuguese self-understanding, and Melaka became an important base of Christian institutions in maritime Asia. The Crown's patronage of the Church in Asia, often called the padroado, linked missionary activity to royal authority. Melaka received a church on the hill overlooking the harbor, a bishopric in the later sixteenth century, a charitable confraternity, and the presence of several religious orders, including the Jesuits. Missionaries used Melaka as a staging point for travel to Maluku, India, Japan, and China.

Francis Xavier is the best-known of these figures. In the 1540s and early 1550s he passed through Melaka several times, preaching, corresponding with colleagues, and arranging travel across maritime Asia. His letters offer a missionary's view of the city and its inhabitants, including criticism of the moral conduct of some Portuguese residents. Melaka also served as a place where news and people from distant locations met, and where Asian Christians, converts, and travelers intersected with missionary networks. After his death, tradition associated his remains with Melaka briefly before they were taken to Goa.

Yet Melaka's religious life cannot be reduced to a story of Catholic conversion. The Portuguese population was small relative to the larger Asian population, and many residents remained Muslim, Hindu, Buddhist, or followers of other traditions. Muslim merchants and communities continued to live and trade in and around Melaka, though their position was shaped by Portuguese hostility to Islam and by political conflict with Muslim rulers. Conversions occurred, particularly among households tied to Portuguese settlers, but they varied in depth and motive, and Christian practice in mixed households often blended with local custom. Historians caution against treating "Luso-Asian," "Muslim," or "Asian merchant" as uniform categories: each included people of differing origins, wealth, and loyalties, and identities were fluid.

Institutions of charity and education illustrate how religious life shaped social ties. A lay confraternity, the Misericórdia, offered assistance to the poor, sick, and imprisoned, and drew wealthy settlers into patronage roles. Jesuit and other schools taught Christian doctrine and, in some cases, literacy, creating networks that linked converts to Portuguese clergy. Hospitals and burial arrangements likewise extended Christian practice into daily life. These institutions served mixed households and gave them recognized places in the colonial order, though the benefits were unevenly distributed and depended on status, patronage, and gender.

The movement of people through these networks connected Melaka to wider worlds. Missionaries, merchants, soldiers, sailors, and enslaved or free Asian travelers moved between Melaka, Goa, Macao, Japan, and the Spice Islands, carrying news, goods, and religious practices. A household in Melaka might have ties to relatives or business partners in several of these places. The importance of Melaka in such networks did not depend on its military position alone but on its role as a stopping place for ships and a place where languages and customs met.

Section 70.15

The Dutch Arrival and the Johor–VOC Alliance of 1606

At the turn of the seventeenth century Dutch ships began to appear in Southeast Asian waters. The Dutch were at war with the Habsburg monarchy, which since 1580 had also ruled Portugal and its overseas possessions, and the Portuguese Estado da Índia thus became a target. The Dutch East India Company, founded in 1602 and examined in Chapter 77, sought commercial advantage and military opportunities against Portuguese ports. Melaka, a fortress with a rich history, was an obvious objective, but the Dutch lacked a local base and needed Asian allies.

Johor was a natural partner. Its rulers, under pressure from both Portugal and Aceh, saw in the Dutch an additional force that could change the balance. The Johor court, in which the sultan's brother Raja Bongsu was a leading diplomat, negotiated with Dutch commanders, notably Cornelis Matelieff de Jonge. On 17 May 1606 representatives of Johor and the Dutch agreed to cooperate against Portuguese Melaka. Peter Borschberg's analysis shows that the agreement concerned military cooperation and the proposed division of the city and its surroundings after victory. Later that year the Dutch besieged Melaka, but a Portuguese relief fleet arrived and Dutch efforts failed.

The agreement was not a simple subordination of Johor to the Dutch or of the Dutch to Johor. Each side pursued distinct aims. Johor sought to recover political standing and to remove a Portuguese presence on its borders, but it also wished to avoid replacing one dominant European neighbor with another. The Dutch sought a base and trading advantage. Disagreements soon arose over interpretation of the terms, including who would hold what after a victory, and over the reliability of the versions in which the arrangements were written down. The treaty thus records an alliance of convenience whose fulfillment and meaning were contested.

The Dutch approach to the Strait reflected strategic calculation. Their ships needed anchorages, provisions, and information, and Johor could offer all three while possessing a long-standing claim to Melaka's legacy. The Dutch commander Matelieff led a fleet that included ships able to support a siege, and negotiations with Johor culminated in the agreement of 1606 and in the joint attack later that year. The Portuguese response was swift: a relief fleet arrived from Goa and confronted the Dutch off the coast, and the Dutch withdrew without taking the fortress. The episode demonstrated that an alliance with Johor was not enough on its own and that naval strength and timing were critical.

The failure did not end the Dutch–Johor relationship. Johor continued to negotiate with the Dutch over the following years, even as it adjusted to pressure from Aceh and Portugal, and the Company's growing regional presence kept the possibility of future cooperation alive. At the same time, the relationship was never frictionless: the question of who would ultimately hold Melaka, and on what terms, remained open, and Johor's rulers were alert to the risk of exchanging one dominant neighbor for another. The treaty therefore stands as evidence of Johor's initiative and strategy, not merely of Dutch ambition.

Section 70.16

The Dependence of Portuguese Melaka on Regional Cooperation

Portuguese Melaka could not survive in isolation. The city depended on outside supplies of rice, brought by sea from Java and mainland ports, and on timber, ship stores, and building materials from the peninsula and islands. Ship repair, an essential service for a maritime fortress, required skilled labor and materials supplied by Asian craftsmen and suppliers. Asian sailors, laborers, and auxiliaries filled the ranks of crews and garrisons. Brokers and moneylenders provided credit, and local intermediaries managed relations with merchants and neighboring communities.

This dependence shaped diplomacy. Captains negotiated with rulers who controlled supplies or access, including at times rulers who were also military opponents. Truces, informal understandings, and commercial arrangements could coexist with hostilities, since both sides often benefited from trade. When a blockade or hostile fleet interrupted supplies, the city quickly felt the effects, and such interruptions explain why Portuguese policy alternated between force and negotiation. Prolonged military survival thus depended on continuous interaction with societies outside Portuguese rule.

The point extends beyond logistics. Because Melaka's commerce was Asian in participants, credit, and demand, Portuguese power could be sustained only so long as these groups found it worthwhile to use the port. A hostile or blockaded Melaka offered fewer benefits to merchants, who could and did go elsewhere. The Portuguese therefore remained both rulers of the fortress and clients of the wider region, a dual position that was a source of strength in good times and weakness in crisis.

Concrete examples show how this worked. Ships required repairs after monsoon voyages, which meant timber, cordage, caulking materials, and workers, many of whom were Asian specialists. Rice shipments from Java or the mainland sustained the population, so that disruptions in those ports or on those routes had immediate consequences. Brokers who spoke several languages negotiated the sale of cargoes, while Tamil and other financiers extended credit to shippers and traders. When the Portuguese were at odds with a particular ruler, they often sought supplies from another, and truces or informal understandings with Johor or Pahang could ease shortages. Such arrangements show that the Portuguese were embedded in regional systems, not standing apart from them.

The Orang Laut and coastal communities also supplied labor and information, as pilots, boatmen, and scouts, though their allegiance could shift. A captain who could secure their cooperation gained access to knowledge of the Strait that was otherwise unavailable, while one who could not faced a harder environment. The dependence thus extended from the grand level of diplomacy to the everyday level of pilots and porters, and it explains why the Portuguese could not simply conquer their way to security.

Section 70.17

The Seventeenth-Century Crisis of Portuguese Melaka

The early seventeenth century brought mounting pressure. The Dutch, whose war with the Habsburg crowns provided a rationale for attacking Portuguese possessions, took or threatened Portuguese positions across maritime Asia, including in the Spice Islands examined in Chapter 71. The Dutch failure at Melaka in 1606 did not end their ambitions; their ships continued to appear in the Strait, and the Company's emerging base at Batavia, examined in Chapter 79, gave it a powerful regional center from which to operate. The Twelve Years' Truce concluded in Europe in 1609 was understood differently in Asia, where local commanders pursued their own advantage, and hostilities did not cease.

Johor's position remained precarious. Johor and Portuguese Melaka had concluded a peace in October 1610, a reminder that hostility could give way to negotiation even while Johor maintained other diplomatic options. In 1613 forces from Aceh, under Sultan Iskandar Muda, invaded Johor and burned the capital at Batu Sawar; Acehnese troops occupied the royal settlement and removed members of the court. The destruction must not be confused with a Portuguese expedition. Peter Borschberg connects the invasion with factional changes surrounding Raja Bongsu and with the fragile diplomacy of the previous decade. Johor's rulers adjusted relationships with Aceh, the Dutch, and the Portuguese as circumstances shifted. Chapter 81 develops the resulting dynastic consequences; here the essential point is that the 1613 attack came from Aceh, not Portuguese Melaka.

For Melaka, the consequences were commercial and logistical. Dutch ships, operating from Batavia and elsewhere, increasingly intercepted shipping and disrupted supply, and from the 1630s blockades by Dutch squadrons became more frequent. Merchants who might otherwise have called at Melaka found it dangerous or costly to do so. Customs revenue and trade volumes, difficult to measure precisely, are generally reported to have declined, while the cost of maintaining the fortress and garrison remained high. Portugal itself was ruled by the Spanish Habsburgs until 1640, and Asian possessions competed for scarce resources with other theaters of war. The captain at Melaka could request reinforcement but could not assume it would come in time.

These pressures did not make Portuguese Melaka's fall inevitable. The city survived the great Acehnese attack of 1629, and its defenders retained substantial advantages. But the cumulative effect of rising costs, falling revenue, insecure supply, and a new, well-resourced competitor made each crisis harder to meet than the last.

The Iberian union of crowns, in effect from 1580 until 1640, meant that Portuguese Asia was drawn into the Habsburg conflict with the Dutch, a war that Portuguese traders and officials in Asia had not chosen. Dutch captures of Portuguese positions and ships, and the blockading of key ports, shifted the balance across the Estado. Melaka was one pressure point among many, and the resources available to defend it competed with those needed for Goa, Colombo, Macao, and other posts. The captain's appeals for help had to compete with other emergencies.

Aceh's position was also changing. After the failure of 1629, its ability to mount large campaigns against Melaka diminished, an outcome examined in Chapter 80, and Johor found new room for maneuver. The Strait thus entered the 1630s with an altered balance: a weakened Aceh, a Johor eager to settle accounts with Portugal, and a Dutch Company with the resources to organize a sustained campaign.

Section 70.18

The Acehnese Siege of 1629 and Portuguese Survival

In 1629 Aceh launched its largest expedition against Melaka. Portuguese sources, including those studied by C. R. Boxer, report an enormous fleet and army, though the figures are partisan estimates and should be attributed rather than accepted. The expedition came at the end of a long period of Acehnese expansion under Iskandar Muda and followed earlier campaigns on the peninsula. The attack combined naval forces and land operations and aimed at removing Portuguese Melaka as a rival for control of the Strait.

The assault failed. Portuguese defenders, with the help of relief naval forces under Nuno Álvares Botelho, destroyed or dispersed much of the Acehnese fleet and forced the withdrawal of the land force. Reported Acehnese losses were heavy, and the failure damaged Aceh's military position, though the extent and duration of the damage are matters of interpretation. Andre Alexandre Martins Murteira's comparative study of the 1629 and 1640–1641 sieges stresses that the different outcomes cannot be explained by simple European superiority, since the same fortress that held in 1629 fell in 1641. Supply, naval relief, the quality of coordination, and the presence or absence of a blockade and local allies appear central.

The survival of Melaka in 1629 did not establish long-term Portuguese security. The costs of the campaign, in men, ships, and supplies, fell on a city already strained, and Aceh remained a regional power with the capacity to rebuild. The victory confirmed what earlier sieges had shown: Portuguese defense could hold the fortress, but did not eliminate rivals. It also gave the Portuguese a celebratory narrative that later accounts amplified and that historians must treat carefully.

Because the expedition followed a series of Acehnese campaigns on the peninsula and in the Strait, contemporaries and later historians have viewed it as an effort to settle the question of Melaka by main force. Failure had consequences beyond the battlefield. The loss of men and ships weakened Aceh's capacity for subsequent campaigns, and the reputation of the ruler suffered, though the long-term political effects belong to Chapter 80. For the Portuguese, the victory was a reprieve, not a solution: the same logistical and diplomatic pressures remained, and the city's reliance on relief fleets and regional supplies was unchanged.

Section 70.19

The Dutch–Johor Conquest of Melaka, 1640–1641

In August 1640 a Dutch fleet and army, supported by forces of Johor, began the final siege. The Dutch had learned from earlier failures that a direct assault on the fortress was unlikely to succeed and that a prolonged siege backed by naval blockade offered better prospects. Johor, under its ruler Abdul Jalil Shah III, provided land forces, supplies, boats, and local knowledge, and its participation reflected an alliance rooted in the arrangements of 1606 and in the continuing aim of removing the Portuguese. The siege was directed from Batavia under Governor-General Antonio van Diemen. Dutch field command changed repeatedly during the siege because of illness and deaths; Minne Willemsz Caertekoe assumed final command only in January 1641, with Sergeant Major Johannes Lamotius directing the decisive assault while Caertekoe was ill.

The siege lasted roughly five months. The Dutch and Johor forces bombarded the walls and cut the city's supply by sea and land. Portuguese defenders faced shortages of food and increasing disease. Contemporary and later accounts report severe suffering, including deaths from hunger and illness among inhabitants as well as soldiers, but casualty estimates vary and are often drawn from partisan sources. The defenders' hopes depended on relief from Goa, which did not arrive in time. While the siege was under way, political events in Europe, the restoration of Portuguese independence from Spanish rule at the end of 1640, altered the diplomatic landscape, but the news and its effects did not reach the besiegers or defenders in time to change the outcome.

On 14 January 1641 the Portuguese commander, Manuel de Sousa Coutinho, surrendered after Dutch troops stormed the defenses, and the Company took possession. The broader siege must be attributed to Dutch–Johor cooperation, not to Dutch efforts in isolation: Johor contributed supplies, maritime intelligence, and allied pressure, and its rulers expected recognition. Dutch reports nevertheless describe the decisive 14 January storming as undertaken primarily by Dutch troops and sailors, rather than by a mixed assault column. The surrender transferred a fortified city but did not give the Dutch complete political control of the Strait. Johor, Aceh, and other powers retained their authority, and the Dutch soon found themselves in disputes with Johor over the interpretation of earlier agreements and the division of benefits. Many inhabitants, including Luso-Asian families, remained in Melaka under the new regime, and the city continued as a smaller port within the Company's network, increasingly overshadowed by Batavia.

The terms of surrender and the treatment of the inhabitants are reported variously, and students should be cautious about both triumphant Dutch narratives and Portuguese laments. The sources agree that the city was in dire condition, that many residents had died or fled, and that the new rulers took control of the fortress and administration. Some Portuguese and Luso-Asian residents left; others remained and became part of the city's Dutch-era population. The conquest also reshaped commerce: the Dutch Company, with its main base at Batavia, treated Melaka partly as a strategic outpost and partly as a trading station, and its ability to dominate the surrounding waters remained limited.

Johor's expectations were central to what followed. Having contributed forces and supplies, its rulers anticipated a share of the benefits and recognition of their role, in line with the earlier agreements as they understood them. Disputes with the Dutch over such expectations show that the alliance had been one of convenience with divergent aims. The result was not Dutch regional supremacy but a new arrangement in which the Company, Johor, Aceh, and other powers continued to bargain over trade and authority.

Section 70.20

Portuguese Melaka and the Limits of Maritime Empire: Historical Synthesis

The history of Portuguese Melaka shows how a European power could establish and maintain a fortified commercial center in the heart of a long-established Asian maritime world without exercising sovereignty over that world. The Portuguese possessed Melaka by force. They participated in its commerce as a crown, as officials, and as private traders. But they did not control the Strait, and they never replaced the Asian merchants, brokers, laborers, and food suppliers on whom the city depended. Possession, participation, and sovereignty remained distinct throughout.

Portuguese resilience can be explained by identifiable factors: the fortress and its artillery, naval capacity and relief from Goa, the continued commercial vitality of the port, local intermediaries, and diplomatic flexibility. Vulnerability had equally identifiable causes: a small European population, dependence on outside food and materials, high costs, rival ports and routes, and the persistent political ambitions of Johor and Aceh. The Dutch conquest of 1641 resulted from the combination of a new, well-resourced rival, an indigenous ally whose forces and local knowledge were essential, blockade and supply failure, and the exhaustion of Portuguese resources.

The history also shows the agency of Southeast Asian polities. Johor reconstituted Melakan authority and negotiated with European powers on its own terms. Aceh built a regional power and repeatedly challenged the Portuguese. The Orang Laut, merchants, brokers, and households shaped outcomes through their choices. And the end of Portuguese rule did not end the contest for the Strait, which continued under Dutch, Johor, Acehnese, and later other powers.

Portuguese Melaka should also be placed within the wider Estado da Índia, whose fortified ports at Goa, Hormuz, Colombo, Macao, and elsewhere shared its basic character as enclaves dependent on local cooperation and seaborne supply. Historians have debated whether such networks should be understood primarily as instruments of royal power or as collections of private and official interests operating in a largely Asian commercial world. The evidence from Melaka supports a mixed view: the Crown's presence mattered, but so did the activity of private traders, local intermediaries, and neighboring rulers. The history of Melaka thus contributes to a broader reassessment of early modern European empires in Asia as limited, negotiated, and contested.

Historical Synthesis

What Portuguese Melaka Reveals

  • Administration: A European command structure dependent on Asian offices, skills, and cooperation.
  • Commerce: A transformed but continuing Asian trade, with rivals able to redirect shipping.
  • Society: A fortified core and a much larger Asian urban population, with growing Luso-Asian households.
  • Resistance: Johor and Aceh pursued their own ambitions and also fought each other.
  • War: Tactical defense succeeded repeatedly; strategic control was never achieved.
  • 1641: A Dutch–Johor victory that changed the city's ruler but not the contested nature of the Strait.

Concluding interpretation: Portuguese Melaka endured from 1511 to 1641 as a fortified center of commerce, administration, and cultural interaction, but its rulers never succeeded in monopolizing the Strait or eliminating competing Malay and Sumatran political authority. Johor and Aceh maintained their own regional ambitions, merchants redirected commerce, and Portuguese power remained dependent on Asian labor, food supplies, markets, and diplomacy. The Dutch–Johor conquest of 1641 ended Portuguese possession of Melaka, but it did not end the struggle over the Strait, which remained a contested maritime world.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Portuguese Melaka survived from 1511 to 1641 as a fortified commercial and administrative center while facing continuous military, diplomatic, and economic competition from Johor, Aceh, and other regional powers. Its central argument is that fortification, naval capacity, Asian commercial participation, and opportunistic diplomacy allowed the Portuguese to endure, while the impossibility of eliminating rival ports, alternative routes, competing rulers, and dependence on outside supplies prevented them from mastering the Strait.

The chapter began by distinguishing military possession, commercial participation, and sovereignty. The Portuguese held a fortress and its immediate surroundings, took part in commerce as a crown and as private traders, and never exercised sovereignty over the Strait. Holding these three phenomena apart prevents the common error of equating a European-held port with European control of a maritime region.

Division I reconstructed Portuguese Melaka as a functioning society. Evidence from Portuguese correspondence and chronicles, merchant and missionary writings, Malay and Acehnese traditions, Dutch records, maps, and archaeology allows a partial reconstruction, with significant silences about the lives of ordinary residents. The fortress, begun in 1511 and elaborated later, formed the administrative core under a captain, a factor, and other officials responsible to Goa and Lisbon, whose orders depended on local brokers, merchants, and neighbors. Trade was transformed rather than destroyed: Gujarati, Tamil, Chinese, Javanese, Malay, and other communities remained, though some moved to rival ports. Everyday life unfolded largely outside the walls, among households, laborers, enslaved people, and mixed families.

Division II introduced the competing centers. Sultan Mahmud Shah's court withdrew to Bintan and then Sumatra, and after the destruction of Bintan in 1526 and the sultan's death in 1528 the dynasty continued through Johor, with another branch in Perak. Johor was a successor polity, not a Portuguese creation. Aceh, a trading and military state built on pepper and Muslim commerce, conquered Pasai and challenged Melaka. Rival ports, including Patani, Banten, and others, and alternative routes limited the Portuguese ability to monopolize shipping. The three-way balance was fluid, with shifting alliances, overlapping claims, and the essential participation of the Orang Laut.

Division III examined war. Early campaigns against Bintan and Johor and repeated Acehnese threats showed that the Portuguese could strike but not eliminate opponents. The Acehnese attack on Johor in 1564 revealed rivalry between the two Muslim powers, and Portuguese expeditions against Johor continued into the late sixteenth century, including the destruction of Johor Lama in 1587. The siege of 1568, documented by a Portuguese pictorial source, tested the fortress, which held. The military balance was not a contest between European firearms and unprepared Asian armies: Southeast Asian courts used artillery, galleys, and fortifications, and outcomes turned on supply, relief, disease, and alliances. Tactical success did not produce strategic control.

Division IV showed that commerce, religion, and diplomacy continued during conflict. Portuguese commercial policy, including customs duties, royal and private trade, and the cartaz system, claimed authority that enforcement could not match. Melaka was a hub of Christian institutions and missionary travel, but remained religiously plural, and Luso-Asian society emerged through mixed households whose identities were fluid. The Dutch arrival and the Johor–Dutch agreement of 17 May 1606 demonstrated Johor's active diplomacy; the 1606 attack on Melaka failed, and disagreements over the agreement's terms emerged. Daily dependence on regional rice, timber, labor, credit, and negotiation underlay Portuguese survival.

Division V explained the end. The seventeenth century brought Dutch competition tied to the war with the Habsburg crowns, Dutch blockades, insecure supply, rising costs, and the strain of defending a distant port. The great Acehnese expedition of 1629 failed, demonstrating Portuguese defensive resilience but not long-term security. In 1640 and 1641 a Dutch–Johor siege, relying on blockade and supply cutoff, ended with the Portuguese surrender on 14 January 1641. Comparison of the two sieges shows that outcomes depended on logistics, relief, coordination, and local alliance rather than on simple military superiority.

Throughout, the chapter emphasized agency. Johor reconstituted Melakan authority; Aceh built a regional power; Orang Laut communities, merchants, brokers, laborers, and households shaped events through their choices. The sources are uneven, and the chapter has attributed reported fleet sizes and casualties to their sources rather than accepting them as fact.

The chapter's concluding interpretation is that Portuguese Melaka endured as a fortified center of commerce, administration, and cultural interaction but never monopolized the Strait or eliminated competing Malay and Sumatran authority. The Dutch–Johor conquest of 1641 ended Portuguese possession of the city but not the contest for the Strait. That contest is taken up in the chapters on Ternate and Tidore (Chapter 71), the Dutch East India Company (Chapter 77), Aceh under Iskandar Muda (Chapter 80), and the Johor-Riau world (Chapter 81).

Chapter Reflection

Portuguese Melaka matters because it corrects a familiar picture. A fortress taken in 1511 is often imagined as the beginning of European mastery over Asian seas. The history of the following 130 years shows something more complicated: a European-held port that survived because Asian merchants, laborers, brokers, and neighboring rulers made it worth using, and that never converted possession into control of the waters around it.

The chapter also reveals processes that reach beyond Melaka. The dynastic continuity of the Melakan line through Bintan, Kampar, and Johor shows how Southeast Asian political authority could survive the loss of a capital. The rise of Aceh shows how new commercial and military powers formed in response to, and independently of, European intrusion. The Orang Laut illustrate that maritime mobility and knowledge were political resources. And the Dutch–Johor conquest shows that European and Southeast Asian actors made alliances for their own purposes, each seeking advantage from the other.

These developments shaped what followed. The Dutch East India Company, whose formation Chapter 77 examines, inherited a city but not the Strait, and its relations with Johor, Aceh, and later Bugis and other communities continued the pattern of negotiation and rivalry. The long history of Johor-Riau (Chapter 81) and Aceh's seventeenth-century power (Chapter 80) cannot be understood without the experience of resisting and bargaining with Portuguese Melaka. Meanwhile the Luso-Asian communities, the Catholic institutions, and the commercial memory of the port persisted in altered form.

For the larger textbook, the chapter reinforces a principle stated in its opening: Southeast Asian societies were historical actors that selected, transformed, resisted, and transmitted outside influences. The Strait remained, after 1641 as before, a contested maritime world in which no single power held permanent mastery, and that fact is among the most important continuities in the region's early modern history.
