Chapter Opening

In 1509 a small Portuguese squadron entered Melaka’s roadstead as a trading mission and left with some of its men in captivity. In 1511 a far larger force returned under Afonso de Albuquerque and took the city. The conquest ended the sultan’s rule over his capital, but it did not end the sultanate, and it did not give Portugal command of the Strait.

The two Portuguese arrivals at Melaka, separated by less than two years, framed one of the most consequential events in the early modern history of maritime Southeast Asia. The first, under Diogo Lopes de Sequeira in September 1509, came in the language of commerce and diplomacy. Portuguese ships anchored off a port that had by then been a major commercial center for roughly a century, and for a short time trade appears to have begun. Within weeks the visit had collapsed. Portuguese personnel ashore were seized, the squadron withdrew, and the men left behind became hostages whose letters later shaped Portuguese planning. The second arrival, in July 1511, came as a military expedition with explicit demands and a plan for a permanent fortified base.

This chapter reconstructs how the first encounter became the second, how the campaign of 1511 was fought, and what the fall of the capital meant for the people who lived and traded there. Its governing question is how Albuquerque’s expedition captured one of Southeast Asia’s most important port cities, and why the loss of Melaka’s capital transformed, but did not terminate, the political and commercial world of the Melaka Sultanate. The question has two halves, and the chapter treats both with equal seriousness: the military and political explanation of a Portuguese success, and the evidence that Malay political authority survived the loss of its principal city.

That second half is easily lost. Popular accounts often compress 1511 into a single dramatic ending, as though the capture of the city extinguished the Malay political order. Specialists, notably Peter Borschberg, emphasize instead that Sultan Mahmud Shah retained followers, bases, and claims after 1511, and that the Melaka polity in a recognizable form persisted until the sultan’s death in 1528. “Conquest of Melaka” in this chapter therefore means the seizure and occupation of the city, not the permanent subjugation of every community that had once acknowledged the sultan.

The chapter also resists the opposite simplification, in which Melaka is imagined as a rich but defenseless emporium that fell to superior European weapons. Melaka’s rulers possessed firearms, artillery, war vessels, elephants, and a long familiarity with maritime warfare, and the Portuguese needed tactical improvisation, local intelligence, and Asian auxiliaries to prevail. Merchant communities within the city did not act as a bloc. Gujarati, Tamil, Javanese, Chinese, Malay, and other traders weighed religion, kinship, commercial interest, and survival differently, and their decisions were part of the story.

Historians approach these events through sources that disagree about almost everything except the outcome. Portuguese letters, a documentary corpus assembled and translated by M. J. Pintado, preserve the viewpoint of captives and commanders. Tomé Pires’s Suma Oriental, written in the years immediately after the conquest, describes Melaka’s institutions with unusual detail. The Sejarah Melayu, a Malay dynastic history compiled later, interprets the fall in moral and genealogical terms. Each source has its own purpose, and the chapter treats the reconstruction of 1511 as an exercise in comparing them. Three Primary Source Windows place students inside that comparison, one for a Portuguese captive, one for the conquering command, and one for Malay historical memory.

Within the textbook, Chapter 64 examined the rise and institutions of the sultanate, Chapter 67 surveyed the region around 1500, and Chapter 68 explained how Portugal built its presence in Asian waters. Chapter 69 asks what happened when that expansion confronted an established Southeast Asian sultanate at its principal port. Its closing sections look ahead to the contests over the Strait that Chapter 70 examines, without narrating them.

Historical Context

By 1509 Melaka had been the leading entrepôt of the Strait for most of a century. Chapter 64 traced how a ruling house founded around 1400 built a port on the western shore of the peninsula, entered the Ming tributary system, adopted Islam, and developed a body of officials, harbor masters, and law that regulated an extraordinarily mixed commercial population. The city did not produce the pepper, cloves, nutmeg, textiles, or porcelain that passed through it. Its wealth came from providing a secure place of exchange where monsoon sailing seasons from India, Java, Maluku, and China could be joined, and from the customs and services that the court and its officials collected.

Portugal’s entry into this system is the subject of Chapter 68. Portuguese ships had reached India in 1498, and during the following decade the crown sought to control the sea trade by force, using fortified bases and armed ships. By 1509 the Portuguese had defeated a combined Muslim fleet off Diu in early 1509, though the sources on its consequences are contested, and the crown’s agents were extending their reach eastward. King Manuel I wanted knowledge of Melaka, the port on which the spice trade to the Indian Ocean depended, and the Portuguese viceroy and then governor of India had to decide how that interest would be pursued. The chapter that follows begins where that decision became concrete.

Two cautions shape the reading of everything below. First, the geographic frame of this chapter is the Strait and its connected ports, not the modern map: Melaka’s relationship to the peninsula’s hinterland, to Sumatra across the water, and to the islands to the south was one of overlapping and shifting allegiance, and nothing in the 1511 campaign should be read as a conflict between modern states. Second, the religious vocabulary of the sources, Christian and Muslim, “Moor” and “gentile,” was real but also instrumental. Portuguese commanders wrote to a crown that valued crusading rhetoric, and Malay chroniclers wrote within a court tradition that shaped how kingship, loyalty, and misfortune could be described. Chapter 69 treats religious antagonism as one factor operating alongside commerce, court politics, and military calculation.

Historical Vocabulary
Shahbandar

Definition. Harbor master and chief officer for a merchant community, an office found in many Indian Ocean and Southeast Asian ports. Historical context. Tomé Pires describes four shahbandars at Melaka, each responsible for a group of merchants from a different part of the maritime world: Gujarat, South Asia east of Gujarat together with Bengal and Pegu, the Indonesian archipelago, and the Chinese and Ryukyuan trade. Why it matters. The shahbandar managed disputes, assessed duties, and linked the court to the traders. Potential misunderstanding. Reading the office as a simple customs collector overlooks its role as a communal representative, which is why the loyalties of shahbandars and their constituents mattered in 1511.

Historical Vocabulary
Bendahara

Definition. The senior minister of the Melaka court, in Malay tradition a chief of the officials who exercised wide authority under the ruler. Historical context. The office is associated with powerful families and with the management of court politics and succession; Tun Mutahir held it in the years before the conquest. Why it matters. The fate of a bendahara could change the balance among court factions and merchant groups. Potential misunderstanding. The title should not be translated as “prime minister” without qualification, since its powers, and the lines between it and other offices such as the temenggung and the laksamana, are reconstructed from varying sources.

Section 69.1

Reconstructing the Portuguese Conquest of Melaka

The conquest of Melaka is unusually well documented for a sixteenth-century Southeast Asian event, but the documentation comes from a narrow range of vantage points. The earliest and most direct evidence is Portuguese: letters written by commanders, officials, and captives, many of them gathered and translated in M. J. Pintado’s Portuguese Documents on Malacca from 1509 to 1511. These are contemporary, which gives them real value, but they were written to superiors who controlled careers and royal favor. A commander describing his own campaign had reasons to emphasize obstacles overcome, enemy treachery, and the legitimacy of his decisions, and a captive writing from Melaka had reasons to advise the course that might free him.

A second body of Portuguese writing is retrospective. The Commentaries of Afonso de Albuquerque were compiled by his son, who drew on his father’s papers and on other accounts and published the work decades after the events. The narrative is vivid and detailed, and it is the source of many of the best-known episodes, but it is a later literary reconstruction and not the governor’s own voice. Modern editors have worked to distinguish Albuquerque’s letters from the Commentaries, and this chapter does likewise. A third Portuguese source stands somewhat apart: Tomé Pires wrote his Suma Oriental in the years after the conquest, partly on the basis of residence in Melaka, and it records harbor offices, merchant communities, and commodities that no battle narrative would mention.

The Malay perspective is preserved mainly in the Sejarah Melayu, often translated as the Malay Annals. It exists in several recensions, and the surviving manuscripts are later than the events; one version is traditionally associated with a compilation ordered at the court of Johor in 1612. Its concerns are dynastic, ethical, and genealogical, and it treats the fall as part of the history of a royal line that continued. Chinese records and other regional sources illuminate Melaka’s diplomatic position but say little about the fighting. Archaeology adds the physical dimension: the layers of later Portuguese, Dutch, and British building have survived better than the early sixteenth-century city, so material evidence must be read with care.

A further methodological problem is that different witnesses used the same word, Melaka, for different things. A Portuguese commander might mean the town and its waterfront; an Indian merchant might mean the market and its customs regime; a Malay court text might mean a royal center embedded in a much larger network of loyalties. A claim that Melaka had been conquered therefore requires an object: the bridge, the built-up port, the ruler's residence, or the political relationships extending beyond the town. The chapter makes those objects explicit rather than treating the conquest as a single instant that changed everything equally.

Reconstructing the battle therefore means comparing partial and partisan accounts. Dates differ between sources and are sometimes conflated with later commemorations. Numbers of troops, ships, and cannon were reported by chroniclers with no means of counting and with narrative reasons to enlarge the odds. Place names and the layout of the city are described by people who knew parts of it from the water or from captivity. This chapter adopts a consistent rule: it states what multiple independent sources support, attributes figures to their authors, and marks traditional dates as traditional.

Section 69.2

Melaka’s Geography, Harbor, River, and Urban Defenses

Melaka stood on the western coast of the Malay Peninsula, at a point where the Strait is narrow enough to be crossed in a short sea passage from Sumatra and where the monsoon winds of the Bay of Bengal and the South China Sea could be joined. The city had no deep natural harbor. Ships rode at anchor in an open roadstead and cargoes moved by lighter to the shore, so the port’s security depended on the authority of the court and on the control of the river mouth and waterfront rather than on a sheltered basin. The sources describe a long strip of settlement along the coast, extending inland on both banks of a river that ran through the city to the sea.

The river and its bridge are central to every account of the fighting. The river divided the city into two parts, and the crossing connected them. Portuguese descriptions imply that important institutions, including royal and religious buildings, stood close to the river crossing, though the exact placement of individual structures is reconstructed from imperfect testimony and should not be treated as precise. For an attacker, the bridge was both a bottleneck and a prize: whoever held it could divide the defenders, move men between sections of the city, and cut off retreat. For a defender, the crossing could be blocked, fortified, and swept with fire. The chapter returns to this geography in the narration of the assaults.

The commercial city was organized by merchant community. Pires describes the four harbor offices mentioned in the vocabulary box above, and the sources suggest that foreign traders lived in quarters associated with their origin, with warehouses and dwellings near the waterfront. Ordinary residents, including Malay households, craft workers, boatmen, and the Orang Laut who provided maritime labor and local knowledge, lived in a mix of wood-and-thatch dwellings that burned easily, an important fact in a city fought over with fire and artillery. The sultan’s court stood within the city, and the sultanate’s relationship with the surrounding peninsula and nearby islands, including Muar to the south and the Riau–Lingga region, gave the ruler places of retreat.

An urban battle on this shore differed from a naval engagement in the open Strait. Portuguese cannon could cover approaches from the roadstead, but troops had to move through streets where buildings and fences blocked lines of fire. Boats carrying men toward the shore were vulnerable during the moment of landing, and larger ships could not simply sail through shallow approaches to support every unit. Defenders who understood the channels could concentrate their strength on a few landing places, while attackers sought ways to hold a crossing once gained. The bridge mattered not because control of it magically transferred sovereignty but because it joined the spaces in which the expedition had to operate.

Environmental factors constrained both sides without determining the outcome. The monsoon calendar governed the arrival of merchant fleets and the sailing of ships back toward India, so a force that arrived late in the season risked being unable to return home before the winds changed. Tidal conditions in the river affected the movement of boats. The coastal swamp and the surrounding forest limited maneuver, which made the few routes through and around the city, and the river crossing above all, central to military planning.

Section 69.3

Diogo Lopes de Sequeira and the Breakdown of Relations, 1509

Diogo Lopes de Sequeira’s expedition was sent by King Manuel I to learn about Melaka and to establish relations with its ruler. It reached the port in September 1509 with a small squadron. The sources agree that Sequeira’s arrival was at first received with ceremony and that trade began: Portuguese agents were allowed to buy goods, and an exchange of messages between the ships and the court opened. The Bendahara is associated in later accounts with the negotiations, and a Portuguese factor, Ruy de Araújo, was among those who went ashore to arrange commerce.

What happened next is documented in outline and disputed in motive. Portuguese reports describe a plot against the expedition, in which Melakan authorities, urged on by Muslim merchants alarmed at the arrival of a competitor, sought to seize the ships or their men. In the sequence the Portuguese sources give, boats ashore were attacked, a number of Portuguese were captured or killed, and the ships, warned in time, withdrew without recovering the prisoners. The size of the Portuguese party seized is reported variously. Francisco Serrão, who later reached Maluku and whose career is traced in Chapter 71, took part in the expedition and is among those named in accounts of the escape.

The Portuguese explanation, that a hostile Muslim merchant faction organized a conspiracy, should be treated as an interpretation. It reflects a Portuguese vocabulary in which Muslim traders in the Indian Ocean were presumed enemies, and it served a purpose in the letters: it justified retaliation. The documents also leave open other explanations. Melakan rulers may have judged that the Portuguese, who were arriving with armed ships and had a reputation, among Indian Ocean merchants, for attacking Muslim shipping, posed a threat to the port’s commerce and security. Merchants with interests in the existing trade may have sought to prevent the establishment of a rival. The court may have been divided. Contemporary testimony does not establish the private intentions of the sultan or of every merchant accused in the reports, and a careful reconstruction records the events of the seizure while keeping the question of motive open.

The breakdown is better understood as a collision of expectations than as proof that one community acted with a single intention. The Portuguese sought recognition as royal agents and commercial partners, yet the same fleet represented a monarchy that had used force against shipping in the western Indian Ocean. Melaka's government had to protect its position as an intermediary among many trading communities. Admitting one new armed entrant on privileged terms could threaten arrangements that brought the court customs revenue and political support. The sources do not allow historians to reconstruct a unified council decision in the palace, still less the private reasoning of every trader.

The affair mattered for what it left behind. The captives became a source of intelligence for the Portuguese and, in their own correspondence, advocates of a military response. The squadron returned to India with news of failure, and the fate of the men at Melaka entered the calculations of the governor who would soon take up his post.

Section 69.4

From the Failed Mission to Armed Confrontation, 1509–1511

Between Sequeira’s withdrawal and Albuquerque’s departure from Goa lay roughly a year and a half of decisions in which war was only one possibility. Albuquerque became governor of Portuguese India in late 1509, and his first priorities were in India itself. He attacked Goa in early 1510, lost it within months, and retook it in November 1510. Only after securing Goa as a base did he turn east. The sequence matters: the Melaka expedition was launched from a position of newly won strength, and it formed part of a strategy of controlling nodes of Indian Ocean commerce through fortified bases, a strategy traced in Chapter 68.

The Portuguese had alternatives. They might have sought a renewed diplomatic arrangement with Melaka, pressed for the release of the captives by negotiation, or redirected attention to other ports on the Strait. Albuquerque appears to have chosen force for strategic reasons that his own letters make clear: the captives’ situation provided a pretext, the intelligence he held suggested the city could be taken, and a fortified base at Melaka would serve both the crown’s commercial ambitions and its competition with Muslim merchant networks. The choice of war was a decision made by a particular commander with particular aims, not a necessary result of the 1509 failure.

The years 1509 to 1511 also altered the diplomatic language of the confrontation. What began as a dispute over permission to trade became a demand for restitution, submission to negotiated terms, and security for a Portuguese establishment. The surviving Portuguese narrative tended to place the captives at its moral center, while the larger plan for a permanent base appears in the governor's strategic correspondence and later actions. Treating the rescue as a sufficient explanation would leave unexplained why occupying and fortifying the port followed the release of prisoners. A historian must place stated justifications beside the institutional outcomes that commanders pursued.

Developments in Melaka also shaped the road to confrontation. The court faced internal tensions, discussed in the next section, and the sultan and his officials had to decide how to respond to the possibility of a Portuguese return. The sources suggest that Melaka made defensive preparations after 1509, though their extent is known mainly through Portuguese reports of what they found in 1511. The Portuguese demanded the release of the captives and, as the situation developed, additional terms, which are examined in Section 69.9.

Division II — The Sultanate, Its Communities, and Military Preparations

Section 69.5

Sultan Mahmud Shah, Court Politics, and the Crisis of Royal Authority

Sultan Mahmud Shah had ruled Melaka since 1488, and by 1511 his reign had lasted over two decades. The sources portray a court of considerable complexity: a royal household with its own interests, ministers drawn from powerful families, officials whose offices were distinct from the harbor posts held by merchants, and relatives with claims on succession. Portuguese accounts also refer to the sultan’s son, Ahmad, as active in the defense, though how authority was shared between father and son in 1511 is not clear from the sources.

The execution of Bendahara Tun Mutahir is the best-known episode of court politics before the conquest, and also one of the least certain. The Sejarah Melayu places the Bendahara’s fall in the years just before the Portuguese attack and attributes it to the slander of court rivals, including officials of South Indian background, whose accusations persuaded the sultan to order the death of a powerful minister and members of his family. Portuguese sources refer to the killing of a senior minister at Melaka but differ in detail and emphasis. The precise date, the accusers, and the sultan’s reasons are not securely established, and Malay tradition may reflect a later moral reading of the episode as a royal misjudgment that weakened the realm.

The episode nevertheless points to genuine structural pressures. A port polity governed through a small number of senior offices depended on balance among them. When a senior minister was removed, the families and clients attached to him lost position, and rivals gained it. The consequences for the defense can be inferred but not measured: it is plausible that the killing damaged confidence in the court and the loyalty of the Bendahara’s allies, and equally plausible that rivals closed ranks when the Portuguese returned. Historians therefore treat the court crisis as a cause of vulnerability whose weight remains debated.

The ministerial crisis should not be simplified into a story in which the execution of a single official caused a military defeat. In courtly historical writing, the unjust punishment of a trusted minister can serve as a moral explanation for subsequent catastrophe. In Portuguese accounts it may serve a different purpose, depicting a divided enemy whose instability enabled conquest. Both interpretive purposes are historically significant, but neither allows a modern reader to quantify the precise military effect of Tun Mutahir's removal. What can be argued more securely is that succession, access to royal favor, and disputes over merchant patronage mattered in a port where political office and commercial revenue were intertwined.

The sultan’s own authority had institutional foundations that outlasted the crisis. His position was supported by genealogy, by the religious legitimacy of Islamic kingship, by the fiscal income of the port, and by ties of allegiance and marriage across the peninsula and Sumatra. These foundations help explain why the sultan could survive the loss of the city.

Section 69.6

Merchants, Political Alliances, and Competing Interests within Melaka

Melaka’s commercial population was among the most diverse in the early sixteenth-century world. Gujarati merchants, who carried Indian textiles eastward and returned with spices, formed the largest and most influential foreign group in Pires’s account. Traders from the Coromandel coast and other parts of South India, often called Kling in Malay usage, included Hindu and Muslim merchants with their own networks and officials. Javanese traders brought rice and supplied connections to the spice islands. Chinese merchants arrived on junks and maintained a presence in the port, and merchants from Bengal, Pegu, and other places added to the mix. Malay traders and the officials of the court had their own stake, as did the laborers, boatmen, and Orang Laut who made the port function.

It would be a serious mistake to treat these communities as political blocs. The Portuguese sources often speak of a Muslim merchant opposition, and religion mattered: Muslim merchants of the Indian Ocean had reason to fear a power that attacked their shipping. But religious identity did not mechanically determine loyalty. A Gujarati merchant might see the Portuguese as a threat to a trade that had lasted generations, while a Hindu Tamil merchant, unconstrained by the same religious alarm, might see an opportunity to negotiate. Javanese traders had their own relations with the sultan and with Portuguese rivals. Chinese captains present in the harbor appear in Portuguese accounts as potential allies who were treated with courtesy, though the details of their role are known through Portuguese reports.

Individual merchants and intermediaries pursued their own strategies. Nina Chatu, a merchant of South Indian origin, emerges in the sources as a key intermediary who was involved in negotiating the release of captives and who later played an administrative role under the Portuguese. His choices illustrate the range of options: resist, negotiate, collaborate, withdraw, or hedge. Many merchants’ decisions were driven by calculation of risk and profit and by the need to protect goods, families, and credit.

A merchant's choices were conditioned by assets that could not easily be moved. Cargo stored in a warehouse, credit advanced to a trading partner, property purchased near a landing place, and family members resident in the town all increased the cost of abandoning Melaka. Other merchants held ships ready to sail and could shift their business to a rival port. The result was not a division between loyal local people and disloyal foreigners. Residents who had traded there for generations might seek accommodation; visitors might support the sultan because they feared Portuguese regulation of the routes on which their profits depended. Neither language nor religion alone predicted a political response.

The commercial interests at stake also extended beyond the port. Merchants who had invested in cargoes had to weigh the cost of a prolonged siege against the possibility that a Portuguese victory would redirect trade. Some sought ways to shift their business to other ports, and the sources hint at movement of merchants and ships once the fighting began. The decisions merchants made in 1511 helped shape the commercial geography of the Strait for the following decades.

Section 69.7

Albuquerque’s Expedition: Fleet, Soldiers, Intelligence, and Logistics

Albuquerque’s fleet left Goa in mid-April 1511. Portuguese sources describe a force of roughly eighteen or nineteen vessels, with about eight hundred to nine hundred Portuguese and several hundred auxiliaries from South Asia, though the figures vary by author and no independent count exists. The ships included large carracks and smaller vessels, and the force carried artillery and the supplies for a campaign in which the Portuguese would have no friendly base nearby. The expedition thus depended on shipborne firepower and on the discipline of men fighting far from reinforcement.

The composition of the force reflects the character of Portuguese Asia. The Portuguese were few, and the expedition relied on Asian personnel, sailors, laborers, interpreters, and fighting men whose roles are often obscure in Portuguese narratives. The use of auxiliaries meant that the force was not simply European, and that its success rested partly on the labor and skill of people the sources name only in passing.

Information moved through commercial as well as military networks. Portuguese commanders learned from captives, sympathetic merchants, sailors who had visited the port, and local residents whose reasons for cooperation differed. Intelligence could identify a crossing or a defensive concentration; it could not guarantee that promised assistance would arrive or that a faction would change sides. Albuquerque also had to plan for a result beyond capture. A permanent base required powder, builders, food imports, a secure anchorage, a garrison, and officials capable of working with an existing Asian commercial system. The campaign thus combined an assault plan with an uncertain occupation plan.

Intelligence was a significant advantage. The Portuguese had accounts from Sequeira’s expedition and from the captives, who had described the city and its leaders, and they could use interpreters and local informants. Such information was imperfect but valuable, particularly in planning an assault on a city divided by a river. Logistics posed a different set of problems. The voyage depended on the monsoon winds, the fleet needed water and food, and ammunition and powder had to last through a campaign of uncertain length. En route, the fleet encountered merchant shipping on the Strait, and Portuguese accounts record encounters with ships that Albuquerque treated as hostile, an indication of the aggressive posture the expedition adopted before it reached Melaka.

Section 69.8

Melaka’s Defenders: Artillery, War Elephants, Vessels, and Urban Fortifications

The Portuguese did not face an undefended city. Melaka possessed firearms and cannon, including light pieces of the kind used widely in Southeast Asian warfare, and the city’s rulers had access to the arms trade of the Indian Ocean. The sources record artillery in the defense, and military historians such as Michael Charney have documented the use of gunpowder weapons and the tactical adaptations made by both sides during the campaign. The Melakans were not unfamiliar with firearms, and any suggestion that they were militarily primitive misreads the evidence.

The defenders also deployed war elephants, vessels, and prepared positions. Portuguese accounts describe barricades and obstacles, including spiked devices placed in streets to injure advancing troops, and the sultan’s elephants appear in the narratives of the fighting. The sultan’s forces drew on retainers of the court, on contingents provided by allies, and on soldiers recruited from the city’s population. Merchants’ ships in the harbor were a potential resource, though how they were used is uncertain.

Numbers are the weakest part of the evidence. Portuguese chroniclers give figures for the defenders, and for the artillery captured after the city fell, that run to thousands of pieces and tens of thousands of men. Such figures reflect the narrative aim of magnifying the victory and the difficulty of counting a force moving through a built-up area. A cautious reading accepts that Melaka’s forces substantially outnumbered the Portuguese and possessed considerable artillery, but cannot give reliable totals.

Describing Melaka's military system requires attention to the different tasks performed by its weapons. A cannon behind a barrier could disrupt an approach or defend a landing site; lighter pieces might be moved as fighting shifted. Firearms in the hands of defenders increased danger in streets and near the bridge, while larger guns were more difficult to move and resupply. War elephants could intimidate troops in an open space but were less predictable amid smoke, gunfire, and confined streets. An inventory of weapons, even if complete, would not reveal how well they were placed, served, or coordinated on a particular day.

The forces on which a port ruler relied were similarly heterogeneous. Court retainers, territorial followers, seaborne fighters, and contingents associated with neighboring centers had different chains of obligation. Later Portuguese authors frequently presented large figures for the defenders, but no neutral muster roll survives that would establish a reliable total. It is more useful to ask where resistance was effective: at obstacles near the crossing, at landing points, and along approaches where attackers could be exposed to concentrated fire. The defenders' eventual defeat should not erase evidence of sustained preparation and tactical learning.

The defense had constraints. A city of wood and thatch was vulnerable to fire. The defenders’ cohesion was affected by the court’s internal tensions and the varied loyalties of the city’s residents. And although Melaka had ships and cannon, its leaders had little experience of the kind of amphibious assault supported by heavy naval artillery that the Portuguese brought. The campaign tested these capabilities against each other, and the narrative that follows shows how the balance shifted.

Division III — The Campaign and Capture of Melaka, 1511

Section 69.9

Albuquerque’s Arrival, Demands, and Final Negotiations

Albuquerque’s fleet reached Melaka in early July 1511 and anchored off the city. The Portuguese opened with demands. The first was the release of the captives held since 1509, among them Ruy de Araújo. The sources then describe further demands, including compensation for the losses of 1509 and permission to establish a fortified Portuguese trading post within the city. The last demand was the most consequential: a fortified factory would give the Portuguese a permanent armed presence at the center of the port’s commerce, and the sultan could not easily accept it without surrendering control of his capital’s security.

Negotiations proceeded through intermediaries. Nina Chatu appears in the Portuguese accounts as an envoy between the fleet and the court, and the captives’ release was part of the exchange. The sultan’s responses, as reported by Portuguese writers, combined delay and partial concession. Portuguese writers read the delay as bad faith and an opportunity for the defenders to strengthen their positions. It is equally plausible that the court was divided, and that the sultan sought time to assess the Portuguese force, to gather allies, and to bargain over terms. The released captives gave the Portuguese updated intelligence at the moment negotiations ended.

Negotiation occurred in the shadow of coercion. The Portuguese fleet's presence gave weight to demands for compensation and security; it also made those demands difficult for the Melakan government to interpret as ordinary commercial bargaining. A concession allowing a permanent foreign fort could have altered the balance between the court and resident merchant communities even if no battle followed. From the Portuguese perspective, an agreement without a defensible base might appear too easily reversed when the squadron sailed away. The parties therefore differed not just over particular hostages or gifts but over the political meaning of a continuing Portuguese presence.

The period before the first attack offered both sides reasons to seek delay. A ruler could move valuables, prepare defenses, and learn more about the enemy; a naval commander could gather pilots, examine waterways, and negotiate with those willing to provide information. But the same delay exposed ships to shortages, seasonal constraints, and the possible arrival of additional defenders. Portuguese narratives commonly convert these competing calculations into proof of Melakan deceit or Albuquerque's resolution. The historian should reconstruct the incentives and outcomes separately rather than making the commander's retrospective explanation stand for a fully documented exchange between two courts.

Time pressed on Albuquerque. A fleet that remained at anchor on a hostile coast consumed supplies, and the monsoon calendar set a limit after which return to India became difficult. Portuguese sources imply that Albuquerque felt these constraints and that they influenced his decision to attack rather than prolong talks. The decision was a calculated one: the governor judged that the chance of obtaining a fortress by negotiation was small and that delay carried costs. Whether the sultan could have accepted terms that preserved his authority, and whether the court contemplated doing so, cannot be established from Portuguese reports alone.

Section 69.10

The First Portuguese Assault and the Struggle for the Bridge

The first assault is traditionally dated 25 July 1511, the feast of St James, patron saint invoked by Portuguese soldiers, and the date is repeated in the sources. The Portuguese landed in separate bodies on opposite sides of the river, an arrangement designed to take the city’s two parts and seize the bridge between them. Ship-borne artillery covered the landings, and the troops advanced into streets contested by defenders with firearms, blocking positions, and spiked obstacles.

The fighting concentrated on the bridge. Portuguese accounts describe an advance to the crossing, a struggle for control against sustained resistance, and the use of the sultan’s elephants against the attackers. The bridge was a place where a few men could hold many, and where fire from both banks could be concentrated. The defenders’ resistance, and the heat and confusion of fighting in unfamiliar streets, brought heavy losses to the Portuguese, who found their positions exposed.

The Portuguese withdrew from the most exposed positions and returned to their ships. The sources interpret the withdrawal differently. Portuguese writers present it as a prudent regrouping after seizing a foothold, while the failure to hold the bridge on the first day shows that the city was not taken in a single rush. A reconstruction that confuses the first penetration of the city with its final occupation misreads the campaign: the 25 July assault demonstrated the difficulty of the task and the importance of the bridge, and it was followed by a pause for preparation.

The operational difficulty was not simply reaching the bridge. Separate Portuguese landing groups had to advance through exposed approaches while remaining aware of one another's positions. Control of a crossing is particularly fragile when the streets beyond it remain contested, when defenders can fire from protected positions, and when tired troops cannot carry enough materials to hold an overnight perimeter. The accounts of stockades, artillery, counterattacks, and elephants, though varying in detail, point to a defense that imposed costs on any attempt to turn a temporary penetration into an enduring foothold.

Exact details, including casualties on either side and the order of events within the day, vary between sources and cannot be settled. The sources agree on the central features: separate landings, a bitter struggle for the crossing, effective Melakan resistance, and a Portuguese withdrawal that left the outcome undecided.

Section 69.11

Renewed Assault, Urban Fighting, and the Portuguese Capture of Melaka

The Portuguese regrouped in the days after 25 July, and the second major assault took place in early August, with traditional accounts placing it around 10 August. Portuguese writers describe adaptations after the first failure: a heavier use of the fleet’s artillery, vessels positioned to support the attack on the river and waterfront, and a plan concentrating on the bridge. A large ship placed near the crossing is reported to have served as a platform and a means of bringing fire to bear, though the details differ among accounts.

The attack on the bridge succeeded, and the Portuguese secured it and fortified their position there. The sources describe days of fighting in the streets, with the Portuguese advancing against stockades and the defenders contesting each section. Fire was a hazard to both sides. After the crossing was held, Portuguese forces pushed into the quarters beyond it, and resistance weakened. By the closing days of August, in traditional dating, the sultan had withdrawn and the Portuguese controlled the city. The dates the sources give for the final stages are not uniform, and later commemorative dates should not be treated as identical with the moment effective control was established.

Several factors help explain the shift between the first and second assaults. The Portuguese had learned the ground and the defenders’ methods, and their fleet’s artillery gave them concentrated firepower along the waterfront. The defenders faced the strain of continuous fighting, and the sources hint at the sultan’s loss of confidence as the bridge fell. Asian auxiliaries and the intelligence provided by former captives and intermediaries contributed, though the sources record their role unevenly. The Portuguese also profited from the loyalties of some communities within the city, a point examined in the next sections.

The most distinctive preparation for the renewed assault was the use, in the Portuguese narrative discussed by Michael W. Charney, of a large captured Asian junk. Its height could create a fighting position above some waterside defenses, and troops placed aboard could fire toward the bridge without first forcing the same vulnerable shoreward streets. This was not simply the superiority of a European vessel: the Portuguese adapted a locally built ship to a tactical problem posed by Melaka's geography. Accounts describe the junk grounding before it could reach the intended position, leaving the attackers exposed while they awaited a favorable tide.

According to the later campaign accounts, Melakan defenders then sent burning craft toward the immobilized vessel. The narrative describes repeated attempts to divert those craft, repairs and preparation aboard the junk, and the making of protective equipment and materials for stockades before another assault became possible. Such detail helps explain an interval that a compressed battle story might otherwise leave empty. It also illustrates mutual adaptation: the defenders exploited current, fire, and the vulnerability of a large anchored vessel, while the attackers sought to bring the vessel into a position where height, concentrated fire, and a renewed landing could be combined.

When the water level permitted the junk to advance, Portuguese accounts depict a more concentrated attack supported by troops in smaller boats. The degree to which particular episodes can be dated independently remains uncertain, and the often-repeated early-August dates should be attributed to the accounts that supply them. The decisive change was that the expedition could now try to support and maintain a position beyond the initial river crossing rather than withdrawing at the end of a costly day. This explains success more adequately than a formula that attributes victory to artillery alone, although later writers' heroic details must still be separated from what the sequence actually establishes.

Portuguese celebratory narratives, especially the later Commentaries, describe heroic episodes and the governor’s leadership. These accounts are valuable for their detail but were composed to honor the victory, and they should not be treated as neutral records of the fighting.

Section 69.12

The Withdrawal of the Sultan and the End of Melaka’s Rule over Its Capital

As the Portuguese took the bridge and the quarters beyond it, Sultan Mahmud Shah withdrew from the city with members of his household and followers. Malay tradition names Muar to the south as an early place of refuge, and later chapters of his career take him to other bases. The withdrawal was not a flight into oblivion but a movement of the court to places where it could regroup and where local allegiance could be called upon. The royal household’s departure, along with loyal defenders, meant that the Portuguese entered a city whose ruler had left but not surrendered.

The loss of the capital had severe consequences. The sultan lost the customs and harbor dues that had supported the court, along with the ceremonial and institutional apparatus attached to the city: the royal residence, the harbor offices, the places of ritual, and the symbols of a capital’s authority. A court that had lived on the revenue of a port suddenly lacked its financial foundation. The officials and families tied to the city faced choices between following the sultan, accepting Portuguese rule, or moving to other ports.

Withdrawal was a political decision as well as a battlefield movement. Mahmud Shah had to preserve a household, followers, and the possibility of continued rule; remaining in a burning, increasingly occupied city could expose the royal line to capture. The court's access to people and places outside the urban center made retreat feasible. A city-centered military report tends to treat a sovereign's departure as proof of complete defeat, but a dynasty whose power depended on alliances and mobility could understand survival differently. This does not make the loss of Melaka minor: the port's customs income, stored wealth, ceremonial center, and capacity to gather merchants were extraordinarily difficult to replace.

Historians therefore distinguish the fall of the city from the continuation of the sultan’s political authority. The sultan lost control of his capital in 1511, and the sources show him retaining followers and alliances afterward. His authority, though diminished, extended across parts of the peninsula and the islands, and he continued to resist Portuguese rule until his death in 1528. This is the interpretation Peter Borschberg emphasizes, and it shapes the structure of the chapter: the end of Melaka’s rule over its capital was a decisive loss, but the story of the sultanate did not end in August 1511.

Division IV — Occupation, Urban Transformation, and the Consequences of Conquest

Section 69.13

Violence, Plunder, Displacement, and the Experience of Conquest

The capture of a densely built port was violent. Portuguese accounts describe fighting in the streets, the burning of buildings, and the seizure of goods after the defense collapsed. The sources permitted a period of plunder, and the wealth taken from the city, including goods held by merchants and the treasure of the court, became a principal subject of Portuguese narratives. The sources do not allow reliable counts of the dead, the wounded, or the people displaced, and later figures should not be repeated as established fact.

The conquest did not fall equally on every community. Portuguese writers indicate that Albuquerque sought to protect the property and persons of some groups, particularly those who had cooperated or whose goodwill he wished to secure, and that Muslim merchants who had resisted, along with the sultan’s supporters, were treated as enemies. Such selective protection reflects both Portuguese policy and the need to win intermediaries, and it left different communities in sharply different positions. The reports must be read with caution: they describe Portuguese intentions as the Portuguese presented them, and the practice on the ground was probably less orderly.

Violence was shaped by the city's physical and commercial structure. Goods stored near the river, dwellings built close to warehouses, and congested streets exposed the same communities to military fire, looting, and the disruption of trade. A chronicle might enumerate captured cannon or lavish objects because those items demonstrated the conqueror's success; it tells much less about lost wages, household provisions, debt records, and the work of rebuilding. The social costs of conquest cannot be measured by the value of royal treasure alone. Fire could destroy the working infrastructure of a market even where some wealthy merchants escaped with their cargoes.

The treatment of residents was selective rather than uniform. Portuguese commanders needed merchants to return, pilots to sail, interpreters to negotiate, and artisans to construct defenses. These practical needs could produce safeguards for some groups even as others were exposed to confiscation or violence. Yet Portuguese narratives tend to classify people through religious and political labels that obscure individual conduct. A claim that a category of residents was protected or punished should be treated as an attributed policy or report, not proof that every member experienced the same outcome.

For ordinary residents the experience was one of danger and dislocation. Households lost homes and goods to fire and looting, and some people were killed or enslaved, as was common in sixteenth-century warfare on all sides. Others fled the city, joining the court’s retreat or moving to nearby ports, and the sources hint at movements of population and shipping away from Melaka. The people who remained had to adapt to a new authority, to find ways of continuing their work under Portuguese control, and to negotiate their position with the new rulers.

Everyday Life and Social Consequences

Who Bore the Cost of Conquest?

The loss of a capital meant different things to different residents. Wealthy merchants had ships and capital that could be moved, and some could shift their business to other ports; those who stayed had to bargain for protection. Court families lost income and position and faced the choice between exile with the sultan and accommodation with the Portuguese. Craft workers, boatmen, laborers, and the Orang Laut who served the harbor lost the commerce that gave them work, at least temporarily, and many lived in dwellings that were easily destroyed. Enslaved people, who formed part of the city’s population, were vulnerable to being seized, sold, or moved.

The surviving evidence preserves these experiences mostly in indirect ways: in Portuguese descriptions of plunder and protection, in the movements of merchants recorded by Pires and others, and in later Malay memory. Historians cannot reconstruct individual lives, but they can recognize that the conquest redistributed risk and opportunity along lines of wealth, status, and community.

Section 69.14

Building Portuguese Melaka: Fortress, Streets, and Institutions

Albuquerque’s central aim was a fortified base, and construction began soon after the capture. The fortress that became known as A Famosa was built near the river mouth and beside the hill that rises over the city, and Portuguese accounts describe the reuse of materials from the buildings of the former city, including religious structures and tombs. The destruction and reuse of such materials had symbolic as well as practical weight: the fortress rose on the ruins of the previous order and its stones carried a message about the change of rule.

A fortress required a garrison, supplies, and an administration. The Portuguese had to feed and pay their men, maintain ships, and secure the water and food supply of the city. They established a captaincy, a customs house, and offices for justice and finance, and they reshaped the use of urban space: religious buildings changed hands, the area around the fortress was reorganized, and the social geography of the port was altered. Albuquerque remained in Melaka until around late 1511, overseeing initial fortification and appointing a captain before the return voyage on which Flor de la Mar was wrecked; some later accounts differ on the exact departure date.

A fortress was simultaneously a defensive work and a public claim to permanence. Building walls, securing artillery positions, and provisioning a garrison were urgent tasks, but construction also required access to stone, timber, transport, and skilled or compelled labor. Portuguese sources describe the reuse of materials from earlier structures; historians must ask what was actually dismantled, what later traditions identify, and what can be verified in the archaeological record. A statement about the early fortress should not be inferred directly from an engraving or standing wall produced in a later phase of the colonial town.

The built environment changed because the administration's needs differed from those of the former court. Space was required for commanders, soldiers, storehouses, worship, and the custody of goods collected by officials. The waterfront needed to remain available for commercial shipping even while the fortress was being completed. That combination produced an occupation dependent on cooperation from craftspeople and residents whose allegiance was uncertain. The fortress did not remove the city's exposure to attacks on food supplies or to the movement of merchants elsewhere; it made a small garrison harder to dislodge while creating new costs of maintenance.

The fortress as it stood in 1511 and 1512 was an early work, adapted to the immediate need to hold the city. Later additions, phases of rebuilding, and the changes made under Dutch and British rule altered the site considerably. The Porta de Santiago, the best-known surviving remnant, reflects those later phases, and visitors today should not assume that it reproduces the fortress of 1511. UNESCO’s historical assessment of the site describes both the Portuguese construction and the layered history of later rule.

Material Culture and Historical Archaeology Study

Reading A Famosa as a Layered Site

The surviving remains at Melaka record at least three episodes: Portuguese construction beginning in 1511, later Portuguese rebuilding, and the alterations of subsequent rulers. Archaeologists and historians use written descriptions, early plans, excavated foundations, and the visible gate to distinguish the phases, and uncertainty remains about the original layout. The reuse of materials from the earlier city means that some stones in the fortress may have come from the buildings it replaced, a possibility the sources suggest but physical evidence cannot always confirm.

Section 69.15

Melaka’s Fall in Malay Historical Memory

The Sejarah Melayu gives the fall of Melaka a place in a long account of a royal line, and it interprets the event through concepts of kingship, loyalty, and responsibility that shaped Malay political thought. The work was compiled after the conquest by writers connected to the successor court, and it exists in several recensions. It is not a record of the battle but a reflection on why the capital was lost and what the loss meant for the dynasty.

In the work’s moral framework, the realm’s misfortune is connected to royal conduct, including the killing of a senior minister, and to the failure of those who should have counseled the ruler. The text also preserves the sense that the dynasty continued: the sultan withdraws, the line persists, and the later history it narrates connects the rulers of the successor courts to Melaka’s kings. Memory of the fall thus served the legitimacy of the later rulers, who needed to explain the loss without surrendering the claim to descend from Melaka’s kings.

The Malay account asks different causal questions from a Portuguese victory report. Courtly episodes about the conduct of rulers and ministers can express a conception of justice in which a king's treatment of loyal servants matters to the realm's fortunes. Read in that way, the fall is neither a simple tactical mishap nor a statement that military equipment was irrelevant. It becomes a problem of political order: how rulers should act, what loyalty requires, and how legitimate descent survives the loss of a place. The story is also retrospective, preserved within textual traditions shaped by successor courts that needed to defend their inheritance of Melaka's prestige.

Comparing the Malay and Portuguese accounts reveals different concerns. The Portuguese narratives emphasize strategy, the heroism of the conquerors, and the religious and commercial justifications of the attack. The Malay narrative emphasizes dynastic continuity and moral causation. The two traditions do not simply contradict one another; they answer different questions, and together they show how a single event was woven into the self-understanding of two political communities.

Section 69.16

Portuguese Administration and the Struggle to Retain Commerce

Capturing the port did not guarantee that merchants would keep trading there. The Portuguese needed the commerce that had made Melaka valuable, and Albuquerque’s early policy sought to reassure merchants who would deal with the new regime and to establish structures for collecting duties. Sources report the appointment of officials, the institution of a customs house, and the use of intermediaries such as Nina Chatu, who could connect the Portuguese with the city’s trading communities. Albuquerque is also said to have arranged a coinage for the new administration, and Tomé Pires arrived to manage the crown’s trade in Melaka’s commodities.

The limits of military control were evident from the start. Merchants who disliked Portuguese rule could trade elsewhere, and the Strait offered other ports. Pires’s writing shows a Portuguese official aware of how dependent the city’s prosperity was on the goodwill of trading communities. The Portuguese could seize a harbor, but they could not compel ships to call there, and capturing the city did not confer ownership of the goods that passed through the Strait. The tension between a fortified port that taxed trade and a commerce that could relocate shaped Portuguese policy for the following decades.

At the port level, the conquerors faced a problem of credibility. A merchant who had relied on the sultan's customs officers and arbitration had to decide whether the new officials could protect cargo, settle disputes, and honor promised terms. Introducing a garrison did not automatically recreate the relationships that had drawn ships to Melaka. Portuguese administrators needed experienced intermediaries, including Nina Chatu and surviving commercial specialists, precisely because those relationships rested on knowledge, credit, language, and local reputation that foreigners could not acquire immediately.

Coinage provides a concrete example of administrative action beyond warfare. In a letter dated 1 April 1512, Albuquerque reported that Nuno Vaz was carrying samples of gold, silver, and copper coins struck at Melaka in King Manuel's name. That report supports the claim that the administration attempted to express royal authority through money; it does not by itself determine how widely the issues circulated, their exact denominations, or the date of each specimen preserved by collectors. Royal symbols on a coin could advertise a new regime, but traders still judged currency by weight, metal, and acceptability in particular markets.

The new government likewise required fiscal records and officials to collect dues, supervise warehouses, purchase supplies, and communicate with ships. These administrative capacities developed in the aftermath of the conquest rather than appearing fully formed on the day of victory. Fortification and merchant reassurance could pull in opposite directions: walls signaled security to some traders, while fear of confiscation or forced regulation encouraged others to seek alternative ports. An explanation of Portuguese Melaka must therefore account for cooperation as well as coercion and recognize that formal sovereignty over the waterfront was only a starting point.

Administration was also a matter of security. The garrison was small, the surrounding peninsula remained under Malay influence, and the exiled sultan was close enough to threaten supplies. The Portuguese depended on provisions brought from outside and on the cooperation of intermediaries whose loyalty could change. These conditions framed the immediate aftermath, and Chapter 70 examines how they developed into a sustained struggle for the Strait.

Section 69.17

Sultan Mahmud Shah and the Continuation of Malay Political Authority

After leaving Melaka, Sultan Mahmud Shah did not accept the loss of his capital as final. The sources and later scholarship show a ruler moving among bases in the southern peninsula and the islands, including locations on the mainland to the south of Melaka and the island of Bintan in the Riau archipelago. These places provided shelter, access to the seafaring communities of the Riau–Lingga region, and connections to other Malay and Sumatran rulers. The Orang Laut, whose maritime skills and loyalty had long been resources of the court, remained available to the exiled sultan.

The exiled court retained followers, claims, and legitimacy. It continued to be regarded by many as the rightful sovereign, and it kept up a pressure on Portuguese Melaka through raids and efforts to restrict its supplies and trade. Such attacks, and the Portuguese attempts to suppress them, belong principally to the next chapter. What matters here is that the political entity centered on Mahmud Shah persisted: it kept a ruler, a following, and a claim to authority over parts of the peninsula and its seas.

It is helpful to think of the displaced court as a political network rather than a miniature capital wandering intact from site to site. Mahmud Shah could draw upon relations of marriage, allegiance, shared political language, and access to maritime communities. Those relationships were capable of carrying royal authority across different locations even when the machinery of customs collection at Melaka had been lost. A ruler's standing might endure because communities continued to recognize his lineage, but the practical strength of that standing could change whenever followers moved or allies revised their commitments.

The pattern ended with the sultan’s final years. In the mid-1520s the Portuguese attacked his base at Bintan, and the sultan moved to Kampar in Sumatra, where he died in 1528. His descendants continued the royal line in successor courts, traditions examined in Chapter 81 on Johor-Riau and the Malay successor world. The decisive point for this chapter is that the political life of the sultanate extended nearly two decades beyond the loss of the capital.

Section 69.18

Why Did Melaka Fall? Competing Historical Explanations

Historians offer several explanations for the Portuguese victory, and each captures part of the story. One emphasizes naval warfare and firepower: the Portuguese brought armed ships whose artillery could dominate the waterfront, and the city, lacking a sheltered harbor or a fleet able to contest the sea, could be attacked from the water. A second emphasizes tactics and geography: the campaign turned on the bridge, and the Portuguese adaptation after the first assault, together with their concentration of force at the crossing, proved decisive. A third emphasizes intelligence and local assistance, including information from the captives and cooperation from intermediaries and auxiliaries.

Other explanations look to Melaka itself. The court’s divisions, notably the killing of the Bendahara, may have weakened cohesion, and the varied loyalties of the city’s merchant communities meant that the defense did not command a united population. Contemporary decisions by the sultan and his commanders, whether prudent or mistaken, also shaped the outcome. Finally, contingency mattered: chance events, weather, and the fortunes of individual engagements might have produced a different result.

A technological explanation is strongest when it specifies the tactical setting. Portuguese ordnance and experience in shipboard assault were formidable, yet Melaka also possessed firearms and artillery. Firepower mattered because it could be brought against particular waterfront obstacles, and the attackers had to learn how to use it in concert with landings and a protected position at the bridge. The failed attempt to remain ashore after the first assault is evidence against any claim that weapons alone made victory automatic. An army can enjoy advantages in one domain and still lack the numbers or supplies needed to occupy exposed ground.

An institutional explanation stresses differing incentives among the city's court and commercial communities. Rivalries and mistrust may have weakened cohesion, while the Portuguese could strike bargains with selected intermediaries. But evidence for particular defections is uneven and often filtered through victorious writers, so 'treachery' must not become a universal explanation for defeat. The most productive account asks how political fragmentation, tactical adaptation, geography, and intelligence interacted over a series of choices. It also asks a comparative question: why did victory over an important urban target fail to establish complete control over the wider commercial system? The answer requires attention to the mobility of merchants and the survival of rival courts.

The debate is ultimately about the scale at which success is measured. The Portuguese accomplished the immediate military objective of occupying the capital and established a permanent fortress. Their inability to control every alternative port, every merchant voyage, or the sultan's remaining followers limits a larger claim of conquest. Malay historical memory and Portuguese command reports selected different evidence because they judged outcomes by different standards. A balanced interpretation should hold together those distinct scales instead of declaring either that the conquest transformed everything or that the persistence of regional trade made it unimportant.

A single-cause explanation is inadequate, because each factor depended on the others. Naval artillery was effective because the city lay along the water; the bridge mattered because of the river’s division of the city; intelligence helped because the Portuguese could use it in planning the assault; and political divisions mattered because they influenced how the defenders responded. Moreover, the evidence for each factor comes from sources with their own interests: Portuguese authors emphasized their own skill and the enemy’s discord, and Malay tradition emphasized moral and political failure.

Section 69.19

The Strait of Malacca after 1511: Immediate Changes and Unresolved Conflicts

The fall of Melaka disrupted the Strait’s commercial pattern without ending it. Merchants who disliked Portuguese rule moved their trade to other ports, among them ports on the northern Sumatran coast and elsewhere on the peninsula and in Java, and the sources suggest that some of Melaka’s former traders reappeared in these places. The diversion of trade gave rival ports an opportunity to grow, a process that Chapters 70, 80, and 81 follow in the cases of Johor and Aceh.

The Portuguese pursued further aims from their new base. Within months of the conquest, Albuquerque dispatched missions toward Maluku and to the mainland court of Ayutthaya, extending Portuguese contacts. The Maluku voyage and its consequences are treated in Chapter 71. The conquest also raised questions of diplomacy with other powers, among them the Ming court, which regarded Melaka as a tributary and received complaints from the exiled sultan’s side. The first Portuguese–Ming exchanges were difficult, and the Chinese response should not be portrayed as an immediate and uniform policy caused solely by Melaka’s fall; later chapters trace the developments.

A commercial route is not identical with the city through which it passes at one moment. Traders who knew several markets could redistribute voyages after a political shock, although the process imposed costs and could not happen instantly for every household. Established connections between Sumatra, Java, the peninsula, India, and China provided alternatives to the captured port. Some ships continued to use Melaka; others explored markets where rulers offered favorable customs terms or protection. Consequently, the Portuguese gained an important customs station without monopolizing the exchange systems that had made it attractive.

The political consequences were equally uneven. A neighboring ruler might welcome the weakening of Melaka's court but resist Portuguese demands, while a merchant might use the Portuguese harbor without recognizing broader Portuguese claims. Chinese diplomatic tensions developed over time and should not be narrated as a single uniform reaction immediately following the battle. Nor should the later prominence of Aceh or Johor be read backward as an inevitable answer to 1511. What the conquest demonstrably did was change the terms of competition among ports and royal centers; the specific outcomes of that competition are the historical business of Chapter 70 and the subsequent regional narratives.

The conflicts that remained unresolved shaped the following decades: the exiled sultan’s pressure on Portuguese Melaka, competition among Asian ports for the trade the Portuguese could not fully control, and the Portuguese effort to impose a licensing system on Asian shipping. These belong to Chapter 70 and the chapters that follow. For this chapter, the key point is that the conquest began a contest and did not settle it.

Section 69.20

The Portuguese Conquest of Melaka: A Historical Synthesis

The conquest of Melaka in 1511 was the product of a chosen strategy, a failed diplomatic encounter, a court in crisis, and a hard-fought campaign decided at the river crossing. The Portuguese captured a major port, displaced a sultan from his capital, and began a fortified presence that would last, with changes, for more than a century. These were substantial achievements, and they altered the commercial and political geography of the Strait.

The events from Sequeira's arrival in 1509 to the occupation of 1511 demonstrate that the Portuguese did not simply arrive with sufficient guns and take an undefended prize. They entered a commercial system that already connected Indian textiles, Chinese goods, and Southeast Asian spices through institutions sustained by local rulers and merchants. Their first attempt at formal contact failed, and subsequent commanders combined intelligence from captives, alliances with selected residents, naval technology, and coercive demands. The Melakan court faced decisions about concessions, defensive arrangements, and the survival of the royal household. Those choices were made under pressure, and the contemporary record does not fully recover either side's intentions.

The bridge provides the most revealing tactical illustration. The first Portuguese assault could take ground but could not retain it; the renewed operation relied on adjustment to local conditions, including use of a large Asian junk, tidal timing, and measures against fire attacks. Defenders possessed artillery, vessels, and protected positions, and they adapted as the attackers did. The eventual Portuguese success thus arose from a sequence of tactical and political developments rather than a simple opposition between modern and traditional weapons. It also carried a heavy human cost that surviving elite sources describe only selectively.

The aftermath was a reorganization, not a clean replacement. Occupiers built fortifications, made administrative arrangements, and sought the confidence of merchants whose networks they depended on. Displaced rulers preserved lineages, followers, and political claims even though the city and its revenue institutions had been lost. Some commercial activity was redirected; some continued under the new authorities. The different ways these developments unfolded explain why the Portuguese victory remained historically consequential without granting Portugal mastery of the Strait. Chapter 69 therefore concludes with a dual finding: the capture was a genuine military and institutional turning point, and the region's preexisting political and commercial capacities defined the limits of that turning point.

The conquest also had limits. The Portuguese took a city, not the peninsula or the sea. The sultan retained followers and bases, merchants redirected trade, and neighboring states pursued their own interests. Portuguese Melaka depended on a small garrison, on intermediaries, and on provisions from outside, and its authority over the Strait’s commerce was always partial. At the same time, the Malay court’s survival in exile did not restore the old order: the capital, its revenues, and its institutions were gone, and a new political contest had begun.

The seizure of the port displaced Sultan Mahmud Shah, reorganized the city’s political and commercial institutions, and introduced a durable Portuguese presence into the Strait. Chapter Summary

This chapter has reconstructed the Portuguese conquest of Melaka as a sequence of decisions, encounters, and campaigns rather than a single dramatic event. It began from the evidence: Portuguese letters and captives’ correspondence, a later chronicle narrative, Tomé Pires’s description of the city, and the Malay dynastic memory preserved in the Sejarah Melayu. Each source offers a different position, and the reconstruction of 1511 depends on reading them against one another.

Melaka in 1511 was a commercial port divided by a river, organized around harbor offices that represented distinct merchant communities, and governed by a court whose authority rested on genealogy, religion, and the revenue of the port. The geography of the roadstead, the river, and the bridge shaped the military problem that Albuquerque faced, and the monsoon calendar shaped the time he had to solve it.

The breakdown of relations began with Sequeira’s mission of 1509, which opened with trade and ended with Portuguese personnel seized and the squadron withdrawn. The Portuguese explained the episode as a Muslim merchant conspiracy, an interpretation that reflects Portuguese assumptions and purposes, and the surviving evidence leaves the motives of the sultan and merchants open. The captives became a source of intelligence and advocacy, and Albuquerque, strengthened by his control of Goa, chose force over renewed diplomacy.

On the Melakan side, the court faced tensions highlighted by the execution of the Bendahara, whose date and motives remain debated. The city’s merchant communities, Gujarati, Tamil, Javanese, Chinese, Malay, and others, pursued different strategies shaped by religion, commerce, and risk, and could not be treated as political blocs. The defenders possessed firearms, artillery, ships, elephants, and prepared positions, although figures for their numbers come from Portuguese chroniclers and are unreliable.

The campaign of July and August 1511 turned on the bridge. After demands and negotiation, the first assault on the traditional date of 25 July penetrated the city but did not hold it. A renewed attack in August, with heavier use of ships and artillery and a concentrated effort at the crossing, secured the bridge and the city, and the sultan withdrew. The distinction between the first penetration and the final occupation is essential, as is the distinction between traditional dates and the moment control was established.

The consequences were severe and uneven. Fighting, fire, and plunder devastated parts of the city, some communities were protected for political reasons while others were treated as enemies, and ordinary residents experienced danger and dislocation. The Portuguese built a fortress, later called A Famosa, using materials from the former city, and established a captaincy and customs administration. The surviving remains reflect later phases and should not be read as the fortress of 1511.

Capturing the port did not give Portugal command of the commerce that passed through it, and merchants who disliked Portuguese rule traded elsewhere. Meanwhile Sultan Mahmud Shah retained followers and bases in the southern peninsula and islands and maintained his authority until his death in Sumatra in 1528, as Peter Borschberg emphasizes. Malay tradition, preserved in the Sejarah Melayu, interpreted the fall in moral and dynastic terms that served the legitimacy of the successor court.

Historians explain the Portuguese success through several interacting factors: naval artillery and firepower, tactics and geography at the bridge, intelligence and local assistance, court discord and divided loyalties, and contingency. No single factor suffices. The conquest was a major military and commercial turning point but not a complete transfer of regional power, and it opened a new contest over the Strait that Chapter 70 and later chapters examine.

Chapter Reflection

The conquest of Melaka matters because it joined two histories that had been developing separately: the Portuguese effort to control Indian Ocean commerce by fortified bases, and the long history of a Malay sultanate that organized exchange in the Strait. The meeting produced a violent rupture, but it also revealed how much the outcome depended on geography, intelligence, internal politics, and the choices of merchants and rulers on both sides.

The chapter also shows why precise language matters. “Conquest” described the capture of a city, while the sultanate continued to act, and later Malay courts claimed descent from it. “Fall” described the loss of a capital, not the end of a political tradition. Careful reading of Portuguese and Malay sources shows how each community made sense of the event for its own purposes.

The consequences extended well beyond 1511. The displacement of commerce to other ports helped shape the growth of rival states, the exiled court’s successors helped define the political landscape of the southern peninsula and islands, and the Portuguese presence introduced a fortified, armed form of commerce into the Strait. Chapter 70 follows the struggle for the Strait that these conditions produced, and the chapters that follow trace its transformation of the region.
