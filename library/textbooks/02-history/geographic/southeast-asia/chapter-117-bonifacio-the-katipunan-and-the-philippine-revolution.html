Chapter Opening

Between July 1892 and December 1898 a clandestine association of Manila clerks, artisans, and small traders became an armed movement, an army, and a would-be republic, only to find that the defeat of the old colonial power did not settle who would rule the archipelago.

On the night of July 7, 1892, a small group of men met in a house in the Tondo district of Manila, one day after the colonial authorities arrested José Rizal and before his deportation to Mindanao. Rizal's civic association, La Liga Filipina, which had been founded only days earlier, was effectively finished. The men who gathered that evening, among them a warehouse employee named Andrés Bonifacio, did not accept that the end of the Liga meant the end of the political project. They founded a different organization, the Kataastaasan, Kagalanggalangang Katipunan ng mga Anak ng Bayan, the "Highest and Most Respected Society of the Sons of the People," soon known simply as the Katipunan. The National Historical Commission of the Philippines (NHCP) identifies July 7, 1892, as its founding date.

Chapter 116 examined the movement that preceded it: the reformers, journalists, and novelists who sought representation, equal treatment, and the secularization of the Philippine church within the Spanish empire. This chapter follows a different line of political action. The Katipunan did not petition Madrid. Its stated aim was separation from Spain, and it prepared to pursue that aim by force. Within four years it had spread from a handful of founders in Manila to a network of local councils in the capital region and nearby provinces; within six years it had been transformed, defeated, divided, revived, and overtaken by events that none of its founders had controlled.

The chapter's central question is how a clandestine independence movement became an emerging national government, and why that government's struggle for sovereignty remained unresolved after Spain's defeat. The answer does not lie in a single leader or a single battle. It involves the social composition of the movement, the geography of Luzon, the logistics of fighting with few firearms, the competing local revolutionary institutions of Cavite, and a series of disputes about who possessed the authority to speak for the revolution. It also involves forces outside the archipelago: the war between Spain and the United States in 1898 abruptly changed the military balance and introduced a power whose intentions toward the Philippines were, at best, undefined.

The story includes the most painful episode in the revolution's own history. In 1897, a convention held at Tejeros in Cavite established a new revolutionary government, Bonifacio rejected its legitimacy, and he was arrested, tried by a revolutionary council of war, and executed on May 10, 1897, on the authority of the movement he had founded. Historians still debate responsibility for that decision. The chapter treats the episode as neither a simple betrayal nor a simple necessity, but as a case study in the problem of revolutionary legitimacy.

The chapter also addresses the people who made the revolution, who were not limited to the men whose names appear in national commemoration. Artisans, clerks, small traders, farmers and tenants, soldiers drawn from local communities, and women who carried messages, hid papers, raised money, and in some cases bore arms all appear in the evidence, though unevenly. The revolution's geography was equally uneven. It was fiercely contested in Manila's hinterland and in Cavite, intermittent in parts of central and southern Luzon, and in the Visayas largely a feature of 1898. Participation cannot be assumed to have been uniform across languages, religions, or regions.

The evidence is rich but demands caution. Katipunan documents were seized by Spanish authorities and preserved in colonial investigations; participants wrote memoirs decades later, often shaped by the political quarrels of the revolution itself; and national commemoration has attached fixed dates and places to events that contemporaries described differently. Historians such as Teodoro Agoncillo, Reynaldo Ileto, and Jim Richardson have read this record in markedly different ways, and their interpretations form part of the chapter's analysis. Students should come away understanding not only what happened, but why different generations of scholars have told the story differently.

The chapter ends in December 1898 with the Treaty of Paris, by which Spain agreed to cede the Philippines to the United States even though a Filipino revolutionary government was already organizing a republic at Malolos. That contradiction defines the chapter's close. The war that followed belongs to Chapter 118.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Katipunan was founded in 1892 and how its aims and methods differed from those of La Liga Filipina and the reform movement.
  2. Describe the organizational structure, recruitment, and secrecy practices of the Katipunan and assess their strengths and weaknesses.
  3. Analyze the roles of Andrés Bonifacio and Emilio Jacinto and distinguish formal schooling from intellectual formation.
  4. Evaluate the social composition of the revolution, including the participation of women, workers, and provincial communities.
  5. Trace the exposure of the Katipunan in August 1896 and the outbreak of armed conflict around Manila.
  6. Interpret the competing accounts of the "Cry" of August 1896 and explain how commemoration shapes historical memory.
  7. Explain how terrain, weapons, logistics, and Spanish reinforcements shaped the military course of the revolution.
  8. Compare the revolutionary experience of Manila, Cavite, and other provinces, and explain why mobilization was uneven.
  9. Assess the Tejeros Convention, the trial and execution of Bonifacio, and competing arguments about legitimacy and responsibility.
  10. Evaluate the Pact of Biak-na-Bato as defeat, truce, compromise, or reorganization.
  11. Distinguish between independence proclaimed, sovereignty exercised, and independence recognized, using the events of 1898.
  12. Analyze the Treaty of Paris in relation to the Philippine revolutionary government and explain why the question of sovereignty remained unresolved.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What was continuous, and what was new, in the transition from reformist politics to revolutionary organization in 1892?
  2. How does a secret society recruit, communicate, and finance itself, and what vulnerabilities do those methods create?
  3. How should historians weigh the testimony of participants who later disagreed bitterly about what had occurred?
  4. Why did the revolution take such different forms in Manila, Cavite, and other regions?
  5. What does the Philippine Revolution reveal about the limits of a military narrative built around a few named commanders?
  6. Who had the authority to reorganize the revolution at Tejeros, and by what standard should that authority be judged?
  7. How have different historians interpreted Bonifacio's execution, and what evidence is available to test their claims?
  8. Did the Pact of Biak-na-Bato end the revolution or merely suspend it?
  9. What is the difference between declaring independence and securing it, and how does the year 1898 illustrate that difference?
  10. Is it accurate to describe the revolution as a national movement, and what do terms such as bayan and Katagalugan suggest about that question?

Historical Context

By the 1890s the Philippines had been under Spanish rule for more than three centuries, as examined in Chapters 73 through 75 and 115. Colonial government rested on a small number of Spanish officials and a powerful body of religious orders, which owned substantial estates and exercised influence at parish level. The nineteenth-century opening of the economy to export agriculture, discussed in Chapter 115, had enriched some landowners and merchants, expanded a Chinese-mestizo and indio middle stratum, and widened inequalities in provincial society. It had also produced a generation of educated Filipinos, the ilustrados, who encountered liberal ideas in Manila, Madrid, and elsewhere in Europe.

Chapter 116 traced how some of that generation organized the Propaganda Movement, wrote for La Solidaridad, and sought reforms within the framework of empire. José Rizal's novels and his founding of La Liga Filipina in July 1892 were the movement's most prominent culmination. Rizal's arrest and deportation to Dapitan within days of the Liga's founding showed how narrow the legal space for reform remained. The reformers had never been a single bloc, and some members of the Liga looked toward a more radical course.

This chapter's history therefore begins where the reformist narrative reaches an impasse, but it should not be read as a simple replacement of one movement by another. Some Katipunan founders had belonged to the Liga; Bonifacio himself had been a member. The new organization recruited more widely than the Liga had, and it embraced armed struggle, but it drew on the same sense of grievance against colonial hierarchy, clerical power, and racial discrimination.

Students should keep two cautions in view. First, the revolution was not preordained: the Katipunan might have remained a small secret society, and in 1896 it was exposed before its preparations were complete. Second, "the Philippines" in the 1890s was not a politically unified nation. Its inhabitants spoke many languages, lived in very different economic circumstances, and related to Spanish authority in different ways. Muslim communities in Mindanao and Sulu, examined in Chapter 76, had a distinct history of resistance and accommodation. When revolutionaries spoke of the bayan, the "people" or "homeland," the extent of the community they meant was itself a question, taken up in Section 117.4.

Section 117.1

The Founding of the Katipunan and the Turn toward Independence, 1892

The Katipunan was founded in a specific political moment. La Liga Filipina, organized by José Rizal in Manila in early July 1892, had proposed a mutual-aid and self-help association that would unite the islands "into one compact, vigorous, and homogeneous body," in the language of its program as usually summarized, and would support education, commerce, and reform. Its public aims were moderate. Yet the colonial authorities arrested Rizal on July 6 and, within days, sent him into exile in Dapitan. The Liga's members were left leaderless, and the lesson that many drew was that even modest organized reform could be suppressed.

On July 7, according to the standard account preserved in the NHCP's registry and in the testimony of participants, a handful of men met in a house on Azcárraga Street in Tondo and founded the Katipunan. The traditional list of founders includes Andrés Bonifacio, Ladislao Diwa, Teodoro Plata, Deodato Arellano, Valentín Díaz, and José Dizon. Arellano served as the first president of the society. Bonifacio, who rose to the leading position by 1895, became the figure most closely identified with it. The names derive largely from later testimony and colonial investigations, so students should treat the list as well attested but not exhaustively documented.

The new society differed from the Liga in at least three ways. First, it was secret. The Liga had been a public association, however cautious; the Katipunan adopted oaths, pseudonyms, and signs of recognition, which reflected both the experience of the Liga's suppression and the influence of Masonic and other fraternal models then familiar in Manila. Second, it set independence as its objective. The movement did not at first publicly announce itself in these terms, but its documents and the later testimony of members make clear that separation from Spain was the aim, with armed force as the means if necessary. Third, it recruited from a wider social range than the educated reformist circles around the Liga, a point developed in Sections 117.2 and 117.5.

Continuities were nonetheless significant. Several early Katipunan members had belonged to the Liga, and the new association preserved the older organization's emphasis on mutual aid and brotherhood. Its members were also drawn from the same urban milieu, in which print, Masonic lodges, theatrical associations, and employment in foreign firms created new circles of contact. Historians sometimes describe the relationship as one of rupture; it is better described as a divergence, in which a portion of a shared reformist milieu concluded that appeals to Spain had failed. Rizal himself, as later testimony indicates, did not endorse the revolutionary course, a point returned to in Section 117.6.

The name chosen by the founders rewards attention. Katipunan derives from a Tagalog root meaning to gather or assemble. Anak ng bayan, "child of the people," used the word bayan, which can mean town, community, people, or homeland. The founders thus described their society in terms of a collective whose boundaries were not defined by a legal concept of nation. Kalayaan, the word for liberty or independence, carried both political and moral connotations and, as Reynaldo Ileto has argued, resonated with Tagalog religious and ethical language that gave it meanings beyond those of European liberalism. These interpretive questions are examined further in Section 117.4.

The evidence for the founding comes from several kinds of source. Surviving Katipunan documents, seized by the Spanish and later published, record rules, oaths, and correspondence. Participants' recollections written years afterward, including those of Pío Valenzuela and Santiago Álvarez, supply detail but also reflect later disputes. The colonial investigations of 1896, which relied partly on coerced testimony, provide another body of evidence whose reliability requires cautious reading. Jim Richardson's documentary compilation, The Light of Liberty, assembled and critically annotated much of this record and remains the principal starting point for the organization's early history.

The founding date has become a point of national historical commemoration, but the evidentiary sequence should remain precise. Rizal founded the Liga on July 3, was arrested on July 6, and did not begin his enforced journey to Dapitan until July 15, reaching the destination on July 17. The Katipunan's organization on July 7 therefore followed the arrest, not the completed deportation. The interval matters because it reveals contemporaries responding to a new political emergency rather than waiting for news of an exile already under way. The colonial state's action demonstrated that apparently lawful association could be treated as suspect, yet it did not mean every Liga member agreed immediately that armed revolt was practicable.

The founding generation also faced an organizational choice: whether to make independence an aspiration expressed in private discussion or a binding obligation requiring money, secrecy, and potential sacrifice. The group's founders brought contacts from urban trades and earlier reform associations, but contact networks were not the same thing as a disciplined revolutionary organization. Early offices, rules, and the initiation process had to be developed before members could claim to act for a wider public. Richardson's publication of organizational statutes and council records is important here because such documents establish institutional activity more firmly than anecdotes about a single dramatic meeting. The society's early history should thus be understood as incremental construction under surveillance, not as an instantly formed national army.

Historical Vocabulary I
Katipunan, kalayaan, katipon, Supremo, and revolutionary independence

Definition. Katipunan means a gathering or association; the organization's full name, abbreviated K.K.K., was the Kataastaasan, Kagalanggalangang Katipunan ng mga Anak ng Bayan. A katipon is a member. Kalayaan means freedom or independence. Supremo, a Spanish-derived title, was used for the movement's leading officer, a position Bonifacio held by 1895.

Historical context. The vocabulary combined Tagalog terms for community and liberty with Spanish administrative and fraternal terms, a mixture characteristic of Manila's colonial urban culture. "Independence" as a political goal meant the end of Spanish sovereignty, but what would replace it, whether a republic, a monarchy, or a federation, was not fixed in the movement's early documents.

Why it matters. The terms show that the revolution articulated its aims in local languages as well as in the political vocabulary of Europe.

Potential misunderstanding. Kalayaan should not be translated automatically as modern national independence; it carried broader senses of liberation that historians continue to analyze.

Section 117.2

Andrés Bonifacio: Social Origins, Education, and Revolutionary Leadership

Andrés Bonifacio was born in Tondo, Manila, in 1863. Accounts of his early life rely on a limited set of sources, including later biographical writing, and some details remain uncertain. The reliable outline is that he came from a modest urban family, lost his parents while still young, and took on responsibility for supporting his younger siblings. He found employment with foreign commercial firms in Manila, working as a clerk or agent, and supplemented his income by making and selling items such as canes and paper fans. His working life thus placed him in the commercial economy of the colonial capital, not in the landed or professional elite.

Older popular portrayals, and some scholarship, have described Bonifacio as an uneducated man of the people. The portrait is misleading. His formal schooling was limited by the standards of the ilustrados, who attended the colleges and universities of Manila and Europe. But the evidence indicates that he read widely. Biographical accounts report that his reading included histories of the French Revolution, novels, biographies, and the writings of Rizal, and his own surviving writings show command of Tagalog prose and verse and of the political language of the period. The distinction is between formal schooling and intellectual formation: Bonifacio's education occurred through work, reading, associations, and conversation. He also took part in amateur theatrical performance, as did many in Manila's artisan communities, and belonged to associational networks that included the Liga Filipina and, according to some accounts, Masonic circles.

His career as an organizer rested on this combination. He understood the world of clerks, warehouse workers, and small traders, because he belonged to it. He also moved among men with more formal education, such as Ladislao Diwa and, from 1894, the young law student Emilio Jacinto. By the mid-1890s he had come to dominate the Katipunan's leadership and used his position to build a membership that extended beyond Manila. His marriage to Gregoria de Jesús, who became closely involved in the movement, ties his personal life to the organization's household networks, as discussed in Section 117.5.

Bonifacio's significance has been interpreted in contrasting ways. Teodoro Agoncillo's The Revolt of the Masses (1956) presented him as the leader of a movement of the lower and middle strata against both Spanish rule and an ilustrado elite who, in Agoncillo's view, later took over the revolution. This reading made Bonifacio a symbol of popular nationalism and helped him become a major figure in modern Philippine commemoration. Later scholars have qualified the interpretation, noting that Katipunan membership included people of diverse social backgrounds, that the line between the "masses" and the "elite" was less clear than Agoncillo suggested, and that Bonifacio's posthumous image was itself constructed over time, as Glenn May has argued in a study of how his reputation was shaped. These qualifications do not remove Bonifacio from the center of the story; they place him within a broader field of actors.

Bonifacio was not the movement's only architect. Deodato Arellano and others organized the first councils. Jacinto helped shape its ethical statement and its administration. Pío Valenzuela, Teodoro Plata, and Ladislao Diwa performed key organizational roles. In the provinces, local leaders adapted the society to their own communities. A narrative that attributes the revolution to one man's will falls into the pattern of single-leader explanation that this textbook seeks to avoid, and it also obscures why the movement succeeded in recruiting so widely before 1896.

Bonifacio's work in Manila mattered not merely because it placed him among employees and traders but because it exposed him to the city's multilingual commercial culture. Orders, ledgers, shipments, and correspondence depended upon habits of attention and practical literacy. The city contained literate manual workers and underemployed clerks alongside the formally educated professions; the familiar contrast between a poorly educated revolutionary and an intellectual reform movement obscures these intermediate social worlds. Bonifacio's political writings must still be evaluated on their own terms, since later admirers sometimes amplified claims about his reading and later critics questioned the provenance of particular anecdotes. What survives most securely is an organizer whose political authority was forged through association, writing, and personal initiative rather than an imperial university credential.

His leadership also raised questions that became decisive in 1897. A secret society's authority flows partly from trust among initiates and from continuity in its central council; a territorial revolutionary government may base authority on soldiers, towns, and the capacity to defend them. Bonifacio's strength in Manila was the former. When provincial commanders began exercising the latter, his title alone did not settle which institution could overrule the other. This was not simply a clash between a poorer man and a wealthier rival, although social differences shaped the dispute. It was a confrontation between political constituencies formed in different circumstances. Historical biography is most useful when it connects the individual's career to those changing forms of authority.

Everyday Life and Social History

The Working, Educational, and Associational Worlds of Manila's Revolutionary Generation

The Katipunan's early members lived in a city of sharp social distinctions. Spanish officials and clergy occupied the walled city of Intramuros and the upper rungs of colonial society; Chinese-mestizo and indio merchants and landowners formed an economic middle and upper stratum; and a large population of clerks, artisans, warehouse workers, stevedores, domestic workers, and small traders lived in districts such as Tondo, Binondo, and Sampaloc. Employment with foreign trading houses, which expanded after the opening of Manila to world trade, created a category of salaried and semi-skilled workers whose livelihoods depended on commerce rather than on land.

These workers participated in an associational life that spanned mutual-aid societies, religious confraternities, theatrical groups, and, among some, Masonic lodges. Reading was shared: newspapers, pamphlets, and novels circulated beyond the individuals who bought them. Literacy in Tagalog and Spanish varied by class and gender. Education for girls was more limited than for boys, though some women gained schooling in Manila's colleges and at home. Tagalog verse, devotional literature, and theatrical convention gave members a common cultural vocabulary on which Katipunan writings drew.

Because most surviving sources were produced by literate men or by colonial investigators, the everyday lives of poorer urban residents and of women are visible mainly in fragments. Historians reconstruct them from court testimony, memoirs, and incidental references, a limitation that should be kept in mind throughout the chapter.

Questions for students:

  1. How did employment with foreign firms create networks through which political ideas could travel?
  2. Why might a secret society have appealed to people whose economic position was precarious but who were not landless cultivators?
  3. What kinds of evidence would historians need in order to learn more about ordinary members' lives?
Section 117.3

The Katipunan as a Secret Revolutionary Organization

The Katipunan's structure combined elements of fraternal organization with the requirements of conspiracy. At the apex was a Supreme Council, the Kataastaasang Sanggunian, composed of a president and other officers. Below it were provincial councils and, at the base, local councils known as balangay, each with its own officers. The structure allowed the society to grow by replication: a district could form a council, which in turn recruited members and formed further groups. The terminology and titles varied somewhat over time, and the organization underwent several reorganizations between 1892 and 1896, so that historians distinguish phases of institutional development rather than a single fixed design.

Recruitment depended on personal trust. Accounts of the "triangle" system describe a method in which a recruiter brought in two others who knew one another only through the recruiter, so that the exposure of one group would limit damage to others. Initiates passed through ceremonies that included oaths of secrecy and loyalty and, according to participants, signing a pledge with their own blood, an act with echoes of the older Southeast Asian practice of blood compact and of fraternal ritual. Members took pseudonyms, used passwords and signs, and in some accounts progressed through graded ranks. These forms created solidarity and a sense of moral commitment; they also made the society exotic to colonial authorities, who later cited its rituals as evidence of sedition.

Emilio Jacinto's contribution to the organization was substantial. Joining in 1894, he served in key administrative roles, wrote for the movement's newspaper, and produced the Kartilya, discussed in Section 117.4. His education and his skill with writing complemented Bonifacio's organizing ability. Their partnership, as Richardson's documentary work shows, was central to the Supreme Council's functioning in 1895 and 1896. The organization raised money through dues and contributions, collected arms where it could, and attempted to build up stocks of materials, though always with scarce resources.

The size of the Katipunan before August 1896 is uncertain. Contemporary Spanish estimates, later participants' recollections, and subsequent scholarship give numbers that range from a few thousand to many tens of thousands, and there is no reliable membership register that survived intact. Historians generally agree that the society had grown considerably, especially in Manila and surrounding provinces, but caution against treating any single figure as established. The caution matters because membership numbers have been used both to claim a mass movement and to suggest a more limited conspiracy.

Secrecy offered obvious advantages: it protected members from reprisal, allowed recruitment under the eyes of an alert colonial state, and created strong internal loyalty. It also imposed costs. A secret society could not easily publicize its aims, and its leaders had limited means of testing opinion among followers. Communication depended on couriers and meetings, which slowed coordination and left the organization vulnerable to informants and to the accidental discovery that eventually occurred. The very rituals that strengthened membership generated documents, such as membership lists and ledgers, that could be seized.

The documentary record reveals an organization more adaptable than a stereotypical secret brotherhood. Founding statutes, later rules, council records, correspondence, and draft newspaper contributions point to different layers of activity. Some documents specify responsibilities and procedures; others aim to encourage loyalty or justify independence. They should not be treated as if all described a single moment or as if a rule written by leaders proved universal compliance. Local organizers may have shortened ceremonies, adjusted recruitment, or relied on familiar community brokers. The existence of overlapping regulations is therefore evidence of institutional development and improvisation, not necessarily of incoherence. It also helps explain why a later historian's neat organizational diagram may misrepresent the movement's actual growth.

Money and printed material created practical limits to secrecy. Contributions were necessary for correspondence, travel, weapons, and presses, yet record keeping created evidence that investigators could exploit. The same dilemma applied to recruiting through friendship and family: social closeness made trust possible but meant an arrest might implicate many relatives and coworkers. A decentralized council could survive the loss of another council while remaining poorly prepared for synchronized action across provinces. Students should distinguish clandestine organizational survival from military effectiveness. The practices that permitted the Katipunan to spread under Spanish rule gave it neither secure arsenals nor a tested national chain of command when events forced the society into open rebellion.

Section 117.4

Revolutionary Ideology: Emilio Jacinto, the Kartilya, and the Meaning of Freedom

The Katipunan's ideas are preserved in several kinds of writing: the Kartilya, a primer of the organization's principles; essays and poems by Bonifacio, such as the Tagalog piece usually titled Pag-ibig sa Tinubuang Lupa ("Love of the Native Land"); Jacinto's essays under pseudonyms; and the articles of the newspaper Kalayaan. Bonifacio is generally credited with an earlier set of precepts, often called a "Decalogue," which Jacinto's Kartilya replaced for the purposes of initiation. The NHCP documents Jacinto's authorship of the Kartilya and related writings.

The Kartilya is notable for being an ethical primer. Its rules, in the paraphrase appropriate for a textbook, urge members to devote their lives to a worthy cause, to treat honor as a matter of conduct rather than of rank or wealth, to respect women and protect the weak, to keep their word, to reject servility and tyranny, and to understand that all people, whatever their physical appearance or status, are equal in their humanity. It borrows the form of a catechism or schoolbook, which would have been familiar to Tagalog readers from religious instruction, but adapts that form to a secular, civic aim. Its stress on conscience and reciprocity bears comparison with older Tagalog moral vocabulary.

Bonifacio's writings are more overtly political. They denounce colonial rule and clerical abuse, call on Filipinos to recognize their mistreatment, and describe the fatherland as a mother whose love demands sacrifice. They draw on the religious idiom of Philippine popular Catholicism as well as on European liberal ideas. Ileto's Pasyon and Revolution (1979) argued that the pasyon, the narrative of Christ's suffering and redemption sung at Lent, gave ordinary Filipinos a framework for understanding revolutionary struggle as a path to liberation, and that kalayaan evoked a restored order of community and light. His book directs attention to popular mentalities that earlier, elite-centered narratives had neglected. Critics have asked how widely the pasyon idiom explains revolutionary participation and whether it can be generalized beyond Tagalog-speaking communities; the question remains open, but the book changed the field by showing that the revolution's meanings cannot be read only through European political theory.

Students should distinguish ideals from practice. The Kartilya's emphasis on equality and moral responsibility described an aspiration; it did not guarantee that the organization lived up to it, as the later conflicts of 1897 illustrate. Rituals of secrecy and oaths of obedience could coexist with factionalism, and the egalitarian language of brotherhood did not remove differences of wealth, education, and region among members.

The vocabulary also complicates the claim that the revolution was "national." The word bayan could mean a town, a people, or the land of one's birth. Katagalugan, from Tagalog, was used in Bonifacio's writings and in some Katipunan documents. Some scholars read it as referring specifically to the Tagalog-speaking areas; others argue that Bonifacio and his colleagues used it to mean all the "sons of the land," that is, the inhabitants of the archipelago more broadly. Documents of late 1896 attributed to the Katipunan leadership use the phrase Haring Bayang Katagalugan, usually rendered as the sovereign Tagalog nation or people; Richardson's reading of these documents suggests that the Supreme Council viewed itself as the nucleus of a government. The evidence supports caution: contemporaries used the language of bayan in ways that cannot simply be translated as the modern nation-state of the Philippines.

The writings of Jacinto and Bonifacio also invite close reading for the political audience they constructed. Their appeal to the bayan asked readers to imagine moral obligations that extended beyond the household and locality. But the precise relation between a Tagalog term for people or homeland and a future archipelago-wide Filipino nation cannot simply be assumed. Revolutionary authors worked in a world of multiple local languages, Catholic devotional forms, and Spanish political categories. Translators often have to choose between equivalent words that carry different histories. A critical edition should therefore preserve key Tagalog expressions and disclose when an English phrase is an interpretive rendering rather than an exact match. This is particularly important when discussing equality, honor, duty, and national independence.

The Kartilya's treatment of women illustrates another necessary distinction. Its language of respect challenges the assumption that rank or male strength alone makes a person honorable; nevertheless it is not a modern program of equal political representation. Women could act as organizers, trusted couriers, protectors of documents, and political thinkers while still facing limits within the leadership and the households of a strongly gendered society. A parallel tension concerned class: moral equality did not by itself redistribute land or abolish the dependence of tenants on estate managers. Revolutionary ideology could unite people across social divisions without supplying an agreed institutional answer to every dispute that independence would raise.

Section 117.5

Women, Workers, Families, and the Social Base of the Revolution

Chapter 116 described a reform movement centered on educated men with access to print and to Madrid. The Katipunan's recruitment reached further down the social scale. Surviving testimony and Spanish investigations identify members who were clerks, artisans, tailors, printers, small traders, boatmen, and minor municipal officials, as well as lawyers, students, and some landowners. The proportions cannot be fixed with confidence. Agoncillo emphasized the lower-class character of the membership; later scholarship has stressed that the Katipunan cut across social strata, with leadership and mass membership often drawn from different backgrounds. The reasonable conclusion is that the movement's social breadth was real and that it was uneven across place and rank.

Provincial recruitment followed networks of kinship, patronage, and shared employment. In towns near Manila, municipal officials and respected local men often brought members of their communities into councils, with the result that the revolution in these places mobilized existing social relationships as well as individual conviction. Tenants and cultivators in estates owned by religious orders or private landlords had their own grievances over rents, land, and taxation. Whether those grievances translated into Katipunan membership depended on the presence of recruiters and on local circumstances, a reminder that "peasant" participation was not automatic.

Women's participation is documented but under-recorded. A women's chapter of the Katipunan was established, and women are identified in the movement's records and in later testimony. Gregoria de Jesús, Bonifacio's wife, took the role of "Lakambini," a term used for a female figure of honor in the society, and is credited with safeguarding the organization's papers and assisting with correspondence. Marina Dizon, sister of founder José Dizon, was among the early women members. Josefa Rizal, the sister of José Rizal, is reported to have served as an officer in the women's group. In Caloocan, Melchora Aquino, later known as Tandang Sora, sheltered revolutionaries in 1896 and was deported by the Spanish authorities to Guam, according to the standard account. Others carried messages, hid arms and documents, collected funds, nursed the wounded, and managed households that served as meeting places.

It would understate women's role to describe it only as "support." Control over documents and communication was essential to a clandestine organization, and the revolutionary household was a site of organization in its own right. Women also took on military roles in some regions, particularly later in 1898 and 1899, a theme that returns in Chapter 118. At the same time, the evidence does not support portraying women as equal participants in the movement's leadership. The Kartilya's respect for women coexisted with the exclusion of women from the highest offices, and much of what is known about their activities comes from male narrators.

The question of class composition needs a more careful method than dividing members into an educated elite and an undifferentiated mass. A small trader who employed apprentices, a municipal clerk answerable to an official, a tenant cultivator, and a printer paid by the day all occupied different positions in a changing colonial economy. Their reasons for joining could overlap while remaining distinct: protection against arbitrary authority, resentment of racial humiliation, hope for local office, religious-order land disputes, or the prospect of a government answerable to its inhabitants. Spanish interrogations give occupational labels but were produced under coercive conditions; memoirs written later often emphasized the social groups that an author wanted to represent. Neither type of record provides a transparent membership census.

Women's networks likewise crossed the line between household and formal organization. Hiding seals or correspondence, obtaining food, organizing visits, and arranging safe accommodation were political activities because disclosure could expose an entire council. Women who were related to founders also possessed their own relationships with friends, merchants, servants, and neighbors. A household might support a member privately without endorsing every proposal of the Supreme Council. Conversely, the imprisonment of one person could endanger children and dependent relatives whose political opinions were never recorded. These wider costs help explain both the movement's persistence and the hesitations of prospective supporters when violence appeared likely. The absence of a woman from a meeting roster cannot establish her absence from revolutionary activity.

Section 117.6

The Growth, Exposure, and Crisis of the Katipunan in 1896

By 1895 and early 1896 the Katipunan had expanded considerably. Bonifacio and Jacinto sought to extend it into provinces near Manila and to prepare for a rising. The organization acquired a printing capacity, and in early 1896 produced an issue of its newspaper, Kalayaan, which carried the movement's rhetoric to a wider readership than initiates alone. One issue is known to have been printed; the paper was soon overtaken by events. Its contents, including pieces attributed to Bonifacio and Jacinto, make it a key source for the movement's public voice.

Debates over timing and strategy accompanied expansion. Some members urged waiting for better preparation and for the support of influential figures; others pressed for early action. According to testimony later given by Pío Valenzuela, the movement sent him in June 1896 to Dapitan to consult Rizal, who advised against a premature rising. The account comes from Valenzuela's later statements, including those given to Spanish investigators, and its details are not independently confirmed; it nevertheless indicates that the Katipunan leadership sought legitimacy from the most celebrated reformer, and that he did not give it. The episode matters because Rizal's later trial and execution, treated in Chapter 116, were connected by the colonial authorities to the revolt he had not endorsed.

The Katipunan's exposure occurred in August 1896. The traditional account, which appears in many narratives, holds that a member of the organization who worked at the printing press of the Diario de Manila quarreled with colleagues and then disclosed the society's existence, through a relative connected with a religious institution, to a parish priest, who alerted the authorities. On August 19 Spanish authorities discovered materials linked to the society and began arrests. Historians generally accept the broad outline that a disclosure led to a search and to arrests around that date, but they caution that the intermediate details, motives, and the names of those involved rest on a small number of accounts written or recorded later. A prudent narrative states the documented fact, the discovery of Katipunan materials and the commencement of arrests in mid-to-late August, and labels the traditional story of its circumstances as tradition.

The discovery forced a decision. Spanish authorities, led by Governor-General Ramón Blanco, began arresting suspects in Manila and nearby areas. Katipunan leaders could either disperse, hoping to avoid arrest, or act before the organization was destroyed. Bonifacio and his associates left Manila for its outskirts, where they gathered followers and debated what to do. The decision to rise was thus partly a response to circumstances: the Katipunan had been preparing for rebellion, but it did not choose the moment in the manner its leaders might have wished. The next sections follow the decisions they made.

The society's newspaper illustrates the relationship between communication and political scale. A printed issue might reach far beyond its nominal subscribers as copies were passed from hand to hand or read aloud. The result was not a modern mass media operation: paper, type, money, distribution, and police searches limited circulation. Its significance is that leaders tried to persuade people who had not taken an oath and might never attend a council meeting. A successful insurrection would require such broader support. Yet publishing political arguments also heightened the danger of discovery; a secret press depended on workplaces, suppliers, and acquaintances whose ordinary movements could be traced after the police found incriminating materials.

The disputed Rizal consultation should be analyzed as evidence about legitimacy as well as military readiness. Later narratives sometimes present Rizal's reported refusal to endorse a premature revolt as proof that reform and revolution were mutually exclusive. That conclusion is too simple. A person might believe Spanish rule unjust and nevertheless judge the existing military preparations inadequate; equally, a conspirator might seek endorsement from an admired reformer without accepting his authority to decide. Valenzuela's testimonies were made in different circumstances and should not be flattened into a verbatim account of the Dapitan conversation. In August the organization confronted a choice shaped by an immediate police emergency. The decision to rise cannot be explained solely through the earlier disputed consultation.

Section 117.7

The Cry of Balintawak or Pugad Lawin: When Did the Revolution Begin?

In the days after August 19, Katipunan leaders and followers assembled in the countryside north of Manila, in an area now within Quezon City and Caloocan, which in 1896 comprised scattered settlements. There, according to many accounts, a large gathering formally resolved to take up arms. A central ritual act, reported in most versions, was the tearing of cédulas personales, the personal tax certificates that every adult was required to carry, as a symbolic rejection of colonial authority. Participants are said to have shouted slogans of defiance; the specific words differ among witnesses.

Beyond that core, the sources diverge. Different participants and later historians have placed the event at Balintawak, at Pugad Lawin, at Bahay Toro, and at other sites, and have assigned it dates ranging across the last week of August. They also differ about who was present, what was said, and whether there was a single event or several gatherings over several days. Some of the differences reflect the fallibility of memory, as the participants gave their accounts at different times and under different circumstances. Others reflect competing political and regional loyalties. Early twentieth-century commemoration favored Balintawak and the date of August 26; later scholarship, drawing on a wider range of testimony, argued for Pugad Lawin and a date of August 23. The NHCP commemorates the Pugad Lawin event on August 23, 1896, which is the official position, but historians continue to emphasize that the precise location and chronology cannot be settled beyond dispute.

What is not in dispute is the substance: in the last days of August 1896, the Katipunan leadership and its followers committed themselves to armed rebellion and symbolically repudiated Spanish authority. Planning for an attack on Manila followed. The legacy of the Cry as a moment of national commemoration, however, has made its details politically sensitive, since the choice of site and date associates the revolution's beginning with particular places, persons, and narratives.

The historiographical difficulty concerns the nature of the event being dated. A gathering that decided on resistance, a ceremonial tearing of tax certificates, a meeting to choose commanders, and an armed encounter with colonial forces might all be described retrospectively as the beginning of the Revolution. If they occurred over several days at different houses and roads north of Manila, witnesses could disagree without necessarily inventing events. The NHCP commemorates a gathering at Pugad Lawin on August 23, 1896, while older narratives identified Balintawak and assigned a different late-August date. This textbook should distinguish a commemoration adopted by a public institution from a precise chronology proven beyond dispute. The two are related but perform different historical functions.

Evidence should be tested by asking when each witness spoke, where that person had been, whether the place-name was in use in 1896, and how a changing landscape affects later attempts to locate a meeting. Reminiscence written decades afterward may preserve genuine experiences while adopting the terminology of later memorial campaigns. Colonial police records may supply dates more firmly but understate meetings to which officials were not admitted. A comparison of independent testimony is stronger than choosing the most dramatic single recollection. Readers should also resist assuming that the tearing of a cédula was a universal act observed by every member: it is best understood as a powerful reported symbol of repudiating colonial taxation and documentation.

The broader significance is that a secret political commitment became public defiance. Such an act made retreat more difficult for participants because the colonial government could now treat their collective action as rebellion. The transition also transformed the society's internal language. An oath-based network had to determine how to command armed groups, obtain provisions, and protect participants who were not professional soldiers. The Cry therefore provides an entry point into the institutional problems that followed, whether the decisive meeting is dated August 23, August 26, or another day in the same sequence.

Historical Debate I

Balintawak, Pugad Lawin, and the Construction of Revolutionary Memory

The question: Where and when did the Cry occur, and why does the answer matter?

Interpretation A — Balintawak, late August: Early accounts and long-standing commemoration identified Balintawak and a date around August 26. Supporters of this view point to the testimony of participants who used that name and to the early adoption of the date in public observance.
Interpretation B — Pugad Lawin, August 23: Later historians, drawing on other participants' recollections and on a re-examination of the testimony, argued that the principal event occurred at Pugad Lawin on August 23. The official commemorative position adopted this account.
Interpretation C — multiple gatherings: Some scholars suggest that the testimony is reconcilable if there were several meetings in a cluster of locations over several days, and that later commemoration compressed them into a single event.

Evidence: Participants' later testimony and memoirs, statements given to Spanish investigators, and later commemorative decisions. None of the accounts is a contemporary, neutral record, and they differ in dates, locations, and details.

Points of disagreement: Chronology, location, number and identity of witnesses, and the relation between the symbolic act and the decision to attack.

Why it matters: The debate shows how national memory selects and fixes a moment. It also illustrates the problem of reconstructing events from retrospective testimony.

Section 117.8

The First Armed Uprisings and the Colonial Response

The plan that emerged from the gatherings, as reconstructed from participants' accounts, called for a coordinated attack on Manila at the end of August. The principal early engagement occurred on August 30 at San Juan del Monte, a settlement just outside Manila where the colonial authorities maintained a powder magazine and a water-pumping station. Katipunan forces led by Bonifacio attacked the position. The rebels were poorly armed, many carrying bolos and other bladed weapons in place of rifles, and a small Spanish detachment held its ground until reinforcements arrived, after which the attackers were driven back. Other clashes took place in nearby communities, including areas of Mandaluyong, Pasig, Marikina, Montalban, and San Mateo, with varying results. Some Spanish posts were threatened; none of the attacks achieved the objective of taking Manila.

The rebels' weaknesses were clear. They lacked firearms and ammunition, their forces were organized locally and were difficult to coordinate, and they confronted a colonial army with rifles, artillery, and the ability to concentrate troops at points of danger. The advantages of the Spanish side included interior lines of communication, control of the capital's fortifications, and the continued loyalty of much of the colonial military, which included Filipino as well as Spanish soldiers. Spain was also able to call on reinforcement, a point examined in Section 117.11. The rebels' advantages, in contrast, were local knowledge, numerical strength in specific districts, and the capacity to melt into the countryside.

On August 30 Governor-General Blanco proclaimed martial law in several provinces, including Manila, Bulacan, Pampanga, Nueva Ecija, Tarlac, Laguna, Cavite, and Batangas, a measure that widened the space for summary action. Arrests multiplied in the capital and the provinces. Suspected members were imprisoned, interrogated, and, in many cases, executed or deported. Property was confiscated. The repression was not limited to active insurgents: relatives, associates, and individuals with reformist reputations were swept in. In the following months the authorities tried and executed many; the execution of Rizal on December 30, 1896, discussed in Chapter 116, was the most famous.

Repression had paradoxical effects. It removed many leaders and members, but it also increased the number of people who had lost relatives or property to the authorities and thus had reasons to support the insurgents. The colonial response, which treated suspicion as evidence, hardened antagonism in communities that had not been committed to the revolution. The pattern, familiar in other colonial insurgencies, helps explain why the rising spread to provinces beyond the capital region even as the Manila attacks failed.

San Juan del Monte should be understood as an operational objective rather than merely as the location of an iconic defeat. A powder magazine promised ammunition that the rebels urgently lacked; control of water infrastructure and routes toward Manila could interfere with colonial operations and perhaps encourage simultaneous risings. The problem was that such targets were also defensible and close to reinforcements from the capital. Even determined fighters armed with bolos could not replace the need for rifles, reserves, communications, and a coherent plan for withdrawing if the first assault failed. When attacks in different districts occurred at different times, the Spanish could defeat groups separately instead of facing one unified assault.

The declaration of martial law also altered the relationship between ordinary residents and the colonial state. Search, detention, and collective suspicion did not require a person to have taken a revolutionary oath. People with the wrong acquaintances could be endangered, while the survival of kinship networks made information about underground activity both valuable and unreliable. Historians must examine the categories used by colonial investigators: 'insurgent,' 'suspect,' and 'accomplice' were labels assigned in a political struggle. Equating a police accusation with proven participation would distort the social reach of the movement. At the same time, not every civilian supported the rebellion; some sought protection from government authorities or feared local rebel commanders.

Revolutionary Warfare and the Expansion of the Conflict
Section 117.9

Geography, Strategy, and the Military Organization of the Revolution

The revolution was fought largely on the island of Luzon, in a landscape of river plains, rice lands, small towns, hills, and coastal marsh. Manila lay at the head of a bay, linked to its hinterland by the Pasig River, by roads and, by the 1890s, by a railway line running north toward Pampanga and Pangasinan. Spain's strength rested on control of this core: the walled city, the arsenals, the port, and the lines of communication. A rebel force that could not seize Manila quickly was therefore compelled to fight a different kind of war, one of dispersed bases, local control, and avoidance of decisive engagements on Spanish terms.

The central military problem was weaponry. Rebel forces possessed a limited number of rifles and shotguns, some captured, some purchased, some taken from defecting municipal police or soldiers; many fighters carried bolos, spears, and improvised arms. Ammunition was scarce. Local workshops produced some munitions and repaired weapons, and rebel forces built trenches and fortified positions to offset the disadvantage. Captured Spanish arms were precious. Provisioning relied on local communities, which supplied rice, livestock, and shelter, sometimes willingly, sometimes under pressure, a reality with consequences for civilians examined in Section 117.11.

Organization varied. Forces raised by local councils often fought under their own commanders, and coordination among separate groups was difficult. Communication depended on messengers on foot or horseback, which was slow and unreliable under pursuit. The Katipunan's structure, designed for secrecy, did not automatically translate into a military hierarchy. As fighting continued, local leaders developed ranks, units, and commissions, and by 1897 a more formal army structure emerged, particularly in Cavite, with generals, colonels, and staff, but the transition generated disputes over rank and authority, as Section 117.13 shows.

Conditions around Manila and in Cavite differed markedly. In the immediate vicinity of the capital, the Spanish could concentrate troops quickly and the terrain offered rebels little protection from counterattack. Cavite, a province bordering Manila Bay and Laguna de Bay with a long coast, many streams, rice fields, and fortifiable towns, offered a different setting. Its local officials were already active in the Katipunan, its population was densely settled and politically organized at the municipal level, and Spanish forces had to cross waterways and marshland to reach rebel strongholds. These conditions allowed local councils to hold territory for months. The province's relative proximity to Manila, however, also meant that Spanish reinforcements could be directed against it once they arrived.

The logistical challenge can be reconstructed from the landscape. Rivers provided transport in normal times but could become barriers when bridges were watched; roads helped a force reach market towns but also helped colonial troops move artillery. Rice harvests made villages essential suppliers, and control of mills or storage places could determine how long a rebel position remained viable. Warfare during planting or harvest could reduce future food supplies even where a village escaped direct destruction. Fighting forces depended on local information about paths, crossings, and the movements of government patrols. Such knowledge was a military resource that did not appear in conventional inventories of weapons, yet reliance on local guides also made armies vulnerable when loyalty shifted.

Cavite's coastal connections and dense settlements did not make it invulnerable. Concentrated revolutionary authority could sustain trench works, collect contributions, and defend approaches, but it produced fixed targets once Spanish reinforcements arrived. Temporary control of a town required judges, revenue collectors, market supervision, and decisions about suspected collaborators. A commander who solved military problems through local levies might create civilian resentment if demands became too heavy. The formation of revolutionary government thus altered the movement's military position and its social relations simultaneously. This helps explain why neighboring provinces could experience the same nominal revolution differently: geography shaped available tactics, while municipal institutions shaped the people capable of implementing them.

Section 117.10

The Cavite Campaign and the Rise of Emilio Aguinaldo

Cavite's revolution had its own organization. The Katipunan in the province was divided into two principal councils, the Magdalo, centered in Kawit and named for Saint Mary Magdalene, and the Magdiwang, centered in Noveleta, with leaders such as Baldomero Aguinaldo of the Magdalo and Mariano Álvarez of the Magdiwang. Each council governed a group of towns, raised troops, and acted with considerable autonomy. Their relationship combined cooperation against Spain and rivalry over authority and prestige, a rivalry that later shaped the crisis of 1897.

Emilio Aguinaldo, born in 1869 in Kawit into a family of local standing, served as the municipal captain of his town before the revolution and joined the Katipunan in early 1896. Educated at the Colegio de San Juan de Letran for a time before leaving to manage family affairs, he was a local administrator rather than a national figure when fighting began. His emergence resulted from combined circumstances: control of local resources and followers, a family network that included other Magdalo leaders, and a series of military successes in the first months of the war.

In late August and early September 1896, Magdalo forces seized Kawit and engaged Spanish troops in the neighboring areas, including Imus, in actions that became central to Aguinaldo's reputation. Later in the year, battles in the Binakayan and Dalahican areas in November, in which Cavite rebels repelled Spanish assaults in part by using fortifications, were cited as major rebel victories. These engagements, with details varying by source, gave Cavite's rebels a reputation for success when other areas had suffered defeat. Commanders such as Edilberto Evangelista, a trained engineer who supervised fortification, and local leaders including Pío del Pilar and Mariano Trías, contributed to the defense. Aguinaldo later described these years in memoirs; his accounts are valuable but self-interested, and students should read them alongside other participants' accounts, such as that of Santiago Álvarez of the Magdiwang.

Success had political effects. Local councils began to behave as governments, appointing officials, collecting resources, and administering justice. Documents from late 1896 show Cavite's councils issuing orders in their own names. Military victory conferred authority; defeat could remove it. Aguinaldo's rising prominence fitted this pattern, and the distinction should be drawn between contemporary military accomplishment and later political reputation, which was shaped by decades of historical writing and by Aguinaldo's own later position. In 1896 he was one notable commander among several. The subsequent Spanish offensive, discussed in Section 117.11, tested the Cavite leadership.

Cavite's experience differed from Manila's in important respects. The province's rebels held territory, so they had to govern. In Manila the Katipunan had been a clandestine society whose members were scattered and hunted; in Cavite it became the administration of a rebel zone. This difference helps explain why the province became the revolution's center and why tensions arose when Bonifacio, whose base had been the capital region, came to Cavite in late 1896 and found local power structures already in place.

The early Cavite engagements were not interchangeable victories. An action involving the capture of a town might remove a colonial garrison and make rifles available, whereas a defensive battle at prepared earthworks could demonstrate the ability to resist an advancing column. Both strengthened local leaders, but neither established a secure province-wide command. Revolutionary officials still had to maintain roads, regulate trade, arrange communication among towns, and decide how to apportion scarce ammunition. The physical engineering of defenses, associated with men such as Edilberto Evangelista, was inseparable from labor mobilization: trenches demanded workers, tools, food, and time. A history of Aguinaldo's ascent should therefore trace the village institutions that sustained his operations, not attribute success exclusively to personal courage.

The presence of two Cavite councils shows why the distinction between 'the revolution' and its component governments matters. Magdalo and Magdiwang could endorse the same objective of ending Spanish rule while disagreeing over which council had jurisdiction or which officer could issue binding commands. Battlefield losses heightened those divisions, because a commander whose troops were depleted could accuse rivals of withholding men or supplies. The struggle for administrative leadership in Cavite was therefore partly a competition to govern actual territory. When Bonifacio entered this environment, he encountered institutions that had developed under the immediate pressures of war, rather than merely regional branches waiting for direction from Manila.

Aguinaldo's Rise as Seen by Different Participants

Aguinaldo's supporters in the Magdalo: They saw in him a commander who had won battles, held territory, and protected Cavite's towns when the Manila-based leadership had failed to take the capital. For them leadership should follow military results.

Spanish commanders: Spanish officers regarded the Cavite rebels as the most dangerous opposition and directed their principal counteroffensive against the province, treating Aguinaldo as a significant, though not unique, opponent.

Section 117.11

Spanish Counteroffensives, Civilian Suffering, and the Costs of War

The initial weakness of the Spanish response ended with the arrival of reinforcements from Spain, drawn partly from forces that had been serving in the Cuban war. In December 1896 Camilo García de Polavieja replaced Ramón Blanco as Governor-General, taking a harder line toward the rebels. In early 1897 Spanish columns advanced into Cavite, supported by artillery and by naval operations on the coast. The campaign, which included hard fighting around Zapote Bridge in February, in which Edilberto Evangelista was killed, and the capture of rebel-held towns in the following weeks, pushed the Cavite forces back from their positions. By late March the Spanish had taken Imus, a central Magdalo position.

Spanish counterinsurgency combined offensive columns with administrative and punitive measures: martial law, arrests, summary trials, confiscation of property, and pressure on communities believed to support the rebels. Villages that sheltered insurgents risked destruction; those that cooperated with the Spanish risked retaliation from the rebels. Accounts of Spanish reprisals against civilians, including burning of houses and killing of suspects, appear in rebel and Filipino sources and in some foreign observers' reports, and the evidence supports the conclusion that civilians suffered considerably, though the scale cannot be quantified reliably from the surviving record.

The rebels, too, drew on civilian resources. Armies needed food, labor, and shelter, and local populations were asked, and at times compelled, to provide them. Rebel councils punished perceived collaboration with Spain, and some civilians suffered from rebel as well as Spanish measures. Households were separated by conscription and arrests; families fled from combat zones; crops were destroyed or left unharvested; and the movement of troops spread hardship and disease. Prisoners, both rebel and Spanish, were held under difficult conditions.

A narrative confined to named commanders and battles would miss these dimensions. For villagers in Cavite the war was an experience of displacement, requisition, and fear, in which survival depended on decisions about whom to feed, whom to shelter, and when to flee. Existing inequalities persisted: wealthier families could sometimes move or buy protection, while poorer ones had fewer options. Local rivalries, including disputes over land and office, were sometimes pursued under the cover of political allegiance, a phenomenon common in civil conflict.

For Spanish commanders the counteroffensive was intended not only to defeat rebel formations but to restore governability in Cavite's towns. Retaking a road junction or church plaza mattered because colonial officials needed places from which to collect information, supply detachments, and announce the return of authority. Yet an occupied town was not necessarily a pacified district. Revolutionary units could withdraw to neighboring settlements while sympathizers remained among residents. This created a recurring problem for both sides: military control could change faster than the loyalties or grievances of the population. The arrival of reinforcements also imposed costs on Spanish administration, since soldiers needed transport, food, and medical support and campaigns competed with the empire's commitments elsewhere.

The surviving descriptions of atrocities require disciplined attribution. Some testimonies come from survivors or revolutionaries who had reasons to publicize Spanish cruelty; official reports might conceal unlawful punishment or depict all victims as combatants. Comparison among such sources is necessary, but recognition of partisanship should not erase credible evidence of coercion. The possibility of arbitrary arrest itself changed behavior, inducing flight and distrust. Residents living near a battlefield could be compelled to serve several masters in succession, each demanding supplies and assurances of loyalty. The human history of the counteroffensive is therefore also a history of uncertain authority, disrupted planting and trading, and families obliged to make decisions with very little protection under either government.

Continuity and Change Over Time I

How Revolutionary Warfare Transformed Colonial Society while Preserving Older Relationships

What changed

  • Rebel councils exercised governmental functions in territories that Spain no longer controlled
  • Many men gained military experience and rank, and local commanders acquired authority not tied to colonial office
  • Households and communities were displaced, and normal agricultural and commercial cycles were disrupted
  • Political language shifted toward liberty, the fatherland, and sovereignty in communities where such language had not been widespread

What continued

  • Local social hierarchies of landholding, kinship, and patronage often shaped who led, who followed, and who benefited
  • Regional and municipal loyalties remained strong
  • The material burdens of war fell most heavily on poorer households

Why the pattern matters: The revolution did not simply replace one order with another; it changed political possibilities while preserving many existing relationships, a mix that influenced later disputes over who should lead.

Section 117.12

Revolution Beyond Manila and Cavite: Regional Participation and Uneven Mobilization

While Cavite became the revolution's center, revolt spread to other provinces of central and southern Luzon. In Bulacan, Pampanga, and Nueva Ecija, local leaders such as Mariano Llanera in Nueva Ecija led uprisings in the first weeks, and Bulacan's uplands later hosted the Biak-na-Bato headquarters. In Tarlac, Francisco Makabulos organized a revolutionary administration in late 1897. In Laguna and Batangas, local councils and commanders, among them figures such as Miguel Malvar in Batangas, led resistance of varying duration and intensity. Each province had its own chronology, shaped by local leaders, the strength of Spanish garrisons, and the relationship of communities to the Katipunan network.

The revolution reached the Visayas mainly in 1898, after Spanish power had been weakened elsewhere. Local uprisings occurred in parts of the central Visayas earlier that year, and in late 1898 revolts in Negros and Panay ended Spanish authority in those islands. Negros saw the proclamation of a local republican government with the participation of its landowning elite, and in Panay Filipino forces took Iloilo after the Spanish evacuation in December. The details of these separate chronologies belong partly to Chapter 118, but the point for this chapter is that Visayan events followed their own logic, with local leaders and conditions, and were not simply an extension of Tagalog-region organization.

Participation cannot be assumed across the archipelago. Many Filipinos lived far from the centers of conflict; others were uncertain or opposed. Some provincial elites remained loyal to Spain or waited to see which side would prevail, and Filipino soldiers served in the Spanish army and auxiliary forces throughout the war. Muslim communities in Mindanao and Sulu, with their own political structures and histories (Chapter 76), were largely outside the revolution's organization, and Spain maintained a presence in parts of the south until 1898. Highland peoples and religious and linguistic minorities responded to the revolution in diverse ways, reflecting local relationships to lowland authorities. The revolution thus asserted a claim to represent a Filipino people that was not yet uniformly organized or even uniformly aware of the claim.

The evidence for regional participation is uneven, because the sources are weighted toward Tagalog-speaking areas and toward leaders. Careful historical writing therefore distinguishes documented local movements from assumptions of national unity. The revolution was a geographically uneven and socially complex conflict, not merely a contest between Spain and two Filipino leaders.

The history of the Visayas makes regional comparison essential. In Cebu, revolutionaries led by Pantaleon Villegas, known as León Kilat, launched the Tres de Abril uprising on April 3, 1898. The movement initially challenged Spanish forces in the city but suffered counterattack and internal violence; Villegas was killed within days. This episode preceded the American naval victory at Manila Bay and therefore cannot be explained simply as an automatic reaction to that victory. Its local networks and grievances deserve a place alongside the much better documented Tagalog revolutionary movement. It also reminds readers that participation could be strong in a city without yielding lasting control of its fortifications or countryside.

A different sequence unfolded in the central Visayas after Spain's position weakened. On November 5, 1898, forces under Juan Anacleto Araneta proclaimed the República de Negros at Bago; the NHCP's historical marker records the date and notes recognition of Aguinaldo's authority. On November 17 a Revolutionary Government of the Visayas was inaugurated at Santa Barbara in Iloilo under Martín Teófilo Delgado, with the Philippine flag raised there. These dates precede the December withdrawal of Spanish authority from Iloilo city and demonstrate political activity independent of a single Manila-issued timetable. Landowners, municipal figures, and military organizers participated for different reasons. The relationship between these locally constituted governments and claims of national sovereignty was negotiated, not simply assumed.

The contrast between Cebu's earlier fighting, Negros's locally proclaimed republic, and Panay's developing revolutionary government also warns against using 'the Visayan Revolution' as though it were one campaign. Economies differed, Spanish garrisons had unequal strength, and provincial leaders had different relationships to merchants and estate laborers. The islands' linguistic diversity further complicated coordination. A textbook centered on Cavite may miss the ways in which provincial governments claimed authority before they had secure recognition from the state taking shape at Malolos.

Historical Vocabulary II
Insurrection, revolutionary army, local authority, provincial mobilization, and counterinsurgency

Definition. An insurrection is an armed rising against established authority; a revolution implies an attempt to replace that authority with a new political order. The revolutionary army was a set of forces, initially local and irregular, later partly organized under a central command. Local authority refers to the governing functions exercised by town and provincial councils. Provincial mobilization is the process by which provinces joined the conflict, and counterinsurgency refers to the methods used by the colonial state to suppress it.

Historical context. Spanish officials called the movement an insurrection and its members rebels or insurrectos; Filipino participants called their cause a revolution (himagsikan). The labels carried legal and political implications, since "insurrection" denied the legitimacy of the movement's claims.

Why it matters. The vocabulary shows that naming the conflict was itself a political act, a point that recurs in Chapter 118, where American officials also used the term "insurrection."

Potential misunderstanding. The "revolutionary army" should not be pictured as a single unified force with a clear chain of command from 1896; its structure developed unevenly.

Section 117.13

Magdalo, Magdiwang, and the Crisis of Revolutionary Leadership

By late 1896 the revolution had no single command. The Supreme Council in the Manila region had been scattered and weakened; Cavite's two councils each governed their own towns. The Magdalo, with Aguinaldo as its most prominent commander, and the Magdiwang, under Mariano Álvarez, cooperated militarily but disagreed over command, territory, and the proper form of government. Reports of Spanish pressure and of rebel losses sharpened these disputes. Some Magdiwang leaders invited Bonifacio to come to Cavite to help resolve them, and he arrived in the province around the end of 1896, bringing the prestige of the Katipunan's founder and the claim that the Supreme Council retained authority over the movement.

The questions at stake were substantial. Who commanded the combined forces? Should the revolution continue under the Katipunan's structure, with its Supremo and Supreme Council, or reorganize as a formal government with executive and military officers? How should territorial authority be divided among councils that had been governing their own areas? And how should rank and promotion be determined, by seniority in the Katipunan, by battlefield success, or by election? Each answer favored some leaders over others.

Bonifacio's relations with the Cavite leaders were complicated. He had been the organization's chief, but his base was outside Cavite, his recent military record was less successful than that of the Cavite commanders, and he arrived as a figure of authority in a province where local leaders held real power. Aguinaldo and the Magdalo, for their part, had achieved military prominence and were willing to consider a reorganization that would recognize it. Mariano Álvarez and the Magdiwang were closer to Bonifacio at first but also had their own interests. Personal feeling, family and local loyalty, ambition, and honor all played roles, but they are difficult to reconstruct with confidence from partisan sources.

The conflict also should not be reduced to a contest between plebeian revolutionaries and wealthy elites. Cavite's leaders included men of local property and education, but the Magdiwang and Magdalo both had mixed memberships; Bonifacio's supporters included people of different social standing; and the elite-versus-masses interpretation associated with Agoncillo simplifies a more varied reality. Differences of region, of institutional loyalty to the Katipunan versus the new revolutionary government, and of personal rivalry cut across social class.

The practical questions of jurisdiction deserve emphasis. If a Magdiwang official requested arms from a Magdalo town, who could compel their transfer? If a commander were accused of withholding aid, which institution could hear the complaint? Who decided whether a local officer held a military commission or a civil appointment? These were matters of war-making as well as political prestige. The revolutionary organization had emerged clandestinely and had not possessed a recognized constitution for governing territory, so local successes generated new administrative questions faster than the central councils could resolve them. To describe the dispute as mere personal jealousy would minimize those institutional problems.

Later testimony about the conflict is unusually shaped by its consequences. Men who supported Aguinaldo in March 1897 had incentives to remember Tejeros as a necessary rationalization of government; men loyal to Bonifacio had reasons to emphasize violations of rules and insults against the Supremo. The historian's task is to reconstruct the sequence before allowing subsequent victory, defeat, or national commemoration to judge legitimacy retrospectively. Accounts should be read alongside the documented correspondence and organizational acts published by Richardson, including the Acta de Tejeros and the Naic Military Agreement. Neither a formal election nor prior standing automatically settled the issue of consent among all revolutionary units, especially those not represented at the convention.

Historical Debate II

Was Revolutionary Fragmentation Primarily Ideological, Organizational, Regional, or Personal?

The question: What best explains the divisions among revolutionary leaders in Cavite in late 1896 and early 1897?

Class and ideology: Agoncillo's influential reading emphasized a contrast between Bonifacio's plebeian, popular orientation and an ilustrado or propertied element that sought to control the revolution. Critics note that the factions were not cleanly divided by class.
Organizational and institutional: The Katipunan was a secret society with a Supreme Council; the Cavite councils were territorial governments. Conflict over whether the older structure or a new one should prevail was a conflict about institutions.
Regional and local: Cavite's leaders had local bases and loyalties, and the arrival of an outsider with claims of authority raised questions of territory and local autonomy.
Personal and political rivalry: Ambition, perceived insult, and mutual suspicion cannot be excluded, though the sources are often partisan.

Evidence: Memoirs, such as those of Aguinaldo and Santiago Álvarez, letters, the proceedings of the Tejeros Convention, and documents collected by later historians, including Richardson. All are shaped by the later outcome.

Points of disagreement: Scholars disagree about causation and interpretation more than about the broad sequence of events.

Why it matters: The answer affects whether the revolution's division is understood as a failure of unity, a clash of rival institutions, or a normal feature of a decentralized rising.

Student task: Identify which kinds of evidence would best distinguish institutional from personal explanations, and explain why these categories may overlap.

Section 117.14

The Tejeros Convention, March 22, 1897

In March 1897, with Spanish forces pressing the province, leaders of the two Cavite councils and Bonifacio's followers gathered at Tejeros, in San Francisco de Malabon, to organize a unified revolutionary government. The NHCP confirms the date of March 22, 1897. Bonifacio presided. The assembly voted by ballot for officers, and the results, in the standard account, named Aguinaldo as president, Mariano Trías as vice president, Artemio Ricarte as captain-general, Emiliano Riego de Dios as director of war, and Bonifacio as director of the interior. Aguinaldo was absent, engaged in military operations near Pasong Santol, and did not attend the election of the officer who would head the new government.

The proceedings then took a turn that proved decisive. According to accounts by participants, including Bonifacio's own letter, an objection was raised to Bonifacio's election as director of the interior by Daniel Tirona, who argued that the office should go to a person with legal training. The remark was understood by Bonifacio as an insult. He reacted angrily, reportedly drawing a weapon, and declared that as chair he would not accept the proceedings, which he said were tainted by irregularity. He and his supporters left. The next day Aguinaldo took an oath as president at Santa Cruz de Malabon (Tanza), and the new government began to function. Bonifacio's group later signed the Naic Military Agreement in April, which proposed a different government and rejected the Tejeros results.

The institutional question was fundamental. Who possessed the authority to reorganize the revolution? Bonifacio claimed that the Katipunan, with its Supreme Council and Supremo, was the legitimate structure and that the convention lacked authority to set it aside or that the election was fraudulent. Aguinaldo's supporters argued that the revolutionary situation required a government capable of directing a war, that the vote had been conducted by the assembled leaders, and that Bonifacio had accepted the convention's authority by presiding and by standing for office. Each view had a coherent logic, and neither could be validated by an impartial constitutional authority, because none existed. Revolutionary legitimacy rested on consent, success, and recognition, all of which were contested.

The election's procedures and results are described differently by different sources. Questions about the counting of ballots, the presence of voters from particular councils, the role of absent candidates, and whether the convention had agreed in advance to abide by its results appear in the testimony. Historians have used such discrepancies to argue both that the election was irregular and that Bonifacio's rejection was an unreasonable repudiation of a decision he had helped to arrange. The sources do not resolve the question, and the strong partisan interests of the participants counsel caution in accepting any single account.

Tejeros can be examined as an election conducted amid a military emergency rather than as the birth of an uncontested republic. Delegates assembled while Spanish forces were advancing through Cavite, and the competing councils had no broadly accepted rule for determining which constituencies could vote. The choice to elect officers rather than merely discuss defense transformed the gathering's stakes. Bonifacio's role as presiding officer and his earlier standing in the Katipunan did not remove the possibility that delegates preferred another leader. Equally, the fact that an election was held did not establish that its composition, procedures, or eventual authority would be accepted by people who understood the Katipunan as the existing government.

The insult over Bonifacio's selection as director of the interior made the dispute personal, but the office concerned the administration of the movement. Questions about qualifications, loyalty, and revolutionary seniority could thus become arguments over whose social standing entitled him to govern. Bonifacio's subsequent declaration that the proceedings were void and the opposing group's insistence on the elections' legitimacy created rival chains of command. A source such as the Acta de Tejeros must be compared with private letters written in anger and with accounts supplied decades later. Reading such documents together makes clear that the disagreement was not settled by the text of a single resolution.

Section 117.15

The Arrest, Trial, and Execution of Andrés Bonifacio

After Tejeros, Bonifacio and his supporters established themselves in parts of Cavite, at Naic and then Indang, and issued documents rejecting the Tejeros government. The Aguinaldo government treated this as a threat to the unity of the revolution at a moment when the Spanish offensive was advancing. In late April forces under Colonel Agapito Bonzón were sent to arrest Bonifacio. In the confrontation that followed, Bonifacio's brother Ciriaco was killed, and Bonifacio, reportedly wounded, was taken with his brother Procopio. The sequence of events and the degree of resistance are given differently by different witnesses.

The Bonifacio brothers were tried before a council of war at Maragondon, headed by General Mariano Noriel, in early May 1897. They were charged with sedition and treason in connection with their rejection of the government and with an alleged plot against it. The NHCP records the court-martial. The proceedings found them guilty and sentenced them to death. According to Aguinaldo's later accounts, he initially commuted the sentence but reversed that decision after being urged by officers, including Noriel and Pío del Pilar, who argued that sparing Bonifacio would endanger the revolution. The brothers were executed on May 10, 1897, in the hills near Maragondon, under the supervision of Colonel Lázaro Macapagal.

The legitimacy of the trial has been disputed since 1897. Critics argue that the tribunal was composed of officers of the government that Bonifacio had rejected, that he did not receive a fair hearing, that the charges conflated political disagreement with treason, and that the killing of the Supremo was an act of political elimination. Defenders argue that a revolutionary government at war could not tolerate a rival armed authority, that Bonifacio had been given opportunities to submit, that the council acted under the rules the revolution then recognized, and that the final decision was made under military pressure. The surviving documents of the trial, preserved in the Philippine archives and discussed in published scholarship, have been analyzed by historians with differing conclusions, and questions about their completeness and use remain.

Responsibility is difficult to allocate. Aguinaldo, as head of the government, bore ultimate political responsibility, a point that even his defenders do not deny; his later statements acknowledged that he had approved the sentence. Noriel, del Pilar, and others had roles in the proceedings and in urging execution. The extent to which each acted from conviction, fear, ambition, or duty is not established by direct evidence, and attribution of precise motives should be avoided. Bonifacio's own conduct, including his rejection of the convention and the formation of a rival body, also formed part of the context in which the council acted.

The proceedings against Bonifacio and Procopio must be situated within the collapsing Cavite military situation. A government confronting Spanish advances might regard a rival claim to authority as a danger to unity; that argument explains why charges could be brought, but it does not establish that the accused received a fair hearing or that execution was necessary. Much of the surviving record was produced by officers associated with the victorious faction or by later witnesses with personal loyalties. Historians need to distinguish the charges, the court's findings, the confirmation or commutation of sentence, and the subsequent order under which the brothers were killed. Collapsing those stages into a single act by one leader can conceal responsibility rather than clarify it.

The geographical record is also more specific than a generic reference to a mountain outside Maragondon. NHCP markers associate the execution of May 10, 1897 with Nagpatong near Mount Buntis, while other descriptions identify Mount Buntis more broadly. This is a difference of scale and local reference, not proof of unrelated execution sites. A careful account should report the marker evidence while noting that reconstructing the final movements of the prisoners depends partly on retrospective testimony. The episode subsequently became central to rival narratives of the Philippine nation: national commemoration elevated Bonifacio, while defenders of Aguinaldo sought to interpret the decision as an emergency response. Neither later use supplies independent proof of the disputed details.

Historical Debate III

The Legitimacy of Bonifacio's Trial and Responsibility for His Execution

The question: Was the trial a legitimate exercise of revolutionary authority, or a political removal of a rival, and who bore responsibility?

The trial as illegitimate: Agoncillo and others argued that the proceedings lacked impartiality, that the evidence of a plot was weak, and that Bonifacio was a victim of factional ambition. This view shaped Bonifacio's later image as a martyr.
The trial as defensible or necessary: Other writers, including some defenders of Aguinaldo, argued that a government at war could not tolerate a rival command, that Bonifacio had himself repudiated the government, and that the sentence followed from the council's findings.
Source-critical approaches: More recent scholarship, including Richardson's documentary work and Glenn May's study of how Bonifacio's image was constructed, examines the surviving record, its gaps, and the political uses to which the episode was later put.

Evidence: Trial records, letters, memoirs by participants, and later testimony.

Points of disagreement: Whether the charges were substantiated, whether the trial met contemporary standards, who made the final decision, and how to weigh later accounts.

Why it matters: The question bears on the character of the first Philippine revolutionary government and on how the nation remembers its founders.

Student task: Evaluate the three approaches, identify the strongest evidence each relies on, and explain why neither hero worship nor condemnation of Aguinaldo alone is a sufficient historical conclusion.

Section 117.16

The Republic and Pact of Biak-na-Bato, 1897

The Spanish offensive in Cavite continued, and Aguinaldo's forces withdrew from the province in the spring of 1897. The new Governor-General, Fernando Primo de Rivera, who had replaced Polavieja in April, combined military pressure with offers of amnesty. Aguinaldo moved north with his officers and followers, and by mid-1897 had established a base in the mountains at Biak-na-Bato in Bulacan, a defensible location with access to supplies. In November a constitution, the Constitution of Biak-na-Bato, was promulgated, creating a Republic of the Philippines with a Supreme Council, with Aguinaldo as president. The NHCP documents the Biak-na-Bato revolutionary government.

Negotiations were conducted by Pedro Paterno, an ilustrado lawyer who offered his services as intermediary between Primo de Rivera and Aguinaldo. They produced an agreement in December 1897. The terms, as reconstructed from the available documents and later accounts, included the departure of Aguinaldo and a group of his associates into exile, the payment by Spain of a sum in installments, and Spanish promises of reform and amnesty. Aguinaldo later reported a principal indemnity of 800,000 Mexican pesos, stipulated as payments of 400,000, 200,000 and 200,000 pesos under stated disarmament conditions; the separately discussed sums and reforms, actual payments and distribution require evidentiary distinction. The agreement also anticipated the surrender of arms. The leaders departed for Hong Kong at the end of December.

Compliance was disputed. The revolutionaries later asserted that Spain had promised specific reforms and had not delivered on payment or reform. Spanish officials presented the settlement as a surrender and pacification. Some revolutionary commanders did not accept the settlement and continued fighting, and the arms were not fully surrendered. In Hong Kong the exiles formed a body, often called the Hong Kong Junta, which controlled the funds received and considered how to resume the struggle. The evidence suggests that the funds were later used for the purchase of arms, though details are contested.

The pact has been interpreted in contrasting ways, discussed in the Continuity and Change feature below. For students, the immediate point is that the revolution, after eighteen months of war, had not defeated Spain but had not been defeated either. Its leadership was in exile, its government was a revolutionary institution without territory, and Spain's financial and military position was strained. Conditions for resumption existed, and they were fulfilled sooner than most observers could have foreseen.

The financial provisions can be stated more exactly than the general phrase 'hundreds of thousands.' In Aguinaldo's published account of the settlement, the principal indemnity was 800,000 Mexican pesos, payable in three promised installments of 400,000, 200,000, and 200,000 tied to the surrender of specified quantities of arms and the ceremonial restoration of peace. This account is a participant's retrospective narrative, not an independent audit of the treasury. A contemporary source collection records that Aguinaldo and his companions reached Hong Kong with the initial 400,000 pesos, while later installments and their disposition became disputed among officials and revolutionary groups. The distinction between agreed amounts, amounts delivered, and allocations to particular people is essential.

Even the description of the agreement as a treaty or a pact should be used analytically. Spanish officials hoped exile and disarmament would diminish military resistance; revolutionaries might understand it as a truce pending reform or a chance to preserve resources for renewed struggle. Promises involving religious orders, administrative change, and amnesty appear differently in participants' accounts and the formal documents. That discrepancy does not justify combining all reported terms into a single uncontested contract. Nor did the arrangement dissolve the revolution throughout the archipelago. Commanders outside Aguinaldo's immediate circle retained their own forces or grievances, and money in Hong Kong could become a disputed instrument of leadership as well as a means of obtaining weapons.

The Defeat of Spain and the Struggle to Establish Independence, 1898
Section 117.17

The Spanish-American War and Aguinaldo's Return, 1898

The war that broke out between Spain and the United States in April 1898 arose chiefly from the Cuban crisis, but it had immediate consequences for the Philippines, where Spain maintained a naval squadron. On May 1, 1898, a U.S. Asiatic Squadron commanded by Commodore George Dewey destroyed the Spanish fleet in Manila Bay. The victory gave the United States control of the bay but not of the city, since Dewey had no land force to occupy it. Spanish authorities remained in Manila, and the American commander awaited army reinforcements.

Aguinaldo and his associates in Hong Kong had meanwhile been in contact with American officials. In April, before the battle, he had met U.S. consuls in Singapore and Hong Kong, and after the victory he was conveyed back to the Philippines aboard a U.S. vessel, arriving on May 19, according to the Library of Congress chronology. What passed between Aguinaldo and the Americans has been fiercely debated. Aguinaldo later claimed that American officials had promised or implied recognition of Philippine independence. The Americans involved denied making any such commitment, and no documentary evidence of a formal pledge has been established. A careful account distinguishes cooperation against Spain, which is documented, from a promise of independence, which is not.

On his return Aguinaldo issued proclamations calling on Filipinos to resume the struggle, and revolutionary forces, reinforced by returning exiles and by arms, moved against Spanish positions. Within weeks they controlled much of Cavite and began operations elsewhere in Luzon. Many Filipino soldiers in Spanish service deserted or surrendered. Spanish garrisons in provincial towns, isolated and short of supplies, increasingly capitulated or were besieged. By June revolutionary forces had gathered around Manila, digging trenches and cutting off the Spanish garrison on the landward side.

Expectations differed. Many Filipinos expected independence, or at least a protectorate that would respect Filipino self-government; others hoped for American assistance in obtaining reforms. American commanders, whose government had not defined its war aims regarding the Philippines, wanted Filipino help against Spain without committing to a political future. The ambiguity was useful in the short term for both sides, because it allowed cooperation. It would prove costly later.

The American intervention was an international war with immediate consequences for a Philippine revolution already in progress. Dewey's success removed the strongest Spanish naval means of maintaining the Manila Bay position, but a naval victory did not itself settle the sovereignty of the countryside or give the Americans the ability to administer thousands of islands. Filipino armies performed much of the work of isolating Spanish garrisons outside the capital; the two forces therefore shared a short-term military objective without agreeing on the political result. This distinction helps explain the significance of the consular conversations. Statements by consuls could encourage expectations among Filipino leaders without amounting to a binding policy decision by the U.S. government.

The return from exile also changed the revolution's leadership structure. Aguinaldo brought experience, a reputation from the Cavite campaigns, and contacts with overseas supporters, while commanders who had continued to resist possessed forces and local claims of their own. Building a government from those overlapping relationships required appointments, correspondence, and promises that authority would eventually rest on representative institutions. It was not enough to proclaim victory over Spanish outposts. The revolution's international predicament arose precisely as its domestic governing capacity was expanding: occupying more towns increased the need for public revenue, security, courts, and recognition beyond the territories where republican officials could enforce decisions.

Section 117.18

The Declaration of Philippine Independence, June 12, 1898

On May 24 Aguinaldo established a dictatorial government, justified as necessary for the conduct of war. On June 12, 1898, at his house in Kawit, Cavite, he proclaimed the independence of the Philippines. The NHCP identifies the Kawit site and the date. The Act of Declaration of Independence was drafted in Spanish and read before an assembly; it was signed by a large number of Filipinos, and in the usual account also by an American officer, whose presence has been interpreted variously. The new national flag, which had been prepared in Hong Kong, was displayed, and a march composed for the occasion by Julián Felipe was played; it later became the basis of the national anthem.

The text proclaimed that the Filipino people were free and independent, absolved of allegiance to the Spanish crown, and, in a phrase that has attracted much commentary, placed themselves under the protection of the United States. The wording reflects the political situation: the revolutionary leadership sought American support while asserting sovereignty. It also illustrates the ambiguity of the situation, since a declaration of independence that invoked the protection of a foreign power shows how the revolutionaries sought a place in the international order that did not yet exist.

Institutions followed. Apolinario Mabini, a paralyzed lawyer and a gifted political thinker who had been a member of the reform circle, joined Aguinaldo's government as its chief adviser and became the principal author of a decree of June 23 that replaced the dictatorship with a revolutionary government, with a cabinet and a structure of provincial and municipal administration. These decrees constituted an attempt to create a government that was not simply a military command and that could claim legitimacy by providing for civil administration, the election of local officials, and, eventually, a constituent assembly.

The declaration raised the problem of how proclamation relates to effective control and recognition. In international practice of the time, the recognition of a new state depended on its capacity to maintain a stable government over a defined territory, and on the decisions of other states. In June 1898 the revolutionary government controlled much of Luzon outside Manila but did not control the capital, and no major power recognized it. The declaration was thus a consequential assertion of sovereignty, which shaped Filipino political understanding and later claims, but it did not settle the question of who would control the archipelago.

The Declaration also needs to be read with attention to the kind of political community that could be present at Kawit. The assembly included military officers and representatives associated with the revolutionary leadership, but its claims reached across an archipelago with many populations that had not elected delegates to that ceremony. That fact does not make the declaration meaningless. Independence movements often state universal claims before they possess the administrative means to represent everyone. It does mean that the text should be treated as a program for creating sovereignty, not a census of freely expressed opinion among all inhabitants of the islands.

The new government faced concrete tests beyond a flag or anthem. Local officials had to establish relations with revolutionary commanders, collect supplies, handle property disputes, and decide how to treat municipal personnel formerly appointed under Spain. The shift from Aguinaldo's May 24 dictatorial decree to the June 23 revolutionary government represented an attempt to put executive action within a more durable public framework. Mabini's role shows why intellectual and legal work mattered during military campaigning: a government preparing to bargain with outsiders needed defined institutions and a claim to legitimate authority. The constitution-making debate that followed in the autumn of 1898 developed from these problems rather than appearing suddenly when the Malolos Congress assembled.

Section 117.19

The Spanish Collapse, the Battle of Manila, and Competing Control

During the summer of 1898, Spanish authority outside Manila eroded. Revolutionary forces took control of Cavite, Batangas, Laguna, Bulacan, Pampanga, and other provinces; Spanish garrisons surrendered or were isolated; and local revolutionary governments were organized. Some isolated Spanish detachments held out far longer, as in the siege of the church at Baler, which was not lifted until 1899. The pattern was one of a collapse of practical Spanish power in the countryside, accompanied by the extension of the revolutionary government's administration, though not uniformly.

U.S. Army forces under General Wesley Merritt arrived in June and July and took over positions on the southern side of Manila, relieving or replacing some of the trenches dug by Filipino forces. Relations between the two armies were tense: Americans refused to share their plans, and Aguinaldo's officers suspected that the United States wished to exclude them from the city. The Spanish governor, Fermín Jáudenes, was aware that he could not hold out indefinitely and preferred to surrender to Americans rather than to the Filipinos, whom the Spanish regarded as rebels. Negotiations were conducted through intermediaries, including the Belgian consul.

On August 13 the American assault on Manila took place, in what is commonly described as a prearranged and limited engagement, after which the Spanish authorities surrendered. The NHCP's registry confirms that Manila's Spanish government surrendered to American forces on August 13, 1898. Filipino forces were not permitted to enter the city, and were excluded from the capitulation arrangements. A peace protocol between the United States and Spain had been signed in Washington the previous day, though news of it had not reached Manila; it provided for American occupation of the city and bay pending a treaty.

Three distinct situations coexisted after August 13. Spain had lost practical power in Manila and most of the archipelago. The United States had occupied Manila by force of arms and held it under military authority, with its eventual claim to the islands undecided. The Filipino revolutionary government claimed independence and controlled much of Luzon. Each of these positions had a different legal and political basis, and none had been accepted by the others. The tension between occupation, capitulation, and claims of sovereignty set the stage for the final events of the year.

The events at Manila revealed the difference between allied military activity and an alliance over sovereignty. American troops arriving by sea needed land positions from which to approach the city, while Filipino forces had already invested substantial effort in isolating its defenders. The Americans' decision to arrange the capitulation without Filipino participation therefore had immediate symbolic and practical consequences. Entry into the capital would have provided influence over archives, customs revenue, administration, and the public announcement of authority. Exclusion was not merely a matter of wounded pride among commanders; it affected the institutions through which an independent government might claim to govern the country's principal port.

The August 12 peace protocol was signed before the American attack on Manila the following day, but the transmission of information and the military arrangements on the ground complicate a simple narrative of an unnecessary battle. Spanish and American leaders had incentives to choreograph a surrender that preserved Spanish military honor and avoided fighting with Filipino forces. Meanwhile revolutionary leaders were not parties to the protocol or the capitulation terms. Students should distinguish what leaders in Washington, the American command at Manila, Spanish officials, and Filipino commanders knew at particular moments. Later hindsight can conceal the uncertainty that allowed tactical cooperation and strategic distrust to coexist in August 1898.

Historical Vocabulary III
Sovereignty, independence, military occupation, capitulation, and diplomatic recognition

Definition. Sovereignty is supreme authority within a territory. Independence is the condition of not being subject to another state's sovereignty. Military occupation is the control of territory by an armed force without necessarily transferring sovereignty. Capitulation is the formal surrender of a military force or place. Diplomatic recognition is a state's formal acceptance of another entity as a state or government.

Historical context. In 1898 these concepts diverged. Spain lost control but had not transferred sovereignty; the United States occupied Manila but had not yet acquired the islands; the Filipino government claimed independence without recognition.

Why it matters. The distinctions explain why Spain's defeat did not resolve the question of Philippine independence.

Potential misunderstanding. A declaration of independence is not the same as recognized independence, and military occupation is not the same as legal annexation.

Section 117.20

Malolos, the Treaty of Paris, and the Unresolved Question of Independence

The Philippine revolutionary government moved to Malolos, Bulacan, in the autumn of 1898. The Malolos Congress, which the Library of Congress records as opening on September 15 at the church of Barasoain, was assembled to draft a constitution and to give the revolutionary government a representative basis. Its delegates were drawn largely from the educated and propertied classes, some elected under conditions of war and others appointed, and the body's claim to represent the whole archipelago rested on a combination of election, selection, and the practical impossibility of holding a complete vote. Pedro Paterno served as its president.

The Congress became the site of debate about the form of the republic. Felipe Calderón, a lawyer, drafted a constitution that was adopted in January 1899 and established a republican government with a strong legislature, with separation of church and state among its principles. Apolinario Mabini, who as chief adviser had earlier outlined a different constitutional program with a stronger executive, expressed doubts about the legislature-centered design and about the exposure of a government at war. These debates concerned representation, executive authority, the role of the church, and the relationship between the government and the military, and they reflected differences of outlook among Filipino leaders that cannot be reduced to a single division.

Diplomacy was an additional front. Felipe Agoncillo, a Batangas lawyer, was sent abroad as the revolutionary government's envoy and sought recognition in Washington and Paris. The Americans declined to receive him formally as the representative of a government, and he was unable to participate in the negotiations that determined the islands' fate. Filipino leaders had counted on international sympathy, but major powers accorded little weight to a revolutionary government that had not been recognized.

Spain and the United States negotiated in Paris from October. The Treaty of Paris, signed on December 10, 1898, provided in Article III that Spain would cede the Philippine Islands to the United States, which would pay Spain a sum of twenty million dollars. The treaty text is preserved by the U.S. State Department. No Filipino delegate took part in the negotiations. Spain, having lost control of most of the archipelago, ceded sovereignty that it could not effectively exercise, and the United States acquired a legal title that was disputed by the Filipino government and not yet backed by control outside Manila. The treaty still required ratification by the U.S. Senate.

The end of 1898 therefore presented a fundamental political contradiction. Filipino revolutionaries claimed independence and were building their own government, with a congress, a constitution in preparation, and administration over much of Luzon. The United States, through its treaty with Spain, claimed the archipelago as a possession. President McKinley's proclamation of "benevolent assimilation," issued in December and published in Manila in January 1899, made American intentions explicit, and in January 1899 the Philippine Republic was inaugurated at Malolos with Aguinaldo as president. How these incompatible claims developed into armed conflict is the subject of Chapter 118.

The Malolos constitutional chronology should be treated with precision. The Congress opened in September 1898 and debated a constitutional design during the final months of Spanish rule. The draft was approved by the Congress on November 29 and considered by Aguinaldo in December; its final formal adoption by Congress occurred on January 20, 1899, followed by presidential promulgation on January 21. Thus the constitutional movement is part of the 1898 story, while the completed Constitution is a document from the next year. Mentioning it as an immediate sequel can help students understand competing claims to sovereignty, provided the chapter does not relabel an 1899 law as a text already in force when the Treaty of Paris was signed.

The Paris settlement crystallized a political problem visible long before December. The Spanish government could negotiate a territorial cession as a recognized state, whereas Filipino representatives had neither a seat at the conference nor recognition by the powers conducting it. Yet the revolutionary government exercised authority over substantial areas beyond Manila, making the treaty's legal logic diverge sharply from conditions on the ground. No single act of international transfer could settle how populations under revolutionary administrations would respond to a newly asserting foreign sovereign. The closing chapter should therefore pose an open question: how would the United States reconcile treaty-based claims with an organized republican movement that regarded independence as already achieved? The military collision belongs to Chapter 118, not to a retrospective assertion that it was unavoidable.

Chapter Summary

This chapter traced how a clandestine society founded in Manila in July 1892 became an armed revolution and the nucleus of an independent government. The Katipunan emerged after the suppression of La Liga Filipina convinced a portion of Manila's reformist milieu that appeals to Spain had failed. It adopted secrecy, ritual, and a commitment to independence, and it recruited more widely than the reform movement had, among clerks, artisans, traders, provincial officials, and women, though the exact membership and its social proportions remain uncertain.

Andrés Bonifacio, the movement's leading organizer, came from Manila's working world and acquired his education through work, reading, and association rather than formal schooling. With Emilio Jacinto and others he built a structure of councils whose ethical statement, the Kartilya, and whose vocabulary of kalayaan and bayan drew on both European political ideas and local moral and religious language. Historians such as Agoncillo, Ileto, and Richardson have read this record differently, emphasizing social conflict, popular religious idiom, and documentary reconstruction respectively.

The discovery of the society in August 1896 forced a decision to rise. The traditional story of its discovery rests on limited testimony, and the precise place and date of the Cry remain disputed, although the symbolic repudiation of colonial authority and the decision to take up arms are not. The attacks around Manila, including San Juan del Monte on August 30, failed against concentrated Spanish power, and the colonial response, with martial law and widespread arrests and executions, widened the conflict.

Warfare was shaped by geography and logistics. Rebels lacked firearms and ammunition, relied on local provisioning, and fought in separate forces. In Cavite, with its organized municipalities, waterways, and local councils, the Magdalo and Magdiwang held territory and governed it, and Emilio Aguinaldo rose to prominence through military success. Spanish reinforcements and the offensive of early 1897 pushed the Cavite rebels back. Civilians bore the costs of requisition, displacement, and reprisal from both sides, and the revolution's participation varied across regions and communities.

The crisis of 1897 exposed the revolution's problem of legitimacy. At Tejeros on March 22 a convention elected a new government with Aguinaldo as president; Bonifacio, who rejected the proceedings, was arrested, tried by a revolutionary council of war, and executed on May 10. The episode is explained by institutional, regional, and personal factors that interacted, and historians disagree about the fairness of the trial and about responsibility, while the sources are largely partisan.

The Pact of Biak-na-Bato in December 1897 ended large-scale fighting through a negotiated settlement involving exile and payments, and its meaning as defeat, truce, compromise, or reorganization depends on perspective. The outbreak of war between Spain and the United States in 1898 transformed the situation. Aguinaldo returned on May 19, revolutionary forces resumed the war and won control of much of Luzon, and independence was proclaimed at Kawit on June 12, with Mabini helping to shape revolutionary government.

Spain's loss of power did not produce recognized independence. The American occupation of Manila on August 13, from which Filipino forces were excluded, created a three-way situation of Spanish defeat, American occupation, and Filipino sovereignty claims. The Malolos Congress and the drafting of a republican constitution gave the revolutionary government institutional form, but the Treaty of Paris of December 10, 1898, transferred Spanish claims to the United States without Filipino participation.

The chapter's argument is that the revolution was socially broad, geographically uneven, institutionally inventive, and politically divided, and that its struggle for sovereignty remained unresolved after Spain's defeat because declaring independence, exercising power, and obtaining recognition are distinct achievements. The conflict that followed is the subject of Chapter 118.

Chapter Reflection

The Philippine Revolution matters because it was among the first large anticolonial revolutions in Asia, a statement that must be qualified, as such comparisons depend on definitions, but its significance lies less in priority than in the questions it raised. How does a movement of diverse people establish a government? Who may speak for a nation not yet fully formed? What is the relationship between declaring a state and being recognized as one? These questions shaped later Philippine politics and recur across the decolonizing world.

The chapter also shows how history is made after the fact. The Cry, the figure of Bonifacio, the reputation of Aguinaldo, and the meaning of Biak-na-Bato have each been shaped by later memory and political use. Understanding the revolution requires reading its sources critically and recognizing that scholars have changed their interpretations as new documents and questions emerged.

The revolution's legacies were mixed. It created institutions, symbols, and political experience that outlasted Spanish rule, and it exposed divisions of region, class, and leadership that persisted. The Katipunan's vocabulary and the revolutionary government's claims informed nationalist movements in the following decades, discussed in later chapters.

For the larger textbook, the chapter marks the end of Spanish rule and the beginning of a new colonial question. The Philippines was entangled in the imperial competition of 1898, but Filipino revolutionaries were agents in that history, not merely objects of it. Chapter 118 examines how the contradiction at the end of this chapter became a war.
