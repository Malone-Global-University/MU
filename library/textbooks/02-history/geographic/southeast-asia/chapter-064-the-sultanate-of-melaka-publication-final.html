The Sultanate of Melaka

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Melaka was not simply a fortunate harbor on a famous strait. Between about 1400 and 1500 it became a functioning maritime kingdom: a court that claimed Malay and Islamic legitimacy, a body of officials who governed a crowded port, a diplomatic actor that secured recognition from China while managing pressure from Ayutthaya, and a market whose reliability drew merchants from the Mediterranean-connected Indian Ocean to the Ryukyu Islands.

The earliest securely dated documents concerning Melaka come not from the Malay world but from the court of the Ming dynasty. Chinese imperial records, preserved in the Ming Shilu (the "Veritable Records" of the Ming reigns), report that in 1403 the Yongle emperor's government ordered an envoy sent to a settlement on the west coast of the Malay Peninsula, and that within two years the ruler of that place had been received, recognized as a king, and presented with the insignia of investiture. The records call the place Manlajia, the Chinese rendering of the name that appears in English as Melaka or, in older spelling, Malacca. Their significance is not that they show Melaka suddenly coming into being. It is that they give historians a fixed point of documentation in a field where almost every other early reference comes from later memory.

From that point, the sultanate's history is unusually dense with evidence, and unusually contested. Within a century, Melaka was described by a Portuguese apothecary, Tomé Pires, as a city where merchants spoke dozens of languages and where several officials called shahbandars looked after the affairs of separate merchant communities. Malay court tradition remembered it as the seat of a line of rulers descended from earlier Malay kings, advised by a bendahara and other great ministers, and governed by laws that regulated everything from royal prerogative to the conduct of a ship's captain. Chinese sources record that a fleet commander of the Ming treasure voyages used the port as a base. Archaeology, constrained by centuries of continuous urban occupation on the site, adds comparatively little to this documentary picture. Each body of evidence was produced by people with their own purposes, and the chapter treats them accordingly.

The governing question of this chapter is how Melaka developed from a small coastal political center around 1400 into one of the most influential commercial and political powers of fifteenth-century Southeast Asia. The question does not assume that the outcome was inevitable. The Strait of Malacca had been a corridor of exchange for many centuries before Melaka existed, and earlier ports and kingdoms had occupied the same waterways, as earlier chapters on Srivijaya and its successors have shown (Chapters 28, 29, and 47) and as Chapter 63 showed for the Muslim courts of northern Sumatra. Geography offered opportunities. It did not supply a ruler's judgment, a merchant's confidence, a harbor master's honesty, or an ally's recognition. Those had to be built, and the chapter's central task is to explain how.

For that reason the chapter treats Melaka as a state and not merely a marketplace. Its rulers drew on Malay conceptions of kingship, adopted Islamic titles and patronage, negotiated with a Chinese emperor and with the rulers of Ayutthaya, Pasai, and Java, and relied on a set of officials whose functions linked government to commerce. How much of this machinery existed in 1410, and how much was described by observers in 1512, is an open question, and the chapter repeatedly distinguishes the documented from the retrospective. It also avoids a narrative in which commercial prosperity automatically led either to greatness or to collapse. The Portuguese conquest of 1511 belongs to Chapter 69. Here it appears only as the moment at which several of our best sources were written.

The chapter is organized in five divisions. The first examines evidence, geography, and the foundation of the polity. The second turns to dynastic politics, Ming diplomacy, Siamese pressure, and royal administration. The third examines the port itself: customs, merchants, commodities, and everyday life. The fourth considers regional diplomacy, the Sejarah Melayu, and the laws of Melaka. The fifth examines the sultanate at its height, evaluates historians' explanations for its rise, and assesses its legacy. The wider processes of conversion and religious change across the Malay-Indonesian world are left to Chapter 65; the northern maritime zone to Chapter 66.

A note on names. This textbook uses the modern Malaysian spelling "Melaka" for the city and polity, and "Strait of Malacca" for the waterway, following common English usage. Rulers' names appear in the forms most widely used in scholarship. Where Chinese, Portuguese, and Malay sources render a name differently, the chapter says so, because the question of whether two names refer to one person is sometimes itself a historical problem. Dates for the foundation, for individual reigns, and for conversion are conventions or approximations unless stated otherwise.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish contemporary evidence for Melaka's early history (Ming records, Ma Huan's account) from retrospective traditions such as the Sejarah Melayu and from Portuguese testimony written after the conquest.
  2. Explain why geography on the Strait of Malacca created commercial opportunities for Melaka without guaranteeing its success.
  3. Trace the sequence of events from Parameswara's association with Palembang and Singapura to the establishment and Ming recognition of Melaka, noting where chronology is uncertain.
  4. Analyze the meaning of tribute, investiture, and diplomatic recognition in Melaka's relations with the Ming court, and distinguish recognition from protection.
  5. Describe the principal officials of the sultanate (bendahara, temenggung, laksamana, and shahbandars) and assess what is known about their functions and when.
  6. Explain the mechanisms by which Melaka earned revenue and attracted merchants, including harbor administration, customs, brokers, and political security.
  7. Reconstruct aspects of everyday life in a cosmopolitan port, including the experiences of merchants, women, laborers, and enslaved people, with attention to what the sources do not reveal.
  8. Compare Melaka's relations with Ayutthaya, Majapahit-era Java, Pasai, and other regional centers, distinguishing alliance, tribute, claim, and administration.
  9. Evaluate the Sejarah Melayu and the Melaka legal texts as layered sources, separating the age of a tradition from the date of a surviving manuscript.
  10. Assess competing historical explanations for Melaka's rise and describe the sultanate's legacy for later Malay political culture without treating its decline as inevitable.

Historical Context

By the late fourteenth century, the Strait of Malacca and its approaches had been the setting for political experiments for more than a thousand years. Chapters 28, 29, and 47 traced the rise and transformation of Srivijaya, whose Palembang-centered networks and Buddhist connections had linked the Malay world to India and China. By about 1400 Srivijaya as a distinct power had long since been transformed, and the political landscape of eastern Sumatra and the peninsula was fragmented among Malay rulers, port polities, and Orang Laut communities (sea-oriented peoples who provided boats, pilots, and maritime labor). Java's court, remembered in tradition as Majapahit (Chapters 50 to 52), claimed or exerted influence across the archipelago, though the extent of its authority is a matter of debate. Northern Sumatra had acquired Muslim courts, above all Samudera Pasai (Chapter 63), whose rulers, scholars, and merchants connected the Malay world to wider Islamic networks.

On the mainland, Ayutthaya (Chapter 57) was expanding its power in the Chao Phraya basin and claimed influence down the peninsula. Peninsular ports on both coasts were drawn into its orbit to differing degrees, and later Malay and Siamese traditions both remember conflict over the status of Melaka. Farther east, the Ming dynasty, founded in 1368, pursued an active diplomacy toward maritime Asia in the early fifteenth century. The court sought recognition from foreign rulers through the tributary system, and between 1405 and 1433 it dispatched large fleets under the eunuch commander Zheng He to Southeast Asia, South Asia, and beyond. These voyages are best understood as an episode in state diplomacy, not as the beginning of Chinese maritime trade, which long predated them. Their consequence for Southeast Asia was to alter, for a time, the diplomatic opportunities available to ports willing to engage with the Ming court.

The commercial environment was shaped by the monsoons, discussed in Chapter 4. Ships traveling between the Bay of Bengal and the South China Sea depended on alternating wind seasons, and merchants often needed to wait in port for favorable winds. A port that could host such merchants safely, store their goods, and provide trustworthy exchange had an advantage. The wider Indian Ocean commercial and religious networks, examined in Chapter 62, supplied much of the merchant community that Melaka would attract. This chapter does not repeat that general analysis. Its task is to explain how a single polity organized itself to benefit from those networks, and why it, rather than another port, did so.

Students should also keep in mind the historiographical problem that runs through the chapter. Melaka was conquered in 1511, and the Malay court tradition about it was written down, in the form we have, only afterward, much of it in the successor states discussed in Chapter 81. The Portuguese accounts were produced by outsiders who had every reason to describe what they had seized. The older Chinese records are the closest to contemporary, but they are concerned primarily with diplomacy. Reading Melaka's history means moving carefully among these records.

Section 64.1

Reconstructing the Early History of Melaka

Few Southeast Asian polities are described by as many kinds of source as Melaka, yet few have a less certain beginning. Four bodies of evidence dominate. The first is Chinese: the Ming Shilu, which records diplomatic exchanges from 1403 onward, and the accounts of men who traveled with Zheng He's fleets, notably Ma Huan and Fei Xin, who described Melaka in the 1430s. These are the earliest sources close in time to the events they describe, but they were written to record the court's relations with foreign rulers and to inform readers in China, and they say little about Melaka's internal government.

The second body of evidence is Portuguese. Tomé Pires wrote his Suma Oriental in the years after 1511, and other Portuguese authors, including chroniclers who drew on the testimony of Melaka's former inhabitants, recorded the history of the sultanate that the Portuguese had just destroyed as an independent power. Their information was valuable because it came from people who had lived in Melaka or traded there, but it was gathered in the aftermath of conquest, with an eye to Portuguese commercial and strategic interests. The third body of evidence is the Malay tradition, above all the Sejarah Melayu (also called the Sulalat al-Salatin, "Genealogy of Kings") and the law texts, which survive in manuscripts copied long after the fifteenth century. The fourth is archaeological, and it is the thinnest. Melaka has been continuously inhabited, rebuilt by the Portuguese, the Dutch, and the British, and modernized in the twentieth century, so that excavation of fifteenth-century layers has been limited and fragmentary.

These limits shape what can be said. Historians can establish, from Chinese documents, that a polity called Manlajia existed by 1403 to 1405, that it had a ruler whom the Ming court recognized, and that it was in diplomatic contact with China and under pressure from Siam. They can reconstruct, from Pires and the Malay tradition, a picture of the sultanate's institutions around 1500. They cannot establish with confidence the year of Melaka's founding, the precise identity and succession of its first rulers, the date of the court's conversion to Islam, or the early form of its administration. The chapter marks these limits throughout, and the Source and Method Study below sets out the framework students should use.

Historical Source and Method Study
Four Questions for Every Melaka Source

When a source gives a fact about early Melaka, historians ask four questions before using it. First, how close is it in time? A Ming record of 1405 is closer to the event than a Malay text redacted in the early seventeenth century, though closeness does not guarantee accuracy. Second, who was the author and what was the purpose? A Ming official recording an embassy was interested in tribute and legitimacy. A Portuguese author was interested in commerce and conquest. A Malay court writer was interested in dynastic honor and instruction. Third, can the claim be corroborated? A fact reported independently by Chinese and Malay sources is more secure than one in a single source. Fourth, what kind of claim is it? A reported embassy and a reported miracle require different kinds of evidence.

Applied together, these questions allow a useful distinction. Documented events, such as Ming recognition in 1405, rest on contemporary records. Probable reconstructions, such as the overall structure of the administration c. 1500, rest on consistent later testimony. Traditions, such as the mouse-deer foundation story, are valuable as evidence of how Malays remembered their origins but are not evidence of what happened c. 1400. The chapter uses this language throughout.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might a Ming record be reliable on the date of an embassy but less reliable on Melaka's internal politics?
  2. Why does the fact that Pires wrote after the conquest affect how his description of institutions should be used?
  3. What kinds of questions cannot be answered by any of the four bodies of evidence?
Section 64.2

The Strait of Malacca, the Melaka River, and the Maritime Landscape

The Strait of Malacca runs for some 800 kilometers between the Malay Peninsula and the island of Sumatra, narrowing toward its southern end, where it opens into the Singapore Strait and the South China Sea. This is a general geographic description and not a measure of sailing distance, which depended on ships, seasons, and conditions. The strait connects the Bay of Bengal and Andaman Sea in the northwest with the South China Sea in the southeast, and it has long served as the most direct maritime passage between the Indian Ocean and East Asia. Other routes existed, including the Sunda Strait between Sumatra and Java and overland portage across the peninsula's narrower parts, as Chapter 26 showed. Melaka's rise was therefore not inevitable, and merchants always had alternatives.

The river mouth on which Melaka grew lies roughly midway along the peninsula's western coast. A tidal river flows into the strait there, and the area offered anchorage in the lee of the land, a source of fresh water, and access to the sea for small craft. The coast nearby is low and, in places, swampy, with mangrove margins and shifting mudflats typical of the strait's eastern shore; the surrounding hinterland was not an obvious agricultural base capable of feeding a large city. Rice and other staples therefore had to be imported, an important fact for understanding why Melaka depended on a flow of merchants and ships. The city's prosperity was bound up with commerce in a way that the prosperity of an inland agrarian capital such as Majapahit's was not.

Geography interacted with the monsoon system (Chapter 4). Ships from India crossing the Bay of Bengal and ships from China crossing the South China Sea relied on seasonal winds, and a port that stood near the meeting point of the two systems offered a place where merchants from both directions could trade within a manageable portion of the year and wait for a return wind. This advantage was real, but it was shared with several other ports along the strait and in northern Sumatra. A merchant sailing from Gujarat or Coromandel could find a market at Pasai, or at Kedah, or at ports on the Sumatran coast, or in Java. What Melaka offered had to be more than position.

The waters around the river mouth also provided human resources. The Orang Laut of the strait and nearby islands were skilled boatmen and pilots, and Malay tradition and Portuguese testimony associate them with the founders of Melaka. The surrounding region offered forest and marine products, tin from the peninsula, and, across the strait, the gold and pepper of Sumatra. Whether the settlement's political leaders could draw these resources into a functioning system was a question of organization and leadership.

Section 64.3

The Ming Court and the First Documentary References to Melaka

The Ming Shilu is a series of annals compiled by court historians for each reign. For the Yongle reign (1403 to 1424), the entries record the emperor's foreign relations in the year-by-year language of palace archives. These entries show that a court order of 28 October 1403 directed the eunuch Yin Qing to Melaka (his actual arrival may have been in 1404), and that by 1405 an embassy from the ruler, whom the records name Bai-li-mi-su-la (usually identified with Parameswara), had arrived at the capital. The emperor recognized him as king, bestowing a seal, patent, and robes. The record also notes that the ruler had been subject to pressure from Siam, and that the court issued a warning to the Siamese ruler concerning Melaka.

Two points require care. The first concerns the meaning of tribute and investiture. In the Ming conception, a foreign ruler who sent envoys with gifts ("tribute") and received recognition entered a relationship in which the emperor stood at the top of a hierarchy of kings. For the Chinese court, this was a statement of its own legitimacy. For the foreign ruler, the relationship offered concrete benefits: prestige, access to Chinese goods and markets, and a diplomatic counterweight to neighbors. Historians do not generally regard the tributary system as implying Chinese administration or conquest of the recipient state. A Southeast Asian ruler who accepted investiture was not thereby governed from Nanjing or Beijing.

The second point concerns what recognition actually secured. Ming sources show the court chiding Siam for its conduct toward Melaka, and Ma Huan, writing around 1433, reports that before the Chinese seal Melaka had paid an annual payment of gold to Siam and that afterward Siam ceased to press the claim. This is useful testimony about how the Chinese presence was perceived, but it should not be read as showing a Chinese guarantee of Melaka's security. The Ming court rarely sent forces to protect Southeast Asian tributaries, and the diplomatic backing it gave worked mainly by raising the political cost of an attack on a recognized king. We return to this issue in Section 64.6.

The Chinese records also show Melaka's rulers using the relationship deliberately. A ruler who visited the Ming court in person, as the records report one doing around 1411, was investing considerable resources in diplomacy, and the Ming court responded with gifts and honors. The surviving records also show a pattern in which later rulers sent envoys to report accessions and seek recognition, a practice that continued with less frequency after the Zheng He voyages ended in 1433.

The sequence itself is instructive. A dated court command, an envoy's actual journey, an embassy's reception, and an imperial investiture were different events even when later narratives collapse them into a single diplomatic 'opening.' Wang Gungwu's comparison of the annals and subsequent traditions helps identify these stages. The 1403 entry belongs to a state decision at the Chinese court; Melaka's reception of Yin Qing likely occurred afterward. The 1405 record establishes an exchange of embassies and recognition, not the founding date of the settlement. This distinction matters because apparent precision in official annals can obscure travel time and the initiative of Southeast Asian rulers.

Editorial paraphrase (not a verbatim excerpt):

The annals report that the emperor sent a eunuch envoy to the place the Chinese call Manlajia. They then record that its ruler dispatched an embassy bearing local products, that the emperor appointed him king of the country and conferred a seal, patent, and garments, and that the court addressed the ruler of Siam regarding Melaka. They also record that the emperor honored the polity with an inscription, and treat the relationship as one of loyal tribute offered to the Ming throne.

Analysis: The entries provide the earliest datable evidence for Melaka as a political entity with a ruler able to conduct diplomacy. They show the court responding to a specific regional situation, namely Melaka's tension with Siam, and indicate that Melaka's ruler sought recognition. They also reveal the language of the Chinese court, in which the foreign ruler's embassy is presented as submission.

Limitations: The annals offer almost nothing about Melaka's population, internal governance, economy, or religion, and the ruler's name is a Chinese transcription whose relation to a Malay or Sanskrit name has to be inferred. The court's account of tribute may exaggerate the degree of deference in a relationship the ruler may have viewed pragmatically. The text also says nothing of how the founding of the settlement happened.

Perspective: The source represents the Ming court's view. The Melaka ruler's own purposes, the Siamese perspective, and the voices of Melaka's residents and merchants are not represented.

Section 64.4

Parameswara, Singapura, and the Foundation of Melaka

Malay and Portuguese traditions agree on a broad outline of Melaka's origins while differing on details. According to the Sejarah Melayu, a royal line descended from a prince who had established a kingdom at Palembang later moved to Singapura, and the last ruler there, called Iskandar Shah, was driven out by an attack attributed to Majapahit. He fled northward and, while hunting near a river mouth, saw a small mouse-deer turn upon the dogs pursuing it. Taking this as an omen, he founded a city at the place and named it after a tree growing there. The story belongs to a wide family of foundation legends in which a ruler's choice of site is validated by an extraordinary sign, and it should be read as a statement about the kingship's character: a place where even the weak might resist the strong.

Pires gives a different outline. His account has a ruler called Parameswara leaving Palembang under pressure from Java, settling at Singapura after killing its local ruler, and then being driven out by forces from Siam or its dependents. After a period at Muar and elsewhere, he settled at Melaka, then a small place inhabited by fishermen, with the help of Orang Laut who came to follow him. Chinese sources name the ruler as Bai-li-mi-su-la, which is generally taken to represent Parameswara, and place his recognition in 1405. The overlap among these accounts supports the view that Melaka's founder had links to Palembang and to the Temasek or Singapura region, and that he had to relocate after conflict with powerful neighbors. Beyond this the accounts diverge, and the names, dates, and sequence of events differ.

The most difficult problem is whether Parameswara of the Chinese and Portuguese sources is the same man as the Iskandar Shah of the Sejarah Melayu. Some scholars identify them, treating the Malay name as an Islamic title adopted later or attributed retrospectively. Others hold that the Malay tradition has conflated two rulers, and a further argument is that the Ming records of 1414, which describe a son visiting China to report his father's death, show that the first ruler died by then and that a successor bearing an Islamic name followed him. Christopher Wake's study of Melaka's early kings and the reception of Islam shows how deeply these uncertainties run. The chapter therefore refers to Parameswara as the ruler known to the Chinese and Portuguese sources and treats the identification with Iskandar Shah as probable to some scholars and unproven.

What can be said securely is limited but significant. By the first years of the fifteenth century a ruler with a following established at the river mouth had gained Ming recognition. His origins lay in a Malay political tradition associated with Palembang and Singapura, and he relied on maritime communities to support his rule. The date "around 1400" is a convention that captures the period in which the settlement became a political center. Chapter 28 examined Palembang's earlier significance, and Chapter 47 traced the transformation of Srivijaya's heritage; Melaka's founders drew on this inheritance in the claims made for their lineage, but they built something new on a different site.

Historical Archaeology and Evidence Study
What Does the Ground Say About Early Melaka?

Archaeologists have found evidence of fourteenth-century occupation at Singapore's Fort Canning Hill, consistent with a significant settlement at Temasek in the century before Melaka, including imported ceramics and structures on the hill. This supports the plausibility of a Singapura phase but does not identify the people living there or confirm the traditional stories of a royal flight.

At Melaka itself the evidence is weaker. The royal hill above the harbor, later fortified by the Portuguese, and the harbor area have been repeatedly rebuilt, and the original shoreline has moved with coastal reclamation. Chinese ceramics of the fifteenth century and other finds attest trade, and the old Chinese burial ground on a nearby hill points to a significant Chinese presence in later periods, but no excavation has yet produced a clear plan of the fifteenth-century city or its palace. Reconstructions of the palace based on the Malay literary tradition are modern interpretations.

Lesson: The silence of the ground does not mean the polity was small; it means the evidence is hard to recover. Historians must hold archaeological absence and documentary presence together.

Division II — Sections 64.5–64.8
Royal Government, Dynastic Development, and Political Consolidation
Section 64.5

The Rise and Consolidation of Melaka, c. 1400–1450

The first half-century of Melaka's history was a period of political consolidation under uncertain conditions. The documentary record for these decades is mainly Chinese and therefore concerned with diplomacy, and the Malay tradition's account of the same decades is a dynastic narrative of rulers and marriages. Taken together they suggest three developments: the establishment of a recognized kingship, the building of external relationships, and the gradual extension of the port's authority over its immediate surroundings.

The establishment of kingship was a matter of both recognition and practice. Ming investiture gave the ruler an external validation that few of his neighbors could match. Within the port, the ruler needed supporters: Orang Laut communities who could provide boats and maritime labor, merchants willing to settle, and followers who had come with him from Palembang and Singapura. The sources suggest that he attracted these by offering security and by creating conditions under which trade could be conducted predictably. The comparatively small early settlement acquired a ruler's residence, and over time the elements of a court.

The Ming connection supplied important resources during these decades. The Zheng He fleets, which began in 1405 and ended in 1433, used Melaka as a station on their route, and Ma Huan describes a fortified storehouse where goods were kept during the fleets' stay. Chinese sources thus show Melaka providing a harbor for a very large imperial enterprise, which would have brought business to local suppliers and visibility to the port among merchants of many origins. The effects should not be overstated: Chinese ships were not the main source of the port's commerce, and the fleets' visits were episodic. But they gave the young polity prominence and security at a formative moment.

Relations with the neighboring regions also developed. Malay tradition remembers marriages and alliances with Pasai, and the Muslim connection with northern Sumatra gradually influenced the court, a subject taken up in Section 64.7 and in Chapter 65. Relations with Siam remained strained, since Melaka's emergence on the strait challenged Siamese claims on the peninsula. Java's own trading interests also meant that Melaka had to find a place within an existing commercial order. By the 1430s, Ma Huan describes Melaka as an established kingdom with a Muslim ruler, a sizeable population, and active foreign trade, which indicates that the early consolidation had succeeded.

Section 64.6

Ming Diplomacy, Siamese Pressure, and the Security of the Sultanate

A new port on a major strait was vulnerable. It stood near several more powerful neighbors, it controlled no extensive agricultural base, and it depended on the willingness of merchants to visit. Security therefore meant several things at once: protection against military attack, protection against subordination, and protection of the trade on which revenue depended. Melaka's rulers pursued all three through different means.

Relations with Ming China addressed the problem mainly by providing diplomatic cover. Recognition raised the cost of aggression, since an attack on a king whom the Chinese emperor had invested could be presented as an offense to the court. The Ming Shilu shows the court admonishing Siam, and Chinese sources attribute to the investiture a change in Siamese behavior. But the Ming did not station troops in Melaka or commit to defend it, and the voyages of Zheng He were not a standing garrison. Chinese diplomatic support was valuable but conditional, and as the Ming court withdrew from maritime activity after 1433, its capacity to affect events in the strait diminished. Melaka continued to send missions and to receive recognition, but increasingly it relied on its own resources.

Relations with Ayutthaya were more difficult. Ayutthaya (Chapter 57) was a powerful mainland state with ambitions in the peninsula, and several peninsular rulers acknowledged it to varying degrees. Ma Huan's remarks about the annual payment to Siam indicate that Melaka's early status in relation to Siam was unclear, and that the question was at stake. Malay tradition remembers repeated Siamese attacks on Melaka in the mid-fifteenth century and credits the defense to the minister Tun Perak and to the ruler's forces. Ayutthaya's own sources, as they survive, advance their own claims regarding the peninsula. Historians do not regard every battle described in the later tradition as independently verified, and the specific campaigns, their dates, and their outcomes must be treated with caution. What the competing traditions together suggest is that Melaka's independence from Siam was contested and that it was maintained through a combination of fighting and negotiation.

Security also depended on Melaka's relationships with the maritime communities of the strait. Orang Laut vessels could protect or threaten merchant shipping, and rulers who secured their loyalty could keep the sea lanes near the port safe. Pires emphasizes the role of these communities in Melaka's naval power. The safe passage of merchant ships to and from Melaka was as important to the port's security as the defense of the city itself, because an unsafe approach would deter the merchants on whom revenue depended.

Melaka's security arrangement also had a fiscal dimension. Merchants would pay dues only if they expected the king's agents to protect vessels, warehouses, and transactions; protection depended in turn on supplies, boat crews, information, and the cooperation of powerful families and sea-oriented communities. A ruler who could not punish predation by his own followers might lose traffic even without a foreign invasion. Conversely, energetic diplomacy alone could not police the waterway at the scale required by regular commerce. Political authority therefore rested on a combination of external recognition and routine local performance, and the balance between those forms of security changed as the city became larger.

Section 64.7

Dynastic Succession, Islamic Kingship, and Royal Legitimacy

The identity and order of Melaka's early rulers is one of the most disputed subjects in the chapter. The Ming records name rulers at 1405, 1414, 1424, and later, using Chinese transcriptions. The Sejarah Melayu provides a royal list, and Pires gives a list that differs in some respects. The three sources do not map neatly onto one another. Chinese records name a ruler who visited in 1414 as the son of the previous king, and Malay and Portuguese traditions describe a series of kings whose names and Islamic titles vary. Conventional reign lists, such as those that place Mansur Shah around 1459 to 1477, Alauddin Riayat Shah around 1477 to 1488, and Mahmud Shah from 1488 to 1511, are widely used in textbooks, but the dates are approximate and the earlier sequence is less secure.

The question of Islam is tied to the question of succession. Malay tradition associates the court's conversion with a scholar who arrived from the Arabian peninsula and with a ruler who took a Muslim title. Pires associates the first ruler's conversion with a marriage connection to the Muslim court of Pasai. Ma Huan, in the 1430s, describes the king and people as Muslim. These sources do not agree on when conversion occurred or who converted, and the safest conclusion is that the court's adoption of Islam took place gradually, across the first half of the fifteenth century, with Muslim practice reaching the royal court by the 1430s. A distinction is essential here: the Islamization of a court, a matter of royal titles, patronage, and ritual, is different from the conversion of the wider population, which unfolded unevenly and is treated in Chapter 65.

Royal legitimacy at Melaka drew on several sources at once. Claims of descent from earlier Malay kings, associated with Palembang and its legendary founder, supplied genealogical prestige. Ming recognition supplied external legitimacy. Islamic titles such as sultan, and the patronage of Muslim scholars, tied the court to the wider Muslim world and to the merchants of Gujarat, Bengal, and elsewhere who frequented the port. Court ceremony, regalia, and the rituals of audience expressed royal dignity. The Malay concept of daulat, the sacred sovereignty of the ruler, and the idea of the subject's duty of loyalty, discussed in Section 64.15, formed part of the framework in which royal power was understood.

Marriage alliances helped bind the dynasty to other courts and to important families within Melaka. Malay tradition records marriages with Pasai, with leading ministerial families, and, according to the Sejarah Melayu, with a Chinese princess, a story that Ming sources do not corroborate and that is regarded by most scholars as a literary tradition. The ministers, discussed in Section 64.8, formed a counterweight to the ruler, and the traditional record describes episodes in which ministerial families played decisive roles in succession disputes. This does not mean the monarchy was weak, but it suggests that royal power was exercised in cooperation with, and sometimes in tension with, powerful elites.

The succession notices in the Chinese record are especially valuable for testing royal lists. A son came to the Ming court in 1414 to report the first ruler's death and obtain recognition; a further accession appears in the 1424 record. Neither event by itself provides a fully certain equivalence between Chinese transcriptions and the personal and regnal names supplied by Malay and Portuguese texts. The chronology becomes unreliable when historians assume that each Islamic-sounding name marks a separate conversion, or that an older Malay name must be the name of a different person. A defensible reconstruction presents the competing lists side by side and tests each identification against dated diplomatic entries.

Dynastic and Material Evidence Study
Reading a Royal List

A royal list is a historical source in its own right. It may be compiled to affirm descent from a prestigious ancestor, to demonstrate continuity, or to teach moral lessons about rulers. Comparing the Chinese record, the Malay list, and Pires's list shows how different aims shaped what was remembered. The Chinese court cared about who held the title and who sent tribute. The Malay court tradition cared about lineage, Islamic dignity, and the achievements attributed to each king. The Portuguese cared about who had been the first to establish the kingdom and how it had come into the hands of the man they fought.

Material evidence for the dynasty is scarce. Fifteenth-century inscriptions at Melaka are not known to survive in any quantity, and coins and tombs can help to establish names and dates when they exist, but for Melaka the surviving material is limited. The scholarly effort to reconcile the lists therefore relies heavily on interpretation of texts.

Section 64.8

The Bendahara, Temenggung, Laksamana, and Shahbandars

By about 1500, according to the Malay tradition and to Pires, Melaka was governed through a group of senior officials who served the ruler and managed different aspects of the kingdom. The best-known are the bendahara, the temenggung, the laksamana, and the shahbandars. This section describes what the sources say about their functions, and it distinguishes what is known about the early sixteenth century from what can be projected back to the foundation period. The caution is important: the fully developed arrangement observed around 1500 should not be assumed to have existed unchanged in 1405.

The bendahara was the chief minister. In the Malay tradition the bendahara advises the ruler, presides over the council of ministers, and acts as the principal officer of the realm; the most famous holder in tradition is Tun Perak, who is credited with directing Melaka's military and political affairs in the mid-fifteenth century. Pires describes the bendahara as a figure of great power, closely involved in the administration of justice and the receipt of foreign envoys. The office's political weight is suggested by the fact that bendahara families were powerful in succession politics, and the tradition records episodes of tension between rulers and great ministers.

The temenggung is associated in the sources with public order and the administration of justice in the city. Pires describes an official responsible for policing the city and for the punishment of crime, with authority in a port where thousands of foreign merchants required a predictable legal environment. The laksamana is associated with naval command. Malay tradition links the office with the fleet and with the legendary Hang Tuah, whose historicity is debated, though Pires does mention a laksamana as a major official. Because a maritime state depended on safe seas and on the cooperation of the Orang Laut, the admiral's role was central to its power.

The shahbandars, discussed more fully in Section 64.9, were harbor officials concerned with the affairs of merchant communities and with trade. Pires reports four of them, each responsible for merchants of particular regions of origin. The tradition also names other officials and chiefs, including officers responsible for the royal treasury and for matters of the palace, and local chiefs who served in various capacities. Taken together, the arrangements show a division of labor among the ruler's principal servants.

Three cautions apply. First, the sources describing these offices are largely retrospective, and they may idealize the arrangement or compress its development. Second, the sources sometimes disagree on specific functions, and offices may have overlapped in practice. Third, the sources tell us more about the elite officials than about the subordinate staff who carried out their instructions. What can be said with confidence is that by about 1500, Melaka had a recognizable ministerial structure in which the functions of government, justice, naval power, and harbor administration were distributed among named officers, and that the structure was important to the port's ability to function. This division of labor is one of the clearest indications that Melaka was a state and not just a market.

Division III — Sections 64.9–64.12
Melaka as an International Port and Commercial Society
Section 64.9

Shahbandars, Customs Duties, and the Administration of Trade

A merchant arriving in a foreign port faced uncertainties: whether goods would be seized, whether weights and prices would be fair, whether the local ruler would protect a stranger's property, and whether disputes would be resolved. A port that reduced these uncertainties could attract trade that otherwise might go elsewhere. Pires's account of Melaka suggests that the sultanate's officials worked deliberately to address them, and modern scholarship has emphasized this deliberate administration as one of the foundations of Melaka's success.

According to Pires, Melaka had four shahbandars, each responsible for the merchants from a defined set of regions. One looked after the Gujarati merchants, who were the most numerous and important group. A second handled merchants from the Coromandel Coast, Bengal, Pegu, and Pasai. A third dealt with merchants from Java, the Maluku Islands, Banda, Palembang, Borneo, and the Philippines. A fourth was responsible for the Chinese and for merchants from the Ryukyu Islands. The shahbandar helped to allocate warehouse space, assessed the value of cargoes for customs purposes, received gifts for the ruler and the officials, and represented the merchant community in dealings with the court. Pires's grouping reflects the commercial geography of the port, with major trading regions each having a point of contact in the administration.

Customs duties were a principal source of royal revenue. Pires reports that merchants from the west paid a fixed percentage on their goods, commonly given as about six percent, while eastern merchants were generally expected to make gifts and to sell a portion of cargo under compulsory discounted terms rather than pay the same western customs percentage. These details should be treated as testimony about the early sixteenth century, not a settled picture of the whole fifteenth, and the exact rates may have varied by group and period. The royal share of commerce was also enlarged by the ruler's trading and by the officials' own commercial involvement. The system of gifts and fees that accompanied formal duties meant that the burden on merchants was higher than the nominal rate suggests, but in return they received services and protection.

Standardized weights and measures further reduced uncertainty. Pires describes the use of recognized units, such as the bahar for heavy goods, with varying equivalents for different commodities, and the port's regulation of these measures helped merchants calculate value. The administrative features were not unique to Melaka, since other ports of the Indian Ocean had harbor officials and fixed practices. What distinguished Melaka, according to the scholarship, was the degree of organization and the emphasis on predictability, in a setting where the ruler's interest in revenue aligned with merchants' interest in security.

The fiscal distinction was more substantial than a contrast between western taxes and symbolic eastern gifts. In his synthesis of Pires, Peter Borschberg reports that western merchants commonly paid around six percent, whereas merchants arriving from eastern regions could be required to sell roughly a quarter of their cargo at a discount, a compulsory transaction imposing an estimated effective burden near five percent of total cargo value. Gifts and other payments could add to that cost. These are approximate descriptions of a particular commercial regime, not proof that every ship and resident trader faced identical charges. The key point is that different legal mechanisms could raise comparable revenue while accommodating merchants' established practices.

Historical Vocabulary and Institutions Study
Shahbandar

Definition: A title of Persian origin, meaning roughly "lord of the harbor," used across the Indian Ocean and Southeast Asia for an official who supervised a port's foreign trade and merchants.

Historical context: The office existed in many ports, and its precise functions varied. At Melaka, according to Pires, there were four shahbandars, each responsible for a group of merchant communities. Their duties included assessing cargoes, assigning storage, receiving customary payments, and acting as intermediaries between merchants and the ruler and his ministers.

Why it matters: The shahbandars show how the state served as an organizer of commerce. They reflect an acknowledgement that the port's success depended on the confidence of foreign merchants.

Potential misunderstanding: The shahbandar is not simply a customs collector. The office combined revenue, administrative, diplomatic, and representative functions. It is also a mistake to assume that the four-part arrangement Pires describes was fixed from the sultanate's foundation.

Section 64.10

Merchants, Families, Women, Labor, and Everyday Life in Melaka

Melaka's population was cosmopolitan, but the evidence about it is uneven. Pires reports that merchants from many regions lived and traded in the city and that a great many languages were spoken. The communities he names include Gujaratis, Tamil (Kling) merchants of the Coromandel Coast, Bengalis, Persians and Arabs, Javanese, Chinese, and merchants from elsewhere in the archipelago. Malays, including the court and the residents of the surrounding area, formed the core of the local community. Many foreign merchants were seasonal, staying only for the sailing season, while others settled, married, and brought up families in the city. Chinese tradition and the later cemetery on a nearby hill indicate a significant Chinese presence, but its size in the fifteenth century cannot be quantified.

The city was organized spatially in ways that reflected this diversity. Merchant communities lived in or near their own quarters, with warehouses and markets near the river and harbor, and the royal residence and the principal mosque in a more prominent position. The river divided the city's quarters, and the movement of goods and people across it was a feature of daily life. Housing for most residents was of light materials, and fire was a recurring hazard in tropical ports, so the burning of buildings is an element of the city's history. These descriptions come largely from later accounts and should be treated as indications and not exact plans.

Women appear in the sources mainly in association with elite families, marriage alliances, and commerce. In the wider Southeast Asian pattern noted in earlier chapters, women commonly played prominent roles in marketing, trade, and household finance, and there is no reason to think Melaka was different, though direct evidence is sparse. Marriage between foreign merchants and local women, a common pattern in port cities, would have created families linking the communities of the port. Pires and the legal texts say little about the details of ordinary households, and historians must infer from the broader regional context.

Labor in Melaka included porters, boatmen, builders, craftspeople, servants, and soldiers, and many of these workers have left almost no record. Slavery and debt bondage were part of the social order, as the Melaka legal texts indicate in their provisions on slaves, debts, and punishments. Enslaved people were bought and sold as part of the port's commerce, and some were used as domestic servants, laborers, or sailors. The legal texts also regulate the relationships between masters and slaves, but they offer a view from the standpoint of owners and rulers, not the experience of the enslaved. Social hierarchy distinguished rulers and great officials, wealthy merchants, free commoners, and dependents, and religious identity added a further dimension. Islam shaped the court, and Muslim merchants were prominent, yet Hindu, Buddhist, and other communities coexisted in the port.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living in a Port of Many Languages

Consider the practical problems of everyday life in a city where merchants spoke different languages, followed different customs, and used different weights and currencies. Brokers and interpreters were needed to make exchanges possible. Malay served as a common language of commerce across much of the archipelago, and it is likely that it played that role in Melaka, though the evidence is mainly indirect. Food, clothing, and religious observance varied among communities, and the port's markets supplied rice, fish, and other provisions, much of it imported, to a population that did not grow its own food.

Historians must read for what is absent. The sources are mostly written by or for merchants, officials, and rulers. The voices of laborers, women, and enslaved people are not recorded directly, and the experiences of the Orang Laut and farmers in the hinterland are known primarily through their service to the court. These gaps are part of the historical evidence and not just an inconvenience.

Section 64.11

Commodities, Merchant Communities, and the Wealth of Melaka

Melaka's role in the exchange of goods can be understood as a set of interlocking flows. From western India came cotton textiles, produced in Gujarat and elsewhere, which were in demand across Southeast Asia and were exchanged for the spices and other products of the archipelago. Textiles from the Coromandel Coast and Bengal followed similar paths. Pires and other sources emphasize the central importance of Indian cloth in Melaka's commerce, as it provided the principal return cargo for ships coming from the east and served as a medium of exchange in many island markets.

From the eastern archipelago came cloves, nutmeg, and mace from the Maluku and Banda islands, which reached Melaka by way of Javanese and other traders. Sandalwood was brought from Timor, aromatic woods and camphor from Sumatra and Borneo, and pepper from Sumatra, particularly the northern ports. Tin from the peninsula was an important local product, and Pires describes its use as a medium of exchange in the form of tin pieces. Gold from Sumatra also passed through the port. From China came porcelain, silk, and other manufactured goods, and Chinese ceramics found in the archaeological record are a material witness to this exchange. Rice from Java and other regions fed the port's population.

The wealth that these flows created was distributed unevenly. The ruler and officials profited from customs duties, gifts, and their own investments. Large merchants, especially those from Gujarat, dominated much of the long-distance trade, and Tamil Hindu merchants also played significant roles as financiers and traders, as Pires's account indicates. Brokers and local traders took shares. The laborers who unloaded, stored, and transported goods, and the farmers and gatherers who produced them, received a much smaller portion. Commerce brought wealth to the port but not necessarily prosperity to all of the people whose work made it possible.

Pires's description of the merchant communities is the most detailed we have, and it requires careful handling. His account was composed after the conquest, at a time when Portuguese interests were bound up with understanding Melaka's trade, and he was an outsider writing in part for an audience in Portugal. He may have overstated the port's importance to underline the value of the conquest, and his descriptions blend observations with information derived from informants. Nevertheless, his account is consistent with Chinese and Malay sources in its broad picture of a port in which Indian textiles, island spices, and Chinese goods were exchanged under the supervision of royal officials.

Section 64.12

Shipping, Brokers, Credit, and the Economic Operation of an Entrepôt

An entrepôt is a port that collects goods from many sources and redistributes them, earning revenue from the exchange rather than from its own production. Operating such a port required a set of services that worked together, and the sources show that Melaka offered several of them: reliable anchorage and storage, officials to regulate trade, brokers to arrange transactions, and conditions in which credit could be extended.

Ships arrived at Melaka according to the rhythm of the monsoons. Vessels from the Indian Ocean and from East Asia sailed on seasonal winds, and a merchant who arrived with one monsoon often had to wait weeks or months for the return wind. This waiting period was an opportunity. It gave merchants time to sell cargoes, purchase goods for the return voyage, and conduct business with others who were also waiting. The seasonal alternation made the port's calendar busy at some times and quiet at others, and officials had to manage the arrivals and departures. The details of specific sailing schedules are not securely known from fifteenth-century evidence and should not be stated with false precision.

Captains and ship owners were important actors. Ships were owned by merchants, groups of investors, or in some cases rulers and officials, and captains had considerable authority at sea, as the maritime law discussed in Section 64.16 shows. Brokers connected buyers and sellers, helped foreign merchants find partners, and provided local knowledge about prices and customs. Credit allowed merchants to buy goods without immediate payment and to share the risk of voyages, and merchants of the Indian Ocean had long-established practices for partnerships and loans. The Tamil and Gujarati merchants of Melaka were well placed to provide credit, and the port benefited from their presence.

What distinguished Melaka from smaller ports was not any one of these services but the combination, together with a political environment in which merchants could expect their property to be protected. The ruler's interest in revenue gave him an incentive to maintain order, and the ministers' authority supported enforcement. Merchant confidence was thus both a cause and a consequence of the port's success: as more merchants came, the port offered more opportunities for exchange, which in turn attracted more. Historians describe such a process as a self-reinforcing one, but it was not automatic, and it could be reversed by loss of security, a change in trade routes, or conflict. Melaka's fifteenth-century history shows how much deliberate effort lay behind the appearance of natural advantage.

Commercial trust was also a matter of enforcing obligations after the original buyer and seller departed. The captain or nakhoda had to coordinate a crew, arrange provisions, and determine when it was prudent to depart; brokers might identify reliable counterparties and help translate between different measures, currencies, and languages. A dispute over short delivery, damaged goods, or an unpaid advance could involve parties with different legal expectations. That helps explain the importance of local intermediaries and an accessible court, although the surviving legal manuscripts cannot establish how regularly individual claims were adjudicated. A thriving entrepôt required repeated cooperation, not simply ships encountering one another by chance.

Division IV — Sections 64.13–64.16
Regional Power, Maritime Diplomacy, and Malay Political Culture
Section 64.13

Mapping Melaka's Maritime and Diplomatic Networks

Melaka's connections extended in several directions, but they were not all of the same kind. A responsible account distinguishes at least four types of relationship: commercial ties, in which merchants and goods moved between ports; diplomatic contacts, in which rulers exchanged envoys and gifts; alliances, in which rulers cooperated through marriage or agreement; and claims of political dependency, in which one ruler asserted or accepted superiority over another. The sources do not always mark the differences, and a trading connection should not be read as evidence of political control.

To the west, Melaka's strongest commercial links were with Gujarat, the Coromandel Coast, and Bengal. Merchants from these regions formed a substantial part of the port's population and supplied the textiles on which exchange depended. These were primarily commercial relationships, mediated by merchant communities and shahbandars. Relations with Pasai, a neighboring Muslim polity, combined commerce, dynastic ties, and religious exchange, and Pasai's scholars were consulted by Melaka's court. As Melaka grew, Pasai's relative commercial importance declined, though the details and chronology of this shift are debated.

To the east and south, Java was a major trading partner, supplying rice and serving as a conduit for the spices of the eastern islands. Javanese merchants frequented Melaka, and the relationship between the two had both commercial and political dimensions, because tradition associates the displacement of Parameswara from Singapura with Javanese pressure. Melaka's connections with the Maluku and Banda islands were largely commercial and passed through intermediaries. On the peninsula and in eastern Sumatra, Melaka's relationships with polities such as Pahang, Kampar, Siak, and Indragiri involved different degrees of authority, discussed in Section 64.14. To the north and east, the connection with China combined trade and diplomacy, and the connection with the Ryukyu Kingdom, mentioned by Pires, shows how far the port's commercial reach extended.

Mapping these links reveals a network with Melaka at its center, but it should not be read as an empire. Direct administration extended mainly over the city and its immediate vicinity. Beyond that, authority took the form of tribute, alliance, or influence, exercised through local rulers who retained a degree of autonomy. This pattern is consistent with the political model discussed in Chapter 1 as the mandala, in which authority radiates from a center and diminishes toward the periphery, though students should remember that the mandala is a modern scholarly tool and not a term Melaka's rulers used.

Section 64.14

Melaka, Ayutthaya, Majapahit, and the Malay World

The regional politics of the fifteenth century involved three kinds of relationship that must be kept apart: conflict with Ayutthaya, relations with the Javanese courts, and the building of influence among Malay polities on the peninsula and in Sumatra. Each reflected a different balance of force and negotiation.

With Ayutthaya, the relationship was contested. Siamese claims on the peninsula conflicted with Melaka's independence, and Malay tradition records a series of attacks that Melaka repelled. The Siamese chronicle tradition, in turn, has its own account of Ayutthaya's relations with the peninsula, and the two traditions are difficult to reconcile. The safest statement is that Ayutthaya and Melaka competed for influence over the peninsular polities and the trade routes of the Gulf of Siam and the strait, that Melaka successfully maintained its independence through the fifteenth century, and that specific battles reported in the later sources have not been independently verified.

With Java, the situation was complicated by the changes in Javanese politics that Chapter 52 describes, including the fragmentation of Majapahit's authority and the rise of Muslim ports on the north coast. Javanese merchants were prominent at Melaka, and Pires gives them a significant place. The extent to which a Javanese court exercised political authority over Melaka in the fifteenth century is unclear, and the Malay tradition's memory of Javanese hostility belongs mainly to the earlier story of Singapura. By the later fifteenth century, Melaka's relationship with Java appears to have been principally commercial.

Within the Malay world, Melaka extended its influence over neighboring polities. Malay tradition and Pires report that rulers on the peninsula, such as Pahang, and on the east coast of Sumatra, such as Kampar, Siak, and Indragiri, came into Melaka's orbit, through conquest, marriage, or acceptance of the ruler's superiority. The Sejarah Melayu attributes major expansion to the reign of Mansur Shah, including the subordination of Pahang. The forms of authority varied. In some cases a local ruler was replaced or married into the Melaka line; in others, a ruler acknowledged Melaka's overlordship while retaining local autonomy. The distinction between direct administration, tribute, and symbolic claim matters here, because the sources often describe all of them in the language of conquest and allegiance.

In judging Melaka's regional reach, historians must distinguish control over a port from control over its surrounding producers. A Malay court might appoint an official in an important settlement, exchange marriages with a local ruling family, receive periodic offerings, or merely claim precedence in a narrative celebrating its royal dignity. Those relationships carried different obligations and could dissolve when local interests changed. A trading kingdom's influence might be strong along river mouths and sea passages but contested farther inland. The same subordinate ruler could negotiate with more than one superior power. Modern maps that color a continuous block as Melakan territory conceal this layered political geography and make later court claims look more stable than the sources warrant.

Section 64.15

The Sejarah Melayu: Kingship, Loyalty, and Historical Memory

The Sejarah Melayu, also known as the Sulalat al-Salatin, is the most important Malay literary and historical work about Melaka. It narrates the descent of the Malay kings from legendary ancestors, the foundation of Singapura and Melaka, the reigns of the Melaka sultans, and the conquest of 1511, and it continues with the story of the successor state at Johor. According to the text itself, a version was prepared in the early seventeenth century at the direction of a Johor ruler, with a senior minister, traditionally identified as Tun Sri Lanang, associated with the editing. The surviving manuscripts are later still, and scholars recognize several recensions. The work is therefore a layered text, shaped by the court of the Johor successors who inherited Melaka's traditions (discussed in Chapter 81), not a record written in the fifteenth century.

The work's value for the historian lies less in its factual reliability on early events than in what it reveals about political ideals. It sets out a vision of kingship in which the ruler is sacred and sovereign, and in which the relationship between ruler and subject is governed by mutual obligations. The most famous expression of this is the covenant attributed to the earliest Malay royal ancestor and a local chief, in which the ruler promises not to shame or humiliate his subjects and the subjects promise never to rebel against the ruler. The term derhaka, treason against the ruler, expresses the gravity attached to disloyalty. Episodes involving ministers, rivals, and royal misconduct illustrate how these ideals were applied, and the text shows tension between the ruler's authority and ministers' loyalty.

The Sejarah Melayu also preserves the memory of particular events, such as the Siamese attacks, the careers of great ministers, and the reigns of individual sultans. Historians use these accounts cautiously. Where the text can be compared to independent evidence, it sometimes aligns, as with the general outline of dynastic succession, and sometimes diverges, as with the story of a Chinese princess's marriage. Its chronology is loose, and its narrative is shaped by the moral and literary aims of its compilers. A responsible approach is to treat it as a source for what later Malay elites believed about Melaka and wished to teach about kingship, and as a source for fifteenth-century events only where it is corroborated or where its details are consistent with other evidence.

The form of the text used is a further part of source criticism. The Raffles MS 18 version and the better-known Shellabear textual tradition do not preserve a single identical sequence of episodes or wording. Scholars have debated a redaction conventionally associated with 1612 and the possibility of earlier material embodied in particular recensions. Henri Chambert-Loir has shown that the work's textual history must be reconstructed through comparison rather than through an assumed original available intact to readers. This chapter's account of the ruler–subject covenant is therefore an editorial summary of a broad narrative tradition, illustrated by Brown's published English translation, not a newly collated translation from a named manuscript folio.

Section 64.16

The Laws of Melaka: Custom, Islamic Law, and Maritime Regulation

Two bodies of law are associated with Melaka: the Undang-undang Melaka, also called the Hukum Kanun Melaka, and the Undang-undang Laut Melaka, or Maritime Laws of Melaka. The first is a compilation concerned with royal authority, the duties of officials, the rights and obligations of subjects, and provisions on matters such as marriage, debts, slaves, theft, and punishment. The second regulates life on ships: the authority of the captain (the nakhoda), the duties and rights of crew and merchants, the allocation of cargo space, and the settlement of disputes at sea. Liaw Yock Fang's critical edition of 1976 is the standard scholarly treatment.

The laws reflect several layers. Many provisions concern Malay custom, which governed relations among rulers, chiefs, and subjects, including rules about royal privilege. Others reflect Islamic law, whose provisions on theft, marriage, and contracts were adopted in the Malay legal tradition as Islam spread. A short inscription in Malay written in the Arabic-derived Jawi script, found in the Terengganu region, which traditionally dated to 702 AH / 1303 CE but redated by Ahmat Adam to 708 AH / 1308 CE, shows that Islamic legal provisions were being expressed in Malay before or around the period of Melaka's rise, though its date remains debated. The maritime laws also draw on the practices of Indian Ocean commerce, and the combination of custom, Islamic law, and commercial practice reflects the character of the port.

The central caution concerns dating and enforcement. The surviving manuscripts of the laws were copied long after the fifteenth century; the British Library, for example, holds a copy of the Undang-undang Melaka made in 1821. A manuscript's date shows when it was copied, not when its provisions were composed, and the texts were compiled, revised, and expanded over time, including in the Johor and other successor courts. Some provisions may reflect fifteenth-century practice, while others may belong to later layers. The age of a legal tradition and the date of a surviving manuscript are separate questions.

The laws also show what rulers and compilers regarded as ideal, which may differ from what was enforced. Provisions on punishment and royal prerogative describe a hierarchical society in which rulers' rights were extensive, and they cannot be taken as proof that every provision was uniformly applied in the fifteenth century. Nevertheless, the existence of a recognized body of law, administered by officials such as the temenggung and supported by the ruler's authority, is consistent with other evidence that Melaka offered merchants a degree of legal predictability. The maritime laws in particular fit the commercial character of the port, and their concern with order on ships mirrors the city's concern with order in the harbor.

The difference between a legal text's authority and its actual enforcement is especially important in a commercial setting. Shipboard articles allocating a captain's jurisdiction could protect a hierarchy of command during voyages, but their appearance in a later manuscript does not demonstrate that every fifteenth-century captain applied them verbatim. British Library Add MS 12397 preserves both the land and maritime legal materials in a copy written in Singapore between February and July 1821. Liaw Yock Fang's critical edition enables comparison of manuscript variants. That evidence makes the texts valuable for studying accumulated Malay legal ideas, while leaving the exact timing of individual provisions open to investigation.

Division V — Sections 64.17–64.20
Melaka at Its Height: Islam, Regional Influence, Historical Interpretation, and Legacy
Section 64.17

Melaka as a Muslim and Cosmopolitan Maritime Kingdom

By the later fifteenth century, Melaka's court was Muslim, and Islam was integral to royal legitimacy. Rulers bore Islamic titles, patronized scholars, and supported mosques and religious learning. The Sejarah Melayu describes scholars arriving from elsewhere in the Muslim world and the court sending questions on religious matters to learned men at Pasai. These details come from later tradition, but they are consistent with the broader picture of Muslim networks traced in Chapters 62 and 63. The port's Muslim merchants, particularly from Gujarat, Bengal, and the Arab and Persian worlds, formed part of the commercial elite and had a natural affinity with a Muslim court.

Yet Melaka was not a religiously uniform city. Pires and other sources show that Hindu, Buddhist, and other merchants traded and lived in the port, and that Tamil Hindu merchants in particular were prominent. Chinese residents followed their own religious practices. The ruler's Muslim identity coexisted with a commercial order in which merchants of various faiths were welcome, so long as they respected the port's regulations. This coexistence did not mean that religion was irrelevant to politics, but it indicates that the court's religious identity operated alongside, and did not replace, the practical pluralism of a trading city.

Islam also coexisted with established Malay traditions. Concepts such as daulat and the hierarchy of the court, descended from earlier Malay kingship, continued within an Islamic framework, and the Sejarah Melayu itself blends Islamic and pre-Islamic motifs. The legal texts blend Islamic provisions with Malay custom. The pattern is one of adaptation and combination and not of replacement. It would be a mistake to see Melaka's Islam as simply imported, or its older traditions as simply preserved. How these changes spread to the wider society, and how Islamic learning developed across the Malay-Indonesian world, are the subjects of Chapter 65, and this chapter confines itself to the court and the port.

Religion could nevertheless structure access to authority unequally. A Muslim ruler might prefer Muslim jurists for questions of court ritual and law, while Hindu, Chinese, or other trading communities relied on their own leaders and negotiated with the shahbandars. Such arrangements were neither a modern ideal of religious equality nor proof of constant antagonism. The surviving evidence chiefly reveals elite patronage and visiting merchants; it says far less about women, domestic worship, enslaved residents, and how different neighborhoods experienced the ruler's religious identity. The coexistence visible at the port should therefore be understood as a practical political arrangement whose terms could differ among people and circumstances.

Section 64.18

Why Did Melaka Become a Major Asian Port? Historical Explanations

Historians have offered several explanations for Melaka's rise, and each has strengths and weaknesses. The question concerns Melaka's emergence as a political and commercial center; the wider spread of Islam is the subject of Chapter 65.

The first explanation emphasizes strategic geography. Melaka's position on the strait, near the meeting point of monsoon systems, made it a natural stopping place. The explanation is plausible but incomplete, because other ports shared similar advantages and because the site itself lacked an agricultural base. The second emphasizes Ming diplomacy. Recognition and the presence of Zheng He's fleets gave the young polity security and prestige. This explanation fits the documentary evidence, but Ming involvement declined after 1433 while Melaka continued to grow. The third emphasizes commercial policy and port administration: the shahbandars, customs system, weights and measures, and legal provisions that made the port reliable. This explanation is supported by Pires and by modern scholarship, although it relies on evidence from near the end of the sultanate.

The fourth emphasizes merchant communities, particularly the Gujarati, Tamil, and other groups whose capital, ships, and networks supplied the port's commerce. The explanation draws attention to the agency of merchants, though it must account for why they chose Melaka. The fifth emphasizes political leadership, including the rulers' strategies and the work of ministers such as Tun Perak in maintaining security and managing relations. This explanation emphasizes human choices, though it relies heavily on a later tradition that tends to celebrate individual figures. The sixth emphasizes regional competition: the weakening of older powers, Pasai's decline, and the divisions among Java's ports opened a space for a new center.

Causation must also be separated by period. In the first decade, Ming investiture and the ability to maintain a safe anchorage could be disproportionately important to a small ruler seeking followers. By the later fifteenth century, merchant quarters, reliable brokers, senior officials, and established routes made the port useful even when no Chinese imperial fleet was present. An explanation based entirely on the Yongle court thus struggles to account for Melaka's continued importance after 1433; an explanation based entirely on Pires's mature institutions risks assuming those institutions existed in identical form at the foundation. A chronological comparison provides a stronger model than an undifferentiated list of causes.

Competition was neither purely destructive nor always zero-sum. Merchants chose among Pasai, peninsular ports, Java, and Melaka according to routes, cargoes, political conditions, and the value of available exchanges. A ship might visit more than one market; one port could serve as an intermediate redistribution point for another. Melaka's rise reorganized these networks without instantly eliminating its competitors. This makes the agency of traders important: rulers could offer attractive conditions, but merchants' repeated decisions established whether those policies worked. The evidence supports a combined explanation in which geography created an opportunity, diplomatic recognition reduced early vulnerability, and institutions and commercial relationships helped sustain later growth.

Historical Debate

Why Did Melaka Rise?

The question: Which factors best explain Melaka's transformation from a small settlement into a major port within roughly a century?

Structural and diplomatic explanations: Some historians stress geography and external diplomacy: Melaka's location, combined with Ming recognition and the Zheng He voyages, gave it advantages that other ports lacked at a critical early moment.
Institutional and commercial explanations: Others stress deliberate policy: harbor administration, predictable duties, legal provisions, and the cultivation of merchant confidence, which sustained the port's growth after the Ming withdrawal.
Agency-centered and regional explanations: A further group emphasizes the choices of merchants and rulers, and the regional context of declining or rival centers, arguing that a successful port was made by many actors responding to opportunities.

Evidence: The explanations draw on Ming records, Ma Huan, Pires, the Sejarah Melayu, and the legal texts. They differ in how much weight they give to early evidence versus testimony from near 1500.

Points of disagreement: The disagreements concern causation and chronology: whether factors that mattered in 1405 were the same as those that mattered in 1480, and whether institutional features observed around 1510 were already in place earlier.

Why it matters: If geography and diplomacy were decisive, Melaka's success looks like a favorable accident. If institutions and policy mattered, it looks like a deliberate achievement, with consequences for how later Malay states and their rulers are understood.

Section 64.19

Melaka under Mansur Shah, Alauddin Riayat Shah, and Mahmud Shah

The later fifteenth century is the period for which Melaka's institutions are best described, though mostly in retrospect. Mansur Shah, whose reign is conventionally placed from about 1459 to 1477, is associated in Malay tradition with expansion on the peninsula and in eastern Sumatra and with a flourishing court. The tradition credits his reign with the subordination of Pahang and other places and with notable ministers. These accounts should be read with the caution already described, but the general picture of a kingdom extending its influence is consistent with Pires's later description of dependent polities.

Alauddin Riayat Shah, conventionally from about 1477 to 1488, is remembered in the tradition as a ruler concerned with justice and order. The Sejarah Melayu associates his reign with efforts to deal with crime and misgovernment in the city, which may reflect an administration grown large enough to require such attention. The accounts are anecdotal, and they cannot be used to measure the actual state of the city, but they show how later writers associated this period with the maturation of the legal and administrative order.

Mahmud Shah, who came to the throne in 1488 and whose reign ended with the fall of the city in 1511, presided over Melaka at the time of Pires's later description. The court had developed an elaborate ministerial structure. Great officials and their families held substantial power, and relations among them and with the ruler affected the stability of the court. The regional situation was complex: Pasai, Aceh's later rise, and the rivalry with Java and Siam were part of the setting, and the presence of Portuguese ships in the Indian Ocean by the early sixteenth century was an emerging factor. Succession politics and the tensions between rulers and ministers are recorded in the tradition for these years.

The situation around 1500 should be described with caution. The sultanate was at the peak of its commercial and political prominence, but it also faced the ordinary strains of any large state: factional rivalry, the cost of defending a trading city, and dependence on merchants' continued confidence. None of these made its later difficulties inevitable. How the sultanate fared when the Portuguese arrived, and why the conquest succeeded in 1511, are matters for Chapters 67 to 69, and the shifting of Malay political life to Johor and elsewhere is treated in Chapter 81. For the purposes of this chapter, the important point is that Melaka around 1500 was a mature maritime kingdom whose institutions and reputation extended well beyond its walls.

Section 64.20

The Sultanate of Melaka: Commerce, Kingship, and the Fifteenth-Century Maritime Order

Melaka's history between about 1400 and 1500 reveals a pattern in which political and commercial development were intertwined. A small polity founded around 1400 by a ruler with connections to older Malay centers gained recognition from the Ming court, survived pressure from neighbors, and built institutions of government and harbor administration that attracted merchants from across the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea. Its rulers combined Malay claims to royal descent with Islamic titles and patronage. Its ministers administered justice, naval power, and the harbor. Its laws, as preserved in later manuscripts, reflected a blending of custom, Islamic law, and commercial regulation.

Melaka's influence on the later Malay world was substantial. The successor states that claimed its heritage, including Johor, looked to Melaka for models of kingship, court ceremony, and law, and the traditions about it were preserved and elaborated in works such as the Sejarah Melayu. The offices of bendahara, temenggung, and laksamana continued in Malay states for centuries. The idea of a Malay court that combined Islam, royal sacredness, and maritime commerce became a template for other polities, a subject examined in Chapters 81 and 82. The legacy lay as much in institutions and memory as in territory.

The history of Melaka also illustrates the larger themes of this textbook. Southeast Asian rulers and merchants were not passive recipients of outside influence: they selected Ming recognition when it was useful, adopted Islamic forms that suited the court, and organized the port according to local needs. At the same time, Melaka's story shows the importance of external networks, since its prosperity depended on merchants from India, China, and elsewhere. The region's historical dynamism arose from this interaction.

The most revealing comparison is between Melaka's founding circumstances and its mature role. At the beginning, the key problem was to recruit people and vessels to one vulnerable riverside center; by about 1500, the key problem was to coordinate a diverse and prosperous regional marketplace while preserving the cooperation of merchants, officials, and nearby rulers. The political technology changed accordingly, from personal alliances and recognition to more elaborate institutions. These stages are reconstructed from different kinds of evidence, so they must not be flattened into an unbroken story of administrative perfection. Melaka's importance lay in how it connected kingship with the practical governance of long-distance commerce.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how Melaka developed between about 1400 and 1500 from a small coastal political center into one of the principal ports and kingdoms of fifteenth-century Asia. Its governing argument is that Melaka's success was a product of political organization, diplomacy, and commercial administration working together with, and not simply because of, a favorable location on the Strait of Malacca.

The chapter began with evidence. Contemporary documentation for Melaka's early years comes mainly from the Ming Shilu and from Chinese travelers such as Ma Huan. Portuguese accounts, above all Tomé Pires's Suma Oriental, were written after the conquest of 1511, and the Malay tradition, including the Sejarah Melayu and the legal texts, survives in later recensions and manuscripts. Archaeology adds comparatively little because of continuous occupation. Each source reflects its author's purposes, and historians therefore distinguish documented events, probable reconstructions, and traditions.

Geography created opportunities, since Melaka stood on a strait near the meeting zone of monsoon systems, but it did not guarantee success. Other ports and routes were available, and Melaka's river-mouth site lacked a large agricultural base, making the city dependent on imported food and thus on shipping. The foundation itself is uncertain. Traditions connect Parameswara with Palembang and Singapura, and Chinese records document his recognition in 1405, but the founding date is a convention, and the identity of Parameswara with Iskandar Shah remains disputed.

Ming recognition, tribute, and investiture gave Melaka diplomatic backing against Siamese pressure, and Zheng He's fleets used the port as a station. But recognition was not protection: the Ming did not station forces, and its involvement declined after 1433. Melaka's independence was maintained through a combination of diplomacy, ministerial leadership, naval and maritime support, and negotiation, while Malay and Siamese traditions disagree about the status of the peninsula.

The dynasty developed a kingship that combined Malay claims of descent, Ming recognition, and Islamic titles and patronage. The court's conversion occurred gradually in the first half of the fifteenth century, and the chapter distinguished court Islamization from the wider conversion of society treated in Chapter 65. Ministers including the bendahara, temenggung, laksamana, and shahbandars formed a governing structure whose mature form is best described around 1500, though it should not be projected back unchanged onto the foundation period.

The port's commercial success rested on mechanisms. Four shahbandars, according to Pires, represented merchant communities; customs and presents provided royal revenue; weights and measures were standardized; brokers and credit facilitated exchange; and security at sea and in the city sustained merchant confidence. Indian textiles, island spices, tin, gold, and Chinese goods flowed through the port, while its population included many merchant communities, women, laborers, and enslaved people whose experiences are only partly recoverable.

Melaka's influence extended through commercial ties, diplomacy, alliances, and claims over neighboring polities. Direct administration was limited, and authority beyond the harbor took forms such as tribute and overlordship. The Sejarah Melayu preserves Malay ideals of kingship and loyalty, and the Melaka legal texts record a blend of custom, Islamic law, and maritime regulation, but both survive in later layers and cannot be treated as direct evidence of fifteenth-century practice.

Historians offer complementary explanations for Melaka's rise, emphasizing geography, Ming diplomacy, commercial policy, merchant communities, political leadership, and regional competition. The evidence suggests that these factors mattered at different times, with diplomacy more significant early and institutions more significant later. The sultanate around 1500 was a mature maritime kingdom, and nothing in its internal development made its later fate inevitable.

The legacy of Melaka lay in the political models, offices, legal traditions, and memories adopted by successor Malay courts. It illustrates the textbook's wider argument that Southeast Asian societies selected and transformed external influences and that their states were active historical creations. The conquest of 1511 (Chapter 69) and the Malay successor world (Chapter 81) continue the story.

Key Terms

Melaka
The port and kingdom at the mouth of the Melaka River, established around 1400 (a convention) and conquered by the Portuguese in 1511.
Entrepôt
A port whose wealth derives from collecting and redistributing goods from many sources, supported by administration and services.
Ming Shilu
The Veritable Records of the Ming dynasty, annals compiled from palace archives that provide the earliest datable references to Melaka.
Tribute system
The Ming court's framework for foreign relations, in which rulers sent envoys with gifts and received recognition; it did not imply Chinese administration of the recipient.
Investiture
The formal recognition of a foreign ruler by the Chinese emperor, marked by a seal, patent, and robes.
Parameswara
The ruler identified in Chinese and Portuguese sources as Melaka's founder; his relationship to Iskandar Shah is disputed.
Singapura (Temasek)
A settlement at the southern end of the strait, associated in tradition with Parameswara before Melaka; archaeological evidence shows fourteenth-century occupation.
Zheng He
Ming eunuch commander of the treasure fleets (1405–1433), whose voyages used Melaka as a station.
Sultan
An Islamic title of ruler adopted at Melaka as the court became Muslim; the chronology of adoption is uncertain.
Daulat
The Malay concept of the sacred sovereignty of the ruler.
Derhaka
In Malay political thought, treason or disloyalty to the ruler, regarded as a grave offense.
Bendahara
The chief minister of the Melaka court, the senior officer of the realm.
Temenggung
The official associated with public order and justice in the city.
Laksamana
The admiral or naval commander, an office central to a maritime state.
Shahbandar
A harbor official, of Persian-derived title; Pires reports four at Melaka, each responsible for merchants from particular regions.
Customs duty
A payment levied on goods, a principal source of royal revenue; Pires reports different practices for western and eastern merchants.
Broker
An intermediary who arranged transactions between buyers and sellers and supplied local knowledge.
Suma Oriental
Tomé Pires's description of Asian commerce, composed c. 1512–1515 after the conquest.
Sejarah Melayu
The Malay Annals or Sulalat al-Salatin, a dynastic chronicle edited in the Johor court tradition and surviving in later recensions.
Recension
A particular edited version of a text; the Sejarah Melayu survives in several.
Undang-undang Melaka
The Laws of Melaka (Hukum Kanun Melaka), a legal compilation of several layers that survives in later manuscripts.
Undang-undang Laut Melaka
The Maritime Laws of Melaka, regulating life aboard ship and the authority of the captain.
Nakhoda
A ship's captain or master, with considerable authority at sea according to the maritime laws.
Orang Laut
Sea-oriented peoples of the strait and nearby islands who supplied maritime labor and support to Melaka's rulers.
Jawi
The Arabic-derived script used for writing Malay.
Mandala
A modern scholarly model of political authority radiating from a center and weakening toward its periphery.
Court Islamization
The adoption of Islamic titles, patronage, and ritual by a ruling court, distinct from conversion of the wider population.
Manuscript tradition
The history of how a text was copied and transmitted; the date of a manuscript is not the date of its contents.
Retrospective source
A source written after the events it describes, often with later concerns shaping its account.

Chapter Reflection

Melaka matters because it shows how a state can be built on commerce without being reducible to it. The sultanate's rulers, ministers, and harbor officials created conditions in which merchants from many regions could trade with confidence, and they did so while negotiating with the Ming court, with Ayutthaya, and with Malay neighbors. The result was a polity that drew on older Malay traditions and on Islam to define its kingship and on practical administration to sustain its revenue.

The chapter also illustrates the difficulty of reconstructing the past from unevenly preserved evidence. The best documentation of Melaka's institutions dates from after its conquest, and the Malay tradition was written down by the heirs of the sultanate. This should make students attentive both to what can be claimed and to the ways later memory shapes the past. The silence of the ground at Melaka, the layered character of the Sejarah Melayu, and the late manuscripts of the laws are not merely obstacles. They are part of the history of how Melaka has been remembered.

Melaka's influence extended beyond its own lifetime. Its offices, legal traditions, and court culture shaped the Malay states that followed, and its example informed the way later rulers understood the relationship between kingship, religion, and trade. Chapter 65 turns to the wider spread of Islam across the Malay-Indonesian world, Chapter 66 to the northern maritime networks, and Chapters 67 to 71 to the arrival of the Portuguese and the transformation of the strait.

In the larger story of this textbook, Melaka stands as an example of Southeast Asian agency. Its rulers and merchants selected from the options available to them, adopted and adapted external forms, and built institutions that proved durable. The region was not a passive crossroads. At Melaka, as elsewhere, it was a place where people made choices that shaped the movement of goods, ideas, and power across the maritime world.
