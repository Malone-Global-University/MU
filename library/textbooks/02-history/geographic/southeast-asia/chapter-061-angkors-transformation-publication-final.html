Angkor's Transformation

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

The transformation of a capital is not the disappearance of a civilization. Between the late thirteenth and the late fifteenth century, Angkor ceased to be the dominant seat of Khmer royal power, yet its temples continued to be used, its landscape continued to be inhabited, and Khmer kingship, language, and religious life continued elsewhere.

In 1296 a Chinese envoy named Zhou Daguan arrived at Angkor as a member of a diplomatic mission sent by the Yuan court. He remained for about a year, and afterward wrote a short account of what he had seen: a walled royal city, stone causeways, gilded images, ceremonies in which the king appeared before his court, markets in which women conducted much of the trading, and monks, Brahmin ritual specialists, and ascetics who followed different religious paths. His text is one of the few surviving descriptions of Angkor by a visitor who saw it as a functioning royal capital. It is also, for historians, an unusually valuable marker, because it was written at a moment when the evidence from inscriptions, excavation, and sediment already shows that Angkor was beginning to change.

For a long time the story of what followed was told in a single sentence. The Thai army of Ayutthaya took Angkor in 1431, the Khmer court withdrew southward, and the great city was abandoned to the forest until European travelers "rediscovered" it in the nineteenth century. Every part of that sentence has now been revised. Archaeologists have shown that land use in the center of Angkor Thom was already declining in the fourteenth century. Climate records indicate that severe droughts and unusually intense monsoons struck the region in the same era. Inscriptions in Sanskrit, the language of royal and temple dedications, grow rare after 1295. At the same time, excavations at Angkor Wat show that people continued to live and worship there, and Khmer communities never ceased to know where the temple stood.

This chapter therefore treats Angkor's transformation as a set of related but separate processes that occurred on different schedules. One is the change in royal-political power: the gradual movement of Khmer kings and courts away from the Angkor plain toward riverine locations better connected to the lower Mekong and to maritime routes. A second is the change in the urban and hydraulic landscape: the reduced intensity of land use in some districts and the strain on a water system that required constant maintenance. A third is religious: the slow ascendancy of Theravada Buddhism and the construction of new kinds of religious buildings. A fourth is regional: the rise of Ayutthaya and the warfare and diplomacy that accompanied it. A fifth is commercial and demographic: changing routes of exchange and the movement of people. A sixth is continuity: the survival of Angkor Wat and of Khmer society itself.

The evidence for these processes is varied and unevenly dated. It includes Khmer inscriptions catalogued and edited over many decades, Chinese diplomatic and travel writing, Thai and Khmer royal chronicles compiled long after the events they describe, radiocarbon dates from excavations and sediment cores, pollen and charcoal records, tree-ring reconstructions of rainfall, airborne laser scanning (lidar) of the landscape beneath vegetation, and the study of Buddhist sculpture. Each type of evidence measures something different. A tree ring says something about rainfall; it does not say who ruled. A chronicle says something about how later courts remembered the past; it cannot by itself date the thinning of a neighborhood. Much of the work of this chapter is to teach students how to hold such different kinds of evidence together without forcing them to say the same thing.

The chapter's chronological focus is roughly 1250 to 1500. Earlier Angkorian institutions, treated at length in Chapters 33 through 39, appear here only to explain how and why their functions changed. Chapter 35 examined the formation of Angkor's vast settlement and water system, Chapters 36 through 38 its religious and royal programs from Suryavarman II to Jayavarman VII, and Chapter 39 ordinary life in the imperial period. Chapter 53 followed the regional spread of Theravada Buddhism and Chapter 57 the rise of Ayutthaya. Later Khmer political history, including the courts at Lovek and Oudong, belongs to Chapter 89, and appears here only to demonstrate continuity.

The chapter's aim is to substitute a better question for the old one. Instead of asking only "Why did Angkor fall?", it asks why Angkor ceased to function as the dominant center of Khmer royal power, how climate variability, changing institutions, religious practice, regional warfare, and commercial opportunity interacted, and what evidence shows that Khmer society continued to develop after the political center moved away. The answer that emerges is not a story of a single catastrophe but of a prolonged and uneven reorganization. Understanding it is a lesson in how historians distinguish the fate of an elite capital from the fate of the people who lived in and around it, and how the same evidence can be misread when a dramatic date is allowed to stand for a long process.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the traditional account of a sudden "fall of Angkor" in 1431 is historically inadequate.
  2. Distinguish Angkor as a royal capital, Greater Angkor as an inhabited landscape, and individual monuments as continuing religious sites.
  3. Identify the kinds of evidence (inscriptions, chronicles, Chinese testimony, excavation, lidar, sediment and pollen cores, tree rings, sculpture) used to reconstruct the period, and assess the strengths and limits of each.
  4. Trace the chronology of political, environmental, and religious change from about 1295 to about 1500, separating independently dated evidence from later narrative.
  5. Analyze how drought, extreme monsoons, sedimentation, and the maintenance demands of Angkor's water system interacted with political and institutional change.
  6. Describe the gradual expansion of Theravada Buddhism in the late Angkorian world and its effect on temples, kingship, and local communities.
  7. Reconstruct aspects of ordinary life and population movement without deriving precise population figures from indirect evidence.
  8. Evaluate the Ayutthayan intervention of 1431–1432, distinguishing the historical likelihood of an attack, the possibility of occupation, and later chronicle elaboration.
  9. Assess the gradual shift of Khmer royal power toward southern riverine centers and the contested chronology of that shift.
  10. Compare invasion-centered, climate-centered, hydraulic, religious-institutional, economic, and demographic explanations, and explain why a multi-causal account is stronger.
  11. Explain why the transformation of a capital should not be equated with the disappearance of Khmer society.

Historical Context

By about 1250, the polity centered on Angkor had existed for roughly four centuries. Chapter 33 examined its origins in the early ninth century and Chapter 34 its institutions of kingship, provincial relations, and temple endowment. Over the following centuries, successive rulers built enormous reservoirs known as barays, canal and embankment systems, roads, and temple complexes of which Angkor Wat, dedicated to Vishnu under Suryavarman II in the twelfth century, and the Buddhist monuments of Jayavarman VII at the end of that century are the best known. Chapter 35 described the resulting landscape as a vast, low-density urban and agricultural complex, not a compact walled city.

The thirteenth century opened a more ambiguous period. Jayavarman VII's reign had left an extraordinary legacy of construction, but his successors undertook fewer large projects. Inscriptional and architectural evidence suggests a reorientation of religious patronage, including an episode, usually attributed to the reign of Jayavarman VIII (r. c. 1243–1295), in which many Buddhist images in Mahayana contexts were altered or removed in favor of Shaiva worship. The same period saw growing contact with the wider Buddhist world, in which the Theravada tradition, with its Pali scriptures and monastic communities, was becoming prominent among Mon, Tai, and Burmese societies, as Chapter 53 explained. Chapter 46 treated the Yuan campaigns and diplomatic pressure that touched many mainland states; Zhou Daguan's visit was itself an outcome of that diplomacy.

Angkor's neighbors were also changing. Tai-speaking communities had been moving into the Chao Phraya basin and the middle Mekong for generations, as Chapter 54 described, and new political centers such as Sukhothai and, from the mid-fourteenth century by traditional dating, Ayutthaya emerged to the west (Chapters 55 and 57). To the east, Đại Việt and Champa were locked in their own struggles. In the Gulf of Thailand and South China Sea, Chinese and Southeast Asian merchants were expanding exchange networks. A Khmer court once accustomed to being the strongest power on the western mainland now confronted competitors who were building their own irrigation systems, monastic networks, and armies.

Students should keep two cautions in mind. First, "Khmer" in this period designates a language community and a set of political and religious institutions, not a modern national identity; the relationship between Khmer-speaking communities and the modern state of Cambodia must not be treated as inevitable. Second, the term "collapse" is itself an interpretation. Some scholars use it for the end of Angkor's imperial role, others reject it because it implies sudden failure and total loss. This chapter uses "transformation" as its governing term and returns to the debate in Section 61.18.

Section 61.1

Reconstructing Angkor's Transformation: Chronicles, Inscriptions, and Archaeology

The history of Angkor's last imperial centuries rests on evidence that was produced at very different times and for very different purposes. The oldest category is epigraphic. Stone inscriptions in Sanskrit and Old Khmer, recorded in the catalogue numbers prefixed "K." and edited above all in George Cœdès's Inscriptions du Cambodge, record temple foundations, royal genealogies, and grants of land and personnel. They are contemporary documents, but they were written by and for the elite, and they were produced only when someone chose to commission a durable text. A second category is outside observation, of which Zhou Daguan's account is the fullest. A third is the royal chronicle. Khmer chronicles in their surviving forms were compiled many generations after the fifteenth century, and the Thai chronicles of Ayutthaya were likewise assembled and revised later. They preserve memories, dynastic claims, and moral lessons as much as verifiable chronology.

A fourth category, now the most dynamic, is scientific. Excavation recovers pottery, building remains, burials, and organic material suitable for radiocarbon dating. Sediment cores drawn from reservoirs, moats, and ponds preserve layers of mud, pollen, and charcoal that record changes in vegetation and land use. Tree rings from long-lived trees elsewhere in the Mekong region provide annual indicators of drought and rainfall. Lidar, in which an aircraft fires laser pulses that return measurements of the ground beneath the canopy, has revealed an extensive built landscape invisible from the surface. Each method has its own uncertainties: radiocarbon results are expressed as calibrated probability ranges, not single years; a core records the conditions at one place; and lidar shows shapes but not dates.

Older accounts framed 1431 as decisive because the chronicles gave them a single, dramatic event. French scholars of the colonial era, working from inscriptions, Thai chronicles, and the ruined state of the monuments, generally treated Angkor's end as a political and military collapse followed by abandonment. Nineteenth-century European writing, notably the posthumously published travel narrative of Henri Mouhot (1863), helped popularize the image of a lost city reclaimed by forest, although Khmer and visiting Japanese and European writers had long known Angkor Wat as an active site. In the 1970s Bernard-Philippe Groslier proposed that Angkor's hydraulic system had become overextended and that ecological strain contributed to the city's decline, an influential idea that later research has tested and refined rather than simply confirmed.

There is a basic chronological asymmetry in this evidence. A dated inscription may establish that a named donor, ruler, or religious foundation existed in a particular year, but it cannot establish the population of an entire city. A radiocarbon result dates sampled organic material within a probability range, not necessarily the construction or abandonment of a neighboring building. A later chronicle may preserve an event while changing its timing or significance to serve a dynastic narrative. The bilingual Pali–Khmer inscription K.754 of 1308, connected with the courtly transition after Śrīndravarman, demonstrates why documents must be read together rather than reduced to a single story about disappearance.

Recent research reconstructs not one transition but several. Land use within Angkor Thom began to change before 1431. Climate stress arrived in two main episodes. The royal court shifted over a period whose chronology is uncertain. Buddhism's institutional presence expanded across the same decades. Parts of Angkor, above all Angkor Wat, remained inhabited. The task is to distinguish political decline, the weakening of the court's capacity to direct the landscape, from total abandonment, the departure of people and cessation of worship. These are different claims and require different evidence.

Source Method

How Do Historians Reconstruct the End of an Imperial Capital?

A historian reconstructing Angkor's transformation works with evidence from five broad classes, each with a different relationship to the events:

Contemporary documentsDated inscriptions; Zhou Daguan's account (1296–1297). Close in time, but elite or outside perspectives, and sparse.
Retrospective narrativeKhmer and Thai chronicles. Later compilations; valuable for memory and claims, weak for precise dating.
Physical and environmental evidenceExcavation, cores, pollen, tree rings, lidar. Independently datable within ranges, but silent about names, titles, and motives.
Art and architectureSculpture styles, building modifications. Datable by stylistic and stratigraphic arguments, often debated.
Modern scholarly synthesisInterpretations that combine the above and may change as new data appear.

The method: a strong argument finds independent lines of evidence that agree, states where they conflict, and avoids letting a later narrative overwrite an earlier physical record. A chronicle's date for an event does not become more certain because an archaeological layer is dated near it; and a sediment record of declining land use does not become an explanation for a particular battle.

Questions for students:

  1. Why is an independent radiocarbon date more useful for testing a chronicle than a second chronicle written later?
  2. What kinds of claims about Angkor can lidar support, and what claims require excavation or texts?
  3. Why might a historian trust a dated inscription more than a chronicle for the year 1295 but not for the motives of a king?
Section 61.2

The Angkorian Heartland: The Tonle Sap, Rivers, Reservoirs, and Regional Networks

Angkor lies on the northern edge of the Tonle Sap lake basin in present-day northwestern Cambodia, on a gently sloping plain between the Kulen uplands to the north and the lake to the south. The Siem Reap River and other streams descend from the Kulen hills across sandy soils that drain quickly. This geography offered both an opportunity and a problem. Seasonal rain was reliable in most years but concentrated in a few months, while the plain's soils held water poorly. The Tonle Sap itself is among the most productive freshwater fisheries in Southeast Asia. In the rainy season the Mekong's flood pushes water up the Tonle Sap River into the lake, expanding its surface several times over; in the dry season the flow reverses. The lake therefore supplied fish and fertile floodplain, and linked the Angkor plain to the Mekong by an annual rhythm that people could use but not control.

Angkorian rulers built a system of embankments, canals, and large rectangular reservoirs to manage this environment. The West Baray, roughly eight kilometers long, and the East Baray, nearly as long, were the largest. Angkor Thom, the walled royal enclosure, is about three kilometers on each side and is surrounded by a moat. Angkor Wat stands to the south within its own moated rectangle. Chapter 35 analyzed how these works formed a landscape; what matters here is that the system combined several functions, which scholars debate in proportion: storing water, damping floods, distributing water to fields, draining the city, supporting transport, and expressing sacred symbolism in which moats and reservoirs evoke the cosmic ocean. Maintaining it required organized labor and endowed institutions.

Angkor was also a node in networks reaching outward. Roads built in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries linked the center to provincial temples and towns. The Tonle Sap and its river connected the plain to the Mekong and onward to the lower Mekong delta, the Cham coast, and the South China Sea. Overland and riverine routes led westward and southwestward toward the Chao Phraya basin and the Gulf of Thailand. Forest products, rice, fish, ceramics, and cloth moved along these routes, as did monks, envoys, and armies.

The geography of Greater Angkor also explains why its districts could follow different trajectories. A family living near a small pond might continue managing water locally even if distant reservoirs ceased to receive the same level of royal attention. Communities around the lake could combine wet-rice cultivation with seasonal fishing; people along navigable rivers could exchange forest products and agricultural surplus through intermediaries. Roads, channels, and embankments linked these landscapes, but they did not eliminate local ecological variation. Consequently, evidence from the monumental core, a northern canal, or a reservoir cannot automatically stand for the entire Tonle Sap basin or all Khmer-speaking communities.

The opportunities and constraints of this inland system are the background to the transformation. A huge, dispersed agricultural-urban complex could support a large court and temple economy when its water was managed and its labor mobilized. It also concentrated royal power far from the river junction through which maritime commerce increasingly passed, and it depended on infrastructure that, in a climate of marked swings between wet and dry, could be damaged by both too little and too much water. None of this made change inevitable. It did mean that when political authority, climate, and commerce shifted together, the center of the system was exposed.

Section 61.3

Zhou Daguan and Angkor at the Close of the Thirteenth Century

Zhou Daguan accompanied an embassy dispatched by the Yuan emperor Temür Khan in 1295 and reached Angkor in 1296. Chinese writers called the country Zhenla; Zhou notes that its people used a name Chinese transcribed as something like Ganbozhi, a rendering of Kambuja. He remained for about a year and wrote his account after returning to China. The text, Zhenla fengtu ji, is usually translated as A Record of Cambodia: The Land and Its People. It survives in later copies and printings, and the English translation by Peter Harris (Silkworm Books, 2007), prepared from the Chinese text with introduction and notes, is the edition used in this chapter.

What Zhou describes is a functioning royal city. He gives an account of the king's public appearances and ceremonies, with elaborate ritual, gilded ornament, and an attending court. He describes the city's walls and gates, the causeways lined with figures, and a central gilded sanctuary that scholars generally associate with the Bayon complex, though identifications of individual structures in his text require caution. He discusses social ranks and officials, the household and its women, slaves drawn largely from upland communities, the daily trade in markets where women were prominent, the products brought in from the forests and rivers, and the agriculture and fishing of the countryside. He records that Chinese sailors and merchants lived in the city, which shows direct commercial contact.

Zhou also distinguishes three groups of religious practitioners, whom he names by Chinese renderings and describes by dress and practice: Brahmin-like ritual specialists, Buddhist monks with shaven heads and yellow robes who ate a single meal and recited from texts, and Shaiva ascetics. His account of the monks is of particular interest for Chapter 53's theme, since it suggests that a Pali-using monastic Buddhism was visibly present at Angkor in the 1290s, although Zhou's descriptions do not by themselves identify every doctrinal affiliation. He also reports, with evident interest, a succession by a king's son-in-law, which fits the political events of 1295 but which he relays as hearsay.

Zhou's testimony is particularly revealing when paired with the different purpose of a dated inscription. He observed the visible behavior of people at a royal center, yet he could not see long-term land-use trends and did not systematically record every district beyond the capital. His description of monks' clothing makes their presence credible, but his Chinese terms alone cannot tell us the language of their liturgy. The 1308 K.754 inscription independently attests Pali in a Buddhist court-related context; it does not retrospectively turn every monk Zhou observed into a securely identifiable member of one religious lineage. These distinctions protect the usefulness of a vivid eyewitness without asking him to answer questions he never investigated.

The account illuminates a royal city at a moment of late splendor, yet its limits are as important as its content. Zhou was a visitor who stayed less than two years at most and likely spoke through intermediaries. He wrote from the perspective of a Chinese official interested in customs, wealth, and rank. He saw the royal city and its ceremonial life, not every district, and he could not know that land-use change in Angkor Thom's interior would be beginning within a few decades. Some of his figures, such as multiple annual rice harvests and details of the court's size, are best treated cautiously. His report is a valuable contemporary snapshot and cannot serve as a baseline for estimating urban population or for predicting what followed.

Section 61.4

Beyond the Stone Temples: Lidar and the Rediscovery of Greater Angkor

For most of the twentieth century, Angkor meant its stone temples. Excavation and mapping by the Greater Angkor Project and other teams in the early twenty-first century showed that stone monuments stood within a much larger settled landscape, which researchers mapped over an area on the order of a thousand square kilometers. Ordinary houses were built of wood, thatch, and other perishable materials and left few surface traces, so the city's inhabited fabric was long invisible. Airborne lidar, flown in 2012 and published by Damian Evans and colleagues in 2013, changed this by recording ground surface features across several hundred square kilometers, including the areas around Angkor, Phnom Kulen, and Koh Ker, through the forest canopy.

The scans revealed networks of roads and canals, low earthen mounds on which houses once stood, small ponds beside them, rectilinear enclosures, and previously unmapped temple sites. They showed that the monumental center was embedded in a vast low-density urban landscape, not isolated from ordinary settlement. For this chapter's argument the importance is conceptual: Greater Angkor was an inhabited landscape, and it cannot be reduced either to a royal capital or to a group of monuments.

Yet lidar requires careful interpretation. It records the shape of the land surface, not its date. A mound visible in the scan might have been occupied for a single generation or for centuries, in the thirteenth century or the sixteenth. Ground plans do not name their inhabitants or establish their social rank. Lidar can show that a neighborhood existed; only excavation, coring, and dating can say when. Lidar also cannot detect what has left no relief, and areas of dense modern disturbance or vegetation may be unevenly resolved. Thus lidar sets the questions, and other methods answer them.

Greater Angkor's aerial topography unsettles a second assumption: that the absence of standing stone must indicate the absence of historical settlement. Earthen house platforms and household ponds were part of an inhabited landscape even where the timber structures once standing above them have decayed. Yet the surface traces also present a sampling problem. To determine whether adjacent mounds were occupied simultaneously, archaeologists must excavate selected locations and compare ceramic sequences, stratigraphy, and dates. A map may therefore show the cumulative footprint of centuries of occupation rather than a snapshot of everyone living there in one decade. The distinction matters whenever estimates of metropolitan size are used to explain political change.

This distinction becomes central in later sections. A city whose structures were largely perishable could change without leaving dramatic ruins, and a declining intensity of land use may be visible in sediment while the earthworks and stone remain on the surface. The Angkor of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries was not a ruin awaiting discovery but a landscape whose parts were used differently over time.

Chronology, monsoon variability, water management, and the changing urban landscape

Section 61.5

From Imperial Expansion to Urban Reorganization, c. 1295–1500

A critical chronology begins with the documentary record of 1295. The Mangalārtha inscription, K.488, is dated to that year and is the last securely dated Sanskrit inscription commonly cited for the central Angkor region. In the same year, by the usual reconstruction, a new king, Indravarman III, came to the throne, ending Jayavarman VIII's long reign. A later dated inscription at the Bayon, K.470, belongs to 1327. Between them and afterward, large new royal construction projects became much less frequent than in the previous century and a half. This is a pattern in the evidence, and its interpretation requires caution: fewer monuments may reflect a change in how patronage was expressed, in the availability of surplus, or in the kinds of materials used, as well as a decline in royal capacity.

Fourteenth-century sediment and pollen evidence from Angkor Thom indicates a change in land use, with decreasing intensity of activity inside the walled enclosure. The research of Penny, Hall, Evans, and Polkinghorne reported this decline as gradual, beginning before the climatic crises and long before 1431. Climate records add two periods of severe drought, in the mid-fourteenth century and again in the early fifteenth century, interrupted by years of exceptionally strong monsoon rains. These environmental indicators are independent of the chronicles and measure different things; their agreement in time is suggestive but does not by itself prove a causal chain.

The conventional date of the Ayutthayan intervention, 1431–1432, comes from later chronicles and receives partial support from material evidence of fifteenth-century Ayutthayan presence at Angkor, discussed in Section 61.15. After this event, and at an uncertain pace, Khmer royal power became associated with locations on the river system to the south and southeast. Later Khmer chronicles frame this as a deliberate move of the capital, but scholars disagree about the date, the reasons, and whether a single move occurred.

One advantage of separating the chronological strands is that apparent contradictions become research questions rather than errors to hide. The 1308 K.754 record places royal and monastic patronage within the early fourteenth-century transition; the 1327 Khmer inscription indicates that locally produced stone texts did not cease in 1295. The timing of occupation at Angkor Wat differs from the evidence for reduced land-use intensity inside Angkor Thom. These findings are compatible with a changing capital whose sacred districts remained active, but they do not establish a comprehensive yearly sequence for the royal court. The intervals between surviving records are genuine evidentiary gaps, not invitations to invent missing reigns or migrations.

The right way to read this chronology is as overlapping processes. Royal construction thinned from the late thirteenth century; land use in a core district weakened from the fourteenth; climate stress fell on the water system in two phases; Ayutthaya's power grew; and in the fifteenth century the court's center of gravity moved. Treating 1431 as the climax of this sequence is acceptable, but treating it as the origin is not.

Section 61.6

Drought, Extreme Monsoons, and the Vulnerabilities of Angkor's Water System

In 2010 Brendan Buckley and colleagues published a tree-ring reconstruction of regional drought. Using long-lived trees in the highlands of southern Vietnam, they built a record of monsoon-related moisture extending back more than seven centuries. It showed prolonged droughts in the mid-fourteenth century and again in the early fifteenth century, separated by periods of exceptionally heavy monsoon rainfall. The tree rings come from a site far from Angkor, so the record reflects regional climate and is not a direct measurement of the Angkor plain, but it matches the broader monsoon system on which Angkor's agriculture and reservoirs depended.

Why would swings between drought and flood matter? Angkor's system of barays, canals, and embankments stored and moved water over a seasonal cycle. Prolonged drought reduces inflow and storage, stresses rice cultivation, and may reduce the surplus on which the temple economy depended. Intense monsoon rain can overload canals and spillways, erode embankments, and carry sediment into reservoirs and channels. Research by Mary Beth Day and colleagues on the West Baray (2012) reported sedimentary evidence of change associated with this unstable period, indicating that the reservoir's behavior altered and that the system was responding to hydrologic stress. Other work documents erosion, sedimentation, and modification of channels at points in the network.

It would be wrong to infer that Angkor's whole water system failed at once. The system was not a single rigid machine under one authority; it comprised many components with different functions, ages, and conditions, from large reservoirs to local ponds. Flood control, storage, drainage, transport, and agricultural distribution are separate functions, and some parts could continue working while others decayed or were modified. Smaller household ponds, local channels, and fields could be managed by communities without direct royal direction. What a drought-and-flood sequence can do is reveal the maintenance burden: the larger elements demanded organized, repeated labor and institutional continuity.

The mechanism linking extreme rainfall to political institutions is easier to understand at the level of a canal or embankment. A prolonged dry season may lower available water and encourage silt deposition; a sudden heavy monsoon can then cut through an already vulnerable structure. Repairs require earth-moving labor, specialist supervision, materials, access to the damaged channel, and an authority capable of mobilizing those resources. Even where the engineering problem is visible today, however, excavated damage must be independently dated before it is assigned to a particular drought. A river-channel failure and a nearby settlement's reduced occupation may occur within the same century without one necessarily proving the other caused it.

The chronology also limits the climate argument. The geoarchaeological evidence for declining land use in Angkor Thom begins before some of the climatic disturbances, so climate cannot be the first cause of urban change. A reasonable reading is that environmental stress may have amplified a transformation already under way, by making maintenance more difficult for a state whose priorities and resources were changing. Section 61.8 returns to the relation between infrastructure and royal capacity.

Section 61.7

Sediment Cores, Pollen, and the Evidence for Gradual Change

Geoarchaeology treats the earth as a record. A sediment core removed from a moat, pond, or reservoir preserves successive layers. Radiocarbon dating of organic material within the layers gives approximate ages; the sediment's texture and chemistry indicate whether water was moving fast or slowly and whether soil was being washed in from disturbed ground; pollen shows what plants grew nearby, whether managed rice and fruit trees or regrowing forest; and charcoal indicates burning. When changes in these indicators line up, they allow researchers to reconstruct sequences of land use.

In 2019 Dan Penny and colleagues reported that cores from Angkor Thom indicated a gradual decline in land-use intensity beginning in the fourteenth century, rather than a catastrophic collapse in the fifteenth. In 2021 Tegan Hall and colleagues presented an integrated palaeoenvironmental record of early modern occupancy and land use within Angkor Thom, indicating that activity did not cease but continued in changed forms. Together these studies show a long decline in certain indicators of urban land use alongside persisting activity, a pattern more consistent with reorganization than with abrupt abandonment.

These records need careful reading. They are local: a core from one place cannot be assumed to represent every district of Greater Angkor. They are indirect: a change in pollen or sediment shows a change in vegetation and soil disturbance, not a number of people. And they are dated within ranges that can span decades or longer after calibration, which limits claims about timing to the century or half-century scale. It follows that the record may not be used to derive precise population losses or to measure royal revenue; it can show that the use of land in particular places changed, and when that change is likely to have begun.

A core's apparent precision can be misleading if students mistake the date of a sampled layer for the exact day that households departed. Pollen can move through water and wind; sediment may be reworked during floods; radiocarbon ages generally require calibration and can overlap across broad intervals. Researchers consequently build interpretations from multiple indicators, not an isolated drop in one category of material. The West Baray and the enclosure of Angkor Thom are different study locations with different depositional histories. Their records can jointly support a picture of ecological and land-use change while leaving open the chronology of particular families, neighborhoods, and political decisions.

The comparison with other districts matters. Where land-use intensity declined inside Angkor Thom, other places, such as the enclosure of Angkor Wat, show occupation continuing into later periods, and rural settlements in the plain could follow different trajectories again. Thus sediment evidence is best seen as a set of local histories that together describe an uneven landscape, in which the walled royal precinct changed earlier and differently from some other places.

Section 61.8

Infrastructure, Labor, and the Changing Capacity of the Royal State

Angkor's landscape was produced and sustained through institutions. Temple foundations held lands and dependent workers, endowed by kings and elites; the court organized labor for roads, embankments, and monuments; and rice production, fishing, and exchange provided the surplus that supported priests, officials, and artisans. The inscriptions record such arrangements in detail for earlier centuries, as Chapters 36, 38, and 39 describe. What they imply is a close relationship among agricultural production, labor mobilization, temple endowments, royal authority, and public works: each supported the others, and each could weaken the others if it faltered.

Several processes may have changed this balance after the late thirteenth century, and the evidence does not allow historians to rank them with confidence. Royal construction slowed, which may indicate that fewer resources were being directed toward large projects or that patronage was taking other forms. Religious change altered what was endowed. The wars and diplomacy that accompanied Ayutthaya's rise may have drawn on manpower and attention. Environmental shocks made maintenance more demanding. Commercial opportunities elsewhere may have drawn people and wealth toward other places. Each possibility is plausible; none has been shown to be the sole explanation.

A key insight is that reduced maintenance could be both a result and a cause of weakening central authority. If a court's resources or political legitimacy declined, it could devote less to upkeep; if upkeep declined, harvests and storage suffered, and so did the surplus that underwrote authority. Such feedback can operate gradually, which fits the slowly changing indicators found in the sediment record better than it fits a single sudden failure. It also helps explain why different parts of the landscape could change at different rates: components with independent local management might persist where those requiring central coordination weakened.

The institutional effects of a weaker center were unlikely to be uniform. A large, centrally sponsored embankment could suffer if the authority financing common works diverted resources toward warfare or competing courts. A neighborhood pond might remain functional through household cooperation, and a monastery could attract new donations despite declining support for monumental royal temples. This unevenness creates a potential feedback loop: decreased capacity to coordinate major works may reduce the benefits of membership in the central network, encouraging patrons and residents to commit labor elsewhere. The hypothesis is plausible, but records of actual workers' movements and annual revenue are insufficient to quantify the effect. Institutional reorganization is therefore a mechanism to investigate, not a measured budgetary fact.

Students should resist treating Angkor as one centrally controlled system. Local communities managed ponds, channels, and fields, temple institutions managed their own lands, and royal power was only one of several organizing forces. Attributing every infrastructural change to a single king's decision, or to a single failure, simplifies a layered system and ignores the many people who built, used, adapted, and left parts of it. The question of capacity is therefore a question about institutions and incentives as much as about technology.

Section 61.9

The Expansion of Theravada Buddhism in the Late Angkorian World

Religion at Angkor was never uniform. Over four centuries the court had patronized Shaiva and Vaishnava worship, Mahayana Buddhism, and local cults of ancestors and territorial spirits, often in combination. By the thirteenth century a further tradition was gaining ground: Theravada Buddhism, which emphasizes the Pali scriptures, a monastic order (the sangha) governed by a monastic code, and the accumulation of merit through gifts to monks and the support of religious institutions. Theravada communities were already established among the Mon and in Burma, were spreading among Tai-speaking peoples, and had links with Sri Lankan monastic lineages, as Chapter 53 described. Their growth at Angkor was therefore part of a regional development, not an isolated Khmer event.

Zhou Daguan's description of shaven-headed monks in yellow robes in 1296 is one indicator that this tradition was visible in the capital. Archaeological work by Andrew Harris and colleagues has examined the "Buddhist Terrace" archaeology of Angkor Thom and found evidence of a gradual process of religious transformation beginning in the thirteenth century, including terraces and wooden prayer halls built within the walled city. The contemporary bilingual Pali–Khmer K.754 of 1308 documents court-connected Buddhist patronage, while later inscriptions and monastic records attest changing uses of Khmer and Pali. The pattern, however, was one of accumulation across generations, not a single conversion associated with 1431.

The new emphasis changed the relationship among temples, kingship, and communities. Earlier Angkorian religion centered on large stone temples that were royal or elite foundations, with endowments of land and workers and with images connecting the king to a deity. Theravada practice placed greater weight on monastic communities that depended on lay donations from a broad range of supporters, from kings to villagers. A merit-oriented religion of this kind could be sustained by smaller, more distributed acts of giving and by modest, often wooden, buildings, and was less dependent on large central endowments than the temple-mountain tradition. This did not diminish the king's religious role, since Buddhist kings also sponsored monasteries and presented themselves as supporters of the sangha, but it changed how legitimacy and resources were expressed.

The dated K.754 inscription is an important safeguard against making Theravada patronage a mere invention of later storytellers. Its Pali and Khmer faces record a Buddhist donation associated with the transition involving Śrīndravarman, commonly called Indravarman III, and his successor. It documents a monastic endowment and the language of its commemoration in 1308; the precise assignments of donor and reign should follow a critical edition rather than be inferred from an abbreviated king list. One record proves neither that all of Angkor converted nor that older court ritual immediately ended. It does show that Buddhist institutional patronage operated in an elite setting before the events conventionally dated 1431.

Older traditions did not vanish. Hindu and Mahayana sacred places continued to be reused and reinterpreted, Brahmin ritual specialists continued to serve royal ceremonies in later Khmer courts, and local spirit worship persisted alongside monastic Buddhism. Religious change should therefore be read as a layered institutional shift in which new practices spread and older ones were recontextualized, not as a rejection of Angkor's sacred landscape.

Section 61.10

Farmers, Craftspeople, Households, and Population Mobility

Most people in the Angkor region were farmers, fishers, and workers, and their lives are the hardest to reconstruct. Their houses were of timber, bamboo, and thatch, raised on mounds with household ponds, as lidar and excavation indicate. They cultivated rice, kept fish and livestock, gathered forest products, and supplied labor and goods to temples and to the court. Specialized crafts, including pottery and stoneware production, metalwork, textiles, and stone carving, supported local needs and exchange. Zhou Daguan's description of markets, in which women were prominent, and of an economy tied to river, lake, and forest, is the closest contemporary glimpse, filtered through a foreign visitor's eyes.

Changes in elite patronage and public works affected communities in different ways. When a temple's endowment weakened, those who farmed its lands or served it might lose security or obligation, or gain autonomy. Monastic communities, which relied on local donors, provided new kinds of attachment. Households near the Tonle Sap and its fisheries could continue to rely on a rich aquatic resource regardless of the court's fortunes, whereas those dependent on reservoir irrigation faced greater exposure to hydrologic change. Experiences therefore varied by place, occupation, and status, and women, enslaved people, and dependent workers had different stakes from officials and temple elites.

People moved. Droughts, warfare, and shifting opportunities could draw households toward the lake shore, the rivers, or other regions. Later chronicles report that Ayutthayan forces carried off captives, including skilled people, and the strong Khmer influence on Ayutthayan court ritual and vocabulary is often cited as the legacy of such contact, although the scale of any forced movement cannot be established from the surviving sources. Movement is not the same as abandonment: some people left Angkor, some stayed, and some moved in. There is no basis for claiming that the whole population departed after 1431, and no reliable means of estimating how many moved where.

Migration must likewise be understood as a range of decisions rather than a single departing crowd. A dependent worker could change employer or patron without leaving the Angkor plain. A family might establish a second field near a productive channel while keeping relatives at an older village. Skilled artisans might follow a court or monastery to a southern center, whereas fishers continued to exploit familiar lake environments. Archaeological sequences can reveal occupation, reuse, or altered household activity, but rarely identify a person's motives. Warfare might produce coercive movement, while trade and changing religious patronage created other incentives. No reliable evidence permits a numerical count of people supposedly evacuated in 1431.

Ordinary life also carried continuities. Rice cultivation, fishing, household production, kin and village ties, and local religious practice continued through the period, as they did in later centuries, even as royal authority, elite temple culture, and the use of particular districts changed. Recognizing this prevents a history of the capital from being mistaken for a history of everyone who lived around it.

Section 61.11

The Late Angkorian Inscriptions and the Problem of Historical Silence

Inscriptions are among the most important sources for Angkorian history, and their changing pattern is one reason scholars recognize a transition in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. K.488, the Mangalārtha inscription, is dated to 1295 and is the last securely dated Sanskrit inscription commonly cited for the central Angkor region. K.470, a Khmer inscription at the Bayon, is dated to 1327. Their dates and catalogue identities are established; their texts are published in the Cœdès corpus and in later scholarly work, to which students should turn for translation.

Taken together, these records show an epigraphic landscape in transition: Sanskrit, the prestige language of earlier royal and temple dedications, is no longer found in dated central inscriptions after 1295, and the language of later texts shifts toward Khmer and, in Buddhist contexts, Pali. Linguists conventionally place the transition from Old to Middle Khmer in roughly the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, which fits a world in which the language of writing was changing along with the institutions that used it. Large, formal royal inscriptions on stone also become much less frequent, perhaps because patronage found new forms, such as manuscripts and wooden monastic buildings, that have not survived.

The danger is to read silence as disappearance. Fewer inscriptions do not mean that writing ceased, that kings ceased to rule, or that temples emptied. Writing on palm-leaf and other perishable materials was likely common but has largely vanished in the humid climate. Later records, including inscriptions of the sixteenth century at Angkor Wat, show that literacy, royal patronage, and religious activity persisted. The accurate statement is narrower and more interesting: the documentary record changed form, and that change is itself evidence that the institutions producing documents were changing.

The 1308 K.754 inscription complicates any claim that the documentary record simply stopped in the late thirteenth century. Its bilingual Pali–Khmer form records Buddhist patronage amid royal succession and offers a different kind of elite commemoration from the earlier Sanskrit temple inscriptions. Equally important is a negative distinction: K.488 is often identified as the last securely dated Sanskrit inscription of the central Angkorian series, not the final Sanskrit composition ever produced there. The undated Sanskrit K.300 has been associated by scholars with a reign after the 1327 K.470 inscription; its dating remains a matter of interpretation. A precise description of language, place, content, and dating avoids converting a change in documentary survival into a false absolute.

Historians should therefore ask two questions of any inscription. What does its content say about the world of its commissioners? And what does the pattern of dated inscriptions reveal about who was able and willing to commission durable texts? The first question yields information about a particular moment; the second helps to chart transformation across time.

Section 61.12

From Royal Temple-Mountains to Buddhist Worship Halls and Local Religious Institutions

The built environment of religion changed visibly. Earlier Angkorian kings had expressed power through monumental stone and laterite temples, which fixed the ruler's relationship to a deity in permanent form. In the late Angkorian period, new construction increasingly took the form of modest buildings: wooden prayer halls on stone or laterite terraces, smaller shrines, and monasteries whose superstructures were timber and tile. Archaeological work on Angkor Thom's Buddhist Terrace identified such features, including postholes and platform foundations, and dated them as part of a gradual process extending from the thirteenth century.

Older monuments were not abandoned but reused. Buddhist communities took over parts of former Hindu and Mahayana temples, installed Buddha images in galleries and sanctuaries, and added structures within the walls. At Angkor Wat, built as a Vishnu temple, Buddhist images and monastic activity became prominent. At some monuments in Angkor Thom, sculptures and sanctuaries were modified to serve Buddhist worship. Such reuse reflects an adaptive relationship to inherited sacred space, whereby the sanctity of a place was reinterpreted to fit new religious frameworks, a pattern known elsewhere in Southeast Asia.

These changes reflected different institutional priorities. A temple-mountain concentrated resources into a single enduring statement of royal and divine order; a monastery depended on continuing community support and a living order of monks. The shift in building style thus corresponds to a shift in how religious authority was organized, from centrally endowed foundations toward a more distributed network of monastic and lay institutions. Perishable construction also means that much of this religious activity is under-recorded archaeologically, which risks underestimating it.

A wooden worship hall leaves a very different archaeological signature from a stone temple-mountain. Posts decay and roofing disappears, while an elevated terrace, a foundation line, or displaced Buddha images may remain. This means that a reduction in stone building cannot straightforwardly measure a reduction in religious investment. Smaller donations could support teachers, manuscripts, ordination sites, food for monks, or maintenance of an existing sanctuary. Some patronage remained elite, while other contributions could be assembled locally over time. Historians must not assume that every terrace is precisely dated or has the same liturgical function; stratigraphy and related finds determine how confidently particular spaces can be associated with developing Theravada practice.

Students should not overstate the break. Changes in architectural form did not imply the rejection of Angkor's older sacred landscapes; instead, communities treated them as inherited places of power and merit. Continuing pilgrimage and the care of images at Angkor Wat, discussed in Section 61.17, show how older monuments acquired new meanings within a changed religious order.

Section 61.13

Shifting Trade Patterns: The Tonle Sap, Mekong, and Maritime Connections

Angkor's economy was anchored in rice, fish, forest products, and temple-centered redistribution, but it was never closed. Zhou Daguan lists forest and river products that attracted traders, such as feathers, ivory, resins, and wax, and records Chinese sailors and merchants in residence. Khmer ports and riverine markets linked the interior to the lower Mekong and the sea. Over the thirteenth through fifteenth centuries, however, the larger context of maritime trade was changing. Chinese demand for Southeast Asian products remained strong, Ming-period diplomacy and tribute missions brought Cambodian embassies to China, and merchants from many origins used the ports of the Gulf of Thailand, the Mekong delta, and the Malay world.

These changes offered opportunities to communities on the rivers and coasts. Locations at the junction of the Tonle Sap, Mekong, and Bassac rivers, such as Chaktomuk, could command movement between the interior and the sea. Ayutthaya, near the Gulf of Thailand, also had direct access to maritime commerce and was building a commercial state, as Chapter 57 described. A royal court based on the northern edge of the Tonle Sap, far from the river confluence and dependent on a vast inland landscape, was less well placed to capture this growth than centers farther south.

It does not follow that trade alone caused the court to relocate. The historical evidence shows that maritime commerce expanded and that southern locations became important; it does not show that Khmer rulers abandoned Angkor in order to pursue trade, nor does it measure the share of royal income that came from commerce. Trade is best treated as one attraction among several, working alongside the pressures of war, the strain on Angkor's water system, and the changing religious and political environment. Trade also benefited some people more than others: merchants, riverine communities, and rulers at the nodes of exchange gained influence, while inland temple-based economies could be left relatively less advantaged.

The strength of the southern waterways lay less in a simple replacement of inland agriculture by overseas commerce than in their capacity to connect different economies. Rice and fish from the Tonle Sap region could circulate alongside forest products moving down tributaries, while merchants working through the Mekong system encountered wider regional networks. Court access to such exchanges could matter politically even when the surviving documents do not provide customs accounts or annual trade totals. A court based near a confluence might also be able to negotiate with several riverine constituencies and respond to threats from more than one direction. These are geographic and political incentives, not proof of a single commercial decision to abandon Angkor.

Hence the chapter's treatment of trade is deliberately limited. Commercial change provides context for why southern riverine centers became attractive, and it must be weighed alongside other factors in the explanation discussed in Section 61.18.

Section 61.14

Khmer–Ayutthayan Rivalry and the Changing Mainland Political Order

In the fourteenth century the western flank of the Khmer world was transformed. Tai-speaking polities, including Sukhothai and the newly founded Ayutthaya, grew in strength. According to traditional dating, Ayutthaya was founded around 1351 on the Chao Phraya, where it could draw on rice agriculture, riverine trade, and access to the Gulf. Its rulers expanded by conquest and alliance, absorbing neighboring centers, as Chapter 57 recounts. In this competitive setting, the Khmer court faced a rival with comparable ambitions and with advantages in access to maritime commerce.

Thai chronicles report a series of Ayutthayan campaigns against Angkor in the late fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries, culminating in the intervention conventionally dated to 1431–1432. These reports are late and difficult to date, and their order and details vary among versions. Khmer traditions preserve their own memories of conflict with Siam, again compiled later. The mutual hostility described by the chronicles should be read as a product of how later courts remembered the past; the historical reality probably included periods of warfare, diplomacy, tribute, marriage ties, and cooperation.

Frontiers in this era were zones of overlapping loyalty rather than lines. Local lords, monastic communities, and merchants could shift allegiance, and populations were mobile. Elites moved between courts, and cultural exchange was intense: Ayutthaya adopted Khmer court vocabulary, ritual, and administrative models, while Khmer religious life took part in the wider Theravada network. Interpreting the conflict as the transfer of power from one nation-state to another would be anachronistic. Khmer and Ayutthayan rulers pursued dynastic and regional interests, and neither "Thai" nor "Khmer" identity had the national character it acquired in modern times.

Ayutthaya's growing leverage was not simply a story of a new people displacing an old one. Courts in the Chao Phraya and Khmer-speaking regions exchanged officials, captives, religious objects, artisans, and political practices even as they competed for supremacy. A local ruler near a contested frontier could recognize one overlord at one moment and another later, depending on coercion and the rewards of alliance. Chronicle reports of campaigns therefore require attention to who was writing, what legitimating purpose a victory served, and whether independent material evidence survives. A reconstructed military chronology should keep campaign claims, territorial control, and later courtly memories analytically separate.

This context is important for understanding the 1431 intervention. It was a particular episode in a prolonged competition, not a bolt from nowhere, and its outcomes depended on the condition of the Khmer court, the aims of Ayutthaya's king, and the preferences of local communities and elites who had to decide whom to support.

Section 61.15

The Ayutthayan Intervention of 1431–1432: Conquest, Occupation, and Historical Memory

The conventional account comes from the Ayutthayan chronicles. Their versions describe a campaign by the Ayutthayan king, generally identified as Borommarachathirat II, against the Khmer capital, which the chronicles call Nakhon Luang. Several versions report that the city was taken after a siege and that a royal prince was installed to govern it. Dates in the chronicles correspond to about 1431, with some accounts or conversions giving 1432. The chronicles were compiled and revised well after the events, and the versions differ in details, so they preserve a tradition about the campaign rather than a contemporary record.

Khmer traditions, in their later chronicle form, remember the period differently. They associate the movement of the court southward with a Khmer king, Ponhea Yat, and with the aftermath of Siamese pressure, but chronicle versions disagree on names, sequence, and dates. Michael Vickery's analysis of the chronicles demonstrates the difficulty of reconstructing fourteenth- and fifteenth-century Khmer history from them. A historian must therefore avoid forcing the Thai and Khmer traditions into a single smooth narrative.

Material evidence adds weight to the likelihood of an intervention. Martin Polkinghorne, Christophe Pottier, and Christian Fischer (2018) identified more than fifty Buddhist sculptures of early Ayutthayan type associated with Angkor, evidence relevant to fifteenth-century Ayutthayan presence there. This supports the view that Ayutthayan forces and officials were active at the site, and may indicate a period of occupation, although the sculptures alone do not say how long such a presence lasted or how it was administered.

The sculptural evidence is important precisely because it is independent of a later royal chronicle. More than fifty images identified as comparable to early Ayutthayan Buddhist styles support substantial artistic and religious contact, and may fit a period of Ayutthayan presence at Angkor. Yet stylistic similarity alone cannot locate a garrison, identify a ruling official, or establish how long military control lasted. Objects could be transported, commissioned by mobile patrons, or produced by craftspeople working across political boundaries. Thai chronicle traditions foreground dynastic conquest; Khmer chronicles connect the period with the movement and reconstitution of Khmer kingship. Reading both alongside archaeological context gives a stronger interpretation than either an uncritical siege narrative or an attempt to dismiss intervention altogether.

It is therefore necessary to separate four things. An Ayutthayan military intervention in the early 1430s is historically likely and significant. A period of Ayutthayan occupation is plausible, but its duration and character are uncertain. The weakening of Angkor's royal-administrative centrality was a long process that began earlier. And the continued occupation and religious use of parts of the Angkor landscape persisted afterward. These are related but cannot be assigned the same date or explanation, and 1431 should be treated as the conventional date of one event, not as the unquestioned date of Angkor's abandonment.

Section 61.16

The Reorientation of Khmer Royal Power toward Southern Riverine Centers

In the fifteenth century Khmer royal activity became associated with locations to the south and southeast of Angkor. Later Khmer chronicles name Chaktomuk, the river confluence at modern Phnom Penh, and other centers such as Basan and Srei Santhor in the Mekong valley as royal seats. Popular tradition at Phnom Penh preserves a legend of a woman named Penh who is said to have discovered Buddha images and built a hill sanctuary, which became Wat Phnom; this belongs to later legend and cannot be treated as a record of the foundation. The chronicle accounts generally credit King Ponhea Yat with establishing a court at Chaktomuk, but they differ among themselves, and modern scholars disagree about the date and the number of moves.

The reasons for the reorientation are matters of inference. Chaktomuk offered a position at the junction of the Tonle Sap, Mekong, and Bassac, with access to both the interior and the sea, greater distance from Ayutthayan pressure than Angkor, and proximity to commercial routes. The Tonle Sap basin remained important for fisheries and agriculture, and the movement of royal power did not mean the loss of that region. Fifteenth-century Khmer courts probably moved among several locations, as courts did elsewhere in the region, and the evidence supports a gradual shift more readily than a single, securely dated, permanent departure in 1431.

Students should be wary of two later projections. The first is the idea of Lovek as an established fifteenth-century capital. Lovek became a major royal center in the sixteenth century, and its prominence belongs to Chapter 89. The second is the assumption that the relocation of the court caused or reflected a complete abandonment of Angkor. The court's priorities changed, but the Angkor region continued to be inhabited and worshiped in, as the next division shows. Documentary silence about the later fifteenth century further limits what can be said, and the sources for the period are mainly later chronicles and a few foreign references.

Southern reorientation should be visualized as overlapping royal circuits, not as a modern government instantly transferring every department to a newly designated capital. A ruler could maintain followers, religious obligations, and revenue interests across several centers. River junctions enabled communication and provisioning, but their actual strategic value changed with seasonal navigation, military pressure, and ties to local elites. Chaktomuk and Basan enter the historical narrative through chronicle traditions whose sequence requires caution; the later rise of Lovek should not be pushed backward to fill fifteenth-century gaps. The movement of the court altered the geography of political patronage without erasing the economic and sacred functions of the former center.

The reorientation was thus a process, shaped by political danger, commercial opportunity, environmental and infrastructural change, and religious reorganization. It created a Khmer kingdom with a different geography from Angkor's, centered more closely on the river system, but it did not create a different people or end the continuity of Khmer language, kingship, and Buddhist institutions.

Division V · Sections 61.17–61.20

Continued Khmer Life, Historical Memory, and the Meaning of Angkor's Transformation

Section 61.17

Angkor after the Political Shift: Continuing Occupation, Worship, and Pilgrimage

Excavations at Angkor Wat reported by Alison Carter and colleagues in 2019 examined the temple's enclosure and concluded that Angkor Wat was neither permanently abandoned nor forgotten by descendant communities. Radiocarbon dates and stratigraphic evidence from the enclosure indicate that occupation and activity continued through the late medieval and early modern periods, even as the intensity and character of use changed. The finding is of special significance because Angkor Wat was long cited as an emblem of a lost city, and because it complicates the notion that the political transfer of the court emptied the landscape.

The picture within Angkor Thom is different and more mixed. The geoarchaeological research summarized in Sections 61.5 and 61.7 identifies a long decline in some indicators of urban land use, while the Hall et al. integrated record shows continuing activity in later centuries. Buddhist communities used and reshaped former Hindu and Mahayana monuments, and wooden religious buildings were constructed within the enclosure. Thus the Angkor landscape continued to be a lived, worshiped-in place, with the walled royal precinct and the Angkor Wat enclosure following distinct trajectories.

Later evidence confirms the persistence of memory and devotion. In the sixteenth century, inscriptions at Angkor Wat and royal attention to the temple show that Khmer rulers and communities treated it as a major Buddhist site, and European visitors of the late sixteenth century described a functioning monument. In the seventeenth century Japanese visitors left records at Angkor Wat, believing it to be a famous Buddhist site of India. These later events and the restoration programs of subsequent periods are introduced here only as evidence of continuity and not as the main subject of the chapter.

The archaeological contrast between Angkor Thom and Angkor Wat is especially instructive. Penny and colleagues analyze proxies for less intensive land use in the walled city, while Carter and colleagues excavated occupation mounds within Angkor Wat's enclosure and constructed a model from a limited set of radiocarbon dates. Their sampled sequence contains a gap followed by renewed or continued use from the late fourteenth or early fifteenth century into later centuries. This is not evidence that every household remained in place continuously, nor does it require all Angkor Thom districts to have followed the same timetable. It establishes that the language of total abandonment is too broad for a landscape whose religious use and residential histories varied by site.

Continuity at a sacred place is not the same as continuity of the old order. People who prayed at Angkor Wat in the sixteenth century did so within a Theravada Buddhist framework, under different patrons and with different institutions than those of Suryavarman II. The monument's meaning changed, and so did the communities that cared for it, but its sacred character persisted.

Section 61.18

Why Did Angkor Transform? Competing Explanations and New Evidence

Scholars have proposed several explanations. The invasion-centered model, rooted in the chronicles and early colonial scholarship, treats the Ayutthayan conquest of 1431 as the decisive event. The climate model, associated with the tree-ring work of Buckley and colleagues, stresses drought and extreme monsoon. The hydraulic model, partly inspired by Groslier and supported by sediment evidence from the West Baray and elsewhere, emphasizes the overextension and vulnerability of a vast water system. Religious-institutional explanations point to the rise of Theravada Buddhism and a decline of the temple-based economy and royal cults. Economic explanations point to the shift of maritime trade toward riverine and coastal centers. Demographic explanations point to the movement of people and, sometimes, to disease, although evidence for epidemic disease at Angkor is lacking and the idea should be treated as a hypothesis.

Each model finds support in some evidence and difficulty with other evidence. The invasion model is supported by chronicles and sculpture but cannot explain why land use was declining before 1431 or why Angkor Wat continued to be used. The climate model rests on a regional proxy and a plausible mechanism, but land-use decline predates some of the droughts. The hydraulic model is supported by sediment evidence of stress but does not demonstrate simultaneous failure everywhere and does not explain political choices. Religious and economic explanations have good circumstantial support but are harder to test with dated physical evidence.

A multi-causal account is stronger because it explains the pattern of timing and unevenness that single-cause models cannot. In such an account, changes in royal patronage and institutional priorities, religious reorganization, environmental stress on a demanding infrastructure, the rise of a powerful regional rival, and attractive commercial opportunities to the south interacted over generations. The scholarly synthesis by Damian Evans and colleagues, "Perspectives on the 'Collapse' of Angkor and the Khmer Empire," in The Angkorian World (2023), reflects this approach, while acknowledging that the relative weight of causes remains debated.

A useful test of competing interpretations is to ask what each would predict at several independent locations. A sudden conquest model might predict a concentrated horizon of destruction or rapid departure across the major centers, but the available archaeological trajectories are not uniformly synchronized. A purely climatic model should explain why some religious and residential activity continued despite regional hydrological stress. A purely religious model must account for changing infrastructure, warfare, and shifting riverine political interests. A model emphasizing commercial geography still needs evidence connecting particular royal patrons to particular routes. Comparisons do not produce a precise percentage contribution for each cause; they expose which arguments fit the evidence and which depend on untested assumptions.

Students should note that causal debates in history are rarely resolved by a single decisive find. New studies refine chronologies and may shift emphasis. The sound response is to hold the explanation provisionally, to keep track of which parts rest on strong evidence and which on inference, and to avoid selecting only the evidence that fits a favored narrative.

Historical Debate

Collapse, Relocation, or Long-Term Transformation?

The question: Should the end of Angkor's imperial role be described as a collapse, as a relocation of the court, or as a long-term transformation?

Collapse: Emphasizes the end of large-scale monumental building, the weakening of the royal state, the stress on infrastructure, and the loss of the capital's centrality as a rupture with the imperial order.
Relocation: Emphasizes the movement of the court and elites toward southern centers, often tied to the 1431 campaign and to commercial advantage, treating Khmer polity as continuing in a new place.
Long-term transformation: Emphasizes gradual, uneven change beginning before 1431, interacting causes, and continued occupation and religious life, arguing that "collapse" misdescribes the evidence.

Points of disagreement: Chronology (how early change began); terminology (what "collapse" implies); causation (relative weight of climate, institutions, and war); and scale (how far districts differ).

Section 61.19

Khmer Political and Cultural Life around 1500

By about 1500 Khmer kingship was a continuing institution, even though its seat had moved from Angkor. Later chronicles list a succession of kings through the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, and the Khmer court maintained relationships with Ayutthaya and Đại Việt that included tribute, diplomacy, rivalry, and warfare. The details of these relationships in the later fifteenth century are poorly documented. What is clear is that a Khmer polity with a king, officials, and territorial claims existed, centered on the river system and still drawing on the Tonle Sap basin.

Khmer language continued to develop and was increasingly written in forms scholars call Middle Khmer. Theravada Buddhism became more firmly established through monasteries and lay devotion, and Pali learning and merit-making shaped religious life, while Brahmin ritual specialists continued to serve royal ceremonies. Rice agriculture and fishing continued to support the population, and communities at river junctions took part in regional trade. Cultural memory of Angkor persisted in stories, in the veneration of its monuments, and in the later royal use of its imagery.

The difference between the transformation of an imperial capital and the survival of Khmer society is the key lesson of this period. The capital's centrality, its great stone temple-building tradition, and its distinctive hydraulic-urban configuration declined or changed. The people, language, religion, and economic life of the Khmer world did not disappear, but adapted to a new geography and a new religious order. Historians must also remember that "Khmer" in this period describes a language community and political tradition, not a modern national category, and the later Cambodian state was not the inevitable outcome of this history.

For ordinary Khmer communities around 1500, continuity would have been visible in agriculture, fishing, linguistic practice, marriage networks, and devotion to local sacred places, even while royal itineraries changed. A monk's monastery or a village shrine might serve as a durable point of identity where the political ambitions of rival courts were unstable. At the same time, changes in patronage and military power could redistribute labor obligations and opportunities unequally. These realities resist a neat division between an extinct Angkorian civilization and a wholly new post-Angkorian society. Historical continuity does not mean unchanged institutions; it means that people adapted existing practices and relationships to new political and economic circumstances.

The limits of the record are severe. Few contemporary Khmer sources survive for the later fifteenth century. Chronicles written later impose a neat sequence of kings on a period in which events were probably more complicated, and foreign sources, such as Chinese records of diplomatic contact, give only partial glimpses. Claims about the extent of royal power, the size of the population, or the condition of the countryside around 1500 should therefore be made cautiously.

Section 61.20

Angkor's Transformation: Environment, Warfare, Religion, Mobility, and Historical Continuity

Looking across the whole period, Angkor's transformation can be understood as the interaction of several processes. Royal authority and patronage changed, with fewer large construction projects and a shift in religious emphasis. The urban and hydraulic landscape faced the strain of a demanding maintenance regime in an unstable climate. Theravada Buddhism spread through distributed monastic institutions. Ayutthaya's rise and its campaigns introduced a powerful rival and, in the early 1430s, a historically significant intervention. Commercial opportunity drew attention to the lower Mekong and the sea. People moved in ways that cannot be reconstructed in detail. And throughout, parts of the Angkor landscape continued to be inhabited and revered.

Different parts of the Khmer world experienced these changes on different timescales. Angkor Thom's interior shows a long decline in land-use intensity beginning in the fourteenth century. Angkor Wat shows continued occupation. The court's relocation unfolded over decades. Religious change spanned centuries. Climate episodes were measured in decades. A single date cannot summarize such varied tempos, and the idea of a sudden "fall of Angkor" should be replaced with a more accurate picture of uneven reorganization.

The synthesis also warns against selective reading. Using only the invasion narrative erases the earlier changes; using only climate erases politics and religion; claiming that Angkor was never abandoned ignores the real decline of the capital's role; and claiming total abandonment ignores the archaeological evidence of continuity. The task is to hold disruption and resilience together.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Angkor's transformation between roughly 1250 and 1500 cannot be explained by one invasion, one drought, or one religious conversion. It was a prolonged reorganization of royal, environmental, urban, religious, and economic systems that began before the Ayutthayan intervention conventionally dated 1431 and continued afterward. The chapter's central distinction is between the political decline of a capital and the disappearance of a society.

Division I established the evidence and setting. Inscriptions, chronicles, Chinese testimony, excavation, lidar, sediment cores, pollen, and tree rings each measure different things, and a careful reconstruction combines them without forcing agreement. Angkor lay on the northern margin of the Tonle Sap in a landscape of reservoirs, canals, and settlements, and Zhou Daguan's account of 1296–1297 describes a functioning royal city while remaining limited in coverage and perspective. Lidar showed that monuments were embedded in a vast inhabited landscape, and that capital, landscape, and monument must be distinguished.

Division II examined chronology and environment. The Mangalārtha inscription (1295) and the Bayon inscription (1327) frame a period of thinning royal documentation. Geoarchaeological evidence shows declining land-use intensity in Angkor Thom from the fourteenth century. Tree rings reveal droughts and intense monsoons that stressed a maintenance-intensive water system, and sediment evidence from the West Baray reflects changing hydrology. Because land-use decline predates some climatic disturbances, climate is best regarded as a contributing factor that may have amplified an existing transformation. Infrastructure and royal capacity were linked in a feedback relationship, and Angkor's water system was not a single rigid system failing everywhere at once.

Division III turned to religion and society. Theravada Buddhism expanded gradually from the thirteenth century, with monastic communities, merit-making, wooden halls, and Pali learning shaping a more distributed pattern of patronage, while older Hindu and Mahayana sacred places were reused and reinterpreted. Ordinary farmers, fishers, and craftspeople experienced change unevenly and moved in ways that cannot be quantified. The changing pattern of inscriptions reflects changing institutions and must not be read as the disappearance of writing or kingship.

Division IV addressed regional politics and trade. Ayutthaya's rise, traditionally dated from about 1351, created a powerful competitor with maritime access. Thai chronicles report campaigns culminating in the intervention of 1431–1432, and the discovery of more than fifty Buddhist sculptures of early Ayutthayan type supports the likelihood of fifteenth-century presence at Angkor. The details of duration and consequence remain uncertain, and chronicles on both sides were compiled later. Khmer royal power gradually became associated with southern riverine centers such as Chaktomuk, a process whose chronology is contested and which should not be collapsed into a single move in 1431 or connected to the later prominence of Lovek.

Division V examined continuity and interpretation. Excavations at Angkor Wat indicate that the temple was neither permanently abandoned nor forgotten, while Angkor Thom's interior shows long decline in certain indicators of land use, demonstrating uneven change across the landscape. Competing explanations (invasion, climate, hydraulic strain, religious change, commerce, and demography) each have strengths and weaknesses, and a multi-causal account best explains the timing and unevenness of the evidence, though the relative weights remain debated.

By around 1500 Khmer kingship, language, Buddhist institutions, agricultural life, and cultural memory continued, with a new geography centered on the river system. The documentary record for this period is thin, and later chronicles impose a neat narrative on a more complicated reality. Historians should therefore make cautious claims about royal power, population, and the condition of the countryside.

The chapter also taught method. Dates from chronicles must be tested against independently dated evidence; lidar reveals shapes but not dates; sediment cores show land-use change but not population counts; inscriptions record elite commissions and not everyone's lives; and silence in the record is not proof of absence. These habits apply to any study of the decline of a capital or state.

Finally, the chapter has emphasized that Angkor's transformation should not be read teleologically. Neither the rise of Ayutthaya nor the emergence of later Cambodia was inevitable, and the Khmer past should not be treated as a steady progression toward the modern national state. Angkor remained a place of inhabitation, worship, and memory, while the Khmer world developed in other centers. Chapter 62 turns from the mainland to the wider Islamic maritime networks of the Indian Ocean.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Angkor
The royal capital and surrounding landscape of Khmer kings on the northern edge of the Tonle Sap basin, used here for the political center, as distinct from Greater Angkor and individual monuments.
Greater Angkor
The extensive inhabited landscape of roads, canals, house mounds, ponds, and temples around the monumental core, mapped over hundreds to about a thousand square kilometers.
Baray
A large rectangular reservoir enclosed by earthen embankments; functions, including storage, flood management, irrigation, and ritual symbolism, are debated.
Tonle Sap
The large Cambodian lake and river system whose flow reverses seasonally with the Mekong flood, supporting fisheries and floodplain agriculture.
Lidar
Airborne laser scanning that measures ground-surface relief beneath vegetation; it shows shapes but not dates.
Radiocarbon dating
A method of estimating the age of organic material from decay of carbon-14, reported as a range after calibration.
Calibration
The conversion of raw radiocarbon measurements into calendar-year probability ranges.
Sediment core
A cylinder of layered mud extracted from a moat, pond, or reservoir that records changing conditions over time.
Pollen analysis
The identification of preserved pollen grains to reconstruct past vegetation and land use.
Dendroclimatology
The reconstruction of past climate from tree-ring widths, used here for regional monsoon and drought history.
Land-use intensity
The degree to which a place was actively farmed, built on, or otherwise managed, inferred from environmental indicators; not a population count.
Epigraphy
The study of inscriptions; for Angkor, texts in Sanskrit, Old Khmer, Middle Khmer, and Pali.
K. number
The catalogue number assigned to a Khmer inscription in the standard corpus, such as K.488 or K.470.
Chronicle
A narrative record of rulers and events compiled by a court or monastery, often long after the events; useful for memory and claims, cautious for dating.
Mangalārtha inscription (K.488)
A Sanskrit inscription dated 1295, the last securely dated Sanskrit inscription commonly cited for the central Angkor region.
Bayon inscription (K.470)
A Khmer inscription at the Bayon dated 1327.
Theravada
The form of Buddhism emphasizing the Pali scriptures and a monastic order, expanding across mainland Southeast Asia in this period.
Mahayana
A Buddhist tradition patronized by Jayavarman VII and others at Angkor, distinct from Theravada.
Sangha
The community of Buddhist monks, governed by a monastic code and supported by lay donors.
Vihara
A monastic residence or worship hall; in Angkor Thom, wooden halls on terraces are characteristic of late Angkorian Buddhism.
Merit
Spiritual benefit believed to result from virtuous acts, especially giving to monks and supporting monasteries.
Sima
A boundary stone consecrating ritual space for monastic ordination and other formal acts.
Religious patronage
Material support of religious institutions by rulers, elites, and ordinary donors in exchange for merit, status, or legitimacy.
Chaktomuk
The river confluence of the Mekong, Tonle Sap, and Bassac rivers, site of modern Phnom Penh and a later Khmer royal center.
Nakhon Luang
The name by which Ayutthayan chronicles refer to the Khmer capital in their accounts of the early-fifteenth-century campaign.
Middle Khmer
The stage of the Khmer language conventionally dated to roughly the fourteenth through eighteenth centuries, following Old Khmer.
Anachronism
Applying a later concept, such as a modern nation-state, to an earlier period where it did not apply.
Transformation
This chapter's governing term for the prolonged, uneven reorganization of Angkor's political, environmental, religious, and economic systems, as opposed to a sudden collapse.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it replaces a dramatic story with a more demanding one. The image of a great city falling on a single date and vanishing into the forest is memorable, but it fits neither the dated inscriptions, the sediment and pollen records, the tree rings, nor the excavations at Angkor Wat. What the evidence shows is a long reorganization in which political authority, religion, environment, regional rivalry, and commerce shifted together.

It also shows how historians work. They combine different kinds of evidence, distinguish contemporary documents from retrospective narrative, and resist converting indirect indicators into precise numbers. They also distinguish the fate of elites and capitals from that of the many people who farmed, fished, worshiped, and made things in a landscape that outlasted the court's presence.

The developments traced here shaped what came after. The Khmer kingdom that emerged around the river system, treated in Chapters 89 and 90, was shaped by the reorientation of royal power, the expansion of Theravada Buddhism, and the pressure and exchange with Ayutthaya and Đại Việt. Angkor Wat itself became a continuing Buddhist site and a lasting symbol of Khmer memory.

For the larger textbook, the chapter reinforces a theme from Chapter 1: Southeast Asian societies were historical actors that adapted to changing conditions rather than passive recipients of forces from outside. The lesson that the transformation of a capital is not the disappearance of a civilization applies well beyond Angkor, and it prepares students for the next chapter's turn to the Islamic networks that linked Southeast Asia to the wider Indian Ocean world.
