Chapter Opening

In the last decades of Spanish rule, a small, educated, and internationally mobile group of Filipinos turned colonial grievances into arguments, newspapers, novels, associations, and petitions. They asked Spain to keep its own constitutional promises. When Spain would not, they left behind a political vocabulary, and a political problem, that no later movement could ignore.

On the evening of 25 June 1884, in a Madrid banquet hall, a group of Filipino and Spanish guests gathered to honor two painters from the Philippines. Juan Luna had recently won a gold medal at the Madrid national exhibition of fine arts for his monumental canvas Spoliarium, and Félix Resurrección Hidalgo had won a silver medal. The young medical student José Rizal rose to propose a toast. According to the surviving accounts of the speech, he treated the painters’ success as evidence that talent was not confined to any one people, and as an implicit argument that Filipinos deserved to be judged by their abilities rather than by the colonial categories into which they had been sorted. The scene is modest. No law was passed, no governor was challenged, and no army moved. Yet it condenses much of what this chapter studies: young Filipinos in the metropole, using a language and an institutional setting borrowed from Spanish liberalism, claiming a public standing that the colonial order denied them.

This chapter examines the movement of which that evening was a small part. The movement had no single founding date, no formal membership, and no unified program. Historians conventionally call it the Propaganda Movement, and they associate it chiefly with the 1880s and first half of the 1890s. It consisted of Filipino students, professionals, journalists, artists, and expatriates, mostly in Spain, together with supporters and correspondents in the Philippines. They sought equality before the law, representation in the Spanish parliament, freedom of expression, protection against arbitrary authority, educational advancement, and a reduced political role for the Spanish religious orders. They did so through speeches, pamphlets, periodicals, novels, scholarly editions, private letters, fundraising, and lobbying. Their most famous figure, José Rizal, was a physician, novelist, historian, and essayist whose two novels remain among the most widely read books in Philippine history. But the movement was larger than Rizal, and a history that reduces it to his biography misunderstands both him and it.

The chapter’s central question concerns transformation and failure. How did educated Filipinos convert grievances rooted in landholding, clerical power, racial hierarchy, censorship, and unequal citizenship into a developing national consciousness? And why did the campaign for reform, conducted largely within the boundaries of Spanish law and with considerable restraint, fail to secure the changes it sought? The two questions are connected. The reformers did not begin as revolutionaries, and most of them did not describe themselves as separatists. Yet the experience of petitioning an unresponsive imperial state taught lessons that outlasted the movement’s own methods. The chapter therefore avoids two opposite errors. It does not present the reformers as covert revolutionaries who merely disguised their intentions, and it does not present revolution in 1896 as the inevitable result of their work. Contemporaries did not know how the story would end.

The chapter draws on an unusually rich body of evidence for a colonial Southeast Asian movement. Rizal’s novels, essays, and letters survive in quantity; the periodical La Solidaridad (1889–1895) has been digitized and republished; the statutes of La Liga Filipina and the records of Rizal’s trial are preserved; and the memoirs and correspondence of participants allow historians to see disagreements that public statements concealed. These sources must be read critically. Novels are literary constructions, not documentary reports. Newspapers addressed Spanish readers and were written with censorship and political strategy in mind. Trial records were produced by a military court determined to establish guilt. Later memory, shaped by revolution, American colonial rule, and national commemoration, turned the participants into heroes and sometimes simplified them in the process.

The chapter also relies on the earlier chapters of this volume. Chapter 115 examined the nineteenth-century Spanish Philippines: its export economy, social hierarchy, education, provincial society, religious orders, and the secularization dispute of 1872. Chapter 116 does not repeat that history. It asks how educated Filipinos interpreted these conditions and tried to change them. It also stops short of the revolutionary organization examined in Chapter 117. The Katipunan was founded in the same month that La Liga Filipina was suppressed, but its organization, ideology, and campaigns belong to that later chapter. Between the two lies the problem this chapter tries to explain: how a political culture based on petition, journalism, and constitutional argument encountered its limits.

Finally, the chapter belongs to the wider history of colonial Southeast Asia examined in this textbook. Educated colonial subjects in other parts of the region, in Java, Burma, Malaya, and Vietnam, would also use print, schools, associations, and borrowed political languages in the twentieth century to challenge imperial rule, as later chapters examine. The Philippine case is distinctive in its timing, its relationship to Spanish liberalism, and the central role of Catholic institutions and secular-clergy politics. But it also shows a process that recurred: colonial education produced subjects capable of using the empire’s own principles against it, and imperial resistance to reform pushed some of them toward more radical conclusions. Filipino reformers were not passive recipients of European ideas. They selected, translated, argued with, and reshaped what they encountered.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the events of 1872 and the memory of the executed priests shaped the political outlook of the generation of Filipino reformers.
  2. Describe the social composition of the ilustrado class and distinguish its different economic positions, regional origins, and political ambitions.
  3. Analyze the vocabulary of Spanish liberalism, including citizenship, assimilation, and representation, and explain how reformers used it.
  4. Trace the transnational geography of Filipino political activity among Manila, Madrid, Barcelona, Paris, Berlin, London, Hong Kong, and the provinces.
  5. Interpret Noli Me Tangere and El Filibusterismo as historical interventions, distinguishing literary representation from documentary evidence.
  6. Evaluate the founding, editorial strategy, financing, and political significance of La Solidaridad.
  7. Compare the roles and priorities of Rizal, Marcelo H. del Pilar, Graciano López Jaena, Mariano Ponce, and other participants in the reform network.
  8. Identify the principal reform demands and explain why Spanish institutions and colonial interests resisted them.
  9. Distinguish opposition to the political power of the religious orders from opposition to Catholicism itself.
  10. Assess the changing meaning of “Filipino” and the critique of racial hierarchy, including Rizal’s edition of Morga’s Sucesos de las Islas Filipinas.
  11. Examine the social reach of reform, including the activities of women, provincial readers, and communities divided by class, language, and region.
  12. Evaluate competing interpretations of Rizal’s relationship to reform, nationalism, and revolution, distinguishing contemporary evidence from later heroic memory.

Historical Context

Spanish rule in the Philippines began with the expeditions examined in Chapter 73 and was consolidated through a colonial order built on tribute, parish administration, and the Manila galleon trade (Chapter 74). By the nineteenth century this order was changing. Manila was opened to foreign commerce, export agriculture expanded, and a Chinese-mestizo and indigenous landholding and commercial elite grew in wealth and ambition, as examined in Chapter 115. Steamships and the opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 shortened travel between Manila and Europe, making it possible for more families to send sons to study in Spain and elsewhere. The colonial educational decree of 1863 and the expansion of schools, including the Jesuit-run Ateneo Municipal and the Dominican University of Santo Tomás, produced a larger Spanish-literate group than had existed earlier, though a small minority of the total population.

These developments coincided with instability in Spain itself. The revolution of 1868 briefly opened a liberal period in which the Philippines received a reform-minded governor, Carlos María de la Torre. The restoration of conservative authority in the early 1870s brought a more reactionary approach. The Cavite uprising of January 1872 and the executions of Fathers Mariano Gómez, José Burgos, and Jacinto Zamora the following month became, in effect, the political prehistory of the movement studied here. After 1874 the Spanish Restoration monarchy, with its constitution of 1876, provided a constitutional framework in which the Philippines was governed under “special laws” rather than through ordinary representation in the Cortes. This distinction, between a constitutional Spain and a colonial Philippines administered by decree, is the legal ground on which Filipino reformers would later argue.

The chapter’s boundaries are important. The origins of the colonial hierarchy, the structure of the friar estates and parish system, the economic conditions of the nineteenth century, and the secularization dispute belong chiefly to Chapter 115 and are summarized here only as needed. The organization, program, and campaigns of the Katipunan and the revolution that began in 1896 belong to Chapter 117. Rizal’s execution is narrated here, because it is indispensable to his biography and to the fate of the reform campaign, but the revolutionary meaning of the event belongs to the next chapter. What Chapter 116 contributes is the intellectual and political middle ground: the attempt to secure change through argument and law, the development of a Filipino public sphere stretching across oceans, and the gradual discovery of how narrow the available space for peaceful reform had become.

Section 116.1

The Legacy of 1872 and the Making of a Reformist Generation

The events of early 1872 are narrated in Chapter 115. What matters here is how they were remembered, because the reformist generation of the 1880s was formed in their shadow. On 17 February 1872 the colonial authorities executed Fathers Mariano Gómez, José Burgos, and Jacinto Zamora by garrote at Bagumbayan, a few weeks after a mutiny among arsenal workers and soldiers at Cavite. Spanish officials and many friar writers presented the uprising as a broad separatist conspiracy in which the three secular priests were leading participants. Many Filipinos, and some Spaniards, instead saw the executions as reprisal against a group of Filipino clergy and professionals who had advocated parish rights, educational opportunity, and a more equal colonial order. Historians still debate how extensive and coordinated the Cavite rising was, and the surviving evidence consists largely of official proceedings and partisan accounts rather than neutral reports.

The reformers did not need to settle that debate to draw conclusions from it. For them, the case demonstrated that Filipino priests, however learned and loyal, could be tried and killed under an arbitrary system whose judges were also their rivals. It showed that advocacy of equal clerical treatment could be treated as treason. And it showed that the colonial state’s most severe instruments could be used against educated Filipinos who sought changes within the existing framework. Rizal later dedicated his second novel to the three priests, a gesture that fixed them in the public record as symbolic ancestors of the reform cause. The dedication is a literary and political act, not a historical report about the 1872 proceedings, and it reveals the memory’s function rather than the events’ details.

The transmission of that memory ran through families, schools, and exile networks. Rizal’s elder brother Paciano had been associated with Burgos, and Rizal’s later account of his family’s experience makes clear that the executions shaped his household’s understanding of colonial power. After 1872 the authorities also deported a number of Filipino lawyers, merchants, and professionals, including Joaquín Pardo de Tavera and Antonio María Regidor, to the Mariana Islands; several later reached Europe or Hong Kong, where they joined the expatriate networks that sustained later political activity. Their presence in these centers connected the generation of 1872 to the younger students arriving in the 1880s.

The memory also had a religious dimension. The priests were not merely political suspects; they were clergymen at the center of a long dispute over whether Filipino secular priests should hold parishes then held by Spanish friars. Their deaths therefore tied grievances about clerical rank, racial discrimination, and political loyalty into a single story. When reformers later criticized the friar orders, they could present themselves as defenders of a Filipino clergy that had already paid heavily, rather than as enemies of the Church. This framing matters for Section 116.13, which examines the difference between anticlericalism and religious unbelief.

It would be a mistake, however, to treat 1872 as a simple origin point from which a straight line leads to revolution. The reformers’ immediate response was caution. Many of the educated men who emerged in the following decade pursued their goals in Spain, within the law, and often in the language of loyalty. The lesson they drew was that the colonial government in Manila could not be trusted to protect reformist speech, and that metropolitan politics, where press freedom and parliamentary argument were possible, offered a safer arena. In that sense 1872 shaped the movement’s geography as much as its grievances. It pushed advocacy outward, to places where the authorities of Manila could not easily reach, and it made the memory of martyrdom a resource that later writers could invoke without having to reconstruct the events themselves.

The documentary afterlife of 1872 illustrates why memory and proof must be distinguished. Accounts composed by colonial authorities were produced in the setting of prosecution; later nationalist accounts often treated the priests' innocence as a settled moral foundation. A responsible historian can recognize the enormous political consequences of the executions without assuming that either set of interested accounts is a neutral transcript of the past. Reformists used the priests' names to make a larger claim: political loyalty and professional competence should not have exposed a colonial subject to punishment because of ancestry. By attaching a publicly remembered injustice to a legal argument about equal treatment, the younger generation supplied continuity between the parish controversies of the 1860s and the print campaigns of the 1880s. It is this transformation of remembered suffering into a demand for accountable institutions—not a supposed inevitability of rebellion—that explains 1872's central place in the reform movement.

Historical Vocabulary
Secularization, Reformism, and Political Martyrdom

Secularization, in the context of the Spanish Catholic Church, refers to the transfer of parishes from members of the religious orders (the regular clergy, or friars) to diocesan priests under the authority of the bishop (the secular clergy). It does not mean the removal of religion from public life. In the Philippines the term carried sharp political weight, because the secular clergy included growing numbers of Filipinos, while the friar orders were predominantly Spanish. A dispute about parish assignments therefore became a dispute about ethnic equality and institutional power. Students should not confuse this ecclesiastical meaning with the modern idea of secularism as separation of church and state.

Reformism names the political position that existing institutions can and should be improved through legal and peaceful means. Philippine reformers asked Spain to change its colonial policy; they did not, in most cases during the 1880s, ask for independence. The term describes methods and stated goals and should not be used to imply that reformers lacked deeper aspirations or that reformist politics was weak or timid.

Political martyrdom refers to the commemoration of a person killed by authority as a symbol of a cause. Martyrdom is a process of memory as well as an event. The 1872 priests were executed by the colonial state, but they became political martyrs because later writers, families, and movements retold their deaths in particular ways. Potential misunderstanding: calling the priests “martyrs” in a historical description is not a claim about the justice or truth of the original accusations, which are separately examined by historians.

Section 116.2

The Ilustrados and the Social Foundations of Filipino Reform

The Spanish word ilustrado means “enlightened” or “learned,” and in Philippine history it designates educated Filipinos of the late nineteenth century who combined learning, some economic security, and a claim to public voice. The term is useful but easily overstated. It did not name a formal body with a membership list. It described a loosely connected stratum whose members shared experiences, such as higher schooling in Manila or Europe, Spanish-language literacy, and exposure to liberal ideas, while differing sharply in wealth, ancestry, regional ties, and political aims.

The social foundations lay in the economic changes surveyed in Chapter 115. Export agriculture, urban commerce, and landholding had enriched families of Chinese-mestizo, Spanish-mestizo, and indigenous principal descent, and some of them invested their gains in education. Ateneo, Santo Tomás, San Juan de Letrán, and other schools trained lawyers, physicians, notaries, teachers, and clergy. Wealthy families then sent sons abroad. Pedro Paterno, Trinidad Pardo de Tavera, Juan Luna, and Antonio Luna belonged to families with considerable resources. Others came from more modest circumstances and relied on kin, patrons, or scholarships. Marcelo H. del Pilar was a provincial lawyer from Bulacan with a respected family name but limited ready funds, and Graciano López Jaena of Iloilo depended heavily on patrons and on his own talents as a writer and speaker. Rizal’s family leased land on a religious estate and was prosperous, but remained subject to a landlord whose power it could not escape.

These differences shaped political outlooks. Some wealthy ilustrados were primarily interested in social recognition and legal equality for people of their own class and may have been more cautious about measures that threatened property or hierarchy. Others, especially those who experienced direct conflicts with friar estates or officials, were more willing to criticize the existing order. The term ilustrado therefore describes a social category, not an ideology. Historians who treat it as a single political party obscure the debates within the group, as Section 116.16 shows.

Equally important is who was excluded. The great majority of the population, farmers, tenants, fishing families, urban laborers, and many women, had little access to Spanish-language schooling and left few direct records in this movement’s sources. Whether reformist ideas reached them, and in what forms, is a question considered in Section 116.15. The ilustrados often spoke for “the Filipino people,” but they did so as an educated minority, and their claim to represent a wider population was both a rhetorical strategy and a historical problem. The reformers’ own writings sometimes acknowledge this distance, as when they argue that education should be extended to the majority, and sometimes obscure it.

Ilustrado life depended on institutions that connected local society to wider currents. Families sent letters, remittances, and gifts; teachers and priests formed networks; Masonic lodges in Spain, where several Filipinos took part, provided meeting places and political contacts. Churches, lodges, schools, and families were all places where ideas circulated and where loyalties competed. The movement’s social foundations were therefore neither purely elite nor entirely separate from provincial communities, but they were narrow, and the reformers’ long-term difficulty was to enlarge them.

The category also becomes clearer if one separates the capacity to speak publicly from the authority to represent others. A medical student in Madrid could compose a persuasive essay about taxation or parish administration without having been elected by the tenants or parishioners whose interests he discussed. His social access, literacy, and command of Spanish made the intervention possible; they did not automatically make its generalizations representative. This tension recurs throughout the movement. Donations and correspondence linked overseas writers to relatives and supporters in the islands, but these links were neither a mass electoral mandate nor a stable national party apparatus. Some reformers wanted primarily civil equality, while others increasingly questioned the imperial relationship itself. Readers should therefore ask whose resources made a particular campaign possible, whose experience furnished its evidence, and whose preferences remained invisible in the published record. These questions help explain both the movement's impressive intellectual reach and its difficulties with sustained organization.

Section 116.3

Spanish Liberalism, Assimilation, and the Problem of Colonial Citizenship

The reformers wrote in a political language they had inherited from Spain. Spanish liberalism, as it developed after the constitutional experiments of the early nineteenth century, included commitments to written constitutions, representative government, civil liberties, legal equality, and the rights of citizens. Its history was turbulent. The Constitution of Cádiz in 1812 had provided for representation from overseas provinces, and Philippine deputies sat in the Spanish parliament in the early nineteenth century. In 1837, however, the constitutional order was revised to exclude the overseas provinces from ordinary representation, declaring that they would be governed by “special laws.” Subsequent constitutions retained this approach, so that in principle Spain was a constitutional monarchy while its Philippine possessions were administered largely by decree and by the discretion of governors-general.

That discrepancy was the reformers’ principal opening. They could argue that Spain’s own principles required the Philippines to be treated as a province entitled to the same rights as peninsular provinces. The term for this position was asimilación, or assimilation: the extension of Spanish law, representation, and civil rights to the Philippines. Assimilation was not a call for cultural erasure. In the reformers’ usage it meant legal and political equality within the Spanish state, and some advocates assumed that Filipinos could maintain distinct local identities and customs. Others, as Section 116.16 shows, differed over how far assimilation implied cultural convergence with Spain.

The problem of colonial citizenship ran through this argument. Filipinos were Spanish nationals in the sense that they owed obedience to the Spanish crown. But being a national did not carry the same practical rights as being a citizen of metropolitan Spain. Tribute obligations, restrictions on speech and association, the governor-general’s power to deport without trial, and the political influence of religious orders made colonial subjecthood a lesser status. The reformers demanded that this gap be closed. Their arguments were not merely rhetorical. They pointed to specific features of Spanish law, such as press regulations, judicial procedures, and the constitutional article providing for special laws, and argued that these could be repealed or revised.

Why did the reformers use this vocabulary rather than the language of independence? Several reasons are visible in the evidence. First, assimilationist arguments were legally available and relatively safe; open advocacy of separation would have been treasonous and could have led to prosecution. Second, many reformers had real intellectual and emotional ties to Spanish culture, language, and liberal ideals, and some believed reform within Spain was achievable. Third, the Philippines was a diverse archipelago with no unified political structure that reformers could plausibly claim to represent, and a project of independence would have required organizational resources that did not yet exist. Fourth, Spanish liberal and republican politicians, including Miguel Morayta and others sympathetic to colonial reform, offered allies within the metropolitan system, which made persuasion seem worthwhile.

At the same time, the vocabulary was strategic. Some contemporaries, and later historians, have asked whether assimilationist language concealed deeper separatist hopes or merely reflected a pragmatic response to circumstances. The evidence does not support one answer for all participants. Rizal’s essays contemplated the possibility of separation if reform were denied, while still arguing for reform; del Pilar’s journalism remained committed to reform for much of the period; and others changed positions over time. The most defensible interpretation is that assimilation functioned simultaneously as a genuine goal for many, a tactical necessity for others, and an unstable position that could be abandoned when its premises failed.

The constitutional question can be tested through institutions rather than slogans. Representation would have required an electoral mechanism and seats through which Philippine interests could be raised in the Cortes; protection of press freedom would have required limits on the governor-general's administrative power; equal legal treatment would have required workable courts and remedies rather than statements of principle alone. These reforms were related but not identical. A ministry might grant changes in municipal organization while refusing colonial parliamentary representation, or endorse education while leaving censorship intact. Nor did extension of a metropolitan legal code necessarily dismantle the practical advantages of a religious order or provincial official. The separation of formal law from enforcement helps explain why the propagandists measured reform through concrete institutional outcomes. It also reveals an enduring ambiguity: demanding rights as Spanish citizens might strengthen claims to equality, while the assertion of a distinctive Filipino political community might eventually challenge Spain's authority to determine the nation's future.

Historical Debate

Reform within Spain or Eventual Separation?

The question: Were the reformers sincerely seeking integration into the Spanish constitutional order, or was assimilationist language a cautious preliminary to a longer-term goal of separation?

Interpretation A — assimilation as a genuine, durable goal: Scholars who stress the reformers’ own words and organizational choices argue that most participants wanted equal rights within the Spanish monarchy for a substantial period. They emphasize that the campaign expended great energy on lobbying, journalism, and parliamentary contact, which would have made little sense if the aim had been separation from the outset.
Interpretation B — assimilation as strategy and developing consciousness: Others stress evidence that reformist arguments contained the seeds of separate national identity, such as the insistence on a distinct Filipino history and character, and that disillusionment gradually moved some participants toward more radical positions. On this view the movement created a Filipino consciousness whose implications exceeded its stated aims.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on La Solidaridad, Rizal’s essays and letters, the correspondence among reformers, and the later statements of participants. The disagreement concerns interpretation and emphasis rather than the existence of the texts.

Why it matters: If assimilation was sincere, the failure of reform becomes a story of Spanish intransigence in ending a viable peaceful path. If it was mainly tactical, the movement appears as a long preparation for separation. The most careful accounts recognize that individuals and moments differed.

Student task: Select two named participants from this chapter and identify evidence that would help determine whether each regarded assimilation as a goal, a tactic, or a position under strain. Explain what evidence you would still need.

Section 116.4

Manila, Madrid, Barcelona, and the Geography of Political Reform

The movement operated across at least three kinds of space: the Philippines, the Spanish metropole, and a wider network of European and Asian cities. Its geography was not accidental. It followed the lines of education, communication, censorship, and legal risk. In the Philippines, public political organization was dangerous, the press was censored, and the governor-general and religious orders held substantial influence. In Spain, particularly after the liberalizing political changes of the 1880s, there was more room for public debate and for associations, even though many Spaniards were indifferent or hostile to colonial reform.

Madrid was the principal political center. The Universidad Central drew Filipino students, the Cortes and the national press offered audiences, and Spanish republicans and liberals provided potential allies. Associations such as the Círculo Hispano-Filipino, founded in the early 1880s, and later the Asociación Hispano-Filipina gave expatriates organizational forms. Barcelona, with its tradition of republicanism and federalism and a lively press, became the birthplace of La Solidaridad. Paris, Ghent, Brussels, Berlin, and London served other purposes: Rizal completed or published works in several of them, drew on libraries and scholars, and benefited from lower printing costs and fewer legal restrictions than Spain or the Philippines offered. Berlin provided intellectual contacts through its anthropological and scientific circles, and the British Museum in London held a copy of Morga’s work that Rizal consulted for his annotated edition.

Hong Kong, closer to home, was a refuge and meeting point for Filipino exiles and a center of commercial and Masonic contacts. Rizal used it as a base in 1891–92. The Philippines itself was not merely a recipient. Manila’s lawyers, teachers, clergy, merchants, and provincial families supplied funds, sent information, circulated copies of publications, and sometimes organized committees. The correspondence among these places is itself historical evidence, and it shows both the opportunities and the strains of operating so widely. Letters took weeks, remittances were slow, and misunderstandings or rivalries could harden before they could be resolved.

The movement’s transnational geography has analytical significance. It shows that Filipino reform was not an isolated colonial protest but part of a wider European and global circulation of people, texts, and ideas made possible by steamship, rail, post, and the printing press. It also indicates a structural weakness. A movement based largely abroad could write for foreign readers and lobby foreign politicians, but it was physically distant from the people it sought to represent and dependent on remittances from home. Authorities in Manila could harass relatives, restrict circulation of publications, and punish suspected supporters, as happened in the Calamba affair that affected Rizal’s family. The geography that protected the reformers’ speech also exposed their families to retaliation.

The distance between a printing office in Spain and a reader in the Philippines created a particular politics of information. An editorial could respond quickly to a controversy in Madrid but depend on correspondence, money, and intermediaries to learn what had occurred in a Philippine town. Mail could be delayed; messages might be incomplete; colonial officials could monitor or seize papers at arrival. Supporters sometimes relied on private delivery and personal introductions, a reminder that print circulation was not simply the mechanical distribution of identical copies. Meanwhile, Spanish sympathizers had their own political priorities and could support a colonial reform without endorsing every Filipino demand. The geography of the movement consequently produced both opportunities and distortions. Distance protected some writers from immediate local retaliation but made them dependent on second-hand reports and exposed relatives at home. Studying these communication routes reveals why the movement could be international in argument yet fragile in organization.

Division II · José Rizal and the Intellectual Challenge to Colonialism
Section 116.5

José Rizal: Calamba, Family, Education, and Social Formation

José Rizal was born on 19 June 1861 in Calamba, a town on the shore of Laguna de Bay, to Francisco Mercado and Teodora Alonso. The family belonged to a Chinese-mestizo lineage of the kind examined in Chapter 115 and held a comfortable but dependent position. They rented agricultural land from the Dominican-administered estate that surrounded the town, and their prosperity therefore coexisted with a tenancy relationship over which they had limited control. Rizal grew up in a large household with several siblings. His elder brother Paciano, who had studied in Manila and had been connected to Father José Burgos, became an important intellectual and financial supporter. His mother, who was literate and well read, taught him early lessons; many biographers emphasize her role, though the evidence is mostly family recollection.

Rizal’s schooling followed the path available to ambitious families. After early instruction in Calamba and Biñan, he entered the Jesuit-run Ateneo Municipal in Manila in 1872, the year of the executions, and graduated with distinction in 1877. He then studied at the University of Santo Tomás, beginning philosophy and then medicine, and showed talents in poetry, drawing, sculpture, and languages. The surname Rizal, which he and some siblings adopted after 1872, distanced the family from the Mercado name that had been associated with Paciano’s link to Burgos; the details of this change are drawn from family tradition and should be treated accordingly. By his early twenties he had experienced both the possibilities and the humiliations of colonial education, including friction with Dominican professors and, as he later described, racial condescension.

The Calamba environment also taught him something about landholding. The estate’s administrators and the tenants had long disputes over rents, boundaries, and obligations, and in the late 1880s these conflicts intensified. Rizal’s family, and he personally, became involved in a legal and political struggle that culminated in evictions around 1890–1891. These events, the particular subject of Chapter 115’s treatment of religious estates, informed his portrayals of clerical economic power and of the vulnerability of tenants. They also affected his family in ways that complicate a simple picture of a hero acting alone: his relatives faced financial loss, harassment, and legal threats because of his writings and the family’s resistance.

Biographical writing about Rizal is vulnerable to teleology. Because he later became the national hero, there is a temptation to read his childhood as a rehearsal for martyrdom, to treat each anecdote as a sign of destiny. The evidence does not support that approach. Much of what is known about his early years comes from his own later recollections, from family memoirs, and from commemorative writings produced after his death, all of which shaped the narrative. A historian should therefore treat the childhood stories as sources about memory and family tradition as much as about events. What can be said more securely is that Rizal grew up in a family that valued education, that experienced the pressures of landholding and clerical power at close range, that belonged to the educated provincial elite, and that provided economic and emotional support for his studies.

The Calamba estate conflict is especially revealing because it resists a simple division between wealthy elite and impoverished peasant. Rizal's family possessed educational opportunities and resources unavailable to most tenant households, yet its tenancy remained vulnerable to a powerful landlord and to colonial institutions that could enforce contested claims. This did not erase the family's advantages; rather, it showed how overlapping structures of property, parish influence, and administration affected differently situated residents. Rizal's educational trajectory also depended on kin who provided financial and emotional assistance, especially his brother Paciano. The famous individual career was therefore a family undertaking. When examining his subsequent criticism of the colonial order, students should connect his broad language of justice to these particular experiences without reducing an entire political philosophy to a single family dispute. The distinction matters because literary diagnoses seek general explanations, whereas historians must still establish what each documented episode can prove.

Everyday Life and Social History

Education, Family Expectations, and Intellectual Life in Rizal’s Generation

For families like Rizal’s, education was a household project. Parents and siblings pooled resources; a brother’s studies might depend on a sister’s dowry decisions, a father’s harvest, or a landlord’s goodwill. Home instruction, often given by mothers, taught reading and religion before formal schooling began. Boys moved from provincial teachers to Manila colleges, lodging with relatives or in boarding houses, while girls usually remained at home or attended convent schools with limited curricula, a restriction that later became a subject of reformist criticism (Section 116.15).

The intellectual world these students entered was religious, hierarchical, and heavily regulated. Instruction was conducted in Spanish and Latin; scholastic methods were widely used; and curriculum and discipline were supervised by religious institutions. Yet the same students encountered newspapers, imported books, and conversation with teachers and peers, and they sometimes read banned or foreign works clandestinely. Everyday encounters with discrimination, such as being addressed disparagingly as indio, ignored in public spaces, or disciplined more harshly than Spanish students, formed part of their education in the politics of colonial hierarchy. Historians reconstruct this world from memoirs, school records, and letters, all of which reflect the perspective of educated families and say little about the great majority who never entered such schools.

Section 116.6

Rizal in Europe: Education, Science, and International Intellectual Networks

In May 1882 Rizal left Manila for Spain, a move that he apparently kept relatively quiet from colonial authorities. In Madrid he enrolled at the Universidad Central, completing studies in medicine and pursuing philosophy and letters. He joined the circle of Filipino students, took part in Masonic and social life, attended lectures, visited museums, and read widely. The Madrid banquet of 1884 described at the beginning of this chapter belongs to this period. In 1885 he moved to Paris to study ophthalmology under a leading specialist and in 1886 to Heidelberg, where he worked in an eye clinic and lived for a time with a Protestant pastor’s family in the nearby village of Wilhelmsfeld. His choice of ophthalmology was personal as well as professional; he wanted to treat his mother’s failing eyesight.

His European years were also a period of intense intellectual formation. Rizal read European fiction, history, and science, studied languages, and observed European societies at close range. He encountered scientific and ethnographic scholarship that treated non-European societies as objects of study, and he began to use that scholarship against the racial assumptions it sometimes carried. In Berlin he joined learned societies and met anthropologists and scholars who took an interest in the Philippines; the earlier travel writing of the German scholar Feodor Jagor, who had visited the islands in the 1860s, was a significant influence. Letters from this period show a young man testing his abilities against European standards and trying to understand why Europeans so often misunderstood the Philippines.

The most important relationship of these years was with Ferdinand Blumentritt, an Austrian ethnographer and schoolteacher in Leitmeritz in Bohemia who had written on Philippine ethnography and languages. Rizal first wrote to him in 1886, and their correspondence, which continued until Rizal’s death, became one of the most extensive surviving exchanges of the period. Blumentritt supplied scholarly information, encouragement, and a European audience sympathetic to Philippine claims; he later contributed a prologue to Rizal’s annotated edition of Morga. Their relationship also shows the asymmetries of intellectual life: Rizal sought legitimacy from a European scholar, but Blumentritt in turn learned from Rizal’s direct knowledge of Philippine society, an exchange of authority that complicated the usual direction of colonial knowledge.

Rizal’s European experience should not be romanticized as simple enlightenment. He encountered racism, poverty, loneliness, and political indifference. He lived on remittances from his family, sometimes in straitened circumstances. He was also, increasingly, engaged in practical political work: writing for La Solidaridad, participating in debates among Filipinos in Madrid, and negotiating with other reformers whose strategies differed from his own. The tension between his scholarly inclinations and the demands of political leadership appears repeatedly in his letters and in the conflicts discussed in Section 116.11. He was a significant intellectual of the movement, but not always its organizer, and his reluctance, or inability, to take command of the Madrid group in the early 1890s was one factor in his decision to leave Europe.

Rizal's international intellectual environment was not one homogeneous 'Europe.' Medicine and ophthalmology supplied forms of practical knowledge; philological and ethnographic interests prompted investigations of language and historical sources; participation in political discussion exposed him to rival liberal and nationalist positions. His correspondents did not all share his commitments, and some Europeans supported Filipino reform from perspectives different from his own. Ferdinand Blumentritt's exchanges with him are particularly useful evidence of dialogue rather than one-way transmission of ideas. In evaluating those letters, chronology is essential: a statement made during study abroad cannot automatically be treated as Rizal's settled position at Dapitan or in 1896. His later historical research also illustrates an intellectual strategy: by documenting indigenous capacities and practices before sustained Spanish rule, he challenged colonial claims that advancement depended entirely on European tutelage. His scientific and literary pursuits were thus connected to politics, but neither can be reduced to a disguised political pamphlet.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Rizal’s Intellectual Development between Philippine and European Environments

What Changed

  • He moved from a Catholic, scholastic educational environment to the scientific, secular, and liberal currents of European universities and societies
  • He changed from a student poet and aspiring professional into a political writer, novelist, historian, and organizer
  • His audience expanded from family and teachers to European scholars and the metropolitan reading public
  • His understanding of the Philippines shifted from lived experience to comparative analysis, as he measured colonial conditions against European political thought

What Stayed the Same

  • His attachment to family, and his reliance on family support for his work
  • His concern with education as the basis of social improvement
  • His engagement with the grievances arising from Calamba, clerical power, and racial discrimination
  • His habit of writing across genres, including poetry, fiction, essays, and scholarship

Why the pattern matters: Rizal’s development was gradual and uneven rather than a sudden conversion. European experience supplied tools and an audience, but the problems he addressed came from the Philippines, and his work reflects continuous negotiation between the two settings.

Section 116.7

Noli Me Tangere (1887): Literature as an Indictment of Colonial Society

Rizal began writing Noli Me Tangere in Madrid and continued in Paris, Heidelberg, and Berlin. It was published in Berlin in 1887, with financial help from a friend, Máximo Viola, after Rizal’s own funds proved insufficient. The title is Latin, “Touch me not,” words associated with the Gospel of John. In an opening dedication addressed to his country, Rizal compared the book’s purpose to that of a physician who uncovers a patient’s illness so that it may be treated; he describes the novel as an examination of the “social cancer” afflicting his homeland. This framing signals the novel’s ambition to function as a diagnosis rather than only as entertainment.

The novel’s protagonist is Crisóstomo Ibarra, a young man of a wealthy Filipino family who returns to the Philippines after years of study in Europe. He wants to build a school, to honor his late father, and to marry María Clara, the daughter, in name, of the wealthy Capitán Tiago. His plans are obstructed by Father Dámaso, an overbearing friar who had earlier quarreled with Ibarra’s father, and by Father Salvi, a more calculating cleric. Around Ibarra move many figures who together form a cross-section of colonial society: the pretentious Doña Victorina, who masquerades as Spanish; the philosophical Pilósopo Tasyo, whose views are ahead of his time; the schoolteacher; the municipal officials; the poor and vulnerable family of Sisa, whose sons Basilio and Crispín suffer under the sacristan system; and the enigmatic Elías, who raises the question of whether entrenched injustice can be remedied without a more radical struggle. The plot moves through social gatherings, a church scandal, a festival, and a conspiracy that destroys Ibarra’s hopes.

As an indictment, the novel works through accumulation of social detail. Clerical power is shown not only through the arrogance of individual priests but through the way parish authority, landholding, education, and the colonial administration reinforce one another. Education is portrayed as repetitive and fear-laden. Family relationships are shaped by deference, ambition, and the desire for social advancement through proximity to Spaniards. Racial hierarchy appears in everyday slights, in Spaniards’ presumptions, and in some Filipinos’ imitation of them. The novel is also satirical about Filipinos, including the elite, who accept or exploit the system. This internal criticism is important: Rizal did not merely blame outsiders but asked what social conditions allowed the system to persist.

The novel’s reception shows its political impact. Colonial authorities and church authorities reacted strongly. Ecclesiastical censors and a commission set up under the governor-general examined the book and judged it offensive to religion and Spanish authority, and its importation and circulation were restricted. Friar writers published rebuttals. Some defenders, including a Filipino priest writing under a pseudonym from Singapore, argued that the book did not attack religion itself. Many copies reached the Philippines clandestinely, and reading the novel became a risk as well as an act of identification. Historians cannot reliably count its readers; what is clear is that it circulated in a small Spanish-literate public, that it was discussed in the reformist community, and that authorities treated it as a political danger.

Students must use the novel carefully as evidence. It is fiction. Its characters are composites, its episodes are shaped for dramatic and rhetorical effect, and its depiction of friar behavior reflects Rizal’s purpose of exposure. At the same time, it reproduces social details, speech patterns, institutional structures, and attitudes from a society Rizal knew intimately and presents these in a form that contemporary readers recognized. The novel is therefore more reliable as evidence of how an educated Filipino perceived and wished to portray colonial society than as a documentary record of particular events. It also has literary limitations that historians and critics have noted, including its melodramatic elements and its schematic treatment of some characters. These do not diminish its historical significance as an intervention, which depends on its reception and effects as much as on its artistry.

Rizal's choice of a school project as a central plot conflict rewards close reading. Ibarra believes that education can improve provincial society without requiring a complete political rupture. Yet the project is vulnerable to parish rivalries and official dependency, indicating that a benevolent reform may fail when the institutions needed to protect it are compromised. Elías poses a different challenge: he asks whether an unjust order can be corrected by isolated projects when many people face injury without access to patrons or courts. Their exchanges are not a referendum in which the reader must simply select the author's preferred spokesman. They stage competing accounts of social change, responsibility, and the moral costs of resistance. The novel's treatment of Sisa and her sons presses the problem further because people without status suffer consequences that privileged protagonists can more easily narrate or escape. This is why plot summary alone misses the novel's force: its multiple storylines compare the possibilities of reform for inhabitants placed differently within the same colonial society.

Section 116.8

El Filibusterismo (1891): Reform, Violence, and Political Disillusionment

Rizal’s second novel, El Filibusterismo, was completed after years of further political experience and was published in Ghent in September 1891, with financial help from a friend, Valentín Ventura. It is dedicated to the three priests executed in 1872. The word filibustero in nineteenth-century Spanish colonial usage referred to a troublemaker or subversive, and in the Philippines it was applied to those suspected of disloyalty. The title, roughly translated as “subversion” or “filibusterism,” therefore evokes the colonial authorities’ fear and Rizal’s own treatment of the subject.

The novel is set about thirteen years after the events of the first. Its central figure is Simoun, a wealthy and influential jeweler who has the ear of the governor-general and is widely believed to be a Cuban-born foreigner; he is in fact Ibarra, who survived the end of the earlier novel and returns in disguise. Simoun uses his access to power to encourage abuses, intending to drive the population toward rebellion. He plans a violent act, a bomb concealed in a decorative lamp at a wedding feast. Around him are students seeking permission to open an academy of Spanish, including the idealistic Isagani; the pragmatic Basilio, now a medical student; the opportunist Juanito Pelaez; the journalist Ben Zayb; the Spanish friars and officials; and the moral voice of the priest Padre Florentino. The plot ends with the failure of Simoun’s plan, his death, and a conversation in which Florentino reflects on what freedom requires.

The atmosphere of the second novel is darker than that of the first. Satire remains, but the tone is more bitter, and the political world is more corrupt and unyielding. The students’ petition for Spanish-language instruction, a reformist goal, is obstructed by bureaucrats and friars. Reform appears futile, while the conspiratorial alternative is morally compromised. Historians often read the novel as expressing the reformers’ disillusionment: after years of petitions and journalism, the practical avenues of change seemed blocked. It is also a response to personal experience, including the Calamba evictions and the harassment of Rizal’s family.

Interpreting the novel as a political statement requires care. It would be mistaken to read it as simply endorsing revolutionary violence, or as simply condemning it. The narrative shows the cruelty and corruption that make rebellion plausible, but it also shows Simoun’s plan as the product of bitterness and as morally and practically flawed; it fails, and the ending places moral emphasis on preparation, education, and character as preconditions for freedom. At the same time, the novel does not claim that peaceful reform is guaranteed to succeed. Different characters embody different responses, and the author does not simply endorse any one of them. A novel that portrays political violence cannot be read as its author’s endorsement of every character’s actions, any more than a novel that portrays a corrupt official endorses corruption.

The two novels together form a distinctive literary-political intervention. The first diagnoses colonial society and warns that its injustices endanger it; the second explores what happens when reform fails. Their reception differed. El Filibusterismo circulated mostly in the same clandestine ways as the earlier book, was likewise prohibited, and reached fewer readers in its early years, partly because of its more difficult text and the intensifying surveillance of the early 1890s. Later generations read both as foundational texts. Their legacy, including the twentieth-century debates over their place in education, is examined in Section 116.20.

The students' proposal for a Spanish-language academy is a useful complement to Simoun's plot. These young men attempt a modest institutional reform through authorized channels; the opposition they encounter shows how seemingly technical decisions about language could protect established hierarchies. Simoun, meanwhile, deliberately encourages corruption as a means of hastening rupture. That strategy implicates him in the suffering he claims to avenge. The juxtaposition prevents a simple reading of the novel as a progression from failed petitions to justified violence. Padre Florentino's final judgment challenges political methods that instrumentalize other people, while the novel does not suggest that oppression is benign. Comparison with the earlier novel also exposes a change in fictional form: the possibility of building a school gives way to plots, surveillance, and attempted destruction. These differences may illuminate Rizal's changing political imagination, but they should be checked against his essays, correspondence, and documented conduct before being turned into biographical conclusions about what he 'must' have believed.

Section 116.9

The Emergence of the Propaganda Movement in the 1880s

The Propaganda Movement did not begin with a manifesto. It grew out of the daily life of Filipino students and professionals in Spain during the 1880s, who formed friendships, held gatherings, wrote for newspapers, and discussed how to improve conditions at home. Early efforts included the Círculo Hispano-Filipino in Madrid and the contributions of figures such as Gregorio Sancianco, whose 1881 book El Progreso de Filipinas argued for economic and political reform, and Pedro Paterno, who published literary and cultural works. The successes of Luna and Hidalgo in 1884 gave the community a moment of public attention, and the banquet in their honor became an early demonstration of collective identity.

The community was not united. Students came from different regions and social backgrounds, spoke different languages at home, and held different views about priorities. Some preferred social and cultural activities; others pressed for political action. Rivalries and personal disagreements, including competition for leadership and recognition, repeatedly affected the group. What gave the movement coherence was a shared set of grievances and a growing sense that the colonial relationship needed to be renegotiated, rather than a single organization.

Links to the Philippines were essential. Supporters in Manila and provincial towns sent money, news, and information; lawyers and professionals formed informal committees; and families provided financial and moral support. Correspondence networks conveyed accounts of abuses, which could then be used in Spanish newspapers and parliamentary lobbying. Spanish allies, including republican politicians and journalists, also contributed. Their motives varied: some sympathized with colonial reform in principle, others used the Philippine question to attack conservative or clerical opponents in Spain, and many remained indifferent. The movement’s success depended partly on whether Filipino issues could be linked to Spanish political quarrels.

The word propaganda itself needs explanation. In the nineteenth-century Spanish context it referred to the dissemination of ideas, derived from the Latin and ecclesiastical usage in which the Catholic Church’s own missionary organization was a “propaganda.” It had not yet acquired the modern association with systematic deception. For the reformers, to carry on “propaganda” meant to publicize arguments, to educate Spanish and Filipino readers, and to persuade politicians. Historians use the term for the movement because the participants used it, though students should be careful not to import its twentieth-century connotations.

By the late 1880s the community had moved from scattered activities to more organized efforts. The founding of the Asociación La Solidaridad at the end of 1888 in Barcelona, and the launch of its periodical in early 1889, gave the movement an institutional form. It was still modest, composed of a few dozen active participants and dependent on small contributions. But it represented a decision to pursue sustained public persuasion rather than occasional appeals.

The movement should not be imagined as a sudden organization created by one meeting. Several overlapping initiatives preceded and accompanied the newspaper years: student associations, commemorative gatherings, speeches, petitions, and personal correspondence. Their participants often disagreed about how openly to criticize Spanish institutions and how much energy to devote to cultural recognition rather than legislation. A successful public banquet could display Filipino excellence in a metropolitan venue without securing administrative concessions in Manila. Conversely, a newspaper article might shape arguments among readers who never met its author. These were different measures of influence. Another distinction is essential: public reform campaigns were visible to authorities, whereas the private convictions of contributors are recoverable only imperfectly through letters and later testimony. The historian must not read the common label 'Propaganda Movement' backward as evidence of a fixed membership, a single headquarters, or a consistent political platform throughout the 1880s and 1890s.

Historical Vocabulary
Propaganda, Assimilation, and Political Association

Propaganda in this period meant advocacy: the spreading of arguments and information to win support. The word’s religious origins, in the Catholic missionary congregation devoted to “propagating” the faith, made it available as a neutral term for organized persuasion. Modern readers often equate it with manipulation, which can mislead them about how contemporaries understood the movement’s name.

Assimilation (asimilación) meant extending Spanish law, representation, and civil rights to the Philippines so that it would be treated as a province of Spain. It referred to political and legal equality; it was not necessarily a program of cultural replacement. Its meaning, however, was contested, as Section 116.16 explains.

Political association refers to groups organized to pursue political aims: clubs, committees, lodges, and societies. In Spain such associations were allowed within limits; in the colonial Philippines, they were closely watched. The legal environment shaped what activists could do openly and what they had to do discreetly.

Section 116.10

La Solidaridad (1889–1895): The Newspaper of Filipino Reform

The first issue of La Solidaridad appeared in Barcelona on 15 February 1889. It was a fortnightly periodical published in Spanish, and Graciano López Jaena served as its first editor. In late 1889 the paper moved to Madrid, and Marcelo H. del Pilar assumed the editorship, which he held for most of the paper’s remaining life. The final issue appeared in November 1895. Over nearly seven years it published hundreds of articles on Philippine affairs, Spanish politics, colonial administration, religion, education, and social questions.

The paper’s purpose was to bring Philippine problems before the Spanish public and politicians. Its opening statement of purpose, in paraphrase, said that it would oppose reaction and retrogression, welcome liberal ideas, defend progress, and promote democratic ideals. In practice the paper focused on a set of reform demands: representation of the Philippines in the Cortes, equality before the law, freedom of speech and press, the secularization of parishes, and limits on the power of religious orders and of colonial officials. It also published accounts of abuses, answered critics in the conservative and clerical Spanish press, and reported on the activities of Filipino communities.

Contributors included many members of the Filipino community in Spain. Rizal wrote some of his best-known essays for the paper, including “The Philippines a Century Hence” and “On the Indolence of the Filipino,” in which he argued that the supposed laziness attributed to Filipinos by colonial writers reflected climate, historical conditions, forced labor, lack of incentives, and colonial policy rather than innate character. Other contributors included Mariano Ponce, Antonio Luna, and several Spanish sympathizers. Writers frequently used pseudonyms, partly for protection and partly as stylistic play; del Pilar wrote as “Plaridel” and Rizal as “Laong Laan” and “Dimasalang,” among others.

Financing was a constant difficulty. The paper depended on subscriptions, contributions from supporters in the Philippines and abroad, and the personal resources of its staff. Money did not always arrive on time or in sufficient amounts, and debts accumulated. Copies sent to the Philippines were subject to confiscation and to the risks faced by anyone caught with them. Circulation figures are uncertain and probably modest; the paper’s influence derived less from large numbers than from the fact that it reached particular political and journalistic audiences in Spain and a clandestine readership in the colony. Because the paper was written in Spanish and addressed metropolitan audiences, it reached few Filipinos who did not read the language, a limitation discussed in Section 116.15.

As evidence, La Solidaridad is invaluable and must be read with attention to its purposes. It is a public document, shaped by editorial strategy, by the need to avoid prosecution, and by an audience that was often unsympathetic. It shows what reformers chose to say in print, not necessarily what they believed or discussed privately. Where private correspondence survives, historians use it to test the public record. The paper’s importance also lies in its existence: it demonstrated that Filipinos could sustain a political publication in the metropole for years, and it gave the movement a continuing platform that single publications could not.

The opening declaration of La Solidaridad permits a more exact assessment of the publication's audience and program. The statement titled 'Our Aims' described the paper as an opponent of reaction and a defender of liberal and democratic ideas, then drew attention to the Philippines' lack of representation in the Cortes. This juxtaposition matters: the editors presented a specific colonial grievance as part of a wider democratic project rather than as an isolated complaint from expatriates. Students can compare that statement with later contributions and ask whether political emphasis changed as the paper's finances and the reform effort deteriorated. The full text and an English translation were subsequently reproduced in Guadalupe Fores-Ganzon's edition of the newspaper; the National Commission for Culture and the Arts' Philippine History Source Book identifies the opening article and gives its documentary location (volume 1, pages 3–5). This traceable witness makes it possible to test the chapter's paraphrase without confusing editorial interpretation with quoted wording.

Section 116.11

Marcelo H. del Pilar, Graciano López Jaena, Mariano Ponce, and the Reformist Network

Rizal is the most famous participant in the movement, but the movement’s daily labor was shared. Marcelo H. del Pilar, born in Bulacan in 1850, trained as a lawyer and had been active in Manila journalism, including the bilingual Diariong Tagalog in the early 1880s. His opposition to the Bulacan friars and the resulting threat of arrest led him to leave the Philippines in 1888. In Spain he became the movement’s principal organizer and, from late 1889, editor of La Solidaridad. He wrote a satirical Tagalog work parodying catechetical forms and a critique of friar power, La Soberanía Monacal en Filipinas (1889). He directed the paper’s finances and correspondence with unusual persistence, often at personal sacrifice. He died of illness in Barcelona in July 1896, just as the revolution was starting.

Graciano López Jaena, born in Jaro, Iloilo, in 1856, was known for oratory and satire, including an early satirical piece against a friar, Fray Botod. He lived in Spain from 1880, supported himself through writing and the assistance of patrons, and was the first editor of La Solidaridad. His strengths were rhetorical and public; his weaknesses, according to colleagues, included difficulty managing money and sustained editorial routine. He stepped aside from the editorship as the paper moved to Madrid, and he died in Barcelona in January 1896, a few months after the paper’s final issue. His career shows how talented speakers and writers could be central to the movement while remaining personally precarious.

Mariano Ponce, a physician from Baliuag, Bulacan, took part in Madrid’s Filipino circles and became a steady contributor, secretary, and organizer. He wrote under several pseudonyms, collaborated closely with del Pilar and Rizal, and later worked in Hong Kong and other places supporting nationalist causes. Others contributed to the movement’s functioning: Antonio Luna as a writer and later military figure; Dominador Gómez and other students; and numerous Filipinos and sympathetic Spaniards who provided funds, translations, and political contacts. Historians frequently emphasize the leading three or four names, but the evidence shows a wider group, including supporters in Manila, whose letters and contributions made publication possible.

The leaders did not always agree. Del Pilar was a pragmatic organizer committed to sustaining a long campaign within Spanish politics. Rizal was more inclined toward scholarly work, moral argument, and, at times, independent initiatives. López Jaena was individualistic and charismatic. Disagreements surfaced over leadership of the Madrid community, the use of limited funds, the tone of the paper, and the best strategy. A notable dispute in 1891 over leadership of the Asociación reflected personal rivalry as well as differences of view, and Rizal’s withdrawal from the Madrid group is part of the background to his departure for Hong Kong in late 1891. Historians should avoid treating these tensions as proof that the movement was weak or as evidence that one leader was simply right; they illustrate ordinary difficulties of a financially strained, geographically scattered, volunteer enterprise.

The reformist network also shows the importance of Masonry and associations in Spanish life. Several leading Filipinos joined Masonic lodges, where they met Spanish liberals and republicans and found support, and the lodges also provided forms of mutual aid. The Masonic connection fed the friar orders’ suspicion that the reformers were enemies of the Church, a charge reformers sometimes had to answer. At the same time, the movement’s relationships with Spanish politicians were uncertain. Allies could be generous but also unreliable, since they were often preoccupied with Spanish domestic politics. The network’s resilience lay in personal loyalty and shared purpose, but also in its fragility.

Different participants also contributed different kinds of institutional labor. Oratory attracted attention and useful allies; sustained editorship required collecting manuscripts, negotiating disagreements, arranging publication, and keeping a paper solvent; confidential correspondence connected European advocates to people facing retaliation in the Philippines. The symbolic prominence of Rizal's novels can therefore obscure mundane activities without which no regular campaign would have survived. In examining internal quarrels, students should distinguish disputes over ideas from struggles over scarce funds, exhausted volunteers, and authority to speak for an absent constituency. The loss of cohesion cannot be explained exclusively by personality or rivalry. It was also an organizational problem: the movement lacked a secure institutional revenue source and could not easily protect its supporters at home. These material limitations help place the achievements of its best-known writers within a wider collective history.

Section 116.12

The Reform Program: Representation, Rights, Education, and Equality

The reformers’ demands, expressed repeatedly in La Solidaridad and in pamphlets and petitions, can be grouped into several categories. The first was political representation. They asked that the Philippines be treated as a province of Spain and be represented in the Cortes by deputies, restoring a right that had existed briefly early in the century. Representation would give Filipinos a voice in lawmaking and a platform for raising grievances. The second was legal equality and protection of rights: equal treatment of Filipinos and Spaniards before the law, regular judicial procedures, an end to arbitrary deportation and imprisonment by executive order, and application of Spanish civil and penal law.

A third set of demands concerned civil liberties: freedom of speech, press, and association. The colonial regime’s censorship and surveillance were major complaints, and reformers argued that without open discussion no real improvement was possible. A fourth involved education. The reformers wanted wider access to schools, better instruction, instruction in Spanish for those who wanted it, and reduction of clerical control over curricula. They connected education both to individual advancement and to collective progress. A fifth concerned the Church: secularization of parishes in favor of Filipino secular clergy and limits on the political and economic influence of the friar orders, topics developed in Section 116.13. Finally, they raised economic grievances, including taxation, forced labor obligations, and land disputes such as that at Calamba, although these received less systematic treatment than the political and legal issues.

How were these goals to be achieved? Reformers pursued several strategies at once. They wrote to persuade Spanish public opinion. They sought contacts with deputies, ministers, and newspapers. They publicized abuses to embarrass authorities. They organized associations and, in the Philippines, committees to gather support and funds. And they used Spanish constitutional language to claim that their demands were consistent with existing principles. The approach assumed that Spain’s political institutions, though flawed, could be moved by argument and pressure.

This assumption had some basis. Spain’s liberal and republican press included sympathizers; some politicians took an interest; and occasional reforms did occur. In the early 1890s, for example, the Spanish government introduced measures concerning colonial administration, among them a municipal reorganization in 1893 associated with the minister Antonio Maura, and the extension of Spanish civil law to the Philippines in 1889. But these changes were limited, were often administrative rather than constitutional, and did not provide the parliamentary representation, civil liberties, or reductions in friar power that reformers sought. Their effects, and their reception among Filipinos, were mixed, and historians continue to evaluate whether they showed Spanish willingness to adapt or merely tactical concessions.

The reform program also reveals the reformers’ limits. It spoke for educated Filipinos, and it focused heavily on legal and political rights rather than on a thoroughgoing restructuring of land tenure or social hierarchy. It assumed that elite participation in a reformed system would benefit the whole society. Some critics, then and later, considered this too moderate or too narrow. Others have argued that, given the legal and political constraints, the program was a realistic attempt to use the available channels. Both assessments are possible, which is why this chapter treats the program as a historical position rather than as a fixed standard against which to judge participants.

Specific proposals also differed in how directly they threatened colonial power. A new school or a change in a municipal title might be administratively possible without substantially modifying who controlled appointments, courts, public information, or estates. Parliamentary representation was more consequential because it challenged the distinction between a constitutional metropole and a territory governed through exceptional arrangements. An unrestricted press would have allowed criticism of officials to circulate beyond their supervision. Reformers therefore encountered resistance not merely to particular personalities but to changes in the mechanisms through which authority was exercised. This also clarifies why partial administrative adjustments did not resolve the central program. The Maura municipal decree of 1893, directed principally to the towns of Luzon and the Visayas outside Manila's municipal arrangements, could modify local structures without granting the Philippines representation in the Cortes. Reformers assessed such changes against their larger claims about law, consent, and accountability.

Cause and Effect

Why Substantial Reform Proved Difficult to Obtain

Long-term causes

A colonial system based on special laws, executive discretion, and the combined influence of the colonial administration and religious orders, with institutions and interests built up over centuries.

Short-term causes

Spanish political instability and preoccupation with domestic issues; the influence of religious orders and colonial lobbies in Madrid; limited sympathy among Spanish politicians; and the reformers’ modest resources and small numbers.

Immediate trigger or context

The reformers’ attempt in the late 1880s and early 1890s to turn publicity and association into legislative results, coinciding with conflicts such as the Calamba evictions and with the governor-generals’ suspicion of reformist activity.

Immediate effects

Limited administrative changes, continued censorship and surveillance, harassment of supporters and families, and rising frustration among activists.

Long-term consequences

A decline in confidence in peaceful reform among some Filipinos, the development of a Filipino political vocabulary, and the context in which more radical organizations grew.

Historical complexity

Spanish intransigence was real but not uniform; reformist weaknesses were also structural; and contemporaries did not know that revolution would follow.

Why it matters: No single factor explains the outcome. Institutional rigidity, political indifference in Spain, the strength of entrenched interests, and the movement’s own limited base all contributed.

Section 116.13

Secularization, Religious Orders, and the Politics of Anticlericalism

No issue occupied the reformers more consistently than the position of the religious orders. Chapter 115 describes the institutional foundations: the friar orders held many parishes, owned substantial estates, supervised aspects of education, and exercised informal influence over provincial officials. Here the question is how reformers turned this situation into political argument. Their criticisms combined several claims. They charged that friars used parish authority to dominate local society, that estate management produced unjust tenancy relationships, that clerical influence obstructed secular education and liberal reform, and that the orders’ ties to colonial officials gave them power beyond their religious functions. They also claimed that the denial of parishes to Filipino secular priests rested on racial prejudice rather than on competence.

Writers expressed these arguments in different registers. Del Pilar’s La Soberanía Monacal en Filipinas was a systematic critique of the orders’ political power, and his Tagalog satirical writings used familiar religious forms to mock friar abuses. Rizal’s novels portrayed clerical figures of varying character, from the domineering Dámaso to the cunning Salvi and the humane Florentino. La Solidaridad published reports of specific incidents and responded to friar publications. Reformers also drew on Spanish anticlerical traditions, including those of republicans and Freemasons, which gave them allies but also exposed them to the charge of irreligion.

Careful distinction is essential. Most reformers did not reject Catholicism. Many had been raised and educated in the Church, and several were deeply attached to its moral teachings; some of the movement’s strongest defenders were priests. The criticism targeted the orders’ political and economic power, particular practices, and the exclusion of Filipino clergy, not the faith itself. Still, there was variation. Some participants, especially those influenced by European rationalism, held more skeptical views. Rizal’s own religious opinions are debated: he criticized dogma and clerical authority and valued religious ethics, but historians differ over how to describe his beliefs. Because friars could present any attack on their orders as an attack on religion, the reformers faced a constant need to define their position.

The religious dimension also had popular resonance. Parish life was central to community experience, and resentment of fees, discipline, and perceived arrogance among some friars existed independently of the ilustrados. But other Filipinos respected their priests, relied on the Church for social and spiritual support, and viewed anticlerical writing with suspicion. The reformers’ message therefore had mixed reception. It resonated with those who suffered under friar authority or estate disputes and alienated others who equated criticism of friars with irreligion. Later movements, including those examined in Chapter 117 and the independent Filipino church movement that emerged afterward, drew on these tensions in different ways, though this chapter does not treat them.

Anticlerical arguments in the period operated on several registers. A polemic against abusive friars could denounce arbitrary influence over town affairs; a secular-clergy claim could seek control of parishes for diocesan priests; a constitutional argument could insist that civil administrators and courts, rather than a religious corporation, exercise public powers. These objectives overlapped but were not interchangeable. Catholic writers might support changes in parish appointments without endorsing every attack in a reformist newspaper, while some Spanish liberals favored curbing clerical political influence for reasons unrelated to Philippine equality. Conversely, colonial officials sometimes treated criticism of a particular order as an attack on the Church itself. The historian should track those distinctions rather than assuming that every participant held a modern doctrine of separating religion from state. The issue was not simply what reformers believed but which institutions could appoint, punish, educate, and control information.

Section 116.14

Racial Hierarchy, Historical Memory, and the Emergence of Filipino Identity

Colonial society classified people through overlapping legal, social, and ethnic categories. Peninsulares, Spaniards born in Spain, held the highest status. Insulares, Spaniards born in the islands, occupied an ambiguous position. Spanish and Chinese mestizos formed distinct groups with distinct legal and fiscal standings, and the majority of the population was classed as indios, a term that carried connotations of inferiority. These categories affected tribute, office-holding, education, social treatment, and expectations of deference. Reformers experienced them as both a personal insult and a structural barrier. Their demand for equality before the law was in large part a demand to end the legal and social consequences of racial classification.

Reformers also needed a name for themselves. The word “Filipino” had originally referred mainly to Spaniards born in the islands. During the late nineteenth century, educated natives and mestizos increasingly applied it to themselves and, in some writings, to the inhabitants of the archipelago in general. This shift was gradual and uneven. Some contemporaries continued to use older meanings, and in many provincial communities identity was organized more by locality, language, and kinship than by a shared archipelago-wide label. The reformers’ use of “Filipino” thus involved a political choice: to claim a common name that transcended the colonial categories and to suggest a community of interest among peoples of different ethnolinguistic backgrounds. How far that community existed in practice, and how far it was a project, is a question the chapter returns to in later sections.

Historical writing was a central instrument of this project. Colonial historiography, much of it written by friars and officials, tended to depict pre-Spanish society as backward and Spanish rule as civilizing. Rizal responded by preparing an annotated edition of Antonio de Morga’s Sucesos de las Islas Filipinas, an account of the early colony by a Spanish official originally published in Mexico in 1609. Rizal’s edition appeared in Paris in 1890, with a prologue by Blumentritt. Using a copy he consulted in the British Museum, he added notes that argued that the islanders had possessed organized societies, trade, law, arts, and writing before Spanish arrival, and that many of the faults attributed to Filipinos arose from the conquest and its consequences. In the essay “On the Indolence of the Filipino,” he similarly argued that colonial conditions, not innate character, explained the behavior colonial writers criticized.

Rizal chose a Spanish source deliberately, since using a Spanish official’s own account gave his arguments authority with Spanish readers and showed that they could be supported with evidence from the colonizers’ record. But the project has limits that historians have noted. In seeking to refute colonial denigration, Rizal sometimes idealized pre-Spanish society and underplayed its inequalities, slavery, and conflicts, as later scholars of the early Philippines have shown. His scholarship was also constrained by the sources and methods of his time. These limitations do not erase the project’s significance. The edition was an assertion that Filipinos had a history that could be recovered and interpreted by Filipinos, and that this history could ground claims to dignity and political standing. It was a major step in the development of a historical consciousness on which later nationalism drew.

The reformers’ treatment of race also had contradictions. They criticized racial hierarchy yet were themselves products of a stratified society and sometimes reproduced its attitudes toward non-Christian highland peoples or toward Muslims of the south. Their category of “Filipino” was largely defined by educated, Christianized lowland society, and the inclusion of other groups was often implicit or aspirational. Historians have debated how far the Propaganda Movement imagined a nation embracing the entire archipelago, and the answers vary by writer and context.

Rizal's annotated Morga also invites students to examine the method and limits of historical recovery. A seventeenth-century Spanish observer could supply information contradicting later claims that precolonial island societies lacked commerce, knowledge, or political institutions. Yet Morga wrote from within an imperial setting, with his own omissions and assumptions; Rizal's notes were produced as a nineteenth-century nationalist intervention. Reading the two voices together therefore requires identifying which statements belong to the earlier author and which to the later editor. This practice models a broader point about nationality. A developing Filipino historical identity did not require that every region previously possessed the same institutions or that all inhabitants used the same name for themselves. It required reformers to argue that diverse communities could share political grievances and a common future. That claim was an imaginative and organizational achievement, not an uncontested description of existing social uniformity.

Historical Vocabulary
Filipino, Race, Nationality, and Nationalism

Filipino in early colonial usage typically denoted a person of Spanish descent born in the Philippines. By the late nineteenth century educated natives and mestizos increasingly claimed the term, and by the 1890s it was widely used for those who identified with the archipelago as a whole. Students should check the speaker and date to determine which meaning is intended.

Race and related terms (peninsular, insular, mestizo, indio) were colonial legal and social classifications involving ancestry, birthplace, and status rather than modern biological concepts alone. They were enforced through law, custom, and institutional practice.

Nationality and nationalism: nationality in Spanish law could refer to membership in the Spanish state, while “nation” in reformist writing could refer to a community of Filipinos. Nationalism, the political demand that such a community govern itself or be recognized as a political unit, was only one possible development of the new identity and should not be assumed to be present in every reformist statement. Potential misunderstanding: calling a reformer a “Filipino” in the twentieth-century sense may erase the contested meaning of the term in his own time.

Section 116.15

Women, Provincial Society, and the Social Reach of Reform

The overseas movement was largely male and elite, but reform ideas did not travel only through male intellectual circles. Women were active in educational and social life, and their initiatives show that reform was also pursued within the Philippines. The best-known example is the effort of a group of young women in Malolos, Bulacan, who in late 1888 and early 1889 petitioned the colonial government for permission to open a night school to learn Spanish. The request met opposition from the local parish priest and from officials sympathetic to him, and it was ultimately granted only in modified form. The episode illustrates both the obstacles faced by women seeking education and their willingness to challenge them publicly.

On 22 February 1889, writing from London, Rizal addressed a letter in Tagalog to the women of Malolos. In it he praised their courage and urged them to continue pursuing education, to resist forms of obedience that deprived women of independent judgment, and to recognize their role in forming the character of future generations. The letter reflects both an advanced view of women’s education for its time and the limits of that view: it connected women’s learning closely to their roles as mothers and as moral influences, and it was addressed to them by a male intellectual rather than written by them. Historians treat it as significant evidence of how reformers responded to women’s own activism, while noting that the women’s petition came first and that their agency should not be eclipsed by the famous respondent.

Women’s experiences of colonial society varied widely. Elite women could own property, manage households and businesses, and in some cases pursue education in convent schools, but the curriculum was restricted and public political roles were few. Rural and working women labored in agriculture, trade, and domestic work and were seldom reached by Spanish-language writing. Yet women influenced politics through family networks, religious life, and economic activity. Rizal’s own mother and sisters participated in his educational and political life, and family members faced the consequences of his activities.

The reform ideas’ reach beyond the educated elite was limited by language, literacy, class, and region. Spanish was spoken by a minority; most inhabitants communicated in Tagalog, Cebuano, Ilocano, Hiligaynon, and other languages. La Solidaridad and the novels were in Spanish. Some ideas traveled through teachers, parish networks, markets, and word of mouth, and through Tagalog materials such as del Pilar’s satires, but the extent of this circulation is hard to measure. Regional differences were substantial: Tagalog-speaking areas around Manila, Bulacan, and Laguna had denser connections with the capital and with reformist circles than distant Visayan or Mindanao communities. Teachers, local officials, priests, and lawyers acted as intermediaries, but their loyalties could pull in different directions.

These limits matter for interpretation. The movement’s claims to speak for the Filipino people rested on a base that was narrow. Reformers knew this and wrote about the need for education, but the structures of the colonial order and the movement’s own resources prevented them from closing the gap before 1896. The wider popular mobilization that occurred after 1892 took forms different from those of the ilustrados, as Chapter 117 examines.

The Malolos women's initiative supplies an especially important corrective to the notion that reformist political thought simply flowed from celebrated male authors to a passive population. The women's petition for instruction preceded Rizal's February 1889 letter and arose from local demands for educational opportunity. His response has value as evidence that their action became part of a wider debate about reason, family obligation, language, and religious authority; it must not displace their own initiative. Language further shaped the movement's reach. A Spanish periodical could lobby legislators in Madrid while remaining inaccessible to many inhabitants who read or spoke other languages. Oral circulation, local intermediaries, and personal relationships could widen its audience, but the existence of such pathways does not prove comprehensive national distribution. Historians should look for records of subscriptions, confiscations, correspondence, and local associations before asserting that a particular argument was widely accepted beyond educated circles.

Section 116.16

Reform, Nationalism, and the Limits of Assimilationist Politics

By the early 1890s the reformers faced a strategic dilemma. After several years of publication, lobbying, and organizing, the central demands had not been met. This raised questions about whether assimilation was achievable, whether alternative strategies were needed, and whether the goals themselves should change. Participants answered differently, and their disagreements reveal the movement’s diversity as much as its limits.

Del Pilar continued to emphasize the reform campaign and, for most of the period, to work within the framework of Spanish politics. His commitment was sustained by the belief that pressure on Spanish institutions could eventually yield results, and also by the lack of better alternatives. Rizal’s trajectory was more complex. His early essays argued for reform but also contemplated the possibility of separation if Spain continued to refuse change. His second novel conveyed deep pessimism about the prospects of reform. In 1891–92 he explored a different approach: a plan, pursued in Hong Kong and through contacts with British North Borneo authorities, to establish a colony for Filipino emigrants. The scheme did not succeed, but it shows that he was searching for options outside the existing channels. Others, including some younger participants, moved toward more radical views, and some Filipinos in Manila who had never been part of the Madrid circle developed their own conclusions.

Is it accurate to say that assimilation was abandoned? Evidence suggests a more uneven process. Some participants continued to invoke assimilation until the end of the campaign, others treated it as a tactical starting point, and some doubted it from early on. Historians sometimes distinguish three positions: assimilationist reformers who sought equal rights within Spain; reformers who considered gradual autonomy or separation as a longer-term possibility; and those who by the mid-1890s concluded that only revolution could succeed. These categories are analytical tools, and individuals might shift among them or hold views that do not fit neatly.

It is also important not to equate nationalism with armed rebellion. Nationalism, in the sense of a sense of shared Filipino identity and a claim to self-determination, developed among reformers who did not advocate immediate rebellion. Rizal, for example, urged moral and educational preparation and was skeptical about premature insurrection. At the same time, the reformers’ development of a national consciousness created conceptual resources, such as a shared name, a history, a set of grievances, and a vocabulary of rights, that others could use for different purposes. The relationship between reformist nationalism and revolutionary nationalism was therefore one of both continuity and rupture: the same ideas were used in new ways by people with different social backgrounds and strategies.

The failure of assimilation was not simply the failure of one strategy. It reflected a structural problem: the colonial system offered educated Filipinos too little access to power to make integration plausible, while the metropolitan political system offered too little support to make reform likely. As later chapters show, colonial reformers elsewhere in Southeast Asia would face similar dilemmas. In the Philippines, the dilemma produced no single resolution but several competing futures, only one of which, armed revolution, would be tested in 1896.

An influential interpretation requires explicit consideration. In the revised 1997 edition of The Propaganda Movement, 1880–1895, John N. Schumacher argues that a reformist current in roughly 1880–1885 was followed, after the mid-1880s, by a separatist current among leading propagandists, including Rizal and Marcelo H. del Pilar, whose members increasingly envisaged eventual independence. In this reconstruction, the dispute among leaders concerned not only the desirability of improvements under Spain but the timing and methods of a different political future. A published 1998 assessment of Schumacher's revised book summarizes that argument on page 66; this chapter does not claim a page-specific quotation from Schumacher's own text. The interpretation challenges any account that treats public assimilationist demands as a complete inventory of private political aims. Other historians emphasize the varied chronology of individual positions and the danger of reading later revolutionary outcomes back into earlier petitions. Students should test both approaches by dating particular letters, articles, and organizational decisions, asking what a source proves about its writer at that moment rather than ascribing one timeless intention to an entire movement.

La Liga Filipina and Rizal’s Return to the Philippines, 1892

After leaving Europe in late 1891, Rizal spent several months in Hong Kong, where he practiced medicine, was joined by members of his family who had been affected by the Calamba evictions, and pursued the North Borneo colonization scheme. In June 1892 he returned to Manila, a decision that surprised and worried some friends, since the authorities regarded him with suspicion and his novels were prohibited. His reasons appear to have included family concerns, a desire to work within the Philippines, and a wish to establish an organization that could carry the reform campaign forward at home. He had also drafted statutes for such a body.

On 3 July 1892, at a gathering in a house in the Tondo district of Manila, Rizal and a group of supporters founded La Liga Filipina. The Liga’s statutes set out aims that combined mutual assistance with civic and economic improvement. In paraphrase, it aimed to unite the whole archipelago into a single compact body; to provide mutual protection in need and hardship; to defend its members against violence and injustice; to encourage instruction, agriculture, and commerce; and to study and apply reforms. Its motto, in Latin, Unus instar omnium, expressed the idea of one standing for all. Officers were chosen, and a structure of councils and contributions was planned. The language was moderate and legalistic, and the stated methods were peaceful and cooperative.

The Liga’s character was that of a civic association rather than a revolutionary society. It sought to organize support for reform, to create a network of mutual aid that could reduce dependence on friar and official patronage, and to give Filipinos experience of collective organization. In doing so it was ambitious: associations that cut across class and region were rare in the colony, and the Liga’s emphasis on unity addressed a real problem of fragmentation. It was also dangerous. The colonial authorities regarded unauthorized associations with suspicion, and Rizal’s presence and reputation made the Liga a target. Within days, on 6 July 1892, Rizal was arrested, and shortly afterward the governor-general ordered his deportation to Dapitan in Mindanao. The Liga, deprived of its founder, effectively collapsed in its original form.

Its subsequent history was complicated. Some members reorganized to continue supporting La Solidaridad through a body sometimes called the Cuerpo de Compromisarios, with figures such as Domingo Franco and Apolinario Mabini associated with these efforts, and this reformist remnant stayed committed to peaceful methods. Others, notably Andrés Bonifacio, who had been connected to the Liga, concluded that peaceful methods had failed and, around the time of Rizal’s arrest, established the Katipunan, a secret society dedicated to separation from Spain. The relationship between the Liga and the Katipunan is a subject for Chapter 117, but it is clear that the suppression of the Liga influenced the thinking of those who moved toward revolutionary organization. It would be misleading, however, to treat the Katipunan simply as the Liga’s successor, since its leadership, membership, methods, and goals differed.

The statutes offer a practical test of the difference between civic association and immediate insurrection. Their stated objectives join collective solidarity and defense against injustice to education, economic improvement, and the study of reforms. They also describe councils and duties, suggesting that reform required continuing organization rather than occasional petitions. Rizal founded the association on 3 July 1892 at a house on Ilaya Street in Tondo, identified in historical accounts as that of Doroteo Ongjunco. The significance of the location lies less in an exact modern street number than in the establishment of a Philippine-based network after years in which prominent advocacy had been conducted overseas. Students should separate the organization's declared purposes from what its members were actually able to accomplish before official suppression. That distinction keeps La Liga Filipina from being mistaken either for a mature national party or for the Katipunan, whose different structure and armed project are treated in Chapter 117.

Rizal in Dapitan, 1892–1896: Exile, Education, and Civic Practice

Rizal arrived in Dapitan, on the northwestern coast of Mindanao, in July 1892 and remained there until July 1896. Exile removed him from the centers of political activity, and the authorities intended it to do so. Dapitan was a small coastal town under a military commander, with a modest population and few educational or medical facilities. For a man who had lived in Madrid, Paris, and Berlin, it was a drastic change. Yet his exile was not simply a period of inactivity. It became a period of practical work that has led some historians to see in Dapitan a clearer picture of his idea of reform than his political writings supply.

In Dapitan Rizal practiced medicine, including eye surgery, and attracted patients from other areas. He established a school for boys, teaching languages, mathematics, and practical skills, and some of his pupils later became notable. He undertook improvements to the town, including a water supply system and efforts at street lighting and land development, using his own resources and local labor. He pursued scientific collecting, sending specimens of insects, shells, and reptiles to European scholars; several species were later named in his honor. He engaged in farming and commercial ventures, having bought land after sharing in a lottery prize, and he constructed a relief map of Mindanao. He also corresponded widely, continuing his exchange with Blumentritt. In 1895 he began a relationship with Josephine Bracken, a young woman who had come to Dapitan with her adoptive father seeking treatment for his eyes.

These activities express a conception of reform grounded in education, health, improved livelihood, and local initiative. Rizal’s Dapitan work was practical and gradual, in contrast to the high politics of Madrid. It also had limits. His movements were restricted, his correspondence was subject to surveillance, and the authorities treated him as a political prisoner whose behavior had to be monitored. The relative friendliness of the local military commander, Ricardo Carnicero, eased conditions for a time, but the colonial state remained the framework in which his work occurred. Historians debate whether Dapitan shows Rizal’s turn from politics to social action, his adaptation to confinement, or his continued commitment to the long-term preparation of a people. All these readings find some support.

Contacts with the broader movement continued. Rizal received visitors and letters, and his family kept him informed. In 1896, as the Katipunan prepared for action, its emissaries approached him; according to later testimony, a Katipunan member, Pío Valenzuela, visited Dapitan in mid-1896 to learn his views, and Rizal advised against a premature rising. The accounts rely largely on testimony given after the fact, in circumstances that shaped what witnesses said, and must be used cautiously. Soon afterward Rizal, who had applied to serve as a physician with Spanish forces in Cuba, was allowed to leave Dapitan at the end of July 1896. He sailed for Manila, and the course that would lead to his arrest and trial began.

A useful way to evaluate the years in Dapitan is to distinguish the existence of productive activities from freedom of political action. The school, medical work, correspondence, and local projects demonstrate Rizal's capacity to act within confinement; they do not turn his exile into voluntary retirement. His contacts also make Dapitan important to a national story without depicting Mindanao merely as the distant place where a Luzon intellectual was sent. The locality had its own households, economic networks, religious institutions, and regional relationships. Rizal's projects depended on cooperation with residents and on opportunities available there. In 1896 Pío Valenzuela visited him concerning plans associated with revolutionary circles. The meeting is established, but Valenzuela's prison statements and later recollections differ significantly in what they imply about Rizal's response. A careful chapter therefore describes the visit without converting one disputed report into an unqualified declaration of approval or rejection of the impending uprising.

Section 116.19

The Decline of the Propaganda Campaign and the Limits of Peaceful Reform

After 1892 the overseas campaign continued under del Pilar, but with increasing difficulty. Several conditions combined. Funds became harder to raise as supporters in the Philippines faced harassment, as the economic burden of sustaining the paper grew, and as the fortunes of the movement declined. Participation dwindled; some members left Spain, others drifted away or fell out with colleagues. Illness took its toll on leaders, including del Pilar and López Jaena. And the loss of Rizal, who had been the movement’s most famous author, to exile in Mindanao deprived it of a central figure.

The political results were disappointing. The reforms that were enacted were limited, and the central constitutional demands, representation, civil liberties, and legal equality, were not achieved. Spanish interest in Philippine issues remained intermittent, subordinated to domestic quarrels. The influence of the religious orders and colonial interests in the metropole persisted. Censorship and surveillance in the Philippines continued. For many reformers the lesson appeared to be that sustained effort within Spanish institutions yielded little. The final issue of La Solidaridad was published on 15 November 1895, a date that marks the end of the principal overseas reformist enterprise.

It would be a mistake to suggest that reformist political activity ended abruptly. Some participants continued to pursue reform after the paper’s closure, and reformist ideas persisted among Filipinos in Manila and elsewhere. The Cuerpo de Compromisarios and similar groups continued for a time, and individuals such as Mabini would later be significant in different political settings. Moreover, the movement’s legacy extended beyond its own organizations: its writings, its vocabulary, and its example remained available. But as an organized campaign in Spain with a regular publication and a leadership group, the Propaganda Movement had effectively run its course by 1895.

Why did peaceful reform prove insufficient? The Cause and Effect feature in Section 116.12 identifies structural reasons. Here it is worth adding a note on contingency. Spanish politics in the 1890s was not predetermined to ignore colonial reform; a different configuration of ministers, parties, and events might have produced more concessions. Equally, the reformers might have built a broader base or pursued different tactics. Historians should not assume that revolution was the only possible outcome or that the failure of reform was inevitable. What can be said is that, by 1895–96, a growing number of Filipinos had concluded that reform was unlikely, and that an organization committed to revolution was preparing to act. How these developments interacted is the subject of the next chapter.

The closure of the newspaper should also be evaluated against two different standards of success. Its editors did not secure their principal immediate constitutional objective: representation and equal political rights under Spain. Measured solely by enacted reforms, the campaign was disappointing. Yet its writers assembled a durable language of collective rights, historical grievance, and Filipino political identity, material that circulated through later associations and arguments. The distinction does not prove that the revolutionary movement was secretly planned by the newspaper's editors. It instead identifies an unintended or only partially intended consequence: ideas developed for metropolitan lobbying could acquire new meanings in a changing political environment. Economic support, censorship, competing responsibilities, and illness explain much about the end of organized publication, while shifts in political possibility explain why the vocabulary survived that end. Reconstructing both processes is more precise than saying simply that reform 'failed' and revolution 'replaced' it.

Section 116.20

Rizal’s Trial, Execution, and the Historical Legacy of Reform

Rizal left Dapitan in late July 1896 and reached Manila in early August. The Katipunan was discovered in late August, and revolt broke out in the following days (Chapter 117). Rizal, then on a ship bound for Spain en route to Cuba, was arrested in October and returned to Manila in November, where he was held at Fort Santiago. The authorities accused him of involvement in the rebellion, citing his earlier activities, his writings, his association with the Liga, and testimony from others. He denied participation, and he wrote a statement in December disavowing the uprising. Historians continue to discuss the extent to which this statement reflected his convictions, his legal strategy, or both.

His trial took place before a military court on 26 December 1896. He was represented by a Spanish military defense officer, Luis Taviel de Andrade. The charges concerned rebellion, sedition, and illegal association. The proceedings were swift, and the evidence connecting him directly to the Katipunan’s plans was thin; the prosecution relied on his publications and on the argument that his influence had inspired the movement. The court found him guilty, and the governor-general, Camilo de Polavieja, confirmed the sentence. On 30 December 1896, Rizal was shot by a firing squad at Bagumbayan, the same field where the priests had died in 1872. Traditional accounts say he married Josephine Bracken shortly before his execution; details of this and of his last hours rest on varying testimony. The authenticity of a document of religious retraction attributed to him has long been disputed.

The meaning of the execution was immediate and contested. For the colonial authorities it was punishment of a dangerous agitator. For many Filipinos, including those already in revolt, it confirmed the regime’s inability to tolerate even moderate reform. The strongest contemporary evidence for how Filipinos responded is limited, and later commemoration soon shaped the memory. In December 1898 the revolutionary government of Emilio Aguinaldo declared 30 December a day of mourning, and under American colonial rule his death anniversary became an official holiday. American officials also promoted him as a hero whose peaceful and educational orientation they found congenial, a use of his memory that later scholars have criticized or qualified.

Rizal’s legacy has been continually reinterpreted. In 1956 Republic Act No. 1425, originating in a Senate proposal authored by Senator Claro M. Recto and sponsored in committee by Senator José P. Laurel, required the study of Rizal’s life and writings, particularly the two novels, in Philippine educational institutions. The proposal provoked controversy with Catholic Church leaders who objected to the novels’ treatment of the clergy. Writers have portrayed him variously as a moderate reformer, a nationalist, a precursor of revolution, a moral exemplar, a symbol manipulated by colonial and elite interests, and a complex intellectual who resists simple classification. The task for students is to distinguish what the contemporary evidence supports from what later memory has added, and to avoid reducing the reform movement to its most famous member. The Propaganda Movement left a vocabulary of rights, a sense of shared identity, a body of literature, and a record of the limits of petition. How those contributions shaped the revolution and later politics is the subject of the chapters that follow.

The trial and execution illustrate the gulf between the political influence of a writer and documentary proof of participation in an armed conspiracy. The prosecution treated his writings, associations, and public stature as evidence of danger; a historian must analyze those charges in their judicial setting rather than accept them as equivalent to demonstrated operational command of the revolt. The subsequent history of commemoration likewise needs precise dating. Aguinaldo's decree of 20 December 1898 designated 30 December for public mourning in memory of Rizal and other victims of Spanish domination. Republic Act No. 1425, approved on 12 June 1956, required instruction concerning Rizal's life and writings and was debated in a different political setting. Senator Claro M. Recto authored the Senate initiative, while Senator José P. Laurel, chair of the education committee, served as a principal sponsor and defender of the measure. These later acts are evidence of how Rizal's memory was institutionalized, not proof that all contemporaries in the 1890s interpreted his politics in the same way.

Historical Debate

How Should Historians Interpret Rizal’s Relationship to Reform, Nationalism, and Revolution?

The question: Was Rizal fundamentally a reformer who rejected revolution, a nationalist whose ideas made revolution thinkable, or something that resists these categories?

Interpretation A — a reformer and educator: Emphasizes his statements urging preparation, education, and caution, his skepticism about premature insurrection, his Dapitan work, and his disavowal of the rising. On this view he sought change through moral and institutional means and should not be made a revolutionary after the fact.
Interpretation B — a nationalist whose work enabled revolution: Emphasizes that his novels and historical writings created a national consciousness, that he contemplated separation if reform failed, and that revolutionary leaders drew on his writings and example, whether or not he endorsed their methods.
Interpretation C — a figure shaped by memory and use: Emphasizes that the “Rizal” known today is a product of later commemoration by revolutionaries, American colonial officials, nationalists, and the Church, and that historians must separate his own positions from these constructions.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on his novels, essays, and letters, trial records, testimony of contemporaries, the Liga statutes, and the later history of commemoration. Disagreements concern interpretation, emphasis, and the reliability of some testimony.

Why it matters: These readings affect how historians understand the connection between reformist and revolutionary politics and how national memory is constructed.

Student task: Choose one interpretation and identify the evidence that most strongly supports it and the evidence that is hardest for it to explain. Then explain whether the interpretations are mutually exclusive.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Filipino reform movement of the 1880s and 1890s as a political and intellectual response to colonial conditions rather than as the biography of a single hero. It argued that educated Filipinos transformed grievances rooted in clerical power, landholding, racial classification, censorship, and unequal citizenship into a sustained public campaign and a developing national consciousness, and that the failure of that campaign to secure meaningful reform widened the range of political futures available in 1896.

The movement’s formative memory was 1872. The executions of Gómez, Burgos, and Zamora showed that moderate advocacy could be treated as treason, tied the secularization dispute to questions of racial and political equality, and pushed advocacy toward Spain, where speech was freer. The generation that emerged drew on this memory, but it did not move in a straight line toward revolution; its first response was caution and legal argument.

The social foundations lay in the ilustrados: educated Filipinos from landowning, commercial, and professional families who enjoyed access to schools and, increasingly, to Europe. They were not a unified political party. They differed in wealth, region, and ambition, and they represented a small minority of the population. Their claim to speak for the Filipino people was both a rhetorical strategy and a historical problem.

Reformers pressed their case in the language of Spanish liberalism, demanding assimilation: the extension of constitutional rights, parliamentary representation, and legal equality to the Philippines. This strategy was legally available, intellectually sincere for many, and tactically prudent, but its premises proved fragile. Whether assimilation was a goal, a tactic, or an unstable position varied among participants and over time.

José Rizal gave the movement its most enduring literary and scholarly works. Noli Me Tangere diagnosed colonial society through the lives of characters who embodied clerical authority, social aspiration, and injustice; El Filibusterismo explored the darker terrain of frustrated reform and violent conspiracy; his annotated Morga and essays challenged colonial portrayals of Filipinos and their past. These works were interventions whose circulation and prohibition mattered as much as their content, and they must be read as literature and argument rather than as documentary reports.

The Propaganda Movement itself was a collective enterprise. La Solidaridad, edited first by López Jaena and then by del Pilar, provided a platform for reformist arguments and a focus for organization. Del Pilar, López Jaena, Ponce, and many others shared the labor of writing, fundraising, and lobbying, and they disagreed over leadership, strategy, and priorities. The movement’s transnational geography protected its speech but exposed families at home and strained its finances.

Anticlericalism and racial critique were central to the program. Reformers distinguished opposition to the political and economic power of the friar orders from opposition to Catholicism, though the boundary was contested. They also used the term “Filipino” to claim a shared identity beyond colonial categories, and they developed historical argument to support that claim, while their own conception of the nation remained shaped by the educated, Christianized lowland society from which they came. Women’s educational activism, exemplified by the Malolos petition, and the circulation of ideas in provincial society show that reform reached beyond overseas male circles, though unevenly and within limits of language and class.

La Liga Filipina in 1892 was the movement’s principal attempt at organized reform within the colony. Its quick suppression and the exile of Rizal to Dapitan removed its founder from politics and coincided with the founding of the Katipunan, whose revolutionary program belongs to Chapter 117. The overseas campaign declined through exhaustion, lack of funds, illness, and the failure to secure central reforms; La Solidaridad ended in November 1895. Rizal’s arrest, trial, and execution in December 1896 ended his life and began a long process of commemoration and reinterpretation.

Two cautions run through the chapter. First, reform and revolution were distinct, and nationalism did not require rebellion. Second, the revolution of 1896 was not inevitable; contemporaries did not know how events would unfold, and more than one future remained possible until late in the period. The reformers’ lasting legacy lay in a vocabulary of rights, a sense of shared identity, a body of literature and scholarship, and a demonstration of the limits of petition within an unresponsive imperial system.

Novelist, physician, and scholar; central intellectual figure of reform, executed 30 December 1896.
Marcelo H. del Pilar (1850–1896)
Lawyer, organizer, and editor of La Solidaridad from late 1889.
Graciano López Jaena (1856–1896)
Orator and first editor of La Solidaridad.
Mariano Ponce (1863–1918)
Physician and organizer within the Madrid and later Hong Kong networks.
Ferdinand Blumentritt (1853–1913)
Bohemian ethnographer, Rizal’s correspondent and prologue-writer for the Morga edition.
Juan Luna and Félix Resurrección Hidalgo
Painters whose Madrid successes in 1884 became a symbol of Filipino achievement.
Gómez, Burgos, and Zamora
Priests executed in 1872, remembered as political martyrs.
Paciano Rizal
Rizal’s elder brother, connected to Burgos and a key supporter of Rizal’s education.
Apolinario Mabini
Reformist associate of the Liga’s remnant who later became prominent in revolutionary politics.
Andrés Bonifacio
Founder of the Katipunan, treated in Chapter 117.
Josephine Bracken
Companion of Rizal in Dapitan, 1895–1896.
Calamba
Rizal’s hometown and site of the estate dispute affecting his family.
Madrid and Barcelona
Principal centers of Filipino association, lobbying, and publication in Spain.
Malolos
Bulacan town where women petitioned for a night school in 1888–1889.
Dapitan
Mindanao town of Rizal’s exile.
Bagumbayan
Execution site of the priests of 1872 and of Rizal.

Constructing a Source Dossier on the Filipino Reform Movement

This project asks you to assemble a small dossier of primary and secondary sources on one aspect of the movement and to evaluate it critically.

  1. Choose a focus. Select one of: an issue or series of articles in La Solidaridad; a chapter or episode of one of Rizal’s novels; Rizal’s letter to the women of Malolos; the Liga statutes; or the career of a lesser-known participant.
  2. Locate sources. Using digitized runs, published translations, and library collections, find at least three primary sources and two scholarly works relevant to your focus.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a colonial society produced its own critics and how those critics worked with, and against, the political language of the empire. The Filipino reformers did not simply receive Spanish liberalism; they tested it. By demanding that Spain apply its principles to the Philippines, they exposed the gap between metropolitan constitutionalism and colonial practice, and they created a vocabulary, “Filipino,” rights, representation, equality, that outlasted the institutions they addressed.

It also reveals a process that recurs across colonial Southeast Asia: education, print, and association create new publics, and imperial resistance to reform narrows the space for moderate politics. Later chapters will show educated Indonesians, Vietnamese, Burmese, and Malayans using similar tools under different circumstances, in contexts shaped by different empires and religious and social structures. The Philippine case, with its Spanish liberal framework and its focus on clerical power, is distinctive, but it contributes to a wider history of colonial intellectuals.

The chapter prepares for Chapter 117 without determining it. The revolution that began in 1896 drew on a broader social base and a different organizational model, yet it inherited grievances and ideas that the reformers had articulated. Understanding the reform movement helps explain both continuities and ruptures: why some Filipinos chose revolution, why others did not, and why leaders such as Rizal could be claimed by different traditions. Rizal’s own legacy, reinterpreted by revolutionaries, colonial officials, nationalists, and the Church, remains an example of how historical memory works.

Finally, the chapter contributes to this textbook’s larger argument that Southeast Asian societies were active historical agents. The Filipino reformers selected, translated, and transformed European ideas to address local conditions, and they did so from within a society that was itself diverse and unequal. Their story is one of achievement and limitation, of ideas that traveled widely and a base that remained narrow, and of a political culture still searching for a form when the events of 1896 began.
