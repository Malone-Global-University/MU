Islam and the Indian Ocean Trading World

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Muslim merchants sailed to Southeast Asia centuries before most Southeast Asians became Muslim. What changed between about 1200 and 1500 was not the arrival of Islam but the slow growth, through ports, families, scholars, rulers, and commercial institutions, of Muslim communities whose forms were shaped as much by Southeast Asian choices as by the wider Islamic world.

In the middle of the fourteenth century a legal scholar from Tangier, Ibn Battuta, whose travels had already carried him across North Africa, Arabia, Persia, India, and the Maldives, reached the port of Samudra on the northern coast of Sumatra. His later account, composed with a court secretary after his return to Morocco, describes a ruler who, in the traveler's telling, was devoted to Shafi‘i jurisprudence, who walked to Friday prayers, and who kept scholars from Persian lands at his court. The passage is among the most vivid contemporary descriptions of a Muslim court in maritime Southeast Asia. It is also the testimony of a visitor with his own interests, expectations, and religious training, and it describes one place at one moment.

Ibn Battuta did not discover a world newly touched by Islam. Arabic and Persian writers had been describing the seas east of India since the ninth century, Muslim merchants had long been present in the ports of southern China, and a much-discussed Arabic-inscribed gravestone at Leran in Java has a conventional eleventh-century reading, although its date and provenance have been debated; two inscriptions formerly claimed for Champa have been reassigned to Tunisia. The central historical problem of this chapter is therefore why a long-standing Muslim commercial presence led, in some places and not in others, to the formation of durable Muslim communities, courts, and centers of learning only from roughly the thirteenth century onward. The chapter argues that commerce was an essential setting for that transformation but not a sufficient explanation of it.

The Indian Ocean trading world was never a Muslim monopoly. Its ships were built and crewed by Arabs, Persians, Indians, Chinese, Malays, Javanese, and others; its merchants included Hindus, Buddhists, Jews, Christians, and followers of local religions as well as Muslims; and its ports were governed by rulers of many faiths. Muslim traders were increasingly prominent among long-distance carriers, brokers, and financiers, and their shared law, language of scholarship, and religious networks provided real advantages, but those advantages operated alongside Tamil guilds, Gujarati and Malabari merchant houses, Chinese shipowners, and Southeast Asian seafarers who carried their own traditions.

The spread of Islamic institutions along these routes involved more than transactions. Merchants settled, married, built prayer spaces, and were buried in ways that survive archaeologically. Scholars and Sufi teachers traveled between ports, and rulers decided whether to patronize, tax, employ, imitate, or ignore the Muslim communities in their harbors. Southeast Asian actors were not recipients of a finished religion. They selected which legal, devotional, and political forms to adopt, translated them into local languages and scripts, and combined them with existing practices, as the adaptation of Arabic letters into Jawi script illustrates.

The evidence for this process is uneven and often indirect. It includes Arabic and Persian geographies, Chinese records, travelers' narratives, dated tombstones, a legally oriented inscription in Malay from the Terengganu region, shipwrecks and their cargoes, and much later Malay and Javanese chronicles that preserve historical memory more than contemporary documentation. Each source type carries limits, and a recurring task of this chapter is to ask what each can establish and what it cannot.

This chapter treats the transregional network as its analytical unit. It does not narrate the dynastic histories of individual Muslim states, which belong to Chapter 63 (Samudera Pasai and the Muslim States of Sumatra), Chapter 64 (the Sultanate of Melaka), and Chapter 66 (Brunei, Sulu, Mindanao, and the northern maritime networks). Nor does it examine in detail how local societies converted and developed Islamic literatures, which is the subject of Chapter 65. Its task is to explain the commercial, social, intellectual, and political environment in which those developments occurred.

The chapter belongs to the larger argument of this textbook that Southeast Asia was neither a passive crossroads nor a passive recipient. The maritime Islamic world of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries was assembled from many contributions, and Southeast Asian ports, rulers, seafarers, and scholars were among its builders. Understanding how that world worked is necessary background for the fifteenth-century maritime order examined in Part XII and for the arrival of the Portuguese, discussed in Chapters 68 and 69.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish Muslim commercial presence, Muslim patronage and institutions, and the conversion of local populations as separate historical processes.
  2. Explain how monsoon winds, coastal geography, and straits shaped the routes and rhythms of Indian Ocean commerce without determining political outcomes.
  3. Identify the Arab, Persian, South Asian, Chinese, and Southeast Asian communities that sustained Indian Ocean trade, and explain why ethnic and religious labels were often fluid.
  4. Analyze the ships, commodities, credit arrangements, brokerage practices, and port institutions that supported maritime exchange.
  5. Describe how mosques, legal learning, marriage alliances, scholars, Sufi networks, and pilgrimage helped create Muslim communities in port towns.
  6. Evaluate travel narratives, inscriptions, tombstones, shipwrecks, and later chronicles as historical evidence, including their limits and the perspectives they omit.
  7. Assess why Southeast Asian rulers might patronize Muslim merchants or adopt Islamic forms of legitimacy, and why participation in Muslim trade did not automatically produce conversion.
  8. Compare northern Sumatra, the Malay Peninsula, coastal Java, Champa, and other maritime zones in the degree and form of Islamic institutionalization by about 1500.
  9. Interpret competing scholarly explanations for the expansion of Islam in maritime Southeast Asia and explain why no single cause is sufficient.
  10. Contextualize early Muslim networks within the wider Indian Ocean, East Asian, and Southeast Asian histories examined elsewhere in this textbook.

Historical Context

The Indian Ocean exchange that this chapter examines was already ancient by 1200. Chapter 18 traced early connections linking Southeast Asia with South Asia, China, and the wider Indian Ocean, and Chapters 28 and 29 examined Srivijaya's command of the straits and its place in the maritime Buddhist world. From the eighth and ninth centuries, the Abbasid Caliphate's capital at Baghdad and the Persian Gulf ports that served it generated demand for Asian goods, while Tang China's southern ports, notably Guangzhou, received foreign merchants. Archaeological evidence, discussed in Section 62.7, shows that sewn-plank vessels of western Indian Ocean type were carrying Chinese goods through Southeast Asian waters by the early ninth century.

Between roughly 1000 and 1200 the political geography of this commerce changed. The Chola attacks on Srivijaya-linked ports in the eleventh century, examined in Chapter 47, signaled new South Indian involvement in the eastern routes. Song China encouraged maritime commerce and its merchants and shipowners increasingly sailed to Southeast Asia themselves. In the western Indian Ocean, Cairo and the Red Sea became more central under the Fatimids and then the Ayyubids and Mamluks, while Gujarat, Malabar, and Bengal developed as major commercial regions. Southeast Asian ports, meanwhile, continued to develop their own regional trading systems; Chapters 50 through 52 trace how Majapahit-era Java participated in them.

The thirteenth century added a further dimension. The Mongol conquests integrated much of Eurasia under related political systems, and Yuan China employed Muslim officials and merchants from Central and Western Asia. The Yuan court's naval expeditions to Southeast Asia, discussed in Chapter 46, intersected with this commercial world. After the Ming dynasty's founding in 1368 the state restricted private overseas trade, but the fleets associated with Zheng He between 1405 and 1433 reasserted Chinese state engagement with the ports of Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean. By then Muslim communities were established in many of those ports, as the sources examined in this chapter show.

Students should keep three cautions in mind. First, "Islamization" in Southeast Asia was neither a single event nor a uniform process, a point developed in Chapter 65. Second, the Muslim presence must not be conflated with the Arab presence: the earliest communities included Persians, South Asians, and Chinese Muslims, and by the fourteenth century many Muslims in Southeast Asian ports had local ancestry. Third, the Indian Ocean was a system of many overlapping networks, not a single trade route. The chapter that follows therefore builds from geography and evidence, through commerce, communities, and political agency, to interpretation.

Section 62.1

Reconstructing Islam and Indian Ocean Commerce

Historians who study early Islam in Southeast Asia face a paradox. The period from about 1200 to 1500 saw major changes in the religious life of many ports, yet the surviving record is thin, scattered, and mostly written by outsiders. Most local societies did not produce chronicles or administrative archives that survive from this period, and the writers who did describe Southeast Asian ports, such as geographers in Baghdad or Cairo, officials in China, or travelers passing through, had their own purposes and limited acquaintance with local life. Reconstruction therefore depends on combining sources that were never designed to answer the questions modern historians ask.

The first analytical task is to separate three processes that are often collapsed. The first is Muslim presence: the arrival, residence, or burial of Muslims, usually merchants, in a locality. The second is institutionalization: the emergence of mosques, legal authorities, schools, communal leadership, or rulers who adopted Islamic titles and patronized Islamic scholars. The third is conversion of societies: the gradual adoption of Islamic belief and practice by farmers, fishers, artisans, and households who were not part of the merchant elite. These processes occurred at different times and speeds in different places, and evidence for the first does not prove the second or third.

The available evidence falls into several families. Arabic and Persian geographical and travel literature, beginning in the ninth century, describes routes, ports, and commodities. Chinese sources, including the thirteenth-century trade compendium of Zhao Rukuo, the fourteenth-century account of Wang Dayuan, and the fifteenth-century narrative of Ma Huan, record ports, products, and religious customs. European travelers such as Marco Polo offer brief observations. Local evidence includes dated tombstones, inscriptions, coins, and archaeological remains of ports, ships, and trade goods. Finally, Malay and Javanese chronicles such as the Hikayat Raja-raja Pasai and the Sejarah Melayu preserve traditions about conversion, but they reached their surviving forms centuries after the events they describe and reflect the concerns of later courts. They are valuable for historical memory, a subject returned to in Chapter 65, but they cannot be treated as contemporary documentation.

Modern scholarship has used these sources in different ways. Some historians have emphasized Chinese records to reconstruct the growth of Muslim networks, while others have placed greater weight on epigraphy, manuscripts, or the study of later religious institutions. Disagreements concern chronology, the relative importance of different regions of origin, and how much weight to give commerce relative to scholarship and politics, issues examined in Section 62.18. The chapter's method is to ask of every source who made it, for what audience, and what it can and cannot show.

Section 62.2

Monsoon Seas, Coastal Routes, and the Geography of Exchange

The Indian Ocean commercial world was organized around seasonal wind systems, examined in general terms in Chapter 4. In the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal, the southwest monsoon blows from roughly late spring into the summer, carrying sailing vessels from Arabia and western India toward the east, while the northeast monsoon of roughly late autumn through early spring carries them back. These patterns made voyages predictable in outline, but they also imposed schedules: a ship that missed the favorable wind might have to wait months. Winds in the Strait of Malacca and the Java and South China Seas follow more complex local patterns, and navigators adapted routes to coastal currents, headlands, and the shelter provided by islands.

Several seas formed linked but distinct sailing zones. The Arabian Sea connected the Red Sea and Persian Gulf with Gujarat, the Konkan, and Malabar. The Bay of Bengal linked Malabar, Sri Lanka, the Coromandel Coast, and Bengal with northern Sumatra and the Malay Peninsula. The Strait of Malacca, the narrow channel between the Malay Peninsula and Sumatra, concentrated traffic and offered sheltered ports, while the Sunda Strait and the Java Sea provided alternative routes and links to the eastern archipelago. Beyond them the South China Sea led to Champa, the Mekong delta, and southern China. Some travelers also crossed the Malay Peninsula by portage routes, explored in Chapter 26, rather than sailing around it.

Geography created possibilities, not outcomes. A port at a strategic point could attract ships, but whether it became a flourishing entrepôt depended on political protection, the ruler's policies, access to produce from its hinterland, harbor facilities, and the competence of the merchants who gathered there. The long career of the straits region as a commercial zone had seen different centers rise and decline, from the Srivijaya-linked ports discussed in Chapters 28 and 29 to the fifteenth-century prominence of Melaka examined in Chapter 64. Monsoon timing helps explain why many ports became meeting places where merchants from different regions exchanged goods rather than sailing the entire route, but it did not determine which ports would play that role.

The geography of exchange also shaped the movement of ideas. Waiting for winds gave merchants time to build relationships, practice religion, and form households in port towns, as examined in Sections 62.6 and 62.10. Rivers connected coastal markets to inland producers of pepper, aromatic woods, resins, and gold, so that religious and commercial contacts could extend upriver. Yet the same geography allowed local variations. A sheltered harbor in northern Sumatra and a river port in eastern Java could encounter resident Muslim communities in different ways. Champa was also linked to overseas trade, but the early Islamic evidence once attributed to it is contested; commercial connections should not be confused with proof of local Muslim settlement.

Section 62.3

Muslim Travelers and the Connected Maritime World

Travel literature provides some of the richest evidence for how educated Muslims perceived the Indian Ocean world. The Arabic and Persian geographical tradition from the ninth and tenth centuries onward collected reports from sailors and merchants about the route to India and China, describing ports, products, and rulers. Such works often combined firsthand testimony with older material and marvelous stories, so historians treat them as layered compilations rather than simple observations. They show, nevertheless, that scholars in the central Islamic lands regarded the eastern seas as a connected and economically important space well before the thirteenth century.

Ibn Battuta's Rihla belongs to a later and different category. Born in Tangier in the early fourteenth century, he left on pilgrimage in 1325 and spent about three decades traveling, serving as a judge in the Delhi Sultanate and in the Maldives and visiting ports along the Indian coast. His journey eastward took him to Bengal and then to Samudra in northern Sumatra on the way to China, probably in the mid-1340s, though the chronology of his journeys is debated. After his return, he dictated his recollections to Ibn Juzayy in 1355, and the resulting text blends memory, notes, and borrowed material, so that scholars use it with care.

What does a traveler such as Ibn Battuta reveal? He shows that a visiting Muslim scholar could expect hospitality, discussion, and perhaps patronage from rulers in distant ports, and that onward passage could depend on court assistance. He indicates that the court of Samudra included jurists from Persian lands and that it followed the Shafi‘i school of law, the same school prominent in coastal South India. He also reveals the limits of his perspective. He wrote as a Maliki-trained jurist, was interested in rulers, scholars, and pious conduct, and described non-Muslim neighbors largely in terms of the ruler's wars against them. His account therefore tells historians much about learned Muslim networks and little about how farmers, fishermen, or women in the countryside understood religion.

Travelers also illuminate connections across religious lines. Muslim visitors depended on local ships, pilots, and brokers who were not necessarily Muslim, and they encountered Hindu, Buddhist, and local ritual practices along the route. The Rihla thus offers a view of a commercial world that was Muslim at some nodes and not at others, held together by the movement of ships and people. This point is essential to a balanced view: the Indian Ocean was not a Muslim lake but a shared sea in which Muslim visitors found, and helped create, a growing number of hospitable ports.

Primary Source Window I

Ibn Battuta, Rihla (Account of Samudra), Fourteenth Century

Creator: Ibn Battuta, a Moroccan jurist, recorded with the court writer Ibn Juzayy · Date: visit probably c. 1345; text completed 1355 · Type: Travel narrative, composed from memory and notes after the journey

Context: The Rihla describes Ibn Battuta's long journey across the Islamic world and beyond. On his passage to China he stopped at Samudra in northern Sumatra, where he was received at the court of its ruler. The text survives in manuscript copies, and modern scholars use critical translations and editions; the passage below is a paraphrase, not a quotation.

Paraphrase / Synthesis (not a verbatim excerpt):

Ibn Battuta presents the ruler of Samudra as a pious Muslim who was devoted to Shafi‘i jurisprudence, who attended Friday prayers on foot, and who held scholarly discussions with jurists, some of them from Persian lands. He also describes the ruler as engaged in warfare against neighboring non-Muslim peoples and as receiving the visitor with generosity and helping to arrange his onward voyage.

Analysis: The passage shows that by the mid-fourteenth century at least one Southeast Asian court had Muslim legal and scholarly institutions connected to wider learned networks. The reference to jurists from Persian lands and to a particular legal school indicates that religious authority could be imported through scholars as well as merchants.

Limitations: The account describes a single court, written years later, from the viewpoint of a visitor attentive to scholarship and piety. It cannot establish how many people in the surrounding region were Muslim, whether local customs differed from the ruler's practice, or how the court appeared to its non-Muslim subjects.

Perspective: The narrative represents a learned, itinerant Muslim man who benefited from court hospitality. It does not represent the ruler's own words, ordinary residents, women, or non-Muslim communities.

  3. What purposes might have shaped the author's emphasis on the ruler's piety and learning?
  4. What can historians learn from it about the connections between Southeast Asian courts and wider Muslim networks?
  5. What can historians not establish from this source alone?
Section 62.4

Arab, Persian, South Asian, and Chinese Muslim Trading Communities

Popular accounts sometimes picture "Arab traders" bringing Islam to Southeast Asia. The evidence points to a more complicated pattern. Arabs from the Red Sea and southern Arabia, Persians from the Gulf and Iranian towns, Muslims from Gujarat, Malabar, the Coromandel Coast, and Bengal, and Chinese Muslims from southern ports all participated in the eastern trade, and the relative importance of each group varied by period and place. Because identification in the sources is often by a collective label rather than by precise origin, scholars must treat such terms as approximations.

Arab and Persian merchants feature prominently in surviving ninth- and tenth-century western Asian descriptions of commerce between Gulf and Red Sea ports and southern China; these sources cannot establish that they dominated total shipping or exchange. Their role did not disappear, but from the twelfth and thirteenth centuries South Asian Muslim networks grew more prominent in Southeast Asia, as Gujarat, Malabar, and Coromandel ports developed strong ties with Sumatra and the Malay Peninsula. Shared adherence to the Shafi‘i school in many Malabar and Coromandel communities and in northern Sumatra is one reason historians have looked to South Asia as a transmitter of religious institutions, though the evidence does not support a single source of origin, as Section 62.18 explains.

Chinese Muslims form a further strand. The southern ports of Guangzhou and Quanzhou hosted communities of foreign merchants from the Tang through the Yuan period, and Quanzhou preserves Muslim tombstones and a mosque traditionally dated to the early eleventh century and rebuilt in the early fourteenth. Under the Mongol Yuan dynasty, Muslims from Central and Western Asia held prominent administrative and commercial roles in China. Chinese Muslim families later settled in Southeast Asian ports, and some sources, such as Ma Huan's description of Java, mention Chinese settlers who followed Muslim practices.

These communities should not be imagined as fixed ethnic blocs. Merchants married locally, children grew up speaking local languages, and descendants might identify with the port in which they lived rather than with a distant ancestral origin. Muslims from different regions could also differ in language, legal school, and commercial interest. Commercial networks were intertwined with, not isolated from, Hindu, Buddhist, and other merchants: a Tamil merchant guild left an inscription at Barus on Sumatra's west coast dated to 1088, documenting an organized South Indian commercial presence without establishing the religious affiliation of every guild member. The history of Muslim communities in Southeast Asia is thus part of a wider history of diaspora, adaptation, and exchange.

Material evidence itself also changes meaning when moved across ports. An imported gravestone might attest access to a distant stoneworking tradition without identifying where the deceased spent most of their life. A merchant-guild inscription might testify to organized commercial labor while saying little about the creed of each participant. Tombstones in the Trowulan region similarly require distinctions among the place where a stone was found, the date inscribed on it, and the religion of the person commemorated. To date Muslim settlement responsibly, researchers must test these claims separately.

Section 62.5

The Expansion of Muslim Commercial Networks, c. 1200–1500

Between about 1200 and 1500 the commercial connections linking the western Indian Ocean, South Asia, Southeast Asia, and China intensified and changed in character. The change was gradual and regionally varied, and historians generally avoid assigning it a single turning point. Several developments overlapped. Song-era maritime commerce had already drawn Chinese ships and merchants into Southeast Asian ports, and under the Yuan dynasty, Muslim merchants and officials were prominent in China's overseas commercial administration. In the western Indian Ocean, Cairo and the Red Sea ports became important outlets for Asian spices and textiles under the Mamluks, and merchants associated with the spice trade, often called the Karimi, are documented operating between Egypt, Aden, and India.

In South Asia, Gujarat, Malabar, and Bengal strengthened their roles. Gujarat's ports, among them Cambay, exported textiles to both the west and the east, while Malabar controlled the pepper trade and linked the Arabian Sea to the Bay of Bengal. Shifts in political authority in India, including the expansion of the Delhi Sultanate and the later emergence of regional sultanates, affected the merchants who operated in these regions, though the connections between particular rulers and long-distance trade should not be assumed. In Southeast Asia, northern Sumatra's ports on the approaches to the Strait of Malacca developed ties to the Bay of Bengal, and Javanese ports linked the interior rice economy to maritime commerce, as discussed in Chapters 50 through 52.

The fourteenth and fifteenth centuries brought disruptions as well as growth. The Black Death of the mid-fourteenth century affected the Middle East and Egypt, and its effects on the eastern trade are debated. The Ming court's early prohibition on private overseas trade from 1371 coexisted with state-sponsored expeditions between 1405 and 1433, which drew Chinese power into the ports of Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean. When the expeditions ended, private Chinese trade and Southeast Asian shipping continued in new forms. Melaka's rise in the early fifteenth century, examined in Chapter 64, then reorganized the straits trade around a single entrepôt supported by diverse merchants, many of them Muslim.

Students should therefore read the expansion of Muslim commercial networks as the result of numerous interacting developments rather than a conquest of the trade by one group. The network grew as more ports adopted institutions that welcomed Muslim merchants, as families spread across multiple ports, and as demand for pepper, textiles, and other goods rose. It also grew because rulers who benefited from trade protected visiting merchants. The relationship between these commercial developments and religious change, however, was not automatic, and the sections that follow examine the institutions and communities through which commerce and religion intersected.

Section 62.6

Ships, Sailors, Monsoon Timing, and Maritime Risk

Commerce depended on ships, and the Indian Ocean world used several shipbuilding traditions. In the western Indian Ocean, vessels were often built of planks sewn together with fiber rather than fastened with iron nails, an approach documented archaeologically in the ninth-century Belitung wreck and described in Arabic and Persian sources. Indian shipbuilders constructed vessels adapted to coastal and open-sea voyages. Chinese junks, which Song and Yuan builders developed with internal watertight compartments and sturdy hulls, increasingly traveled southward and, at times, farther west. Southeast Asian shipbuilding traditions used lashed and pegged planks and produced vessels, including the large ships that Chinese writers sometimes described, that carried goods across the archipelago and to the mainland.

The crews were as diverse as the vessels. Shipmasters, often called by the Persian-derived term nakhoda, commanded ships and frequently also acted as merchants. Pilots, sailors, and officials might be of different origins, and a single vessel might carry cargo for numerous owners. Navigators relied on knowledge of winds, stars, coastlines, and local landmarks, and some written sailing directions, particularly in Arabic, survive from later centuries. Southeast Asian seafarers, including the Orang Laut discussed in Chapter 1, provided piloting, transport, and sometimes naval power, and their role is easily overlooked because they left few written records.

Risk was constant. Storms, reefs, and wrecks could destroy cargoes, and piracy was an ever-present concern in the straits and along the coasts. Rulers who could provide security, such as by patrolling waters or maintaining safe harbors, gained commercial advantage. Waiting for the monsoon could take months, during which merchants and crews needed lodging, food, and ways to occupy their time. Such pauses created demand for local markets, warehouses, and housing, and they provided occasions for ritual observance, hospitality, and marriage.

Because ships came from diverse traditions, it is misleading to treat the Indian Ocean as the domain of a single maritime culture. Muslim merchants frequently chartered space on ships built and owned by others. Non-Muslim shipowners and crews carried Muslim passengers and goods, and Muslim shipowners carried non-Muslim cargo. The religion of the shipper was therefore only one of many factors shaping commerce. What mattered for the transmission of religion was that the maritime system generated recurring contact among people of different backgrounds and created reasons for some of them to settle.

Not all commercial risks were natural. A ship might find favorable winds yet be delayed by political conflict, local toll collectors, rival rulers, or uncertainty about whether an official would honor an earlier bargain. Merchants therefore cultivated relationships with multiple authorities and avoided depending on a single protected harbor. Such adaptation helps explain why commercial routes survived dynastic changes. It also clarifies why ports competed to provide warehouses, safe anchorage, credible arbitration, and predictable levies: these services reduced the cost of uncertainty more directly than a ruler's religious identity did.

Section 62.7

Spices, Textiles, Ceramics, and the Commodities of Exchange

The goods that moved along these routes explain why ports of many kinds attracted merchants. Southeast Asia supplied pepper from Sumatra and, increasingly, from other regions, as well as camphor and benzoin from Sumatra's west coast, aloeswood and other aromatics, sandalwood from the eastern islands, gold, tin, and forest products. Cloves, nutmeg, and mace from the Maluku and Banda Islands reached Indian Ocean markets through chains of intermediaries, an important commercial network discussed in Chapter 71. Malabar's pepper, Gujarati and Coromandel textiles, Chinese silk and ceramics, Indian and Arabian horses, and metalwork moved in other directions.

Textiles were central. Cotton cloth from Gujarat and the Coromandel Coast was in demand across Southeast Asia, where it served as everyday clothing, ceremonial dress, and a form of wealth, and fragments of Gujarati cloth found in Egypt show that the textile trade reached the western Indian Ocean. Southeast Asian producers did not merely supply raw materials: Javanese and other cultivators supplied rice, and local communities organized the collection of forest and sea products for sale. This production gave Southeast Asian rulers and merchants leverage in commercial negotiations and meant that ports needed hinterlands as well as harbors.

Chinese ceramics were exported in large quantities, and Chinese copper coins circulated widely as currency. Their wide distribution in Southeast Asia gives archaeologists a way to map exchange, though it does not identify who purchased them or why. Gold and silver, and in some ports locally minted coinage in later centuries, supplemented barter and other exchange forms. The commodity trade thus involved a range of buyers and sellers, from rulers who sought luxury goods to households that purchased cloth and ceramics, and it influenced agricultural production, labor organization, and state revenue.

Trade did not benefit all participants equally. Rulers who controlled ports and levied customs duties gained revenue, and merchants with capital and networks prospered, while peasants who supplied pepper or forest products might receive a limited share of the final price. Those who depended on seasonal markets could be exposed to price fluctuations. Understanding the commodities of exchange therefore requires attention to who produced, transported, taxed, and consumed goods, not only to the goods themselves.

The cargo history is also a history of information. Traders needed to know which ceramic styles were desired in one market, which cloth grades could be exchanged for forest goods in another, and where local production was disrupted. Price differences rewarded knowledge that circulated through interpreters, pilots, established households, and visiting captains. Archaeologists can sometimes reconstruct these choices from the distribution of imports, but a bowl found inland cannot establish who carried it, how many hands it passed through, or the religion of its final owner.

Section 62.8

Markets, Credit, Brokerage, and Commercial Trust

Long-distance trade required institutions that could manage distance, delay, and risk. Merchants needed ways to raise capital, share losses, resolve disputes, and trust agents who traveled for months. Documents from the Cairo Geniza, a medieval collection of Jewish records from Egypt, show how merchants in the western Indian Ocean in the eleventh and twelfth centuries used partnerships, correspondence, agents, and commercial advice to organize trade with India. Islamic law offered familiar contract forms, including the commenda arrangement, in which an investor supplied capital and a traveling partner supplied labor, with profits shared. Variants of similar arrangements existed in other commercial traditions, so it would be inaccurate to treat them as uniquely Muslim.

Brokers and local officials were crucial in Southeast Asian ports. Brokers translated languages, set prices, arranged storage, and introduced visitors to buyers. Harbor officials, whose title in later sources is often shahbandar, supervised foreign merchants, collected customs, and mediated between visitors and the ruler; the office is attested in fifteenth-century Melaka and had counterparts in other ports, but its form varied. Customs revenue was a principal source of income for port rulers, who had an interest in keeping harbors orderly and predictable. In this setting a ruler's reputation for fair treatment could attract or repel merchants.

Trust operated through several channels. Kin, shared language, and common religion provided one basis, and Muslim merchants could rely on a shared legal and ritual vocabulary in distant ports. But trust also crossed religious lines. Hindu, Buddhist, Jewish, and Chinese merchants partnered with Muslims, and Tamil and Gujarati merchant groups maintained long-standing networks of their own. Merchants used letters, written contracts, and reputation as enforcement. Legal practice depended on local custom and ruler's decisions as well as on Islamic jurisprudence, and the maritime legal codes of later Melaka, examined in Chapter 64, show that local rulers developed their own rules for ships and cargoes.

Students should therefore avoid assuming a uniform "Islamic commercial system." Muslim merchants contributed techniques, legal frameworks, and long-distance connections, but port institutions reflected local politics and a wide range of commercial traditions. What mattered for religious change was that these institutions created regular contact, shared practices, and economic incentives for rulers and merchants to cooperate, which in turn made it easier for Muslim communities to establish themselves in ports and for Muslim scholars and teachers to find support.

The vocabulary of contracts should not be projected unchanged from Cairo or Gujarat onto every Malay or Javanese harbor. Surviving western Indian Ocean documents demonstrate that partnership, agency, and dispute settlement were available to merchants, but direct evidence for their precise adoption in each Southeast Asian port is thinner. A ruler's harbor officer might adapt foreign customs to local tribute and taxation practices, while resident brokers relied on personal reputation rather than written contracts. Historians should distinguish an institutional possibility, a later recorded office, and a contemporary document establishing that an office existed in a particular year.

Section 62.9

Islam, Religious Law, and the Formation of Maritime Communities

A Muslim community in a port was more than a group of merchants who happened to share a faith. It required institutions that allowed people to practice religion together: a place for prayer, a means of observing the Friday congregational service, burial grounds, a person to lead prayer and teach children, and an authority to resolve disputes in accordance with Islamic norms. Evidence for such institutions in the thirteenth through fifteenth centuries is uneven, but tombstones, inscriptions, and travelers' references indicate that they appeared first in ports with significant resident Muslim populations or sympathetic rulers.

Islamic law, or shari‘a, was elaborated by schools of jurisprudence, and the maritime Southeast Asian world became associated primarily with the Shafi‘i school. Ibn Battuta's mention of Shafi‘i learning at Samudra, the school's prominence along parts of the South Indian coast, and the later predominance of the school in Southeast Asia point to connections across the Bay of Bengal. The existence of a common legal tradition made it easier for scholars to move between ports and for rulers to rely on recognized authorities. It did not mean that everyone in a port followed formal legal doctrine, and local customary law continued to function alongside it.

Historians distinguish between merchant communities and broader Muslim societies. In the early phase, a port might include a settled Muslim minority, with its own leaders and practices, living within a polity ruled by non-Muslims. In a later phase, a ruler might become Muslim, adopt Islamic titles, and support mosques and scholars, but the surrounding population could remain largely non-Muslim for generations. Only in a third phase, discussed more fully in Chapter 65, did Islamic practice spread widely through villages and households. Marco Polo's report from Perlak, in which Muslim traders had converted some townspeople while others did not, illustrates the first phase, and the court of Samudra illustrates the second.

Religious law shaped everyday life in other ways. Questions of marriage, inheritance, contracts, and ritual purity affected how Muslim communities organized themselves, and the presence of Muslim legal authorities could influence how non-Muslims interacted with them. At the same time, Southeast Asian societies had existing legal and customary traditions, including patterns of kinship and property that differed from those in the Middle East, and accommodation between these systems was part of the process by which Islamic practice acquired local forms.

Religious identity could be a resource without functioning as a rigid boundary. Shared prayer and learned jurisprudence made visiting Muslims legible to one another, but local judges still needed to consider the practical realities of mixed households, different forms of property holding, and agreements with non-Muslim partners. Institutional growth could involve negotiated accommodations as much as formal declarations. Moreover, the later prominence of Shafi‘i law should not be read backwards as proof that every fourteenth-century coastal settlement employed the same teachers, judicial offices, or texts.

Section 62.10

Families, Marriage Alliances, Women, and Everyday Life in Port Towns

Marriage and household formation were among the most important ways in which merchant communities became rooted in Southeast Asian ports. Visiting merchants who waited months for the wind to change could marry local women, and their children, raised in local households, often spoke local languages and moved between communities. Over generations such families formed the social base of resident Muslim communities. For rulers and local elites, marriage alliances with merchant families could secure commercial connections, wealth, and political support, and some later traditions claim that rulers married into Muslim merchant families, though such stories need careful treatment because they were composed later and shaped by dynastic legitimation.

The evidence for family life is limited in the period before 1500, and historians are forced to infer from indirect sources. Tombstones sometimes commemorate women, as the Leran inscription does, and later European and Chinese observers noted that Southeast Asian women played prominent roles in markets and in commercial intermediation, a pattern that may have earlier roots but is not securely documented for the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. Southeast Asian kinship systems often allowed women considerable influence over property and household economies, which could shape how marriage alliances worked in practice, though the evidence varies by region and period.

Port towns were socially layered. Rulers, resident merchants, brokers, and shipowners occupied the upper levels, while artisans, boatbuilders, porters, dependents, and enslaved people performed much of the labor. Enslaved and debt-bound people were part of the commercial world, a subject examined further in Chapter 91, and the movement of captives and dependents was one of the ways that populations mixed. Religious affiliation overlapped with, but did not coincide with, social status: a poor local fisherman might be non-Muslim in a port ruled by a Muslim, and a wealthy Hindu merchant might trade with a Muslim ruler.

Daily life in such ports included the rhythms of markets, ship arrivals, prayer, festivals, and the construction and repair of vessels. Muslim residents observed prayer, dietary rules, and burial customs in contexts where many neighbors did not, and negotiation of these differences was part of ordinary life. Neighborhoods often grouped people by trade or origin, which helped communities preserve distinct practices while interacting daily with others. The result was not a segregated society but a layered and permeable one in which religious identity could be a source of solidarity without eliminating other ties.

The movement from temporary lodging to a resident household also affected the meaning of belonging. A merchant's child might inherit kin connections in several ports while living permanently in only one, and social position could depend on local patronage, language, and marriage as much as distant ancestry. Women could sustain property and household relationships through which visiting merchants became locally embedded, although the period's sources provide too little direct testimony to reconstruct their individual experiences confidently. Gender therefore belongs in the explanation while the limits of its documentation remain explicit.

Section 62.11

Inscriptions, Tombstones, and the Documentary Evidence of Early Islam

Dated tombstones and inscriptions provide some of the most concrete evidence for Muslim communities because they are contemporary physical objects. Muslim tombstones carry names, sometimes titles, and dates according to the Hijri calendar (AH, from the year of the Hijra, 622 CE). In Southeast Asia they appear in northern Sumatra, the Malay Peninsula, Java, and other regions, and their style, script, and materials sometimes reveal connections to wider Islamic regions. For instance, certain gravestones in northern Sumatra have been linked by art historians to stone carved in Gujarat, an indication of trade in finished funerary monuments, a subject taken up in Chapter 63.

Interpreting such evidence requires care. A tombstone shows that someone was buried according to Muslim rites at a particular time, and the person's name may suggest origin or status, but it cannot reveal how many other Muslims lived nearby. Stones may be moved or reused. Dates may be damaged, or modern readers may disagree about the numerals. And the surviving sample is limited by preservation: perishable materials, such as wooden mosques, leave few traces, so the stones that survive overrepresent wealthy patrons and certain materials.

The Terengganu inscription, a stone found in the Malay Peninsula (commonly reported as discovered in 1887), illustrates both the value and the difficulty of such evidence. Written in Malay using a form of Arabic-derived script that scholars call Jawi, it is among the earliest known examples of the script, and it sets out provisions that scholars interpret as a ruler's directions to uphold Islamic religious obligations and to follow rules concerning matters such as debt and sexual offenses. Its language shows how Arabic letters and religious vocabulary were adapted for Malay, an example of Southeast Asian agency in shaping Islamic expression. The inscription has been recognized internationally as important documentary heritage.

Its date, however, is disputed. The year in the Hijri date is partly damaged, and scholars have proposed several readings, including the often cited 702 AH (1303 CE), Ahmat Adam's proposed 708 AH (1308 CE), and other later readings such as 727 or 789 AH. The choice affects the interpretation of the early spread of Islamic legal authority on the peninsula, but it does not change the inscription's value as evidence for a ruler's engagement with Islamic law and Jawi writing in the fourteenth century or thereabouts. The inscription is also a lesson in historical humility: an important source can be both precious and uncertain.

The distinction between the Trowulan and Troloyo assemblages illustrates the problem. Some inscribed dates in the former Majapahit heartland have been treated as proof of early Muslim burials, yet Hadi Sidomulyo's reassessment cautions against assigning every stone with Arabic script or a Saka date to a Muslim interment. The Troloyo epitaph naming Zain ud Din, dated 874 AH (1469–1470 CE), offers a more secure identification. This caution does not erase possible earlier Muslims; it separates such a possibility from what individual objects independently prove.

Section 62.12

Scholars, Sufi Networks, Pilgrimage, and Religious Transmission

Merchant communities supplied the setting for Islam, but scholars supplied much of its intellectual and devotional content. Jurists taught the law, preachers led prayer, and teachers instructed children and converts in reciting scripture and performing ritual. The mobility of scholars across the Indian Ocean was an important vector of transmission. Ibn Battuta's observation of jurists from Persian lands at Samudra illustrates the kind of movement that connected Southeast Asian courts to wider learned networks, and the pilgrimage to Mecca, the hajj, drew Southeast Asian Muslims into the Arabian peninsula and brought returning pilgrims who could serve as teachers and prestige figures at home. Evidence for Southeast Asian pilgrims before 1500 is sparse, however, and the better-documented growth of such travel belongs to later centuries.

Sufism, the mystical and devotional dimension of Islam, was another channel. From the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, Sufi orders and teachers spread across the Islamic world, forming networks of teacher-disciple relationships that did not depend on a single political authority. Sufi teachers could adapt devotional practices to local settings, and their emphasis on personal piety, saintly figures, and ritual practices may have resonated with existing religious traditions. Historians of Southeast Asia have given considerable weight to Sufi networks, but the documentary evidence for specific Sufi activity in the thirteenth to fifteenth centuries is limited, and much of what is known comes from later texts. Claims about the role of Sufis should therefore be distinguished by the strength of their evidence.

The same caution applies to later traditions about the spread of Islam in Java. Javanese tradition remembers a group of saints, often called the Nine Saints (wali songo), who are credited with spreading Islam, and chronicles describe their activities in vivid detail. These narratives were composed or shaped well after the fifteenth century and reflect later religious and political concerns, so they cannot serve as direct evidence for the period covered here. They are important as historical memory, discussed in Chapter 65, but historians separate them from documented activity, such as the tombstones at Troloyo and Gresik.

What can be said with reasonable confidence is that by the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, Southeast Asian courts and ports were connected to networks of religious learning, that scholars traveled to and from them, and that local communities were adapting Islamic texts and practices through translation, script adaptation, and teaching. Evidence of manuscripts in Malay and Javanese on Islamic subjects mostly dates from later centuries, so the extent of written religious literature in the earlier period remains uncertain. The surviving record does show that religious transmission was a social process involving teachers, students, patrons, and communities, not a simple consequence of commerce.

Historical reconstruction must also distinguish the movement of a religious specialist from the consolidation of a religious school. A visiting scholar might advise a ruler without founding a lasting institution; a burial marker could show an individual's religious commitments without locating a teaching network; and a later saint's biography might project mature institutions into an earlier age. The historian can still trace connected practices across ports, but each link requires a source appropriate to the claim. Such differentiation protects the explanatory importance of scholarship from becoming a convenient label for poorly documented transformations.

Section 62.13

Mapping the Maritime Islamic Trading Network

A map of the Indian Ocean Islamic trading world between about 1200 and 1500 should not be drawn as one arrow running from Arabia to Southeast Asia. Its more useful image is a set of intersecting circuits. Ships crossed the Arabian Sea between Aden or the Persian Gulf and western India; others linked South Indian and Sri Lankan ports with the Bay of Bengal; still others served northern Sumatra, the Malay Peninsula, Java, Borneo, Champa, and the southern Chinese coast. At every junction goods could change hands and passengers could transfer. A Muslim merchant arriving in Sumatra need not have sailed all the way from the Red Sea, and a local sailor might carry exports toward India without ever entering the Arabian Sea.

Aden occupied a favorable position near the southern end of the Red Sea, serving merchants connected to Egypt, Arabia, and the eastern African coast. Hormuz was a major Gulf-centered meeting place whose influence reached markets on both sides of the Arabian Sea. Gujarati commercial centers, including Cambay, linked inland textile production and western Indian demand to overseas buyers. Malabar ports such as Calicut drew pepper and other produce from surrounding regions while gathering shipmasters, merchants, brokers, and religious specialists. These places were neither identical Muslim enclaves nor interchangeable symbols of Islamic power. The character of their rulers, inhabitants, merchants, and commercial services differed across time.

Farther east, Coromandel and Bengal ports formed interfaces with Sri Lanka and the eastern Bay of Bengal. Northern Sumatra supplied useful anchorage near the entrance to the Strait of Malacca and supported regional commerce whose political institutions are examined in the next chapter. Peninsular ports connected inland production and transpeninsular movement to straits traffic. Javanese ports linked maritime visitors with rice-growing settlements, shipbuilding, craft centers, and the inland Majapahit political world. Beyond Java, ships reached aromatic and spice-producing regions through inter-island routes involving numerous local seafaring communities. No western merchant community controlled these eastern circuits simply by participating in them.

Southern China added another dimension. Quanzhou and Guangzhou had histories of foreign Muslim residents, and Chinese ships increasingly sailed abroad. Connections between southern China and Southeast Asia sometimes carried people who were themselves Muslim and sometimes linked Muslims with non-Muslim Chinese traders. Ma Huan's fifteenth-century descriptions reveal how the same route might support imperial envoys, interpreters, private merchants, and locally resident families. The network thus ran toward East Asia as well as westward to the Indian Ocean. Geography alone cannot tell us who exercised authority: Chinese court initiatives, local Southeast Asian rulers, and Indian Ocean merchants pursued different objectives on overlapping waters.

Students mapping this world must also distinguish ports of departure, final markets, transfer points, production districts, and religious learning centers. A place might perform several roles without becoming a capital; another might collect customs while producing few export goods itself. The strongest commercial node was not necessarily the place with the largest Muslim population. Moreover, reconstructed routes are general patterns, not documented itineraries for every ship. Seasonal weather, changes in political security, and personal obligations altered choices. Mapping connections is therefore a historical argument about possibilities and recurring relationships, not a claim that every port was always linked by direct, uninterrupted voyages.

Section 62.14

Rulers, Merchants, and the Political Advantages and Limits of Islamic Connections

A Southeast Asian ruler confronting the arrival of Muslim merchants possessed several options. The court could permit trade, impose duties, recruit interpreters and financiers, allocate land for visiting merchants, or patronize religious specialists. Such decisions might be useful regardless of the ruler's own religion. A port with predictable customs and effective mediation attracted commerce, while merchants could reward reliable protection with goods, information, gifts, and contacts overseas. The political benefit of accommodating Muslims should therefore be separated from the question of conversion. Courts were capable of negotiating relationships with Muslim commercial communities while maintaining Hindu-Buddhist or other religious forms, as the Javanese evidence suggests.

For certain rulers, however, adopting Muslim identity or supporting Islamic law could provide additional possibilities. It could connect a court to recognizable religious learning, expand its repertoire of legitimate titles and rituals, or encourage the residence of respected jurists. An alliance with established merchant families might also help secure access to shipping and markets. Yet none of these advantages made conversion automatic or inevitable. Non-Muslim courts could be successful trading centers; Muslim merchants had practical reasons to maintain relationships with them. A ruler also faced local obligations to relatives, ritual specialists, landed elites, and communities whose loyalties could not simply be redirected by announcing a new faith.

Merchants brought their own political resources, but merchants were not a united party. A long-established Muslim household might seek exemptions and legal recognition, a recently arrived shipmaster might prioritize speed of clearance, and a non-Muslim broker might oppose exclusive privileges granted to one foreign community. Muslim merchants themselves differed in origin, language, status, and legal expectation. The later terminology of the shahbandar, or harbor official, helps explain the functions ports needed, but historians cannot infer the existence of precisely the same office in every harbor of 1300 merely because the title became common elsewhere. Port government consisted of historically specific bargains, not a single inherited system.

Political authority could also constrain Islam's expansion. A monarch might welcome commercial revenues while refusing to elevate foreign religious institutions. Local elites could seek to protect land, labor, or ceremonial rights associated with older temples and courts. Merchants might be allowed to settle on the shore but possess limited access to the hinterland. In times of dynastic rivalry or military insecurity, protection promised to one commercial group could be withdrawn. Thus the relationship between rulers and Muslim communities depended upon contested local circumstances; religious change often advanced unevenly, and acceptance of a merchant quarter did not necessarily imply a program of rural religious reform.

Political calculations need not cancel religious conviction. A ruler might sincerely adopt Islamic practices while also recognizing the diplomatic and commercial usefulness of doing so; a merchant might seek both livelihood and a place to worship. Historians lack access to inner motives and should not force a choice between calculated opportunism and belief. A stronger explanation identifies observable actions—patronage, legal language, settlement, burial, teaching, and travel—and asks what conditions made those actions durable. Chapter 63 will examine northern Sumatran cases in their own political detail; here the central point is that ports and rulers interacted with transregional Muslim networks through negotiation, advantage, obligation, and genuine cultural choice.

Section 62.15

Ma Huan and the Muslim Communities of Fifteenth-Century Java

Early fifteenth-century Java offers an unusual combination of substantial commercial contact and relatively complex religious evidence. The Majapahit political world remained associated with Hindu-Buddhist court traditions, while foreigners and locally resident communities occupied north-coast trading settlements. One important observer is Ma Huan, a Chinese Muslim interpreter who traveled in the expeditions associated with Zheng He. His Ying-yai Sheng-lan, usually known in English as The Overall Survey of the Ocean's Shores, describes ports and peoples encountered during the voyages. The work's 1433 textual tradition and later manuscript history should be distinguished from an assumption that every sentence reports a single observation made on one day.

Ma Huan grouped people in Java into categories that modern readers must handle carefully. He described foreign merchants from western lands, Chinese residents including Muslims, and inhabitants he classified by their customs and religious practices. His categories help identify a multiethnic port society in which Muslim communities lived alongside people following other traditions. They do not establish that everyone within each category had the same ancestry, profession, or self-understanding. Nor do they supply a census or a map of where all settlements lay. Terms rendered in modern English translations may also carry the prejudices and conceptual distinctions of the original observer.

Chinese Muslim residents were historically significant because they demonstrated that religious connections to the Islamic world did not run only westward through India. Families could come from Quanzhou or other southern Chinese trading communities and still share Muslim ritual practices with visitors from western ports. But 'Chinese Muslim' must not be converted into an exclusive explanation for the spread of Islam in Java. Muslim merchants from South Asian and other routes also operated in the eastern seas, and locally formed Muslim communities increasingly had their own trajectories. A description of visible residents cannot settle the question of how Islam spread among Javanese-speaking households beyond the ports.

Ma Huan's account must be read against other evidence. A north-coast funerary inscription associated with Malik Ibrahim at Gresik carries the date 822 AH / 1419 CE and provides a more localized record of Muslim commemoration. By contrast, the Trowulan and Troloyo stones near the Majapahit heartland present dating and identification problems: a fourteenth-century inscription cannot automatically be counted as a securely Muslim epitaph, while the clearest Muslim epitaph at Troloyo identified in modern reassessment names Zain ud Din and dates to 1469–1470. These materials suggest different kinds of contact and community life, rather than one simple event called the conversion of Java.

The Chinese court's maritime expeditions were not missionary campaigns that can simply be credited with Islamizing the archipelago. Their purposes included diplomacy, prestige, and political-commercial relations; they generated valuable descriptions in the process. Ma Huan's own Muslim identity may have helped him recognize certain practices, but it also shaped what he emphasized. A historian should ask whether his account was based on direct observation, information collected from interpreters, or inherited classifications. The answer may differ by passage. This makes the text especially effective for teaching how outsider records can be indispensable and limited at the same time.

Section 62.16

Coastal Settlements, Inland Societies, and Uneven Religious Change

The association of early Islam with Southeast Asian ports is useful only if it is not turned into a rigid rule. A seaport offered contact with overseas merchants and teachers, while nearby river settlements, cultivated plains, and upland communities participated in markets at different intensities. Those differences influenced the circulation of people and religious practices, but they were not a universal sequence in which every coast converted first and every interior followed later. Rivers could connect distant producers to port economies; political centers might lie inland while supporting maritime trade; migrants and scholars could travel beyond the beach. Evidence must identify the particular society and route under discussion.

On the northern coast of Sumatra, access to Bay of Bengal networks helps explain the presence of Muslim merchants and the courtly scholarly connections described by Ibn Battuta. Yet neither the court's religion nor its coastal geography establishes the beliefs of populations farther inland. On Java, Muslim commercial settlements on the north coast coexisted for a time with the influential Majapahit political world, and the inland burial evidence presents complex chronological questions. The contrast is not between a Muslim coast and a static, isolated interior. Inland producers supplied the goods that made maritime commerce possible, and merchants often depended on locally powerful people who never appeared in foreign travelers' narratives.

The Malay Peninsula illustrates another pattern. Transpeninsular paths, riverine markets, coastal settlements, and networks facing both the Bay of Bengal and the South China Sea created multiple points of contact. The Terengganu inscription indicates that Islamic legal vocabulary could be publicly expressed in Malay through an adapted Arabic script, but it does not prove exactly how extensively its norms were enforced. A dated stone is one source within a much wider landscape. Where material remains are sparse, students should not imagine an empty region awaiting foreign instruction; such conclusions mistake missing evidence for the absence of social life.

Borneo, the eastern archipelago, and Champa also require separate treatment. Their commercial connections did not all produce documented fifteenth-century Muslim courts. Claims of very early Islamic epigraphy from Champa have been undermined by provenance reassessment, demonstrating why the existence of a maritime route cannot substitute for proof of a Muslim community. In some places, archaeological evidence better documents exchange goods than religious institutions. In others, later traditions point to connections whose dates remain uncertain. Chapter 66 will examine the northern maritime zones more fully; Chapter 62's task is to avoid making one local pattern the history of every coast.

The port–interior relationship was shaped by political authority. Port rulers could protect resident merchants but lacked equal capacity to prescribe conduct across dispersed islands or agrarian hinterlands. Conversely, an inland court might profit from seaborne customs while retaining older religious institutions. Local leaders, merchants, teachers, and households negotiated access to land, marriage, inheritance, ritual obligations, and patronage. As those relationships changed, religious practices could pass between linguistic and social groups, but the pace was neither continuous nor uniform. A useful explanation must specify the institutions that carried influence from one settlement to another.

Section 62.17

Religious Coexistence and Continuity in Maritime Southeast Asia

The spread of Muslim communities did not erase the religious diversity of Southeast Asian ports. Merchants and sailors encountered Hindu and Buddhist institutions, local rituals, Chinese ancestral practices, and other forms of communal life, often within the same commercial settlement. A port could contain a Muslim burial ground and still belong to a political world whose rulers commissioned Hindu-Buddhist ceremonies. A merchant's reliance on a local non-Muslim broker did not require the two to share a theological outlook. The conditions of maritime exchange thus created opportunities for cooperation across religious boundaries, but they did not eliminate differences in worship, law, dietary practice, or social status.

The longevity of non-Muslim institutions matters particularly for Java. Majapahit-era evidence should not be compressed into an image of an old religious order suddenly overwhelmed by Muslim merchants. Court ceremony, land-based production, temples, and local communities continued while some port residents observed Muslim practices. The same landscape could include established Buddhist or Hindu patronage and new Islamic burial traditions. This did not necessarily mean harmony: commercial privileges, marriage rules, rights over land, and access to rulers could become contentious. Coexistence is a description of simultaneous traditions, not a guarantee of equal power or peaceful relations.

Shared spaces sometimes produced practical adjustments. Trade required agreement on weights, payment, storage, and the interpretation of contracts, even among people who prayed differently. Families linked to more than one linguistic or regional community negotiated questions of inheritance and social belonging. A local ruler might uphold a customary settlement practice for one group and permit a Muslim household to seek counsel from a religious specialist for another issue. The precise arrangements varied and are often poorly documented before 1500. Historians should resist turning later legal systems into a fixed portrait of how every medieval port resolved conflict.

Continuity also characterized the networks themselves. Sailors learned routes from earlier generations; coastal populations had long traded forest products, pottery, precious metals, cloth, and food. Muslim merchants entered and helped reshape those networks rather than introducing exchange to previously disconnected societies. New ties to Shafi‘i scholarship or Arabic-derived literacy existed alongside local languages and maritime know-how. Even changes of political patronage rarely abolished the labor of pilots, farmers, artisans, or market intermediaries on which commerce depended. Religious transformation therefore formed part of broader historical change without supplying the single clock by which all other change was measured.

The term syncretism, sometimes used to describe the blending of religious forms, needs care. It may describe some historical interactions, but it can also imply that a supposedly pure original religion was diluted by local practice. A better first question is what communities actually did and how different participants understood their actions. Evidence might support the adoption of an Arabic script for Malay or the use of recognizable Islamic funerary formulas; it might not show the beliefs of villagers whose lives left little written record. No one label can substitute for the close study of language, practice, and context.

Section 62.18

Why Did Islam Expand? Commerce, Conversion, and Historical Debate

The question of why Islam expanded across maritime Southeast Asia has prompted several major explanations. One emphasizes merchant activity: traders settled where goods and navigation brought them, and marriage, residence, and religious practice created durable communities. A second stresses rulers: courts that adopted Islamic titles, law, and patronage could lend authority to religious institutions. A third emphasizes scholars, teachers, and Sufi networks; a fourth points to kinship, local-language teaching, and the many decisions through which ordinary families reshaped religious life. These explanations are not mutually exclusive. The historical task is to test where each fits the evidence rather than select a universal mechanism.

The commercial explanation has obvious strengths. It accounts for why early Muslim communities frequently appear near ports and why the routes connecting South Asia, the straits, and southern China matter. It also explains how non-Muslim rulers could encounter Islamic practices before converting. But commerce alone is insufficient: merchants long traveled through the region while evidence for Muslim courts or more widespread local Islamic institutions appeared much later. The presence of Muslims in Quanzhou or the cargo of a western-style ship does not establish religious transformation in Sumatra. A credible commercial argument must specify settlement, households, places of worship, and the institutions through which visitors could become residents.

The ruler-centered argument can explain why religious symbols and scholarly specialists became publicly visible at certain moments. Ibn Battuta's Samudra account describes a court where Shafi‘i learning and the presence of foreign jurists were important. The Terengganu inscription provides another form of evidence for publicly articulated Islamic legal ideas, though neither its date nor its commissioning circumstances can be treated as entirely settled. Such evidence identifies political and religious patronage, but it tells much less about whether farmers and fishers in surrounding districts adopted Islamic practices. Court conversion and societal conversion are related questions, not interchangeable events.

Sufism has often been credited with making Islam adaptable and attractive in Southeast Asia. Sufi teachers and devotional networks indeed form part of Islamic history, and later Southeast Asian texts attach great importance to saints and lines of instruction. Yet historians must avoid projecting sixteenth- or seventeenth-century narratives wholesale onto the thirteenth or fourteenth centuries. It is sometimes easier to document a later memory of a saint than the dated actions of that individual. A satisfactory scholarly argument distinguishes direct contemporary evidence, plausible inference from wider Islamic networks, and traditions recorded much later for different audiences.

A different but complementary emphasis begins with local agency. Southeast Asian traders, rulers, teachers, and families might adopt Islamic practices for devotional, social, diplomatic, or commercial reasons, while preserving or revising local expectations. This perspective does not deny influence from abroad; it asks why some communities selected a particular foreign form and others did not. A coastal family might find a teacher helpful in marriage and inheritance matters, while an inland political elite retained older ritual commitments. Neither decision can be deduced from distance to Arabia, the amount of spice exported, or the presence of a visiting ship alone.

Section 62.19

Regional Variations in Maritime Islam by 1500

By around 1500, connections to the Islamic world were a persistent element of maritime Southeast Asian life, but they were distributed unevenly. Northern Sumatra offers some of the clearest early evidence for Muslim political patronage, learned jurisprudence, and interregional visitors. Marco Polo's late thirteenth-century account and Ibn Battuta's fourteenth-century description provide two different vantage points, while local Muslim epigraphy gives another category of evidence. Even there, a visiting author saw particular ports and courts, not every community in the hinterland. Chapter 63 will study the dynastic and numismatic history of these states; at the network level they illustrate how coastal institutions could connect local authority with wider Muslim worlds.

The Malay Peninsula shows the importance of routes facing different seas. Ports and river systems related to the Bay of Bengal, the straits, and the South China Sea without necessarily following identical political trajectories. The Terengganu inscription reveals the public use of Malay written in Arabic-derived script to communicate Islamic legal norms, but disagreement over its date means it cannot serve as a single unquestioned foundation year for Muslim government. Farther south, Melaka's fifteenth-century development brought an important concentration of merchants and diplomatic connections, a story reserved for Chapter 64. Its prominence did not mean the whole peninsula or every visiting merchant became Muslim on the same timetable.

Coastal Java presents a contrasting relationship between maritime settlement and an inland courtly environment. Muslim residents and burial evidence appear alongside the continued political and ritual significance of Majapahit. Ma Huan's account highlights a multiethnic commercial world, including Chinese Muslim residents, but cannot count converts among rural Javanese communities. The inscribed material at Trowulan and Troloyo further demonstrates that stones require careful dating and identification. Later literary traditions about saints and royal conversion are important, yet their retrospective character must not erase distinctions among early ports, inland polities, and later regional Islamization processes examined in Chapter 65.

The northern and eastern maritime zones offer still different evidence. Borneo's rivers and coasts connected forest products, sultanate-related traditions, and wider trading routes, but the chronology of early Islamic institutions is not equally well established in every settlement. Sulu and Mindanao participated in exchange linking the South China Sea and other island networks; their distinct histories belong to Chapter 66. In Champa, reliance on alleged eleventh-century Arabic stones has proven particularly misleading because the inscriptions were reassigned to Tunisia. Coastal contact certainly existed, but the specific claim of secure eleventh-century Muslim burials in Champa fails the test of provenance.

Elsewhere the evidence can be even thinner. An imported ceramic object shows exchange, not Muslim religious affiliation; a later tradition of a revered teacher may reveal memory rather than an independently dated visit; an Arabic personal name may suggest connections without establishing the religion of the whole settlement. At the same time, sparse evidence is not proof that contact did not happen. Variation in preservation, archaeology, literacy, political patronage, and later archival survival shapes what historians can compare. Students should therefore ask whether two regions differ in documented practice, or only in the kinds and survival of sources available for them.

Section 62.20

Islam and the Indian Ocean Trading World: A Historical Synthesis

The expansion of Islam in Southeast Asia between roughly 1200 and 1500 was neither the sudden arrival of a previously unknown religion nor the automatic effect of foreign trade. Muslim merchants had already participated in eastern sea routes for centuries. What changed was the density and social significance of certain connections: more resident households, publicly visible Islamic institutions, Muslim court patronage in some regions, and scholarly relationships linking ports to other parts of the Indian Ocean world. These developments were historically consequential because they gave communities new ways to organize worship, learning, law, kinship, and diplomacy, even while they continued to operate within diverse local environments.

Geography supplied opportunities rather than a fixed outcome. Seasonal monsoon patterns, the Bay of Bengal, the Strait of Malacca, and the Java and South China Seas linked commercial circuits, but navigators and political communities determined how those opportunities were used. Ships could carry goods for multiple owners, and a merchant might transact through brokers far from the point where a commodity was produced. Local sailors, farmers, artisans, and port workers therefore helped create the same transregional economy often described through famous Muslim travelers. The movement of religious ideas depended on this infrastructure but was not reducible to its existence.

The evidence also imposes discipline. The Rihla records a learned visitor's experience of a Muslim court; the Terengganu stone presents religious and legal language in a locally adapted script; Ma Huan describes religiously mixed communities in a Chinese travel account. These sources illuminate different settings and are not interchangeable. The erroneous assignment of Arabic inscriptions to Champa, the debates over Leran's date and provenance, and the distinction between Trowulan and Troloyo show why historians must test every source before arranging it into a narrative of conversion. An attractive chronology is no substitute for a securely provenanced object or an appropriately interpreted text.

Economic and political explanations remain essential but incomplete on their own. Commercial opportunity might bring merchants to a port, yet residence required protection, family relationships, trust, and institutions. Royal patronage might support scholars and recognizable Islamic practices without changing rural society at once. Religious teachers and devotional traditions could strengthen connections but must be reconstructed with attention to the uneven survival of contemporary documentation. Southeast Asian rulers and ordinary people were not passive recipients: they accepted, modified, resisted, or combined outside influences within circumstances they helped create.

This chapter therefore supplies a framework, not a replacement, for the next four chapters. Chapter 63 will examine the particular states of northern Sumatra; Chapter 64, the political and commercial prominence of Melaka; Chapter 65, the many local processes of Islamization and cultural adaptation; and Chapter 66, the northern maritime networks and Muslim polities of Borneo, Sulu, and Mindanao. Those histories cannot be compressed into a single tale of Muslim traders bringing a finished civilization from abroad. They must be understood as distinctive histories unfolding within a shared but continually changing Indian Ocean and Southeast Asian maritime world.

Chapter Summary

Islam was carried into maritime Southeast Asian worlds through long-established commercial routes, but trade alone did not determine when or where people adopted Muslim religious practices. The central distinction is among merchant presence, the creation of durable communal institutions, and the wider conversion of societies. Each process needs independent evidence.

The Indian Ocean and connected Southeast Asian seas comprised multiple overlapping sailing circuits governed partly by seasonal monsoons. Their connections depended on skilled sailors, brokers, commercial intelligence, political protection, and the ability of ports to manage the costs of distance and delay.

Muslim merchants from western Asia, South Asia, and China participated alongside non-Muslim traders and Southeast Asian seafarers. Their relationships crossed languages and religious boundaries. No single community monopolized the maritime economy, and foreign written accounts cannot be treated as comprehensive measures of market share.

Merchant households, intermarriage, religious learning, and communities of worship helped turn recurring voyages into durable social connections. Muslim legal schools and shared scholarly traditions made some ports familiar to travelers, while locally negotiated practice and customary obligations shaped how those institutions operated.

Historical sources vary in what they establish. Ibn Battuta describes a learned Muslim court in northern Sumatra, while the Terengganu inscription presents Islamic legal language in a Malay-language text written with an Arabic-derived script. Ma Huan reports varied communities in fifteenth-century Java. Each source has different audiences, biases, and chronological limits.

Physical evidence requires provenance and contextual interpretation. Supposed early Champa Arabic inscriptions are not reliable because scholars traced them to Tunisia. The Leran stone has disputed chronological and material interpretations, while Trowulan and Troloyo stones cannot be treated as an undifferentiated series of secure early Muslim burials.

Political power mattered because rulers could provide protection, recognize legal and religious specialists, and cultivate commercially useful partnerships. Their decisions were conditioned by local elites, competing religious institutions, and port economies. Court patronage did not automatically establish the beliefs of a hinterland population.

Muslim communities lived alongside Hindu, Buddhist, local religious, and other groups, sometimes cooperating commercially and sometimes encountering disagreement about privileges and practices. Religious change was uneven in space and time, with different outcomes across northern Sumatra, the Malay Peninsula, Java, and the northern and eastern maritime zones.

Scholars have debated the roles of merchants, royal patronage, Sufi teachers, and local conversion. A robust explanation draws on more than one mechanism while refusing to turn later legends into contemporary evidence. It also recognizes the weak documentation of many ordinary people, especially women, small traders, and rural households.

By around 1500, maritime Southeast Asia contained influential Muslim courts and communities but was not religiously uniform. These network relationships created conditions for the particular dynastic, commercial, and cultural histories addressed in Chapters 63–66 and formed part of the wider context for subsequent European intervention.

Islamization

Material culture
Objects and physical remains used to study production, exchange, belief, status and daily practice.

Method of evaluating a source’s authorship, purpose, textual history, reliability and evidentiary limits.
Historical agency
Capacity of people and institutions to make consequential choices within the circumstances they inhabit.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it reveals how a religious world can develop through travel, relationships and local decisions without a single founding event. Long-distance Muslim commercial activity was real and consequential, yet neither ports nor populations changed their religious practices at one uniform pace. Explaining the difference requires attention to family life, politics, commerce and scholarly institutions alongside the movement of ships.

It also demonstrates the distinctive labor of historians. Ibn Battuta's narrative, Ma Huan's account, the Terengganu inscription and archaeological remains address different questions. A stone found in the wrong place, or a grave assigned a religious identity from an insecure date, can distort centuries of history. Recognizing correction as a scholarly strength is as important as learning the dates of the sources themselves.

The historical actors in this account include not only famous travelers and rulers but merchants, local seafarers, interpreters, spouses, brokers, teachers, producers and households. They negotiated institutions and identities across religious lines, often without leaving their own written testimony. Their activities show why Southeast Asia was a participant in shaping connected worlds rather than merely the destination of outside influence.

Chapters 63–66 will explain the histories of particular Muslim polities and societies across the archipelago. This chapter supplies the essential network framework for that work: seas offered routes, communities made connections durable, and local institutions determined divergent outcomes. The history of maritime Islam thus belongs both to the Indian Ocean world and to the many distinctive societies of Southeast Asia.
