Portuguese Expansion into Asian Waters

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

The Portuguese did not discover the Indian Ocean. They entered a crowded, prosperous, and politically divided maritime world, armed with unusual ships and guns, with a crown that wanted to turn trade into royal revenue, and with a surprising dependence on the pilots, brokers, allies, and markets of the Asians they sought to command.

In the spring of 1498, a small Portuguese fleet anchored off the Malabar coast of southwestern India. Its commander, Vasco da Gama, had left Lisbon the previous July with four vessels and had spent months crossing the Atlantic in a wide arc, rounding southern Africa, and working north along the East African coast. He reached Asian waters because, at the East African port of Malindi, he obtained a pilot who knew the monsoon crossing to India. The Portuguese were not the first outsiders to reach Calicut, and the town was not waiting for them. It was already a node in an exchange that carried pepper, ginger, cinnamon, cotton cloth, horses, gold, and ivory among East Africa, Arabia, Persia, Gujarat, the Malabar coast, the Bay of Bengal, Southeast Asia, and China. A traveler from the Atlantic arrived in the middle of a system, not at its edge.

What followed over the next two decades was a struggle in which the new arrivals tried to turn that system to their advantage. The Portuguese crown claimed a monopoly over the trade in spices that reached Europe, built fortified posts, fought and negotiated with rulers from East Africa to the Persian Gulf, and sold passes that Asian shipowners were expected to carry. These ambitions were pursued by small numbers of men, whose ships were formidable in naval gunnery but who depended on local pilots, food supplies, shipyards, credit, interpreters, and allied rulers. The result was neither a conquest of the Indian Ocean nor a story of mere intrusion without effect. It was a violent and uneven insertion of a new power into an old commercial order.

This chapter places that insertion in its larger setting. It begins by asking how historians know what happened, since the sources are heavily Portuguese in volume yet incomplete in perspective, and then describes the Indian Ocean world of the late fifteenth century in which the Portuguese arrived. It examines the Portuguese background in Atlantic expansion, the voyage of Vasco da Gama, the conflicts with Calicut and the alliance with Cochin, the ships, guns, and fortresses on which Portuguese power rested, and the strategic designs associated with Francisco de Almeida and Afonso de Albuquerque. It then turns to commerce, the monopoly and the cartaz system, the indispensable role of Asian intermediaries, the responses of Asian powers, and the social history of the early Portuguese presence, including its violence.

The chapter has a particular responsibility within Volume V. Chapter 67 described Southeast Asia around 1500 and noted the Portuguese only at its boundary. Chapter 68 explains the maritime expansion that brought Portuguese fleets to the Strait of Malacca's doorstep, up to and including the first years of Albuquerque's governorship. It does not narrate the Portuguese conquest of Melaka in 1511, which belongs to Chapter 69, nor the following struggle for the Straits examined in Chapter 70, nor the Malukan sultanates and the Spanish and Dutch interventions treated in later chapters. Southeast Asia appears here chiefly in two ways: as a distant market and source of information whose goods and merchants were already present in India, and as the eventual destination toward which Portuguese ambitions turned.

Two cautions run through the chapter. First, the relation between Portuguese records and historical reality is not straightforward. Portuguese chroniclers wrote to celebrate a royal project, and their accounts of Asian leaders, Asian ships, and Asian motives are filtered through their own purposes. Historians must read them against Arabic, Persian, Malayalam, Gujarati, Chinese, and archaeological evidence where it exists, and must acknowledge where it does not. Second, hindsight misleads. It is tempting to read the later Portuguese seaborne empire back into the 1490s as though it were inevitable, and to describe Asian rulers as simply failing to see what was coming. Neither the Portuguese crown nor its Asian counterparts knew how the story would unfold, and many outcomes depended on contingencies, such as monsoon timing, a single naval battle, or the decision of a local ruler to accept or refuse an alliance.

Finally, the chapter asks students to keep Southeast Asia in view even where the narrative is centered on India and the Arabian Sea. The spices, rice, textiles, and ports of Southeast Asia were an integral part of the system the Portuguese entered, as Chapters 62 to 67 showed, and the ambitions that eventually led Portuguese ships to Melaka and Maluku arose from knowledge gathered in Indian ports from merchants who had traded across the Bay of Bengal. Understanding the early Portuguese presence in Asian waters is therefore part of understanding the history of Southeast Asia.

Historical Context

Chapter 67 described a Southeast Asia of about 1500 in which no single power commanded the region, ports such as Melaka concentrated trade, mainland courts competed for land and labor, and Muslim, Buddhist, Hindu-Buddhist, and Confucian-influenced institutions coexisted. The commercial world of Southeast Asia was already tied to India and the Red Sea by routes examined in Chapters 18 and 62, and to China by routes and diplomatic ties examined in Chapters 18 and 67. Muslim merchants and scholars had long linked the archipelago to ports in Gujarat, the Coromandel and Malabar coasts, Bengal, Arabia, and East Africa (Chapters 62 to 65). When the Portuguese arrived in Indian waters, they were therefore entering the western half of a system whose eastern half included the Strait of Malacca, the Java Sea, and the clove and nutmeg islands.

Politically, the western Indian Ocean was no less fragmented than Southeast Asia. In India, the Sultanate of Gujarat controlled some of the richest ports and textile-producing regions, the Deccan sultanates and the Hindu empire of Vijayanagara commanded much of the interior south of the Narmada, and the Malabar coast was divided among numerous small kingdoms, of which Calicut under its ruler, the Zamorin, was prominent. Hormuz controlled the entrance to the Persian Gulf as a vassal-like port of considerable independence, and Aden controlled the southern entrance to the Red Sea. Egypt and Syria were ruled by the Mamluk sultans in Cairo, who taxed the spice trade passing through the Red Sea, and from Egypt goods flowed to Venice and other Mediterranean merchants. On the East African coast, Swahili city-states such as Kilwa, Mombasa, and Malindi linked the interior gold and ivory trade with Arabian and Indian ports.

In Europe, Portugal under the Avis dynasty had spent most of the fifteenth century extending its reach into the Atlantic. It captured the North African port of Ceuta in 1415, colonized Madeira and the Azores, and sailed down the West African coast, where Portuguese captains traded for gold and enslaved people and sought a route that bypassed the Mediterranean. By the 1480s Portuguese expeditions had reached southern Africa, and in 1488 Bartolomeu Dias rounded the southern tip of the continent. Portuguese kings combined the language of crusade against Islam with commercial ambitions, and the crown itself sponsored and profited from voyages. These developments, examined in Section 68.3, created the knowledge, the shipbuilding traditions, and the institutional habits on which the voyage to India depended.

Historians frame the resulting encounter in different ways. Older Eurocentric narratives described the arrival of da Gama as the opening of Asia to European power and sometimes celebrated it as the beginning of a modern world economy. Later scholarship, including work by M. N. Pearson, Sanjay Subrahmanyam, and K. N. Chaudhuri, has emphasized the continuity of Asian commerce, the limited reach of Portuguese power, and the Asian side of the story. Others, such as Niels Steensgaard, have analyzed the Portuguese enterprise as a distinctive system of political protection sold to merchants. This chapter draws on all of these perspectives and examines the disagreement directly in Section 68.18.

Section 68.1

Sources, Archives, and the Problem of Portuguese Testimony

The history of the Portuguese in the early Indian Ocean rests on an unusually uneven evidentiary base. By volume, Portuguese sources dominate. Royal letters, instructions to captains, accounts of expeditions, administrative records preserved in Lisbon archives, and later chronicles together give a detailed picture of Portuguese decisions, losses, and quarrels. The anonymous journal of da Gama's first voyage, known as the Roteiro and traditionally associated with Álvaro Velho, though E. G. Ravenstein argued for João de Sá and the attribution remains uncertain, is the closest surviving record of the voyage itself. Afonso de Albuquerque's letters to King Manuel, written between about 1510 and 1515, preserve the governor's strategic reasoning in his own words. Tomé Pires and Duarte Barbosa, both writing in the years after 1510, compiled commercial and geographic information from Asian informants and Portuguese colleagues.

Much of the narrative detail, however, comes from retrospective works composed decades later. João de Barros, Fernão Lopes de Castanheda, and Gaspar Correia wrote histories in the middle of the sixteenth century, and the Commentaries of Afonso de Albuquerque were compiled by his son and published in 1557. These works draw on earlier records but also shape events into narratives of royal achievement, and their descriptions of battles, speeches, and Asian leaders' motives require critical reading. The later the source, the more likely it is to reflect the concerns of its own time.

Asian sources are fewer for these specific years but valuable. Arabic, Persian, and Gujarati chronicles describe the response of Gujarat and the Deccan, and Mamluk and later Ottoman documents record the diplomacy and naval preparations of Egypt and its successors. The Arabic work Tuhfat al-Mujahidin, composed in Kerala in the later sixteenth century by Zayn al-Din al-Malibari, presents a Malabar Muslim view of the Portuguese conflict, though it was written decades after these events and with a polemical purpose of encouraging resistance. Malayalam traditions, Swahili chronicles such as the Kilwa Chronicle (itself preserved in versions shaped by Portuguese contact), and the evidence of Indian Ocean ports in shipwrecks, coins, and inscriptions supplement the written record. Chinese sources provide a more distant perspective and become relevant for this chapter chiefly in connection with Southeast Asia.

Three problems of method recur. First, the language of Portuguese sources was shaped by crusading and commercial categories, so Asian actors often appear as "Moors," "gentiles," or "Christians" rather than as individuals with distinct political interests. Second, Portuguese writers exaggerated numbers, particularly of enemy fleets and casualties, and modern historians treat such figures cautiously. Third, the silence of Asian sources about the Portuguese in the earliest years does not show that Asian rulers ignored them, only that some of the events that mattered most to Portuguese writers were less central to contemporary Asian records. A responsible account therefore distinguishes what is documented from contemporary witnesses, what is reported by later chroniclers, and what remains uncertain.

Historical Source and Method Study

Distinguishing the Record from the Retelling

Compare the anonymous voyage Roteiro, Albuquerque's surviving correspondence, the chronicle of João de Barros, and the later Malabar Muslim text of Zayn al-Dīn al-Malībārī. They differ in author, date, language, intended reader, and political purpose. A contemporary Portuguese administrative report can document an order without proving it was carried out; a later Muslim chronicle can reveal a community's understanding of an event without supplying a verbatim 1502 eyewitness statement.

Section 68.2

The Indian Ocean before 1498: Ports, Circuits, and Political Authority

It is useful to think of the Indian Ocean as a set of overlapping circuits linked by monsoon winds, a pattern examined for Southeast Asia in Chapter 4. The first circuit connected East Africa, Arabia, and the Persian Gulf with western India. The second joined the western Indian ports, above all those of Gujarat and Malabar, with the Coromandel Coast, Sri Lanka, Bengal, and the Bay of Bengal. The third linked the Bay of Bengal with the Strait of Malacca, the Java Sea, the South China Sea, and China. Merchants and ships often worked within one circuit and exchanged goods at junction ports, though some traveled far, and Gujarati and Persian merchants, for example, were found from the Red Sea to Melaka. No single fleet or state controlled all circuits.

The ports of the Malabar coast, where the Portuguese first landed, illustrate the system's character. Calicut, Cochin, Kannur (Cannanore), and Quilon were small kingdoms or city-states governed by Hindu rulers, among them the Zamorin of Calicut and the rulers of Cochin, who profited from pepper, ginger, and other products of the Western Ghats and who welcomed foreign merchants. Muslim merchant communities, including Arab traders and local Muslim families often called Mappilas, were prominent, and in some ports they handled much of the overseas trade. Jewish and Syrian Christian communities were also established, and Hindu merchants, from Gujarat and elsewhere, took part. Rulers typically offered protection and legal arrangements in exchange for duties, and they relied on merchants for revenue and diplomatic connections. The coast's political fragmentation, with rivalries among its kingdoms, meant that outsiders could find allies, but also that no coastal ruler could speak for the whole.

Further north, Gujarat's ports, notably Cambay and Diu, were great centers of textile export. Gujarati cotton cloth was an essential commodity in exchange across the whole ocean, since it purchased gold and ivory in East Africa and was the principal import desired in Southeast Asian ports where spices, pepper, and forest goods were sold, as Chapter 67 described for Melaka. Hormuz, at the mouth of the Persian Gulf, was a rich entrepôt that supplied horses from Persia and Arabia to the Deccan and Vijayanagara, where warhorses were valuable but difficult to breed. Aden sat on the southern approach to the Red Sea, and Jeddah and Cairo connected the Indian Ocean trade to the Mediterranean, where Venetian and other European merchants purchased pepper and spices from Egyptian and Syrian dealers. On the African coast, Kilwa, Mombasa, Malindi, and the southern gold port of Sofala connected the interior to Arabian and Indian markets.

The commercial order depended on conventions that merchants and rulers maintained by custom. Ports typically welcomed foreign merchants of different communities, taxed trade through customs duties, and allowed groups to settle disputes within their own communities. Naval power was not absent, and local rulers and sea communities used armed vessels against piracy and rivals, but the ocean was not governed by a single navy that charged systematic fees for passage. This general feature mattered later when the Portuguese introduced armed enforcement of passes, described in Section 68.14. It would be an exaggeration to describe the system as entirely peaceful, since piracy, local wars, and port rivalries were common, or as a free market, since rulers imposed duties and restrictions. But the political pattern was one of many ports and rulers competing for trade and not of a single maritime hegemon.

Section 68.3

Portugal’s Atlantic Expansion, Motives, and Asian Intelligence

The Portuguese enterprise in Asia grew from fifteenth-century developments in the Atlantic, but it was not a direct, planned continuation of them. The capture of Ceuta in 1415 gave the Avis dynasty a foothold in North Africa and a crusading justification, and over the following decades Portuguese captains settled the Atlantic islands of Madeira and the Azores, where sugar and other crops later became important, and sailed down the West African coast. By the 1440s the Portuguese were purchasing gold and enslaved people along the Guinea coast, and the crown organized trade through royal licenses and monopolies. These ventures taught Portuguese seamen to sail in open ocean, to navigate by the stars and the sun, to exploit Atlantic wind systems, and to use the caravel, a maneuverable vessel suited to coastal exploration. They also accustomed the crown to treat overseas trade as a source of revenue and a field for noble service.

Religious and commercial motives were intertwined. Papal grants in the fifteenth century gave the Portuguese crown a claim to exclusive rights in the areas it explored, justified in the language of crusade against Muslim powers and the conversion of non-Christians, and rumors of a distant Christian ruler, Prester John, sustained hope of finding allies against Islam. At the same time, Portuguese rulers wanted direct access to African gold and Asian spices, which came to Europe through Muslim and Italian intermediaries at high prices. The notion that a direct sea route to the sources of spice would reduce costs and strike at the commercial power of Egypt and Venice was a motive, but it was not the only one, and contemporaries expressed it in different ways. Nobles sought honor and rewards, merchants sought profits, and the crown sought revenue and prestige. In 1494 the Treaty of Tordesillas between Portugal and Castile divided the zones of future exploration in the Atlantic, a settlement that later became entangled in disputes over the eastern hemisphere (see Chapter 74 for the Spanish side of those disputes).

Knowledge about the Indian Ocean reached Portugal through several channels. In 1487 the crown sent Pêro da Covilhã and Afonso de Paiva overland through Cairo to gather information. Covilhã traveled through Arabia and to the Malabar coast, may have visited Goa and Hormuz, reached the East African coast, and later sent reports to Portugal before settling in Ethiopia. Bartolomeu Dias's 1488 voyage showed that southern Africa could be rounded. Portuguese navigators made use of astronomical tables and instruments, including the work of Jewish scholars such as Abraham Zacuto, and of experience in Atlantic sailing. Planners therefore had a basic idea of where to go and what to look for, though they had little knowledge of the monsoon system and none of the political structure of the Malabar coast, which they learned on arrival.

Portugal's planning also illustrates the limits of hindsight. The Atlantic techniques that permitted a Cape voyage did not provide mastery of Malabar's diplomacy, the monsoon, or the market value of European gifts. Intelligence gathered by Covilhã, mariners, and merchants reduced uncertainty but did not resolve it. The first voyages should therefore be understood as expensive experiments with uncertain political consequences, not the execution of an already complete design for a global empire.

Cause and Effect

The Portuguese Entry into the Indian Ocean

Long-term causes

Fifteenth-century Atlantic expansion; crusading ideology and papal grants; Portuguese shipbuilding and navigational experience; royal commitment to overseas revenue; European demand for spices and the high cost of Mediterranean intermediaries.

Short-term causes

Dias's rounding of southern Africa in 1488; information from Covilhã's mission; Manuel I's decision to sponsor an expedition; availability of capital and ships; knowledge of astronomical navigation.

Immediate trigger or context

The sailing of da Gama's fleet in July 1497 and the obtaining of an East African pilot at Malindi, which allowed the monsoon crossing to Calicut in 1498.

Immediate effects

Direct Portuguese contact with Malabar rulers; conflict with merchants and rulers at Calicut; alliance with Cochin; a flow of spices to Lisbon that affected Mediterranean markets.

Long-term consequences

A chain of Portuguese fortified posts; the cartaz system; the Estado da Índia; later extension to Melaka and Maluku; new commercial and political competition in Asian waters.

Historical complexity

The entry cannot be explained by technology alone or by a single motive. It depended on Asian pilots and information, on regional political fragmentation, and on the fiscal needs of a monarchy using international capital.

Historical complexity: Each cause was necessary but not sufficient. Atlantic experience did not guarantee success in a different ocean, and the outcome depended on contingencies such as local alliances and monsoon timing.

Section 68.4

Vasco da Gama’s Voyage, 1497–1499

Vasco da Gama's fleet left Lisbon in July 1497 with four vessels: two carracks, a smaller caravel, and a supply ship, with crews that totaled a few hundred men. Rather than hugging the African coast, the fleet sailed in a wide arc through the South Atlantic to catch westerly winds that carried it toward southern Africa, a navigational achievement that depended on Portuguese experience in Atlantic wind systems. It rounded the Cape in November, worked up the East African coast, and arrived at Mozambique and then at Mombasa, where relations with local authorities turned hostile. At Malindi, a Swahili port whose ruler was on poor terms with Mombasa, the fleet found a friendly reception and obtained a pilot who guided it across the Arabian Sea to the Malabar coast with the monsoon.

The identity of the pilot has been debated. Later tradition, repeated in some Portuguese accounts, identified him with the Arab navigator Ahmad ibn Majid, whose navigational works were noted in Chapter 67, but the Roteiro does not name him in that way and many modern scholars doubt the identification. What is secure is that the crossing depended on a pilot with local knowledge, supplied by a coastal ruler pursuing his own interests, and that the Portuguese did not find the way to India alone.

The voyage showed several things that mattered for the future. It demonstrated that the Cape route to India was navigable, though it exposed the dangers of disease and distance. It revealed that spices could be obtained in quantity, though the Portuguese lacked goods of the kind that Asian merchants wanted. It gave the crown intelligence about Malabar politics and trade and about the presence of Muslim merchant communities, which Portuguese writers interpreted through the lens of religious rivalry. And it indicated that the crown would need a more forceful approach, a conclusion the next expeditions would reinforce.

The Roteiro is particularly revealing because its observations emerge from surprise: the travelers repeatedly had to discover who might authorize trade, which gifts carried prestige, what services merchants expected from rulers, and how much danger could arise from a failed negotiation. The journal's descriptions of religious communities should be read with comparable care. Portuguese visitors often tried to interpret Hindu practices through categories familiar from Christianity and Islam. Their mistakes are historical evidence of cultural distance, not reliable ethnography. Nor did completing the crossing create a regular shipping system by itself: the fleet had to find return winds, keep crews alive, and persuade Lisbon that further voyages were worth financing.

Division II — Calicut, Cochin, Naval Warfare, and Fortified Footholds
Section 68.5

Calicut, Diplomatic Misunderstanding, and Cabral’s Expedition

At Calicut, in May 1498, the Portuguese met the Zamorin and his court. The encounter did not go smoothly. The gifts da Gama had brought, which were intended for African and Atlantic exchange, appeared meager to a court accustomed to dealing with merchants from many lands, and Muslim merchants who dominated Calicut's overseas trade viewed the newcomers with suspicion as competitors. Disputes arose over customs, goods, and the movement of Portuguese factors, and the Portuguese were uncertain how far they could rely on the Zamorin's protection. After months on the coast, with tensions rising, da Gama sailed home with a small cargo, a limited number of ships, and many crew members lost to disease. The fleet's return to Portugal in 1499 was nonetheless treated as a triumph, and Manuel I soon adopted the title claiming lordship over the navigation and commerce of Ethiopia, Arabia, Persia, and India.

The crown responded to da Gama's return with a much larger fleet. Pedro Álvares Cabral sailed in 1500 with thirteen ships, and on the outward voyage the fleet, swinging west in the Atlantic, sighted the coast of Brazil, an episode that belongs to the Atlantic rather than Asian story. Cabral reached Calicut in the autumn of 1500 with instructions to establish a trading post and to secure a commercial agreement. A feitoria was set up, and for a time trade proceeded. Conflict then developed over the loading of cargo and the prior claims of Muslim merchants to the pepper available at the port. In December 1500 a crowd attacked the Portuguese post, and a number of Portuguese were killed. Cabral responded by seizing Muslim-owned ships in the harbor and bombarding the town, an act of retaliation that destroyed any basis for cooperation with the Zamorin.

Cabral then moved south to Cochin, whose ruler, a rival of Calicut, was willing to host the Portuguese and sell them pepper. This was a turning point. The alliance with Cochin gave the Portuguese a base on the coast and a local partner with its own motives. Cochin's ruler gained a powerful ally against Calicut, and the Portuguese gained access to pepper and a place to anchor. The arrangement also established a pattern: Portuguese power rested on cooperation with local rulers, not on conquest, and it exploited rivalry among them. The cost was entanglement in local politics, since the Zamorin made Cochin's ruler a target of war and the Portuguese were drawn into defending him.

A factory at Calicut was not a sovereign enclave. It required the Zamorin's permission, toleration from merchants already competing for pepper, secure access to warehouses, and reliable arrangements for weighing and payment. From the Portuguese view, the destruction of the factory justified retaliation; from the city's perspective, the armed reprisals threatened an established trading order. The difference was institutional as well as religious: the newcomers sought privileges that incumbent participants could contest through local political channels.

Section 68.6

Cochin, Armed Coercion, and the First Portuguese Trading Posts

Cabral returned to Portugal in 1501 with spices but lost several ships. In 1502, da Gama led a second expedition of about twenty ships, a fleet whose size and armament signalled a change in approach. On the way, he extracted tribute from the ruler of Kilwa, and in October 1502 his ships seized the Miri, a vessel returning from Mecca with pilgrims and merchants, and set it on fire. Portuguese accounts record that passengers, including women and children, died, and that the act was deliberate. It remains one of the best-attested episodes of Portuguese violence in these years, and later Malabar Muslim writers remembered it as an example of Portuguese cruelty. Da Gama then bombarded Calicut and established an arrangement with Cochin before returning to Portugal in 1503.

The following years brought further experiments with fixed posts. In 1503 the crown's captains, among them Afonso de Albuquerque and his relatives, helped build a fortification at Cochin, and Portuguese factors established posts at Quilon and Kannur. A Portuguese squadron remained on the coast during the winter monsoon when ships from Lisbon could not arrive, a decision with significant implications: it meant that the Portuguese presence in Asian waters had become permanent, supplied by annual fleets and defended by fortified posts. The Zamorin, for his part, sought assistance from other Muslim powers and organized resistance on the Malabar coast, and conflict continued intermittently.

The 1502–1503 expeditions also exposed the gap between a commercial foothold and a coercive one. A Portuguese officer could demand recognition of Manuel's claims, but an Asian ruler had to consider competing captains, resident merchants, inland producers, and the consequences of retaliation. Cochin's alliance was maintained through repeated practical decisions about defense, provisions, and pepper procurement; its population bore costs as well as receiving advantages. In 1503 the protected Portuguese trading post became a more durable armed installation. These shifts were contingent, not automatic. A failed convoy, an unfavorable monsoon, or the loss of a nearby ally could threaten the entire venture. The coast was never simply divided between a unified Portuguese side and a unified Asian opposition: each port contained factions with different trade connections and assessments of risk.

The siege of Kannur in 1507 also demonstrates the instability of the early footholds. The fort and trading post existed within the domain of the Kolathiri rulers, whose succession politics altered the relative strength of those prepared to cooperate with Portugal. Opposition included local Nair forces and those aligned with Calicut; the ability of a small garrison to withstand an attack depended on supplies and relief as much as stone walls. The outcome did not settle the wider struggle for Malabar commerce. It instead emphasized that a royal Portuguese factory could become a contested political institution within an Asian kingdom, and that its continuing existence required renewed decisions by local actors.

Historical Vocabulary
Feitoria

Definition. A Portuguese term for a factory or trading post, administered by a royal agent called a feitor, where goods were stored, bought, and sold on the crown's behalf.

Historical context. The Portuguese had used feitorias on the West African coast, and they carried the institution to India and East Africa. In Asia, a feitoria might begin as a rented warehouse in a port whose ruler consented to the arrangement, and it might later be fortified, as at Cochin, Kannur, and elsewhere. Its staff included clerks, agents, and soldiers, and it depended on local brokers for dealings with producers.

Why it matters. The feitoria was the commercial heart of Portuguese presence, and its relation to the local ruler, whether cooperative or hostile, often decided whether Portuguese trade could proceed.

Potential misunderstanding. A feitoria was not automatically a colony or a fortress. Many early feitorias existed by permission of local rulers, and the fortified posts that followed were a later and more controversial development.

Section 68.7

Ships, Artillery, and the Diversity of Indian Ocean Naval Warfare

Portuguese success in naval battles in the early sixteenth century rested on a combination of ships, artillery, and tactics that, in the Indian Ocean context, proved unusually effective. The principal long-distance vessel was the carrack or nau, a large, high-sided, square-rigged ship with several decks, capable of carrying cargo, supplies, soldiers, and heavy guns. Smaller caravels, with their lateen sails, provided maneuverability and were useful in scouting. The Portuguese mounted bronze and iron cannon along the sides of their ships, and they developed the practice of fighting at a distance, using heavy shipboard guns in selected firing positions before closing or boarding when circumstances permitted. Fleet commanders tried to preserve formation, to use their guns as the main weapon, and to avoid close-quarter fighting where numbers could count against them.

These advantages were real in the circumstances of the early sixteenth century. Indian Ocean shipbuilding was diverse: some vessels used stitched or lashed plank techniques, while other South Asian vessels used substantial iron fastenings. Cargo ships, regional fighting vessels, and larger Gujarati ships varied widely in design and armament; particular Portuguese warships could carry heavier concentrated artillery, but there was no simple divide between European iron-fastened hulls and universally sewn Asian craft. Naval warfare in the region had traditionally relied more on boarding, archers, and swarming tactics than on heavy shipboard artillery. The Portuguese ability to bring concentrated heavy fire to bear on an opponent therefore changed tactical outcomes, as the clash at Diu in 1509 later showed. At the same time, Asian powers were not ignorant of gunpowder weapons. Mamluk, Gujarati, and other forces used cannon and handguns, and Asian rulers acquired Portuguese weapons and expertise, sometimes through renegades and deserters, within a short time.

A better comparison starts with particular functions, not categories of civilization. Hull strength mattered differently in a deep-water voyage than in coastal transport; ships designed to carry textiles and pepper did not necessarily require the same weapons as royal fighting ships. Some Indian Ocean hulls used sewn-plank methods, others pegged or iron-fastened construction, and one excavated medieval boat from Kerala shows substantial iron fastenings. Portuguese guns could be tactically decisive when vessels were able to maintain position and concentrate fire, but sailing conditions, ammunition, trained crews, and the enemy's formation determined results. The evidence does not justify the claim that Asian naval technology was uniformly primitive or that a mature, standardized broadside battle line already existed in 1500.

Section 68.8

Fortresses, Garrisons, Supply Networks, and Their Limits

Fortifications were the second pillar. A fortress armed with artillery and garrisoned by a small number of soldiers could resist much larger forces, especially if it could be resupplied by sea, and Portuguese engineers adopted European designs adapted to local conditions. The fort at Cochin, the fortress at Kannur, and later those at Goa, Hormuz, and elsewhere gave the Portuguese places to store goods, repair ships, house garrisons, and project force. Fortresses also had costs. They required manpower, supplies, and constant maintenance, and a garrison cut off from the sea could be starved. The major siege of Kannur in 1507, in which a Portuguese garrison endured a prolonged blockade before relief arrived, illustrated both the defensive strength of a fort and its vulnerability to isolation.

Limits on Portuguese capability were serious and persistent. The number of men and ships was small relative to the scale of the ocean. Voyages were dangerous, and fleets lost ships and men to storms, scurvy, and disease, so that the crown had to send repeated reinforcements. The Cape route required timing to monsoon cycles and meant that ships reached India only once a year, leaving the Estado dependent on what it had on the ground for most of the year. Ships required timber, rope, pitch, and skilled workers, and the Portuguese soon depended on Indian shipyards and materials for construction and repair, which tied them to local suppliers. Naval superiority at a particular moment did not give the Portuguese the ability to be everywhere, and Asian ships could and did avoid Portuguese squadrons by adapting routes and timing. Technology was thus a necessary but insufficient basis for Portuguese power, which also depended on alliances, intelligence, and the choices of Asian rulers and merchants.

The physical geography of a fortified port made supply a political issue. A garrison needed drinking water, grains, firewood, medicines, spare sails, timber, powder, and skilled labor; most could not arrive continuously from Portugal. A fort might secure a quay while depending on an inland market that its defenders did not govern. At Kannur, the siege of 1507 arose amid a succession crisis in Kolathunad and the political position of merchants and local armed groups, not from a generic confrontation between two religions. Reinforcement by sea mattered, but so did choices by local rulers who controlled access on land. Permanent occupation therefore deepened the Portuguese need to negotiate and sometimes coerce arrangements that a passing fleet could evade.

A fort's location also determined who paid for its survival. Control of a sheltered beach or river mouth might enable artillery to protect a trading post, but residents of the surrounding town could lose access to shore space, markets, or shipping. The Portuguese did not possess the manpower to make every community comply permanently. At some ports negotiation could restore supplies; at others insecurity, blockade, or local hostility made a nominally strong fortress an expensive liability.

Division III — The First Estado da Índia: Almeida, Albuquerque, and Key Ports
Section 68.9

Francisco de Almeida and the First Institutional Framework

In 1505 Manuel I sent Francisco de Almeida to India with the title of viceroy and with a large fleet of about twenty ships, a sign that the crown wished to organize its presence more systematically. His instructions included building fortresses on the East African coast and in India, taking a leading role in controlling the sea, and enforcing the crown's commercial claims. On the African coast, Almeida's forces took Kilwa and Mombasa, compelling tribute and building a fort at Kilwa, which was intended to secure access to the gold trade through Sofala. In India, he fortified Kannur and other points, and he developed a strategy that later writers described as favoring sea control over territorial conquest, concentrating on a strong fleet that could defeat rivals and protect Portuguese shipping.

Almeida's office was an institutional experiment. The Portuguese Crown was attempting to govern far-flung captains and royal factors from a seat at Cochin while ships from Lisbon arrived intermittently. Orders sent from Europe might be obsolete by the time they reached the Malabar coast; commanders disputed jurisdiction, merchants argued over prices, and royal treasurers demanded accounts. A viceroy could coordinate military operations and diplomacy, but he did not suddenly possess a staffed bureaucracy capable of governing the ocean. The term Estado da Índia is useful for this expanding apparatus only if its early improvisation is kept visible.

Nor was Almeida's maritime strategy simply a doctrine of avoiding territory. Fortifications along the African and Indian coasts provided the fleet with anchorages and revenue-producing posts, while allied rulers controlled much of the productive hinterland. Naval coercion and territorial footholds were already complementary. Later historians often contrast Almeida's sea-control approach with Albuquerque's occupation of strategically important ports, but their policies developed within overlapping fiscal and logistical constraints. Both required a secure base, officers able to enforce orders, and ships supplied from Asian resources.

For the Crown, creating a governorship also meant attempting to restrain its own agents. Royal factors and captains could compete for the same cargo or make incompatible promises to a local ruler; profits from private voyages tempted officials to evade their instructions. Letters could report a problem but not resolve it quickly when communication with Lisbon took many months. Portuguese authority was therefore partly a problem of internal coordination. The headquarters at Cochin was a working base for correspondence, customs, and diplomacy, while nearby rulers and merchants remained necessary to its functioning.

In this institutional setting, a declaration from the Crown could become effective only through negotiations conducted locally, often by officers whose own salaries and commercial opportunities depended on the outcome.

Historical Vocabulary
Estado da Índia

Definition. The "State of India," the name later given to the Portuguese crown's collection of fortresses, factories, fleets, and officials in Asia, headed by a viceroy or governor, initially with headquarters at Cochin and later at Goa.

Historical context. The formal title and organization developed over several decades. In the years covered by this chapter, Portuguese authority in Asia was exercised by captains, governors, and the first viceroy, and its structure was still being improvised. Historians use the term for convenience when discussing the system as a whole.

Why it matters. The name expresses the idea that the Portuguese presence was a state apparatus, not merely a set of private ventures, yet that apparatus was dispersed, thinly staffed, and dependent on local cooperation.

Potential misunderstanding. The Estado da Índia was not a territorial empire comparable to a contiguous land state. It was a network of points along sea routes and coasts, with large gaps between them.

Section 68.10

Chaul, Diu, and the Limits of Naval Victory, 1508–1509

The decisive test came from the Arabian Sea. The Mamluk Sultanate, whose revenue depended on the Red Sea spice trade, and the Sultanate of Gujarat, whose merchants and port governors were losing trade, had reasons to resist the Portuguese. With the support of other interests, including the Zamorin, a Mamluk fleet under Amir Husayn al-Kurdi entered Indian waters, and in March 1508 it joined Gujarati forces in defeating a Portuguese squadron at Chaul, where Almeida's son Lourenço was killed. Almeida, determined to avenge his son and defend Portuguese power, assembled his fleet and in February 1509 met the combined Mamluk, Gujarati, and allied force off Diu. The Portuguese victory, achieved through superior gunnery and tactics and aided by divisions among their opponents, destroyed the Mamluk expedition and established Portuguese naval superiority in the Arabian Sea for the time being. It did not end Gujarati commerce or Mamluk interest, but it did ensure that the Portuguese could not easily be dislodged by sea.

The battle of Chaul and the battle of Diu were not the confrontation of a European navy with an undifferentiated Muslim fleet. The Mamluk commander Amir Husayn, Gujarati authorities, the interests of Malik Ayaz at Diu, and the Zamorin's commercial allies had objectives that did not always coincide. At Chaul in 1508 a Portuguese squadron was defeated, demonstrating that concentrated Portuguese gunfire was not an invulnerable formula. Almeida's victory at Diu in February 1509 changed the immediate military balance, but even a major victory could not close every Arabian Sea route or determine the choices of merchants. Political divisions, tactical circumstances, and changing access to reinforcements belong in the explanation alongside artillery.

There is also an important distinction between defeating a war fleet and transforming commerce. Ships carrying textiles, horses, pepper, and pilgrims could vary their voyages, seek protection from local authorities, or pay for Portuguese passes without abandoning older trading relationships. The Mamluk navy's setback did not erase the Red Sea route, and Gujarati merchants remained central to the circuits Portugal wanted to tax. A battle can establish a temporary capacity to threaten shipping, but not an administrative ability to supervise every cargo or harbor. Students should assess the evidence for strategic advantage separately from claims of permanent naval supremacy.

The documentary evidence for Diu further warns against easy casualty calculations. Chroniclers described ships captured, destroyed, or escaped and often presented enemy losses in terms meant to demonstrate royal achievement. Those numbers are not interchangeable with independent assessments. The battlefield combined ships tied to Gujarat's commercial interests, Mamluk intervention, and local decisions about whether to engage or withdraw. Naval historians must explain a result without treating a victory narrative as a complete tactical map. The Portuguese prevailed at Diu; the conclusion does not require that every allied opponent shared one command or that all Arab and Indian navigation immediately submitted to their rules.

Section 68.11

Afonso de Albuquerque and the Capture of Goa, 1510

When Afonso de Albuquerque succeeded Almeida as governor in late 1509, he gave the Portuguese presence a new shape. A veteran of earlier expeditions, he was a forceful and controversial figure who believed that Portuguese power required fixed territorial bases at strategic points. In February 1510 he captured Goa, an island port on the Konkan coast belonging to the Sultanate of Bijapur, with assistance from local Hindu leaders such as a figure called Timoja in Portuguese sources. Bijapur forces retook the town in the summer, but Albuquerque returned in November 1510 and held it. Goa gave the Portuguese a defensible island, an excellent harbor, shipyards, access to the horse trade with the Deccan, and a population from which revenue and manpower could be drawn. It immediately became a major strategic and revenue base; the administrative headquarters remained at Cochin until their transfer to Goa around 1530, beyond this chapter’s chronology.

Goa's importance lay in its capacity to support a resident power rather than merely host a seasonal factory. The island provided access to docks, stores, fiscal revenues, a regional population, and political connections to the Deccan. It was also embedded in Bijapur's sphere, and the Portuguese assault depended partly on the information and interests of local collaborators, including the figure Portuguese sources call Timoja. Capturing it in February did not make the occupation secure: Bijapur recaptured it during the year, and the November assault was followed by grave violence. The records disagree sharply about numbers killed, and their figures should not be treated as precise population statistics.

The governor's letters reveal the administrative problem behind his victories. He had to explain the cost of fortifications, bargain with local elites, justify extraordinary decisions, and request manpower the king might not be able to send. Although Goa grew into the central Portuguese base in Asia, it did not immediately replace Cochin as the formal headquarters in 1510; that transfer occurred around 1530. Separating these dates helps students distinguish the seizure of a strategic port from a subsequent evolution in government. It also shows that Albuquerque's project was not fully realized during his lifetime.

After the recapture, the organization of customs, property, and religious policy posed problems that battlefield descriptions can obscure. Occupation required choices about retaining local officials, permitting trade, and obtaining skilled shipyard labor. A Portuguese governor could not collect revenue from a deserted port, and the interests of the Crown did not always match the ambitions of soldiers expecting spoils. The contradictory pressures of coercion, settlement, and economic recovery made Goa an experiment in governing people as well as occupying a strategic island.

Section 68.12

Hormuz, Aden, and the Strategic Geography of Oceanic Power

Albuquerque's plan went further. He sought to control the entrances to the principal trading systems: the Red Sea at Aden, the Persian Gulf at Hormuz, and the Strait of Malacca at Melaka. In 1511 he led the expedition that captured Melaka, which is examined in Chapter 69. In 1513 he attacked Aden but failed, and he led a fleet into the Red Sea, where it sheltered for months near Kamaran before returning to the Indian coast by about August 1513, without securing Aden. In 1515 he returned to Hormuz, where the Portuguese had made an earlier attempt to build a fort in 1507 and abandoned it, and this time he established a fortified presence while leaving the local ruler in place as a tributary. He died at sea near Goa in December 1515. His record was mixed: he established Goa and Hormuz and Melaka as bases, but failed at Aden, and his policies strained Portuguese resources and angered critics within the Estado.

Diplomacy extended beyond the coast. The Portuguese sent envoys to Asian courts, seeking alliances against Muslim powers and trading privileges, and in the early decades they attempted contacts with Ethiopia, which they identified with the Christian realm of Prester John, with Vijayanagara, with the rulers of Sri Lanka, and with other powers. Many of these contacts were shaped by Portuguese religious assumptions and had limited results, but they show that the Portuguese operated within a diplomatic system in which recognition and exchanges of embassies mattered. In Sri Lanka, for example, Portuguese interest in cinnamon led to arrangements with the Kotte kingdom in the early sixteenth century, details of which are uncertain in the earliest years.

A map of the entrances to the Red Sea and Persian Gulf can make Albuquerque's program look geometrically simple: take Aden, hold Hormuz, and threaten ships moving between them. The maritime landscape was less cooperative. Aden was fortified and connected to a substantial inland and Red Sea economy; its rulers could resist assault and deny the supplies Portuguese vessels required. In 1513 Albuquerque failed to capture it, moved into the Red Sea, sheltered for months near Kamaran, and returned to India by about August. The expedition exposed how wind, sickness, provisions, local defenses, and the absence of reliable allies could defeat a program built around oceanic chokepoints.

Hormuz offered a different outcome in 1515. Albuquerque established a fortified Portuguese position while preserving the local ruling house under tribute arrangements, a settlement that relied on continued commercial activity rather than the abolition of indigenous political structures. Customs revenues could only be collected if merchants kept using the port. The contrast with Aden demonstrates the difference between claiming control and constructing a workable agreement. Neither success at Hormuz nor failure at Aden gave Portuguese officials command over the inland markets or monsoon routes that sustained these harbors.

Division IV — Commercial Institutions, Asian Responses, and Intermediaries
Section 68.13

The Crown Monopoly, Casa da Índia, and Commercial Finance

Financing was a mixture of royal investment and private capital. The Portuguese crown reserved the pepper and spice trade for itself and invested heavily in fleets, but it also drew on loans and shares from Italian and German merchant firms in Lisbon and Antwerp, among them houses such as the Welsers and Fuggers, who financed or participated in voyages in the early sixteenth century. This pattern, a royal monopoly financed by international capital, shaped the enterprise throughout. It tied Portuguese policy to the need for regular spice shipments and revenue and to the fortunes of European markets, and it explains why Portuguese captains were under constant pressure to deliver cargo.

The Portuguese crown aimed to monopolize the purchase of spices in Asia and their sale in Europe, a claim that rested on the king's financial interest and on the assertion of exclusive navigation rights. The Casa da Índia in Lisbon managed the crown's trade, supervised shipments, and kept accounts, and the crown's factor in Antwerp sold pepper and spices to European merchants. In Asia, royal factors purchased pepper in Malabar and other goods elsewhere, often paying with copper, silver, coral, and other European exports, and later with Asian goods such as Indian cloth. The enterprise's profitability depended on the price of pepper in Europe, on the costs of fleets and losses at sea, and on the crown's success in restricting rivals.

The Portuguese also confronted a structural weakness: Europe produced few goods that Asian markets wanted. Silver, copper, coral, and some manufactured items were exportable, but they did not match the scale of purchases of spices, and the crown therefore sought to enter the intra-Asian trade, using the profits from carrying textiles, gold, and other goods to pay for spices. Gujarati cloth, in particular, was a key commodity in exchange for East African gold and for Southeast Asian spices and pepper, which explains why Portuguese strategists wanted both to control Gujarat's ports and to reach Melaka, where Gujarati cloth was a staple (see Chapters 67 and 69). The shift to participation in Asian trade, not only European purchases, became a defining feature of the later Estado.

Commercial paperwork was as necessary as naval firepower. The Casa da Índia organized the purchase and dispatch of royal cargoes, but collection at a distant port demanded accurate weights, contracts, storage, and factors able to negotiate with brokers. Portuguese fleets also had to pay crews, support garrisons, purchase food, and finance ships before spices could produce revenue in European markets. Lending and private trade blurred the separation between royal monopoly and merchant enterprise. The Crown aimed to direct the profitable spice traffic but could not directly supervise the many Asian transactions that supplied it.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Indian Ocean Trade before and after the Portuguese Entry

What changed? A new armed power introduced systematic naval enforcement, fortified bases, a royal monopoly claim, and a pass system. Spice shipments to Europe by the Cape route began, altering the flow of goods and revenues, and Portuguese-held ports such as Goa and later Melaka became new nodes.

What remained continuous? Asian merchant communities, brokers, shipbuilders, and rulers continued to carry most of the trade. Monsoon timing, the major port hierarchy, textile and spice exchange, and the importance of Gujarat, Malabar, and Southeast Asian markets persisted, and the Red Sea and Persian Gulf routes remained active.

When did change occur? Between roughly 1498 and 1515, unevenly and with reversals. The most visible transformation was the establishment of Portuguese fortified points, and the shifts in trade volume and routes were gradual and are debated.

Who experienced change most directly? Ports and merchants in the zones where Portuguese forces operated, including Calicut's Muslim merchants, Hormuz, and Goa's population. Many producers and consumers in the interior experienced more continuity.

Why it matters: Describing the entry as either a complete transformation or no change at all distorts the evidence. It was a significant intrusion into an enduring system, which adapted to it.

Section 68.14

The Cartaz System: Enforced Passes and Commercial Resistance

Alongside the monopoly on spices, the Portuguese developed a system of passes, the cartaz, that allowed Asian ships to sail in waters the Portuguese claimed to police. A ship that carried a cartaz was permitted to trade and was supposed to avoid enemies of the Portuguese, and a ship without one could be seized. The system turned naval power into a source of revenue and an instrument of political control, and it required Asian shipowners to accept Portuguese authority at least nominally. It also provided information, since cartazes recorded ships and cargoes. Historians such as Niels Steensgaard have described the Portuguese enterprise as a "redistributive" one, in which the main source of profit lay in selling protection and charging fees for access to the sea, as well as trading goods.

The monopoly was never complete. Pepper and other spices continued to reach the Red Sea and the Persian Gulf, partly through ports outside Portuguese control and partly through contraband. The Portuguese lacked ships to blockade every route, and Asian merchants, rulers, and even Portuguese officials, who often traded privately, found ways around the crown's rules. The Red Sea route recovered after the initial shock of Portuguese entry, and Venetian and other merchants continued to buy spices from the Levant, as historians have shown. The extent to which Portuguese shipments reduced European dependence on the Mediterranean route is debated, and different periods show different results. The crown's share of the trade was significant but not exclusive.

A cartaz was not just an administrative certificate. Where Portuguese vessels were strong enough to enforce the system, an Asian ship without an acceptable pass might face interception, seizure, or demands to visit approved ports. The terms could restrict routes, cargoes, or the trading relations of particular merchants. Yet the effects depended on where patrols sailed and on the degree of cooperation from captains, ports, and local rulers. A nominally universal maritime rule was implemented in selective zones. Some merchants paid, some negotiated exemptions, and some avoided Portuguese vessels altogether; others challenged the system with armed support. The consequences included both revenue and resentment, not comprehensive mastery of shipping.

It is important to distinguish two kinds of monopoly. The Crown sought privileged access to spices it bought and shipped to Europe, and it also claimed rights to regulate other people's vessels through sea passes. These were related but different projects. Neither abolished Asian commerce. Indeed, the ability to collect passes and sell protection required the continued operation of the very shipping networks that Portuguese officials sometimes threatened.

Historical Vocabulary
Cartaz

Definition. A pass issued by Portuguese authorities that allowed an Asian ship to sail and trade in Portuguese-claimed waters, usually specifying its route and exempting it from seizure.

Historical context. The word is generally derived from an Arabic term for a piece of paper. The practice grew in the early sixteenth century as the Portuguese tried to regulate Asian shipping with a limited navy, and it became a central tool of the Estado da Índia. Ships carrying passes might also be required to call at Portuguese ports and pay duties.

Why it matters. The cartaz allowed a small naval force to regulate large numbers of ships by creating a legal and financial obligation, and it turned the claim of sea control into a fiscal system.

Potential misunderstanding. The cartaz did not mean that Asian shipping stopped or that all vessels complied. Many Asian merchants obtained passes as an economic calculation, others evaded them, and some powers refused to accept the system at all.

Section 68.15

Asian States, Merchants, and Religious Responses

Asian responses to the Portuguese varied with local interests, resources, and rivalries. Calicut's Zamorin, whose revenue and prestige depended on a large Muslim merchant community and the trade it handled, regarded the Portuguese as a threat after 1500 and led resistance on the Malabar coast, sometimes with help from outside. The Muslim merchants of Calicut, who had the most to lose from Portuguese attempts to monopolize pepper, supported resistance and, in some cases, relocated their business to other ports. Cochin's ruler and the Hindu merchants and others who benefited from the alliance chose differently. These choices were not simple expressions of religious solidarity: they followed political and commercial calculations, and they changed over time.

Gujarat's response was shaped by trade interests. Its rulers and merchants wanted to protect shipping in the Arabian Sea, and the governor of Diu, Malik Ayaz, cooperated with the Mamluks in 1508 to 1509. After the defeat at Diu, Gujarati interests turned to a mix of negotiation, avoidance, and later conflict, and many Gujarati merchants obtained Portuguese passes while continuing trade. The Mamluk Sultanate, with its fiscal stake in the Red Sea trade, mounted the expedition of 1508 and 1509, but it lacked a sustained naval tradition in the Indian Ocean and was weakened by internal strains. The Ottoman conquest of Egypt in 1517 changed the political situation and brought the Ottomans into the story, though that belongs beyond this chapter's period.

Hormuz's rulers, who depended on trade and on the goodwill of Persian and other powers, accepted Portuguese tribute arrangements under pressure. Swahili city-states responded in different ways: Malindi allied with the Portuguese, while Kilwa and Mombasa were attacked. In Southeast Asia, merchants who traded in India heard of Portuguese actions, and rulers such as Melaka's sultan had access to information about events on the Malabar coast, a point discussed in Section 68.19.

Overall, Asian powers did not unite against the Portuguese. The system's political fragmentation, which had made it open to many merchants, also meant that no single authority could coordinate resistance, and Portuguese successes were achieved partly by exploiting divisions. At the same time, Asian merchants and rulers adapted. Some sought passes, some shifted routes to avoid Portuguese-held points, some entered Portuguese service, and some armed their ships more heavily. These adaptations preserved much of the older commercial order.

A later Malabar Muslim historian, Zayn al-Dīn al-Malībārī, provided a powerful account of Portuguese aggression, commercial injury, and the duty of resistance. Because he wrote in the later sixteenth century, his work cannot be treated as a transcript of what Calicut's merchants said in 1498. It is nevertheless invaluable for examining how a Muslim intellectual in Kerala explained the preceding decades of conflict. Nor was his viewpoint identical with that of every Muslim merchant, teacher, or ruler: interests and judgments differed within those communities. His work gives the chapter a necessary non-Portuguese interpretive voice while also demanding its own source criticism.

Section 68.16

Pilots, Brokers, Allies, Shipyards, and Asian Labor

No account of Portuguese power in Asia is complete without recognizing its dependence on Asian knowledge and labor. The first example is navigation. The Malindi pilot who guided da Gama across the Arabian Sea has been mentioned above, but the Portuguese continued to use local pilots on routes they did not know, and they relied on Asian sailors in large numbers. Indian ports supplied seamen, carpenters, caulkers, and laborers. Portuguese ships required constant repair and replacement, and Indian shipyards, with their supplies of teak and skilled workers, became essential to the Estado, a dependence that grew over the sixteenth century.

Supply was a second area of dependence. Fortresses in India and elsewhere had to be fed, and food came from local markets, with the help of brokers. Merchants known as brokers or interpreters, often from Hindu, Muslim, Jewish, or Syrian Christian communities, mediated between the Portuguese and local producers and rulers, advanced credit, and provided information. Portuguese factors frequently could not speak local languages or understand local customs, and brokers filled the gap. The result was that Portuguese commerce, even where it claimed a monopoly, depended on Asian networks of credit and information.

Political alliances were a third. The Cochin alliance has already been noted, and at Goa the Portuguese relied on Hindu leaders opposed to the Sultan of Bijapur. At Hormuz, they left the local ruler in place and collected tribute. Their relations with Vijayanagara, a major land power that needed horses imported via the western coast, were shaped by commercial interest: the Portuguese control of the horse trade at Goa gave them leverage with the empire, and its ruler had reasons to cooperate. In East Africa, the ruler of Malindi allied with the Portuguese against Mombasa and Kilwa. In every case, Portuguese advance exploited rivalry among local powers, and each local ally pursued its own interests rather than serving as a subordinate.

Marriage and settlement were a fourth. Albuquerque promoted marriages between Portuguese men and local women, particularly in Goa, creating a class of casados, married settlers, who were given land and privileges and who formed a base of population loyal to the Portuguese. The policy reflected the shortage of Portuguese settlers and the usefulness of kinship ties, and it created a mixed society, sometimes called Luso-Asian, whose members played roles in commerce and administration. At the same time, unofficial settlers, some of whom entered the service of Asian rulers or traded independently, formed communities that the crown could not control, and Portuguese individuals sometimes served Asian courts as soldiers, gunners, or traders. The Portuguese presence was thus never purely a state enterprise.

The Portuguese enterprise was also small in demographic terms. Portugal's population was limited, and fleets sailing to India carried perhaps a few hundred to a few thousand men at a time, many of whom died of disease or shipwreck. The crown could not populate Asia with settlers, and its presence depended on forts, a few thousand soldiers, sailors, and officials, and cooperation from local societies. This limitation was fundamental to what followed.

Portuguese captains could threaten a vessel but could not construct a harbor economy out of their own manpower. Pilots supplied knowledge of reefs and seasons; Gujarati, Mappila, and other merchants supplied commercial contacts and credit; carpenters and caulkers repaired hulls; local cultivators and fishers fed fleets; Asian rulers provided or withheld harbors. These dependencies were not incidental concessions. They determined the daily scope of European action, especially between the annual arrivals from Lisbon. Portuguese officials frequently tried to control these collaborators through contracts or patronage while remaining vulnerable to withdrawal of their services.

Section 68.17

Violence, Slavery, Settlers, and Everyday Life

Violence was a central feature of the early Portuguese presence and should be described plainly. Portuguese forces bombarded Calicut in 1500 and again in 1502, seized and destroyed ships, and exacted tribute from East African and Indian rulers. The burning of the Miri in 1502 killed pilgrims and merchants who were noncombatants. At Goa in 1510, Portuguese accounts report that after the second capture a large number of Muslim residents were killed, though the numbers in these accounts vary and are of uncertain reliability. Portuguese writers often justified such acts as part of a holy war or as necessary retaliation, while Asian writers remembered them as atrocities. Historians avoid both the language of unqualified glorification and the assumption that Portuguese violence was unique: Asian rulers also fought wars, seized ships, and enslaved captives. What distinguished the Portuguese was the combination of naval gunnery, a royal claim to monopoly, and a religious framing of conflict.

Enslavement was also part of the story. The Portuguese had been involved in the slave trade in West Africa since the 1440s, and in Asia they seized and purchased captives, used enslaved labor on ships and in households, and participated in existing slave markets. Enslaved people included Africans, Indians, and others, and their status varied. The documentation is uneven, and historians estimate scale cautiously.

Religion shaped Portuguese self-understanding and policy. The crown held rights of patronage over the church in its overseas territories, later called the padroado, and Franciscan and later other missionaries accompanied the early expeditions. In India the Portuguese encountered the Saint Thomas Christians of Malabar, an old community with its own traditions and links to West Asian churches, and relations between this community and the Portuguese were complex. In Goa, conversion and religious policy grew stricter over the sixteenth century, and in these early years practice was less systematic. The Portuguese also interacted with Hindu, Muslim, and Jewish communities, and local alliances sometimes cut across religious lines.

The difference between events and justifications deserves emphasis. Portuguese captains sometimes described bombardments, seizure of ships, and massacre as lawful retaliation or holy war; victims and opponents saw such acts as attacks on life, property, and the conventions of commerce. The burning of the pilgrim vessel Miri in 1502 was an intentional attack on noncombatants, not an incidental loss in battle. At Goa in 1510, contemporary narratives disagree about the scale of killings after recapture, so the chapter should describe the documented violence without inventing a reliable total. The early Estado was also a setting of ordinary survival: sailors fell ill, enslaved people labored, intermediaries translated, and households navigated unfamiliar legal and religious claims.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ships, Forts, and Ports in the Early Portuguese Orbit

Life on a Portuguese India fleet was hard. Ships carried nobles and officers, soldiers, sailors, gunners, pilots, clerks, priests, and sometimes convicts sent into exile, together with enslaved servants. Food consisted largely of ship's biscuit, salted meat and fish, and dried foods, and water and provisions were constantly at risk of spoilage. Scurvy, dysentery, and fevers killed many, and storms and shipwreck added to the toll. The long passage around the Cape and the dependence on monsoon timing meant that men spent many months aboard, and the survivors who reached India arrived weakened.

In Asian ports under Portuguese control or influence, daily life mixed communities. In Cochin and later Goa, Portuguese soldiers and officials lived alongside Indian brokers, merchants, craftspeople, boatmen, and servants. Many Portuguese men took local partners or wives, and children of these unions formed part of a growing Luso-Asian community. Local women worked in markets and households and sometimes held property, and their role in mediating between communities is recorded unevenly, mostly in outsiders' accounts. Garrisons suffered from illness and from irregular pay, and complaints about neglect were common.

For Asian residents, the Portuguese presence meant new taxes, new passes, and new risks at sea, but also new employment in shipyards and in service to the garrisons, and new opportunities for merchants who allied with the Portuguese. Fishing communities and port workers adapted to changing demand. The evidence for the lives of ordinary people comes mostly from Portuguese records and later chronicles, which focus on elites and on conflict, so historians must infer the experience of non-elite groups with caution.

Section 68.18

Did the Portuguese Transform the Indian Ocean?

Interpretations of the Portuguese entry have changed considerably. Older narratives, including those written in the colonial era, often presented the arrival of da Gama as the opening of Asia to European enterprise and treated Portuguese naval power as the beginning of European domination. Some Asian historians, such as K. M. Panikkar in the mid-twentieth century, argued that the Indian Ocean had been an open sea before the Portuguese and that Portuguese naval control imposed a new and coercive order, with long-term consequences for Asian maritime power. Later historians, in contrast, have stressed the limits of the Portuguese system and the persistence of Asian trade. Pearson and Subrahmanyam, for example, have examined the entanglement of Portuguese officials with Asian merchants and the degree to which the Portuguese Estado was one among several regional powers.

The evidence of this chapter supports a cautious synthesis. The Portuguese were new and formidable participants whose ships and guns changed particular battles and ports, whose forts and passes created new instruments of control, and whose violence left lasting memories. They were also dependent on Asian pilots, brokers, shipbuilders, allies, and markets, limited in numbers, subject to the monsoon and to disease, and unable to command the ocean. The Indian Ocean after 1515 was not the same as before, and it was not wholly different. Within that continuity and change, Southeast Asia's ports and rulers soon had to decide how to respond to a power that was already established in India and was turning its attention eastward.

A historiographical comparison must ask what each author measures. K. M. Panikkar highlighted the coercive challenge Portuguese naval violence posed to established Asian maritime commerce. Niels Steensgaard described the sale of protection and the armed commercial apparatus as important institutional innovations, although his best-known analysis focused primarily on a later century. M. N. Pearson, K. N. Chaudhuri, and Sanjay Subrahmanyam placed greater weight on older trading networks, local politics, and the dependence of European actors on Asian intermediaries. These approaches are not simply votes for or against Portuguese importance: they select different geographical scales, periods, and forms of power.

Evidence from 1498–1515 supports a measured conclusion. Portuguese fleets could inflict severe damage and secure a handful of strategic points, yet Asian merchants carried enormous volumes of goods through ports not administered by Portugal. The Red Sea and Persian Gulf did not cease to function, and Indian Ocean rulers continued to make decisions based on their own political interests. Transformation is therefore best specified by place and institution—fortified ports, naval encounters, shipping licenses, and the Cape trade—rather than declared for the whole ocean at once.

Historical Debate

Did the Portuguese Transform the Indian Ocean?

The question: To what extent did the Portuguese entry change the political and commercial order of the Indian Ocean, and to what extent was it absorbed within existing patterns?

Interpretation A, a new maritime order: Some historians argue that Portuguese naval violence, fortified bases, the cartaz system, and the introduction of armed enforcement of trade introduced a new principle, the sale of protection by an armed power, and a new level of coercion into a previously more open system. Panikkar's older argument took this view, and Steensgaard's analysis of the Portuguese as a redistributive enterprise emphasized the novelty of selling protection.
Interpretation B, absorption and continuity: Others argue that the Portuguese controlled only a modest part of Asian trade, depended on Asian merchants and rulers, and were integrated into existing commercial networks, with the Red Sea route and Asian shipping recovering and continuing. Pearson, Subrahmanyam, and Chaudhuri's work on the Indian Ocean economy have supported this view, emphasizing continuity and the agency of Asian actors.
A third view, differences by region and period: Many scholars suggest that impacts varied by region, with the greatest disruption in Malabar and Hormuz, more limited effects in Gujarat and the Bay of Bengal, and different outcomes in Southeast Asia, and that effects changed over decades.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on Portuguese administrative records, Asian chronicles, trade statistics where they exist, and evidence for shipping patterns. Quantitative evidence for the early decades is limited, and estimates of the volume of trade by various routes are disputed.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns chiefly scale and interpretation, specifically how much weight to give to violence and monopoly claims versus actual outcomes in trade volumes, and also terminology, since words like "empire" and "monopoly" describe aspirations more than achievements.

Why it matters: The answer affects whether the Portuguese entry is treated as the start of European dominance or as one episode in a long history of Asian maritime commerce, and it shapes how the agency of Asian actors, including Southeast Asians, is understood.

Section 68.19

Southeast Asia on the Horizon: Melaka before 1511

Southeast Asia was the eastern anchor of this world. Cloves, nutmeg, and mace from Maluku and Banda reached western markets through Javanese and Malay carriers, Melaka, and Gujarati and other merchants, as described in Chapter 67. Pepper came both from Malabar and from Sumatra, and Southeast Asian rice, tin, gold, camphor, and aromatic woods entered the exchange. Chinese goods, including porcelain and silk, moved west through Melaka and other ports. Merchants from Melaka, Pasai, and Java were present in Indian ports, and merchants from India and the Middle East were present in Melaka. This is why Portuguese knowledge of Southeast Asian commerce was available in India from the start, and why the later move to Melaka, narrated in Chapter 69, grew from information gathered in Indian ports.

For the Portuguese in India, Southeast Asia was at first a source of information and rumor. Merchants from Melaka, Pasai, and Java were present in Malabar and Gujarat, and they could describe the sources of cloves, nutmeg, and mace, the position of Melaka, and the extent of trade with China. Tomé Pires, writing later, gathered much of this information from Asian merchants, and Duarte Barbosa, who served in India, recorded similar accounts. These descriptions shaped Portuguese expectations: Melaka was understood as the principal market for spices and a point at which Gujarati cloth was exchanged for spice, and a Portuguese presence there would give the crown access to the sources of cloves and nutmeg without relying on the Indian intermediaries who then carried them.

The crown acted on this knowledge in 1508, when Manuel sent Diogo Lopes de Sequeira with instructions to go to Melaka and establish relations. Sequeira arrived in 1509, and the circumstances of that visit and of the events that followed, including the Portuguese conquest in 1511, are the subject of Chapter 69. At this point in the narrative, it is enough to note that the move toward Melaka grew from a strategy developed in India: control of the Strait of Malacca would complement control of the entrances to the Red Sea and the Persian Gulf, and it would allow the Portuguese to participate directly in the trade between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea. Albuquerque's letters and the commentaries that followed present Melaka as one of a series of key points, and the connection with Goa, a base from which expeditions could be mounted, was crucial.

Information also flowed the other way. Southeast Asian rulers and merchants learned of the Portuguese from Indian ports, and their responses to the Portuguese arrival, which later chapters examine, were informed by what had happened on the Malabar coast. Melaka’s rulers and merchants had connections through which reports of Portuguese violence, passes, and new alliances could circulate, but their exact knowledge of particular events at Calicut cannot be independently established. The Portuguese entered Southeast Asia, then, in a region already informed about them, a point that complicates the idea of a sudden encounter.

The chapter's boundary with its successors can be stated simply. Chapter 68 explains how the Portuguese came to be in Asian waters and what kind of power they exercised there, up to the point at which their fleet entered the Strait of Malacca. Chapter 69 analyzes the conquest of Melaka and its consequences for merchants and rulers, Chapter 70 examines Portuguese rule at Melaka and the struggle for the Straits, and Chapter 71 turns to the Malukan sultanates and the spice islands. Students should read these chapters as a continuing sequence, with the Indian Ocean base examined here as the foundation.

Section 68.20

Portuguese Expansion into Asian Waters: Historical Synthesis

By 1515 the Portuguese had established a durable presence in Asian waters, but it was not a comprehensive conquest. Their captains held armed coastal bases and could win major naval battles, while their king sought to turn a transoceanic enterprise into fiscal revenue. From Cochin and Goa to the approaches of Hormuz, the new network of fortified points offered protection, storage, and coercive leverage. It remained small in population, expensive to supply, and vulnerable to the decisions of neighboring rulers and merchants. The very institutions that made Portuguese power visible—fortresses, passes, royal factors, and fleets—revealed the limits of a state trying to govern maritime movement across thousands of kilometers.

The first Portuguese voyages depended on knowledge and infrastructure that Asians already possessed. They found pilots at East African ports, negotiated with the Zamorin and his rivals on the Malabar coast, relied on Gujarati and other merchants for commercial information, and repaired ships using local craftsmen and resources. Naval battles such as Chaul and Diu altered immediate balances of force but did not replace the ocean's older commercial circuits. The violent destruction of shipping and attacks on Calicut and Goa were consequential historical choices, not unavoidable side effects of trade. Different Asian courts had different incentives to resist, cooperate, negotiate, or circumvent the Portuguese.

Three distinctions clarify the chapter. Portuguese intention was greater than Portuguese capacity: claims to maritime monopoly did not amount to full enforcement. Political occupation differed from commercial reach: holding Goa or establishing a fort did not mean administering its inland hinterland or the whole Arabian Sea. And the arrival of ships from Lisbon was not the beginning of international Asian commerce: it was an attempt to intervene in networks that had been created over centuries. These distinctions make the history intelligible without either celebrating a supposedly inevitable European triumph or minimizing the new instruments of coercion and exchange.

Southeast Asia stood at the eastern edge of these experiments but was already connected to their consequences. Its spices, textiles, ports, and merchant communities formed part of the Indian Ocean system on which Portuguese plans depended. Reports from Indian harbors guided the decision to send Sequeira to Melaka in 1509; reports about the Portuguese could also circulate through Southeast Asian commercial networks. The detailed political and military history of the 1511 conquest belongs in Chapter 69. Chapter 68 closes at the threshold, with an established but contested Portuguese maritime power approaching a region whose rulers and merchants already had interests, institutions, and choices of their own.

Military: Could fleets defeat enemies in specific engagements? Yes, sometimes decisively; Chaul is the important counterexample.

Administrative: Could a small overseas apparatus maintain factories, garrisons, and revenue collection? At selected sites, with difficult supply and local cooperation.

Commercial: Did the royal monopoly and the cartaz bring every merchant under Portuguese control? No; regional circuits remained diverse and adaptable.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 68 has examined the entry of the Portuguese into Asian waters between the voyage of Vasco da Gama and the end of Afonso de Albuquerque's governorship. Its central argument is that the Portuguese entered an established, politically fragmented, and commercially sophisticated Indian Ocean system, and that their power, though real in particular respects, was shaped and limited by that system and by their own dependence on Asian assistance.

The historical record is uneven. Portuguese letters, journals, and chronicles are abundant but reflect royal and religious purposes, and many narrative details come from retrospective works composed decades later. Asian sources are fewer for these years but indispensable for understanding Asian responses, and historians must read across languages and genres. Distinguishing contemporary, retrospective, and polemical evidence is a necessary part of the reconstruction.

The Indian Ocean before 1498 consisted of overlapping circuits linked by monsoon winds, from East Africa and Arabia to Gujarat, Malabar, the Bay of Bengal, and Southeast Asia and China. Ports such as Calicut, Cambay, Hormuz, Aden, and Kilwa were governed by rulers who taxed and protected multiethnic merchant communities. No single navy controlled the ocean, and Southeast Asia, through its spices, pepper, rice, tin, and ports, was the eastern anchor of the system.

Portugal's expansion grew from fifteenth-century Atlantic voyages, crusading ideology, royal commercial ambitions, navigational experience, intelligence gathering such as Covilhã's mission, and financing from European merchant houses. The crown's enterprise was small in manpower and depended on annual fleets and forts. Da Gama's voyage of 1497 to 1499 reached Calicut with the help of a pilot obtained at Malindi, and it showed both the feasibility of the route and the difficulty of finding goods and goodwill at Calicut.

Cabral's violent confrontation with Calicut in 1500 and the alliance with Cochin established a pattern in which the Portuguese exploited local rivalries, and da Gama's second expedition, including the burning of the Miri, introduced a policy of coercion. Fortification at Cochin made the presence permanent. These years also showed the Portuguese dependence on local rulers and the entanglement that followed.

Portuguese military technology, including carracks, shipboard artillery, and artillery fortresses, gave real tactical advantages, but it was constrained by small numbers, disease, the annual rhythm of the Cape route, and dependence on Indian shipyards and supplies. Asian powers also used gunpowder weapons and adapted quickly. Technology was necessary but not sufficient.

Under Almeida, the Portuguese pursued sea control and a chain of forts, and the victory at Diu in 1509 over a Mamluk, Gujarati, and allied fleet established naval superiority in the Arabian Sea. Under Albuquerque, the capture of Goa in 1510 provided a territorial base, and the strategy of fortified key points extended to Melaka, Hormuz, and an unsuccessful attempt on Aden. The Estado da Índia was a network of points, not a contiguous territorial empire.

The crown aimed at a monopoly on the spice trade, managed through the Casa da Índia, and sought to turn naval power into revenue through the cartaz system. The monopoly was never complete, the Red Sea route recovered, and Portuguese officials often traded privately. The Portuguese increasingly participated in intra-Asian trade, since Europe offered few goods that Asian markets wanted, and Gujarati cloth was central to exchange for spices and gold.

Asian intermediaries, including pilots, brokers, shipbuilders, allied rulers, and Luso-Asian families, were indispensable. Asian responses varied by interest: Calicut and its Muslim merchants resisted, Cochin allied, Gujarat and the Mamluks fought and negotiated, and Hormuz and Swahili cities adapted differently. Violence, including the bombardment of Calicut, the burning of the Miri, and the killings at Goa, was a documented feature of the period, as were enslavement and the stresses of life on ships and in garrisons.

Southeast Asia was present in this story through the merchants, goods, and information that reached India, and the move toward Melaka grew from the strategy developed there. The chapter leaves the conquest of Melaka to Chapter 69. Historiographical debate between a transformation thesis and a continuity thesis, with attention to regional and chronological variation, remains unresolved, and students should treat the Portuguese entry as a significant intrusion into an enduring system, one in which Asian actors, including Southeast Asians, retained agency.

Key Terms

Carrack (nau)
A large, square-rigged, multi-deck ship used by the Portuguese for long-distance cargo and troop carrying and capable of mounting heavy guns.
Caravel
A smaller, maneuverable lateen-rigged vessel suited to exploration and scouting, developed in Atlantic voyages.
Feitoria
A trading post administered by a royal agent, where goods were stored and exchanged, sometimes later fortified.
Feitor
The Portuguese royal factor in charge of a feitoria and its accounts.
Cartaz
A pass issued by Portuguese authorities allowing an Asian ship to sail and trade in claimed waters, a fiscal and political instrument.
Estado da Índia
The collective name for the Portuguese crown's fortresses, fleets, and officials in Asia, headed from Goa, which developed gradually.
Casa da Índia
The royal institution in Lisbon that supervised the crown's Asian trade, shipments, and accounts.
Carreira da Índia
The annual fleet route between Lisbon and India around the Cape of Good Hope, timed to the monsoon.
Viceroy
A high royal representative ruling in the king's name; Almeida was the first Portuguese viceroy in India.
Monopoly
An exclusive claim to buy or sell a commodity, asserted by the Portuguese crown for spices and only partly realized.
Protection costs
An analytical concept referring to the expenses of providing security for trade, which the Portuguese attempted to convert into revenue through passes and fees.
Monsoon
Seasonal wind reversal that governed the timing of voyages across the Indian Ocean and beyond.
Entrepôt
A port whose prosperity derived from concentrating and redistributing goods, such as Hormuz or Melaka.
Zamorin
The title of the ruler of Calicut on the Malabar coast, the principal early opponent of the Portuguese.
Mamluk Sultanate
The regime ruling Egypt and Syria, which taxed the Red Sea spice trade and sent a fleet to India in 1508.
Mappila
A term for Muslim communities of the Malabar coast, prominent in overseas trade.
Casado
A married Portuguese settler in Asia, encouraged by Albuquerque to settle with a local wife and receive land and privileges.
Fidalgo
A Portuguese noble of lower or middling rank who sought rewards through overseas service.
Broker
An intermediary, often from an Asian merchant or religious community, who mediated between Portuguese factors and local producers and rulers.
Padrão
A stone pillar set up by Portuguese explorers to mark claims and landfalls.
Padroado
The Portuguese crown's right of patronage over the church in its overseas territories.
Roteiro
The Portuguese term for a navigational log; the Roteiro of da Gama's first voyage is a principal source for it.
Prester John
A legendary Christian ruler thought in medieval Europe to live in the East; the legend shaped Portuguese hopes for allies.
Treaty of Tordesillas
The 1494 agreement between Portugal and Castile dividing zones of exploration, whose eastern implications were later disputed.
Key-point strategy
Albuquerque's approach of holding fortified places that command entrances to major trade routes rather than extensive territory.
Luso-Asian
Describing communities and cultures arising from Portuguese settlement and intermarriage with local populations in Asia.
Retrospective source
Evidence composed after the events it describes, such as the chronicles of Barros and Correia.
Historiography
The history of historical interpretation, including debates over the significance of the Portuguese entry.
Tuhfat al-Mujahidin
A later sixteenth-century Arabic work from Kerala presenting a Malabar Muslim view of resistance to the Portuguese.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how a new kind of maritime power became part of the Asian world without either conquering it or being absorbed without trace. The Portuguese brought ships, artillery, a royal monopoly claim, and fortified bases, and they met an Indian Ocean whose merchants, rulers, pilots, and shipbuilders were capable of resisting, bargaining, and adapting. The outcome was a negotiated, unequal, and violent coexistence.

The processes it reveals extend well beyond the first fifteen years. The conversion of naval power into revenue through passes and fees, the reliance on local intermediaries and alliances, the use of fortified points along sea routes, and the tension between a crown's monopoly ambitions and the private interests of its agents would recur in later European enterprises in Asia, including those of the Dutch and the English (see Chapters 77 and 100). The Estado da Índia also provided a model, and a foil, for them.

Continuities are equally important. The monsoon calendar, the textile and spice exchange, the networks of Gujarati, Malabar, Persian, Arab, and Southeast Asian merchants, and the political fragmentation of the Indian Ocean littoral survived the Portuguese entry, as the following chapters on Melaka, Aceh, Johor, and the Straits will show. In Southeast Asia, rulers and merchants who had long managed foreign traders would test the Portuguese against their own experience.

Finally, the chapter contributes to the textbook's central argument that Southeast Asia and its neighbors were never passive recipients of outside power. The Portuguese reached Asian waters with the help of Asian pilots, built their bases with Asian labor, financed their trade partly through Asian markets, and learned of Melaka from Asian merchants. Understanding that dependence is essential to reading the conquest of Melaka in Chapter 69 not as the arrival of an unstoppable force but as an event in a contested and interconnected world.
