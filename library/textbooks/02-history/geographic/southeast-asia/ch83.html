Mataram and the Contest for Java

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

In the late summer of 1628, a Javanese army that had marched across the island from its rice-growing heartland reached the walls of Batavia. It had been assembled by a king who had spent fifteen years subduing rival Javanese rulers, and it was the largest instrument of royal power the island had seen in generations. It did not take the town.

That failure, repeated more decisively in 1629, is among the best-known episodes in the history of early modern Java, yet it is easily misunderstood. Told from the side of the Dutch East India Company (the VOC, examined in Chapters 77 and 79), it becomes a story of European fortifications and artillery holding off a numerically superior Asian force. Told from the side of later Javanese tradition, it becomes a chapter in the heroic biography of Sultan Agung, the ruler of Mataram. Neither version is wholly wrong, and neither is sufficient. The sieges of Batavia were the culmination of a larger struggle: a contest for the control of Java itself, in which the interior court of Mataram, the commercial cities of the northern coast, the sultanate of Banten, and the Company's fortified headquarters pursued incompatible conceptions of authority.

This chapter treats that contest as a political, agricultural, military, economic, religious, and cultural history. Mataram arose from the fertile plains of Central Java, south of Mount Merapi, where wet-rice cultivation supported dense rural populations whose labor and produce could be mobilized by a court. Between about the 1570s and 1613, a lineage descended from Ki Ageng Pemanahan and his son Sutawijaya, later known as Panembahan Senapati, converted a grant of land into a regional kingdom. Under Sultan Agung, who came to the throne in 1613, that kingdom subdued Surabaya, Tuban, Madura, and other eastern and coastal rivals, and made a bid to be recognized as the paramount power of the island. This is a story of conquest, but it is also a story of how conquest was administered, paid for, justified, and resisted.

The chapter's governing argument is that Mataram's rise rested on agricultural resources, royal authority, military mobilization, and the capacity to subordinate rival Javanese rulers, but that the same expansion generated resistance from coastal cities, exposed the limits of long-distance military logistics, and created tensions between court centralization and the interests of regional elites. The failed attacks on Batavia revealed the limits of military power. The crisis that followed Sultan Agung's reign showed how hard it was to sustain his achievements. Yet that later crisis was not an inevitable consequence of his reign; it also reflected the decisions of his successor, Amangkurat I, and the intervention of external actors.

Students should approach the chapter with several cautions. First, Mataram's expansion should not be equated with the unification of Java. Banten, Batavia, and parts of the east remained beyond effective royal control throughout. Second, Mataram's wealth came from people: farmers, laborers, soldiers, and village officials whose experiences are far less visible in the sources than those of kings and commanders. Third, the Dutch and the Javanese should not be treated as monolithic opponents. Batavia's defenders included Asian communities as well as Europeans, and Mataram's armies were composed of nobles and followers whose loyalty depended on negotiation. Fourth, Islamic kingship and Javanese court tradition were not opposites; Sultan Agung's reign is one of the principal places where historians study their interaction.

The evidence is uneven and must be read with care. Javanese court chronicles, above all the Babad Tanah Jawi, preserve dynastic memory but were composed and revised long after the events they describe, and they mingle political history with sacred narrative. Company records, including the daily journals of Batavia Castle and the reports of envoys such as Rijklof van Goens, provide dated, contemporary observation, but from the viewpoint of an adversary and often of a trader. Archaeological and heritage evidence, notably the royal cemetery at Imogiri, supplements both. The twentieth-century reconstruction of Sultan Agung's reign by H. J. de Graaf, and the broader syntheses of M. C. Ricklefs, remain essential, and they disagree on some details, including the year of Agung's death.

This chapter is the concluding chapter of Part XV. It closes with a comparative synthesis of the different forms of state power studied in Chapters 77 through 82: the corporate-state of the VOC, the Acehnese sultanate, the Malay successor world of Johor-Riau, and the Makassar of Gowa-Talloq. Mataram stands apart from these because its governing model depended heavily on the resources of the interior. When that model reached toward the coast, it came into direct contact, and eventually conflict, with merchants, port rulers, and the Company. Chapter 84 will turn from the archipelago to the mainland and the imperial state-building of Toungoo.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the geography and agriculture of Central Java supported the rise of Mataram without automatically producing political unity.
  2. Trace the emergence of Mataram from Pajang through Ki Ageng Pemanahan, Panembahan Senapati, and Panembahan Seda ing Krapyak.
  3. Analyze the ideology of Javanese kingship, including the titles panembahan, susuhunan, and sultan.
  4. Reconstruct the sequence of Sultan Agung's campaigns against eastern Java, Madura, and Surabaya, distinguishing military defeat from political incorporation.
  5. Assess why Mataram could defeat major rivals without permanently controlling all of Java.
  6. Describe the court, revenue system, and village society that sustained Mataram, and explain how war affected rural households.
  7. Evaluate the relationship between the inland court and the commercial and Islamic world of the pasisir.
  8. Compare the two sieges of Batavia in 1628 and 1629 and explain why each failed, weighing logistical, environmental, tactical, and political causes.
  9. Interpret Sultan Agung's religious policy and the 1633 calendar reform as evidence of interaction between Islamic authority and Javanese court culture.
  10. Explain the crisis under Amangkurat I and the role of the 1677 rebellion in deepening VOC intervention, without treating it as inevitable.
  11. Evaluate Javanese chronicles and Company records as sources, identifying the perspective and limitations of each.
  12. Compare Mataram's mode of state power with those of Aceh, Johor-Riau, Makassar, and the VOC.

Historical Context

By the late sixteenth century, the political landscape of Java had been transformed by the fragmentation of the older Hindu-Buddhist order and the rise of Muslim-ruled port polities. The decline of Majapahit (Chapter 52) was accompanied by the growth of coastal courts such as Demak, Jepara, Gresik, Tuban, and Surabaya, and by the Islamization of much of the northern coast (Chapter 65). Demak's brief ascendancy gave way to rivalry among coastal rulers, and the interior kingdom of Pajang, founded in the mid-sixteenth century, claimed the legacy of Demak's authority. Java's northern coastal strip, known in Javanese as the pasisir, remained a zone of commerce, shipbuilding, craft production, and Islamic scholarship, linked to Melaka's successors, to Makassar and the Spice Islands, and to Chinese and Indian Ocean merchants.

Meanwhile, the Dutch had arrived. Following the first Dutch voyages of the 1590s, the VOC was formed in 1602 (Chapter 77) and by 1619 had established a fortified headquarters at Batavia on the site of Jayakarta (Chapter 79). The Company sought to dominate the spice trade and to command key ports, an ambition that overlapped with the interests of Banten, the pepper-trading sultanate of western Java, and with the commercial interests of the pasisir. Its rise coincided with the contests among Aceh, Johor, and Makassar for control of the straits and eastern trade (Chapters 80 through 82).

Central Java's interior offered a contrasting model of power. The volcanic soils and rivers of the plains south of Mount Merapi supported rice agriculture on a scale that allowed rulers to draw on large rural populations. The ancient Hindu-Buddhist kingdoms of Central Java (Chapters 30 through 32) had rested on similar foundations. In the seventeenth century, an inland court could again mobilize agricultural wealth, but it now did so in a world where coastal commerce, Islamic institutions, and European firearms and ships shaped political possibilities. Mataram's story is therefore the story of an agrarian kingdom trying to command a commercial coastline.

This chapter assumes familiarity with these earlier developments but does not retell them. The VOC's institutions and ambitions belong to Chapters 77 and 79; the Acehnese, Johor-Riau, and Makassar experiences to Chapters 80 through 82. Later Javanese succession wars, the upheavals of the 1740s, and the Treaty of Giyanti of 1755 belong to later chapters and to the discussion of the eighteenth century in Chapter 93, and are not examined here.

The Rise of Mataram and the Foundations of Javanese Royal Power

Geography, political origins, succession, and royal authority

Section 83.1

Central Java, Agricultural Geography, and the Political Foundations of Mataram

The kingdom of Mataram took shape in a distinctive landscape. South of the volcano Mount Merapi, the land slopes gently toward the Indian Ocean across a plain watered by rivers, including the Opak and the Progo systems, whose volcanic sediments renew the soil. Rainfall is seasonal, concentrated in the monsoon months examined in Chapter 4, and rivers carry that water to fields that can be irrigated through channels and embankments. This was an environment in which wet-rice cultivation could support dense rural populations, and it had done so in the era of the Hindu-Buddhist kingdoms whose monuments, including Borobudur and Prambanan, stand nearby (Chapters 31 and 32). The seventeenth-century court did not create this agricultural base; it inherited it, and its power depended on the ability to claim a portion of the produce and the labor of those who worked it.

Agricultural productivity mattered politically because it concentrated people. A court situated among populous villages could recruit soldiers, laborers, and servants in numbers that smaller coastal ports could not easily match. Rice fed armies on the march and the households of the court. Yet the relationship between agriculture and political power was never automatic. Fertile land supported many rulers, not one, and the same plains could sustain competing lords whose followers cultivated adjacent districts. The central geographical argument of this section is that agricultural productivity supported military expansion and court government, but it did not by itself create political unity. Unity had to be constructed through warfare, marriage alliances, ceremonial hierarchy, and the distribution of land and office.

The interior was connected to the northern coast by routes that crossed the volcanic uplands and followed river valleys. Along the pasisir lay the ports of Jepara, Semarang, Tegal, Cirebon, Tuban, Gresik, and Surabaya, whose merchants traded rice, timber, cloth, and other goods across the archipelago and beyond. These communities had their own rulers, their own Islamic institutions, and their own commercial interests. For the court of Mataram, the coast was at once a source of revenue and imported goods, an avenue for diplomacy and military transport, and a potential rival. The tension between an inland court that drew wealth from fields and a coastline that drew wealth from ships recurs throughout the chapter.

It is useful to compare Mataram's foundations with the maritime-commercial systems examined in Chapters 80 through 82. Aceh under Iskandar Muda, Johor-Riau, and Makassar derived power chiefly from control of ports, shipping, and the exchange of commodities, though each also relied on hinterland production and manpower. Mataram reversed the emphasis: its core was agricultural, and its relationship to commerce was shaped by the effort to subordinate or tax it. That contrast should not be exaggerated. Java's pasisir was deeply commercial, and Mataram's rulers wanted access to trade. But the court's organizing principle was the command of land and people, and that shaped its strategies and its vulnerabilities.

Historians reconstruct this geography from several kinds of evidence. Chronicles describe places and campaigns but are uneven in precision. Company reports from the early seventeenth century, written by merchants and envoys who traveled between the coast and the interior, describe landscapes, routes, and settlements. Later colonial surveys and modern environmental and archaeological studies help to reconstruct irrigation and settlement. Each source carries its own limitations, and historians must avoid reading later landscapes back into the early seventeenth century without qualification.

The movement of food from fields to court was neither automatic nor costless. Paddy had to be harvested, dried, stored, measured, carried, and guarded, and each step required cooperation from village authorities and local nobles. A prosperous agricultural district could provide provisions for a campaign only if transport animals, porters, roads, and staging places were available at the right moment. Royal orders might allocate supplies, but a distant army still had to receive them before rice spoiled or the cultivators' own needs became urgent. This distinction between productive capacity and usable military supply is fundamental to understanding why Mataram's armies succeeded relatively near their heartland yet struggled during the Batavia expeditions.

The plains south of Merapi were not isolated from the coast. Timber and salt, iron tools and cloth, horses and weapons, information and visitors, all moved along routes joining villages, markets, religious establishments, and harbors. Markets were places where the court's demands encountered household decisions about labor, price, and risk. A village might benefit from better access to buyers while facing new demands for transport and forced service. Political integration could expand exchange and at the same time expose communities to arbitrary requisition or punishment. The chapter therefore distinguishes a king's ambition to command Java's resources from the varied consequences of such ambition for the people who produced those resources.

Environmental conditions also varied from year to year. Monsoon rains could facilitate wet-rice cultivation but make roads and stream crossings difficult; dry seasons could ease marching yet reduce available water. Eruptions, floods, local harvest failures, and warfare could alter the ability of districts to meet demands. Contemporary descriptions seldom measure these effects systematically, so precise claims about crop yields would be misleading. What can be explained securely is the mechanism: military power depended on households and landscapes whose capacity could not be commanded indefinitely. Mataram's geography generated opportunities that its rulers had to convert into durable political arrangements.

Section 83.2

From Pajang to Mataram: Ki Ageng Pemanahan and Panembahan Senapati

Mataram's origins lie in the politics of the later sixteenth century, when the kingdom of Pajang, in the interior of Central Java, claimed authority over the legacy of Demak. Pajang's ruler, remembered as Hadiwijaya, is said by tradition to have rewarded a loyal follower, Ki Ageng Pemanahan, with a grant of land in the district of Mataram. Pemanahan established a settlement at Kotagede, and under his leadership and that of his son Sutawijaya the settlement grew into a power in its own right. The sources for these events are principally dynastic traditions, and their chronology is imprecise: scholars differ on the dates of the grant, the foundation of Kotagede, and the fall of Pajang. The 1570s and 1580s are commonly used as approximate markers, and this chapter treats them as such.

Sutawijaya, later styled Panembahan Senapati, emerged as a military and political leader who first served and then eclipsed the rulers of Pajang. The details of his rise, which involved alliances, marriage connections, and conflict with rival claimants, are reconstructed from chronicles and later scholarship, and they should be read as probable reconstruction rather than established fact. What is clear in outline is that by the late sixteenth century Mataram had replaced Pajang as the dominant power in the interior and had begun to extend its authority over neighboring communities. Military power, alliance by marriage, and the cultivation of religious and ritual prestige were the instruments of this consolidation.

Mataram's ruling lineage also presented its origins in terms of sacred ancestry and divine favor. Dynastic tradition traced the family's descent to earlier royal houses, linked its founders to Islamic holy men, and represented Senapati as chosen by supernatural forces to rule. The most famous of these stories concerns Senapati's relationship with Nyai Roro Kidul, the supernatural Queen of the Southern Ocean, who in later tradition is said to have become a protector of the dynasty. Such stories were not mere decoration. They expressed a conception of kingship in which the ruler's authority derived from a relationship with powers beyond the human, and they helped to justify the dynasty's claim to rule.

Historians therefore use the Babad Tanah Jawi and related chronicles critically. The chronicle tradition, which survives in several recensions revised across generations, was composed to glorify the dynasty and to link it to a long line of Javanese kings. J. J. Ras's study of the genesis and function of the Babad Tanah Jawi is an important methodological foundation, showing how the chronicle was shaped by the interests of the courts that produced it. This does not make the chronicle useless. It preserves names, genealogical claims, and traditions that other sources lack, and it reveals how the dynasty wished to be remembered. But historians must distinguish what the text claims about miraculous events from what can be reconstructed from contemporary or independent evidence, and they must avoid treating later stories as reports of what happened.

The consolidation of Mataram under Senapati should be understood as one among several competing projects in late sixteenth-century Java. The coastal rulers of Surabaya, Demak, Gresik, and Tuban, the sultanate of Banten, and the remnants of Pajang's circle all had their own ambitions. Mataram's eventual dominance was not preordained. It depended on the particular skills and fortunes of its early leaders, on its agricultural base, and on the weaknesses of its rivals. Students should resist the temptation to read the story of Mataram's rise as an inevitable progression toward Sultan Agung's empire.

The older kingdom of Pajang did not disappear the moment Mataram began to grow. Its remaining followers, competing royal relatives, and allies in neighboring districts could support or resist Sutawijaya, depending on what they expected to gain. Dynastic succession was therefore a prolonged process of assembling recognition. Military victory could remove a particular rival without securing the obedience of every local notable. Marriage connected houses that had fought one another, while the distribution of honors and positions gave former opponents a reason to accept a new senior ruler. Such negotiations are difficult to reconstruct because later chronicles prefer a tidy sequence of heroic deeds and divinely ordained success.

Kotagede illustrates how political authority became visible. A settlement associated with a ruling family acquired courts, places of worship, markets, residences for important followers, and burial places that tied authority to ancestors. These were not merely ornaments: they made regular gatherings and the exchange of gifts possible, offered locations for dispute resolution, and enabled the ruler to communicate rank to visitors. The construction and maintenance of a court required materials and labor from surrounding districts, making local resources part of the political story. Archaeological remains can establish the existence of a significant settlement even when stories about particular royal ceremonies remain harder to verify.

The story of the Queen of the Southern Ocean is especially useful if treated as evidence of political imagination rather than a factual account of a meeting. It attached Senapati's family to a powerful sacred geography and created a language in which the dynasty could claim authority beyond its immediate military reach. That does not mean every subject accepted the story in the same way. Coastal Muslim scholars, rival princes, and farmers might have understood royal legitimacy through different commitments. The historian's task is to reconstruct the coexistence of these claims, not to adjudicate supernatural events. Mataram's emergence was political change narrated through both material conflict and sacred history.

Section 83.3

Panembahan Seda ing Krapyak and Mataram before Sultan Agung, 1601–1613

Senapati's successor, known by the posthumous name Panembahan Seda ing Krapyak, ruled from about 1601 until 1613. His reign is poorly documented compared with that of his son, and historians reconstruct it chiefly from chronicles and later Dutch reports. Its importance lies in showing that Mataram's expansion was already underway, and that the kingdom Sultan Agung inherited was expanding but politically contested rather than a unified empire. Disputed succession, family rivalries, and provincial resistance marked the period, and the new ruler had to establish his authority over relatives and subordinate lords as well as over external enemies.

The reign was also a time of mounting pressure on eastern Java. Mataram's rulers sought to extend their authority over the central and eastern plains and over the coastal settlements of the north. This brought them into conflict with Surabaya, the strongest and most commercially important polity in the east, whose rulers led coalitions of eastern lords against Mataram's advances. Demak, Gresik, Jepara, and other coastal settlements stood within this widening regional conflict, and their loyalties, ambitions, and resources affected the balance. Fighting in these years did not produce a decisive result, and Surabaya remained the principal rival.

Kotagede, the early center, developed during these years as a political and ceremonial capital. Its development depended on the court's capacity to draw labor and supplies from surrounding districts, and it expressed the dynasty's aspirations in its layout, its rituals, and its sacred places. The reign also saw the first sustained European contacts with Mataram's sphere. In 1613 the VOC established a trading post at Jepara, a significant coastal port within the orbit of Mataram's ambitions. The post reflected the Company's interest in rice and other goods, and it introduced a new actor into a political landscape that would soon be transformed by Sultan Agung's expansion.

The death of Seda ing Krapyak in 1613 is said by tradition to have occurred in the course of a hunting expedition at Krapyak, which gave rise to his posthumous name. The circumstances are known mainly from chronicles. His death created a succession problem that illustrates the instability of the kingdom: the throne passed not to the eldest son but to Pangeran Rangsang, the future Sultan Agung, a decision that reflected court politics and the balance among factions. The next section examines how Agung secured his position.

The court's succession problem involved more than selecting a son. Leading relatives had armed followings, local landholding connections, and relationships with religious advisers and provincial officials. An heir acceptable in the palace could be opposed in districts where another claimant promised greater autonomy or reward. To establish himself, a ruler needed public recognition at court and practical compliance in the countryside. This helps explain why internal conflict and campaigns against external enemies so often overlapped: success abroad could give a new king the prestige and resources necessary to discipline powerful dependents at home.

The north-coast ports were not simply territories awaiting annexation. Their rulers mediated shipping and supplied goods unavailable in the interior, and their merchants had contacts stretching beyond Java. Jepara in particular connected Mataram's growing sphere with foreign traders and with the movement of timber, rice, and other commodities. The VOC's early trading interest there should not be mistaken for stable Dutch control of the region. Company factors operated through local permissions and relationships, often amid insecurity. They were commercial agents entering an already competitive Javanese political world, not officials administering a Dutch colony along the entire coast.

Continuity across the reigns of Senapati, Seda ing Krapyak, and Agung should also be qualified. The same family line ruled, but the geographic extent of its effective authority and the opportunities available to it changed considerably. A new king inherited not only claims but also promises made to supporters, enemies who remembered past wars, and neighbors capable of forming coalitions. For the historian, this explains why lists of successive kings or conquered districts cannot substitute for analysis of how allegiance worked. The problems Sultan Agung confronted in 1613 had roots in these unresolved relationships.

Historical Vocabulary
Panembahan, Susuhunan, Sultan

Definition. These are three royal titles used by the rulers of Mataram at different times. Panembahan, from a root associated with reverence and homage, was used by Senapati and by his successor. Susuhunan, often rendered "he to whom homage is made," came into use for Sultan Agung in the 1620s according to the usual reconstruction. Sultan, an Arabic-derived title of Muslim rulership, was taken by Agung in 1641 following an embassy to Mecca.

Historical context. Royal titles in Java were not merely labels; they expressed claims about the ruler's status and relationship to other rulers and to the sacred. The shift from panembahan to susuhunan to sultan reflected both the growth of Mataram's power and the evolving relationship between Javanese court culture and Islamic ideas of rulership. The sources do not always agree on exactly when each title came into use, and Dutch and Javanese documents may use different forms for the same ruler.

Why it matters. Titles help historians trace how Mataram's rulers wished to be seen, and they signal changing ideas of legitimacy. The adoption of the title sultan was a claim to a form of authority recognized across the Islamic world.

Potential misunderstanding. Students should not assume that the title sultan replaced older Javanese conceptions of kingship. The titles overlapped and coexisted, and Sultan Agung is often referred to by the name "Agung" regardless of the title in use at a particular moment.

Section 83.4

Sultan Agung's Accession and the Ideology of Javanese Kingship

Pangeran Rangsang, who became known as Sultan Agung, took the throne in 1613. Born in the early 1590s according to the usual reconstruction, he was a young ruler who inherited a kingdom with ambitious military and political objectives. His dynastic connections, through his father's lineage and his marriage ties, helped to secure his position, but he also had to establish his authority over relatives, military commanders, and provincial lords. The early years of his reign were devoted to this task as well as to campaigns against external rivals, and the two objectives were connected: successful campaigns brought prestige, booty, and opportunities to reward followers.

Javanese kingship rested on a set of ideas about legitimacy that historians reconstruct from chronicles, court ceremonial, and the testimony of visitors. The ruler was expected to possess sacred power, manifested in success and in the order of the realm. Divine protection, ancestry, and the possession of sacred heirlooms were signs of legitimacy. Authority was expressed through court hierarchy and ceremony: subordinate rulers and officials were expected to attend the court, to offer tribute, and to render ceremonial submission. A defeated enemy might be incorporated through marriage and through appointment to office. Such practices created a political order in which authority was personal and relational rather than purely territorial.

Foreign merchants and envoys were also expected to recognize the ruler's superior status. Dutch and other visitors found themselves caught up in ceremonial expectations that they did not always understand or accept, and disputes over precedence and form were often disputes about sovereignty. This is an important background to the conflict with the VOC, which is examined in Division IV. For Sultan Agung, the recognition of his paramountcy by Javanese lords and by outside powers was an essential element of kingship. The conquest of Java was therefore a central objective: the ruler's legitimacy required that other rulers acknowledge his superiority.

Interpreting these ideas is not straightforward. Court histories represent rulers as possessing both political authority and supernatural power, and they do not distinguish between the two as modern readers might. Historians therefore face the problem of reading chronicles that describe miraculous events alongside real campaigns. The approach adopted here is to treat such descriptions as evidence of how the court understood and presented royal authority, while seeking independent evidence for events whenever possible. The chronicles are most valuable as evidence of ideology and memory; Company records and other contemporary sources are more reliable for specific dates and military operations.

A royal audience was simultaneously a political meeting and a performance of hierarchy. The placement of officials, the manner of approach, forms of address, and the presentation of gifts could all signal whether a visitor had accepted a ruler's seniority. This helps explain why diplomatic misunderstandings were sometimes difficult to resolve with a straightforward commercial bargain. A VOC representative might understand a gift as an exchange between partners; the court might understand the same act as tribute acknowledging a superior. Neither interpretation was merely linguistic. The ceremonial dispute affected the political status that each side believed a treaty or trading arrangement would confirm.

Royal power was also delegated through persons. A governor acting in a distant district could not be supervised as closely as an official at court. The sovereign therefore relied on ties of kinship, hostages, marriages, appointments, and displays of favor or punishment. These instruments could generate rapid mobilization, but they exposed the court to danger when a respected commander died or a provincial house withdrew its support. Power was not weaker merely because it was personal; it was powerful in particular situations and brittle in others. The vocabulary of absolute kingship needs to be tested against the negotiated work of governance.

The adoption of increasingly exalted titles belongs to this setting. In a political world where conquered rulers might retain subordinate positions, the ruler of Mataram required a language of rank that could accommodate hierarchy without admitting equality. The later use of Islamic titles extended rather than simply replaced older Javanese understandings of sovereignty. Students should distinguish the historical date at which a title appears in particular sources from the date later dynastic narratives assign to it. A title is a clue to changing claims, not proof that every subject or foreign government accepted them.

The Expansion of Mataram and the Conquest of Java

Campaigns, coastal rivals, Surabaya, and the limits of expansion

Section 83.5

Sultan Agung's Early Campaigns and the Subjugation of Eastern Java, 1613–1619

Sultan Agung's early reign was dominated by warfare against the rulers of eastern and north-central Java. The reconstruction of these campaigns rests mainly on chronicles and on H. J. de Graaf's reading of them alongside Dutch reports, and the details vary among sources. The broad pattern is clear: Mataram took the offensive against a coalition of eastern lords led by Surabaya, attacked settlements on the margins of its sphere, and sought to deny Surabaya and its allies the resources of the interior.

Campaigns began in 1614 and continued in the following years. In 1615 Mataram captured Wirasaba, an inland settlement in the east. In 1616 fighting took place near Pajang, where Mataram's forces confronted an eastern coalition; the encounter, often associated with Siwalan, is usually presented as a victory for Mataram that checked the coalition's advance. Subsequent years saw pressure on other inland and coastal settlements, including Lasem and Pasuruan according to the usual reconstruction. In 1619 Mataram captured Tuban, an important coastal port, extending its reach to the northern coastline for the first time on a significant scale. The dating of individual operations is less secure than the overall sequence, and students should treat specific years as the conventional reconstruction.

The armies that conducted these campaigns were not standing forces in the modern sense. They were assembled by summoning subordinate nobles, who brought retinues drawn from their lands, and by recruiting laborers and followers from villages. Commanders were drawn from the court and from provincial elites, and their loyalty depended on the rewards of victory and the ruler's ability to enforce obligations. Strategic river crossings, supply routes, and fortified positions shaped operations. Armies depended on rice and other provisions drawn from the territories they passed through, from stores accumulated in advance, and from the labor of porters and boatmen. Firearms were present, but campaigns turned largely on the control of food, water, and strongholds.

An important distinction is between the destruction of a rival's stronghold and lasting political incorporation. A raid might burn settlements, seize captives, and carry off valuables without producing a durable change in political authority. Incorporation required installing loyal officials, relocating or co-opting local elites, and ensuring that the district contributed revenue and service. Many of Mataram's early victories were of the first kind; the second took years and was never complete. The capture of Tuban and the other towns extended the court's reach but did not by itself bring the commercial communities of the coast under reliable control.

The fighting of the 1610s unfolded through a series of campaigns rather than a single advance across an empty map. Rulers east of the Mataram core could cooperate when they perceived a common danger, yet they also disagreed about leadership and strategy. The defeat of a coalition near Siwalan in 1616, in the historical reconstruction used by de Graaf and Pigeaud, shows the importance of routes and coordination: an army assembled from several polities could suffer if it marched through unfamiliar territory or failed to maintain mutual support. The episode also warns against reading a sequence of battles backward as an inevitable story of Mataram's success.

Victories over Wirasaba and later coastal or near-coastal centers weakened Surabaya's wider support system. Lasem and Pasuruan figure in the campaigning of approximately 1616–1617, while Tuban's capture in 1619 removed an important coastal stronghold. But the dates and circumstances of some encounters depend on later Javanese narratives checked against Dutch records; the chapter should not assign false precision to every raid or surrender. More consequential than a single place-name was the cumulative alteration of alliances. Each defeat made neighboring rulers reassess whether defending Surabaya, negotiating with Mataram, or withholding help promised the best survival.

The logistics of these early campaigns differed from those of the Batavia assaults. Mataram could draw on districts recently brought under its command and exploit shorter routes between its agricultural base and the eastern plains. Even so, victory exposed soldiers and rural households to serious costs. Moving an army meant collecting rice, transporting equipment, and keeping contingents together despite the agricultural calendar. A defeated town might face confiscation or relocation, while a subordinate chief might retain his position in return for providing men and produce. Such uneven outcomes explain why conquest could increase the court's prestige without immediately producing administrative uniformity.

Section 83.6

Madura, the North Coast, and the Struggle for Maritime Access, 1620–1624

Having reached the coast at Tuban, Mataram turned to the control of maritime access. The north coast mattered for several reasons. It supplied revenue through port dues and trade, it offered harbors for transporting troops and provisions, it carried diplomatic communications, and it was the base of the merchants and rulers who might ally with Mataram's enemies. The court's agricultural heartland could produce rice in quantity, but the export of rice and the supply of imported goods depended on the ports. Mataram's ambitions therefore extended to the control of maritime commerce, even though its core was agricultural.

The island of Madura, lying across a narrow strait from Surabaya, was a strategic prize. Its rulers were allied with Surabaya, and its manpower and harbors supported the eastern coalition. Mataram campaigned against Madura in 1624 and conquered it, an operation that required the transport of forces across water and the siege of fortified settlements. The campaign removed the ruling elites of Madura from independent power. Chronicles and later tradition record that members of Madura's ruling family were brought to Mataram's court and that one of them, Prasena, was installed as a subordinate ruler under the name Cakraningrat. Such arrangements illustrate a common pattern: conquest could involve the removal or relocation of local rulers and their reinstallation as dependents of the court.

Other consequences of conquest affected ordinary communities. Campaigns involved destruction of crops and settlements, the seizure of captives, and the relocation of populations to serve the court or to repopulate devastated districts. Military obligations were imposed on subordinate districts, and the independence of commercial communities was restricted. Mataram attempted to control shipping and to extract revenue from ports, which brought it into tension with merchants who preferred the autonomy and low dues characteristic of open ports. These tensions between inland political priorities and coastal commercial interests are central to the conflicts that followed.

The conquest of coastal and island territories did not turn Mataram into a maritime power in the sense of Aceh or Makassar. The court lacked a large naval tradition, and its operations on the sea depended on the ships and sailors of coastal allies and subjects. Its conquests nevertheless gave it a coastline and the potential for a role in maritime trade, and they set the stage for the confrontation with the Company over the control of that coastline. The next section turns to the greatest of these coastal conquests.

Madura mattered because Surabaya was a maritime as well as a land power. Relations across the narrow strait sustained movement of people, provisions, ships, and political allies. An attack on Madura could therefore limit the assistance that Surabaya could expect during a siege. The campaign of 1624 was costly, involving resistance among island communities whose political loyalties were not identical. Court traditions remember the eventual accommodation of the noble Prasena, associated with the later Cakraningrat lineage. The historical details of his status and the timing of the title's use require care, but the underlying pattern is clear: defeating a rival was followed by an effort to turn part of the defeated elite into a dependent instrument of royal government.

Mataram's military strategy also sought to reshape the coastal economy. A port controlled by a loyal ruler might provide shipping, imported weapons, customs revenue, and routes for communication; a hostile port might supply the enemy. Capturing ports was not the same thing as operating them profitably. Merchants could leave, ships could choose alternative anchorages, and the destruction of warehouses or boats could reduce the revenue available to the victor. Officials responsible for the coast had to reconcile the king's demands for control with the practical need to attract trade. The tension between command and commercial flexibility would remain a recurring problem.

The campaign also reveals the limits of using modern territorial labels. A court might impose service on an island's leading families while permitting them to administer their own districts. Other communities could escape direct supervision or preserve ties with outside merchants. Political maps that color all of Madura as completely incorporated after 1624 conceal these differences. A stronger historical account asks what forms of tribute, military obligation, marriage connection, and local authority followed the expedition. This is the same distinction between victory and integration that matters for Surabaya in the following section.

Section 83.7

The Conquest of Surabaya in 1625

Surabaya was the strongest rival Mataram faced in Java. Located on the eastern end of the northern coast, near the mouth of a river that gave access to a populous hinterland, it was a commercial port, a fortified political center, and the leader of a network of relationships across eastern Java and the surrounding maritime world. Its rulers commanded the loyalty of lords in the east and maintained links with Madura, Giri, and other powers. For Sultan Agung, the conquest of Surabaya was the key to the subordination of eastern Java.

Mataram's campaign against Surabaya was a sustained effort rather than a single assault. After years of fighting against its allies, and after the conquest of Madura in 1624, Mataram applied military pressure on the city and its hinterland. Historians reconstructing the campaign emphasize siege warfare, blockade, and the disruption of agriculture and water supply. Mataram's forces are reported to have devastated the surrounding countryside to cut off provisions and to have interfered with the river on which the city depended for water. Hunger and disease, which affected both besieged and besiegers, added to the pressure. The city capitulated in 1625, after a prolonged period of hardship. The precise details of the siege, including the methods used to cut off water and the numbers involved, derive mainly from chronicle tradition and later reconstruction, and should be treated with caution.

The consequences for Surabaya's rulers and population were severe. The city's ruling family was brought under Mataram's authority, and many inhabitants suffered displacement and loss. Yet Mataram did not simply obliterate the Surabayan elite. Following a pattern seen elsewhere, it used dynastic marriage and political accommodation to incorporate members of the former ruling family into its own order. A son of Surabaya's ruler, Pangeran Pekik, is remembered in tradition as having been brought to the court and married to a member of Agung's family, an arrangement that tied the defeated house to the victor. The conquest and the accommodation were two parts of the same process.

The chapter must therefore distinguish Surabaya's military defeat from the longer process of political integration. Capitulation in 1625 ended the city's independence, but it did not instantly transform eastern Java into a loyal province. Local elites had to be reconciled or replaced, commercial networks adjusted to Mataram's demands, and the population induced to supply revenue and service. The conquest strengthened Mataram enormously, removing its principal eastern rival, but it also extended the kingdom's reach into regions where its administrative capacity was limited, and where resistance could re-emerge, as the events of the next years showed.

Surabaya's ability to resist Mataram rested on more than its walls. It commanded an urban population, commerce, connections with other coastal states, and access to provisions arriving by river and sea. For several years, Mataram's expeditions had tried to weaken these advantages without compelling surrender. Contemporary and later narratives give differing emphases to the individual operations, but together they suggest a process of progressive isolation. The conquest of Tuban and the pressure on Madura narrowed Surabaya's regional support. Mataram could now focus resources on a port whose defeat would transform the political balance of eastern Java.

The final campaign in 1625 is often described through a combination of blockade, attacks on supplies, and interference with water. Traditions concerning diversion or contamination of water should be attributed to the historical accounts in which they appear, rather than converted into an exact reconstruction of every action. What matters analytically is that a fortified city depends on resources beyond its defensive perimeter. Even when an attacking army cannot storm the walls, it may threaten harvests, transport routes, and the health of inhabitants. For defenders, prolonged resistance creates a difficult balance between preserving sovereignty and accepting mounting suffering among civilians. A political surrender therefore cannot be understood solely as the outcome of a battlefield encounter.

The aftermath was no less significant than the siege. Surabaya's rulers and leading families possessed relationships that could be dangerous if left intact as independent centers of resistance, but useful if incorporated into Mataram's hierarchy. The Surabayan prince Pangeran Pekik subsequently lived at Mataram and entered a marriage alliance with Sultan Agung's family, according to the reconstruction of de Graaf and Pigeaud. This arrangement linked formerly rival houses while also keeping an influential figure within the royal center's political reach. Marriage was not a sentimental reconciliation; it could secure loyalty, establish claims across lineages, and give descendants a stake in the order created by conquest.

For merchants and ordinary residents, the consequences were mixed and often difficult to measure. Some commercial routes survived because rulers still needed imported goods, ships, and coastal revenue. Others were disrupted by warfare, loss of autonomy, or new demands for tribute. Rural settlements near the city could suffer from requisitioning and failed harvests; people displaced by campaigns could carry specialized skills elsewhere. Surabaya's fall made Mataram the strongest political power in eastern Java, but it did not eliminate local identities or guarantee the court's command over the entire maritime economy. This distinction is essential to explaining why coastal tensions continued after 1625.

Section 83.8

Pati, Giri, Blambangan, and the Limits of Javanese Unification

The conquest of Surabaya did not end resistance. In 1627 a rebellion associated with Pati, a settlement on the north coast, challenged Mataram's authority. Pati's ruler had long enjoyed a degree of autonomy, and the rebellion reflected resentment at the extension of royal control. Mataram suppressed it, but the episode illustrated that the kingdom's authority rested on forcibly bringing together units that retained their own interests and traditions. Defeating a revolt did not automatically restore the loyalty of those who had joined it.

Giri, near Surabaya, represented a different kind of challenge. Its rulers were religious leaders whose authority derived from a lineage of Islamic teachers and whose influence extended widely among coastal communities. Giri's spiritual prestige gave it standing that military conquest could not easily replace. Mataram subordinated Giri in the mid-1630s, an operation usually dated to 1635–1636, but the process involved negotiation with and accommodation of its religious authority rather than simple destruction. Giri's importance reminds us that religious and political authority were intertwined on the coast, and that the ruler of Mataram needed the acquiescence of religious elites as well as military victories.

At the eastern extremity of Java lay Blambangan, a region with close connections to the Balinese kingdom of Gelgel across the narrow strait. Mataram campaigned there during the 1630s, in operations usually dated to roughly 1635–1640, and gained a measure of success, but it did not establish durable control. Terrain, distance, the difficulty of provisioning armies, and Balinese assistance to local rulers combined to limit what could be achieved. Blambangan remained a contested frontier long after Sultan Agung's reign, and later chapters and later Javanese history show that its status remained unresolved.

These episodes demonstrate why Mataram could defeat important rivals without permanently controlling all of Java. Several factors worked against complete unification. Terrain, including forests, mountains, and rivers, made movement and supply difficult. Distance from the core stretched the court's capacity to administer and supervise. Political loyalty remained local, and subordinate lords often retained their own followings. Religious authorities such as Giri could command allegiance independently of the court. And regional rulers could seek external assistance, as Blambangan did from Bali, or later as Banten did from the VOC and other partners. Territorial expansion generated its own limitations: each new conquest extended the frontier that had to be defended and administered.

Banten in the west remained entirely beyond Mataram's reach, and Batavia was soon to challenge its ambitions directly. By the end of the 1620s, Mataram was the dominant power in Central and eastern Java, but "Java" remained a plural political space. Students should therefore resist the common summary that Sultan Agung "unified Java." He subordinated many rulers, imposed a degree of hierarchy, and created a larger realm than any since Majapahit, but his authority was uneven, contested, and dependent on constant effort.

Pati illustrates a special danger of expansion: opposition could emerge inside the political structure created by earlier victories and marriage alliances. Its ruling house was connected to Mataram by kinship, yet attachment to the court did not extinguish claims to autonomy or grievances over subordination. The conflict in 1627 therefore showed that a royal marriage could moderate rivalry without ending it. Later narratives of the campaign sometimes emphasize punishment and devastation. Even if the scale is difficult to establish precisely, the political message was unmistakable: regional lords could not safely assume that past cooperation protected them from coercion when their allegiance was questioned.

Giri presented a different challenge because the authority of its religious leadership was not reducible to military territory. A ruler might conquer fortifications yet still need the recognition of people who looked to Giri for religious learning and prestige. The struggle culminating around 1636 should therefore be examined as an encounter between overlapping forms of authority. Court traditions describe the involvement of Surabayan nobles in the reduction of Giri, a reminder that erstwhile rivals might become instruments of Mataram's subsequent campaigns. Yet religious influence could persist under altered political conditions; conquest did not simply erase local networks of teachers, students, and patrons.

Blambangan was even harder to integrate. Campaigns in 1635 and later renewed operations through roughly 1639–1640 crossed difficult terrain and encountered resistance linked to Bali. Some sources present a sweeping conquest, but durable submission is much less certain. The ability of local rulers to retreat, reform alliances, and exploit distance from the center prevented a single successful expedition from settling the frontier. In historical interpretation, this matters more than assigning an unqualified date on which the entire eastern salient supposedly became Mataram territory. The kingdom's military reach was broad; its capacity to turn distant operations into stable government was variable.

Section 83.9

The Kraton, Royal Ceremonial, and the Organization of Government

The court, or kraton, was the political, ceremonial, and administrative center of Mataram. It was a residence, a ritual space, and a node of communication through which royal commands flowed outward and tribute and petitions flowed inward. Kotagede, the early seat, remained an important sacred and political site. During Sultan Agung's reign a new royal center was developed at Karta, a short distance away, reflecting the growth of the court and the king's desire for a seat suited to his enlarged ambitions. The later palace complex at Plered belongs principally to the reign of Amangkurat I and should not be projected back onto Agung's court.

Around the ruler stood a hierarchy of princes, court nobles, military commanders, regional governors, messengers, and officials entrusted with royal orders. Titles such as pangeran for princes and tumenggung and adipati for leading officials and governors indicated rank, and positions were distributed as favors. The court was a place where status was made visible: seating, dress, order of approach, and forms of address signaled each person's standing. Royal audiences and ceremonies were occasions on which allegiance was renewed and appointments announced. The distribution of offices, titles, and revenue assignments bound followers to the ruler and made the court the focal point of ambition.

Government in this sense was less a bureaucracy in the modern sense than a network of personal relationships maintained by the ruler. Messengers carried orders to governors and commanders; governors reported, sent tribute, and supplied men and provisions when summoned. The ruler's ability to command depended on his capacity to reward and punish and on the willingness of provincial elites to cooperate. Royal authority could be formidable, as the campaigns of Agung's reign demonstrated, yet it remained dependent on negotiated relationships with provincial elites. A ruler who alienated powerful lords, or who failed to provide victories and rewards, risked defection. The tensions inherent in this system later contributed to the crises under his successor, though they did not make those crises inevitable.

Sources for the organization of the court include chronicles, Dutch reports, and later accounts. Because Dutch envoys left their most detailed descriptions of ceremony in the 1640s and 1650s, after Agung's death, historians must be cautious about attributing details to his court. Visitors recorded the elaborate ceremony of audience and the prostrate posture of subjects, but they interpreted what they saw in terms of their own expectations. The chronicles, for their part, present an idealized picture. A balanced reconstruction draws on both while recognizing the limits of each.

The court organized authority through access. Those permitted to approach the ruler, deliver petitions, supervise ceremonies, or communicate orders held advantages that were not always expressed in formal job titles. Officials could translate a royal command into a demand for rice, labor, or men from a particular district. By controlling how information reached the sovereign, they could also shape the court's understanding of provincial conditions. A powerful monarch thus depended on intermediaries whose cooperation was indispensable even when royal ideology portrayed them as subordinate servants. Their rivalries and ambitions formed part of the political machinery, not merely personal intrigue.

The placement of the royal center mattered as well. Kotagede carried the memory of the dynasty's founding; Karta became associated with Sultan Agung's more developed court; and Plered was a later undertaking of Amangkurat I. These centers had practical as well as symbolic functions. They provided rooms and open spaces for audiences, accommodation for courtiers and guards, markets for provisioning, and focal points for roads linking provincial districts. Their construction and maintenance demanded skilled artisans, timber, brick, and food from the countryside. Each relocation reconfigured the distribution of opportunity among suppliers, officials, and surrounding villages.

Government also worked through ceremony. Attendance at court reaffirmed hierarchy, but it brought administrators together so that instructions, complaints, and marriages could be negotiated in person. Punishments publicly displayed the consequences of disobedience; gifts and appointments rewarded service. Neither form of power guaranteed that every district would comply. A distant governor might conceal shortages, negotiate with neighboring elites, or interpret an order according to local conditions. The resulting mixture of personal authority and practical delegation made the realm governable without resembling a modern bureaucracy of fixed territorial departments.

Section 83.10

Land, Tribute, Taxation, and the Political Economy of Mataram

The wealth of Mataram's court rested on agricultural land and the labor of those who worked it. The ruler's claim to revenue took several forms: the produce of royal domains, the assignment of rights over districts to nobles and officials, tribute from subordinate rulers and ports, and obligations of labor and military service. These arrangements were not a uniform system of taxation applied by a standardized bureaucracy. They varied across districts and over time, and the terms used to describe them had changing meanings. Historians reconstruct them from chronicles, Dutch reports, and later colonial-era descriptions, and must be cautious about projecting later practices onto the early seventeenth century.

One central mechanism was the assignment of revenue rights, commonly called lungguh, to princes, officials, and commanders. Rather than receiving salaries, such persons were allotted the right to draw income from specified districts, and the peasants of those districts owed produce and service to the holder. This tied the court's functionaries to the land and gave them an interest in the prosperity and control of their assignments. Village-level officials, often called bekel, were responsible for collecting dues and organizing labor. Regional governors, bupati, administered larger districts. The unit of assessment, often connected with the household or working family and called cacah in later usage, served as a basis for calculating obligations.

Royal revenue supported the households of the court, soldiers on campaign, building projects, and political patronage. Campaigns required stored rice, porters, and craftsmen; construction at Karta, Kotagede, and Imogiri required labor and materials; and the maintenance of loyalty depended on gifts and appointments. The revenue system was therefore intimately connected to military and political capacity. Because it depended on the collection of produce rather than a monetized system, the court's resources were tied to harvests and to the state of the countryside.

Students should distinguish royal claims to revenue from the amount the court could actually extract from particular districts. A ruler might assert a right to a share of the harvest, but the real yield depended on the productivity of the land, the distance from the court, the loyalty of intermediaries, and the degree to which officials retained a portion for themselves. Warfare and rebellion could interrupt collection. Recent historical studies emphasize the relationship among Mataram's territorial administration, tax collection, and court expenditure, and they show that fiscal capacity was a central constraint on expansion. A kingdom that conquered more territory than it could administer risked overextension.

The extraction of agricultural revenue depended on categories of people and obligations that do not map neatly onto modern property taxation. A land grant or appanage was often valuable because it entitled an officeholder to particular payments or services from cultivators, not because it conveyed unrestricted private ownership of every field. Court titles and assignments changed over time; terms such as lungguh, cacah, bekel, and bupati should be explained in relation to the setting in which a source uses them. A numerical assessment of households, for example, might serve political bargaining or military mobilization as much as an exact population count. Reading later colonial records backward can create an illusion of institutional precision not supported for Sultan Agung's reign.

Revenue collection created several layers of negotiation. A village headman could know which households had crops ready, which needed help repairing irrigation, and which were absent because of war. A provincial lord could seek to satisfy the court while protecting the resources that sustained his own household and retinue. An official who over-collected risked flight or resistance; one who under-delivered risked punishment from the center. The court's capacity therefore rested not only on royal commands but also on cooperation, bargaining, and repeated assessments of what the countryside could bear.

Trade and agricultural taxation were connected. Coastal markets offered outlets for produce and sources of imported goods, while customs revenue and merchant gifts could supplement agrarian claims. An expansive campaign could temporarily enlarge the royal income from land, ports, and subordinate lords while increasing military costs and administrative strain. The fiscal effects of conquest were therefore not uniformly positive. Students should distinguish gross resources theoretically available within a conquered district from the net food, money, and labor that actually reached the royal center after local expenses, transport losses, and negotiated exemptions.

Historical Vocabulary
Lungguh, Cacah, Bekel, and Bupati

Definition. Lungguh refers to land or revenue rights assigned to a prince or official as maintenance for service (often called an appanage). Cacah denotes a unit of population, often a household or working family, used for assessing obligations of produce or labor. Bekel refers to a local official who collected dues and organized labor in villages or small districts. Bupati means a regional governor or regent.

Historical context. These terms appear in sources from the seventeenth century onward, but their exact meanings and the details of practice changed over time and varied by region. Many of the most detailed descriptions come from later periods, including the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, and cannot be assumed to describe Sultan Agung's reign precisely.

Why it matters. These terms point to a political economy in which rights over land and people, rather than salaries and a monetized treasury, bound the court and its servants together.

Potential misunderstanding. Appanage holders did not "own" land in the modern sense, and the assigned revenue was not guaranteed. The system could be reorganized by the ruler, and the holders' actual income depended on their control of the district.

Section 83.11

Rice Fields, Irrigation, Villages, and Everyday Life

Behind the politics of kings and commanders lay the lives of agricultural households. Most people in Mataram's heartland lived in villages and worked in rice fields. Their labor followed seasonal cycles governed by the rains: preparing and flooding fields, transplanting seedlings, tending irrigation channels, and harvesting. The maintenance of embankments and channels required cooperation within and among villages, and the allocation of water was a matter of local authority and negotiation. Household members, including women and children, contributed to planting, weeding, harvesting, and the processing of rice, and they also produced cloth, mats, pottery, and other goods for household use and local markets.

Villages were not identical. Some enjoyed fertile soils and reliable water, while others on poorer land or in more exposed locations were more precarious. Village authorities, including headmen and the bekel who mediated between villagers and higher officials, played an important role in organizing labor and collecting dues. Artisans, including potters, smiths, weavers, and builders, served both villagers and the court. Markets, held at regular intervals, linked villages to one another and to towns, and merchants moved goods between the interior and the coast. The existence of an agricultural surplus did not mean universal rural prosperity: the surplus was claimed in part by the court and its officials, and the remainder had to sustain households through years of poor harvests.

Obligations to the ruler and to appanage holders weighed on rural households. Cultivators owed a share of their produce, and they could be called upon for labor on construction projects or as porters and followers on campaign. Warfare affected ordinary households directly. The recruitment of men for armies removed labor from the fields; the requisitioning of rice and animals diminished household stocks; armies on the march destroyed crops and disrupted villages; and campaigns against rival territories displaced populations, sometimes through forced relocation. The conquest of Surabaya and Madura involved devastation of the countryside, and the Batavia campaigns drew on the labor and produce of the north-coast districts through which armies passed.

The sources for village life are limited. Chronicles rarely describe ordinary farmers, and Company reports provide scattered observations of landscapes, markets, and prosperity. Archaeological and later ethnographic evidence provides supplementary insight, but the experiences of women, children, and laborers must be inferred largely from indirect evidence. Historians should resist the temptation to idealize either the peaceful village or the oppressive court; the evidence suggests a mixture of cooperation, exploitation, resilience, and vulnerability.

A cultivation calendar linked political affairs to household labor. Preparing irrigated fields required clearing channels, maintaining embankments, plowing, transplanting seedlings, weeding, harvesting, and storing grain. The timing and division of work varied by ecology and household circumstances, and it would be misleading to describe one uniform pattern across Java. What linked many villages was dependence on coordinated labor. If irrigation failed upstream, households downstream could lose water; if an army removed men during a demanding agricultural period, those remaining had to compensate. Political authority therefore entered daily life through the scheduling of work as much as through extraordinary acts of punishment.

Families combined agricultural and non-agricultural activities. People prepared food, cared for children and elders, raised small animals, exchanged produce in markets, made or repaired tools, and performed ritual obligations. Women contributed in numerous forms to production, exchange, and the management of households, although surviving royal and Company records rarely name them. Children learned work and social roles from kin and neighbors. Differences in rank mattered: a household tied to a powerful patron might enjoy protection from some demands yet owe more service to that patron; a poorer household might face debt, insecurity, or the loss of labor through recruitment. It is inappropriate to project the conditions of later colonial plantation labor backward onto every seventeenth-century village.

War magnified these inequalities. Food requisitions might fall unevenly on households; migration could relieve pressure in one district and impose it in another; a ruined irrigation channel affected more than those who fought. Sources often report troop movements but overlook the people who cooked for soldiers, carried equipment, or reconstructed damaged fields. To recover their history, the textbook must ask what the logistical records imply about labor and resource transfers while clearly separating documented facts from plausible reconstruction. A history of Sultan Agung without this rural dimension would explain the king's victories but not the social system that made them possible.

Everyday Life and Social History

Rice, Villages, and the Cost of War

For a farming household in Mataram's heartland, the year was shaped by the rains and by the claims of others. Planting and harvest brought intense labor in which entire families took part. After harvest, a portion of the grain went to the appanage holder or the court through the village headman or bekel, a portion to the household's own needs, and some might be sold or exchanged in the market. A poor harvest might leave a household short, and the demands of officials did not necessarily diminish.

War introduced additional risks. A summons to join a campaign might take a man away at planting or harvest, leaving others to carry the work. An army passing through might consume stores. In the districts that supplied the Batavia campaigns, villages were required to provide rice, porters, and boatmen, and the destruction of stores at Tegal in 1629 meant the loss of food collected from the countryside. Such burdens fell most heavily on those with the least power to resist. Households that lost workers, stores, or land might fall into debt or dependence, and some fled. Yet villages also adapted: communities negotiated with officials, evaded demands where they could, and rebuilt after devastation. Their experience reminds us that Mataram's military achievements rested on the labor and endurance of people whose names are not recorded.

  2. Why might a village headman or bekel occupy a difficult position between villagers and officials?
  3. What kinds of evidence would historians need to study women's roles in village economy?
Section 83.12

Coastal Cities, Merchants, Islamic Networks, and the Pasisir World

The northern coast of Java was a different world from the inland rice plains. Its ports, including Jepara, Gresik, Tuban, Semarang, and Surabaya, were centers of maritime commerce, shipbuilding, craft production, and political influence. Merchants of many origins, including Javanese, Malay, Chinese, Gujarati and other Indian, and Arab traders, lived and worked in these towns, exporting rice and other products and importing textiles, spices, and other goods. The wealth of the ports came from exchange, and their rulers, often called by titles such as adipati or pangeran, derived revenue from port dues and trade rather than primarily from agriculture.

The pasisir was also a center of Islamic learning and practice. Mosques, religious teachers, and Sufi and scholarly lineages were established in coastal towns, and links to pilgrimage routes and to the wider Islamic world gave coastal communities connections that the interior initially lacked (see Chapters 62 and 65). Giri's religious authority, mentioned in Section 83.8, is an example of the influence of coastal religious lineages. These networks conveyed ideas, texts, and people, and they contributed to a coastal tradition of scholarship and piety that coexisted with the courtly traditions of the interior. It would be mistaken, however, to portray Java as divided between an Islamic coast and a non-Islamic interior: the interior, too, had Muslim communities, teachers, and rulers, and Sultan Agung's reign is a key moment in the synthesis of courtly and Islamic culture (Section 83.17).

Mataram's attitude toward the coast combined desire and suspicion. The court wanted the revenue, goods, and strategic position that the ports offered, and it sought to restrict the independence of coastal rulers and to control shipping and foreign trade. Attempts to impose royal monopolies, to restrict the export of rice, and to direct commerce toward ports under reliable control were part of this effort. Such measures could provoke resentment among merchants whose prosperity depended on open trade, and some merchant communities and port rulers resisted or sought alternative partners. The Company's presence offered some coastal actors an additional commercial and political option.

The political conquest of coastal settlements did not automatically eliminate their commercial traditions or social independence. Merchants adapted, sometimes by cooperating with new officials, sometimes by moving their business to other ports, including Banten, Batavia, and Makassar (Chapter 82). The pasisir retained its identity as a distinct world of trade and Islamic learning, and it continued to shape political events. Understanding Mataram's history requires attention to this persistent coastal world, which neither conquest nor royal policy could simply absorb. The difficulties of integrating it help explain the strategic ambitions that led the court toward Batavia and the VOC, the subject of Division IV.

The pasisir cannot be reduced to a single faction opposed to Mataram. Its cities had different rulers, commercial partners, religious communities, and relationships with the court. Jepara and Tuban did not face identical circumstances, and merchants could be divided between those who benefited from a new ruling patron and those whose privileges were curtailed. Chinese traders, Javanese shipowners, Malay intermediaries, and religious scholars had overlapping but distinct interests. A coastal city could submit formally to Mataram yet continue to negotiate shipping arrangements that were difficult for the interior court to supervise closely.

Ports were also sites where information circulated. News of a change of ruler, a threatened war, or the price of rice might move by ship faster than royal orders could travel from the capital. Maritime connections could give local elites access to partners beyond the immediate reach of Mataram's army. The VOC exploited the same networks when it sought food or diplomatic intelligence. The court's challenge was therefore not simply to capture harbors but to control the choices made by people accustomed to multiple commercial and political relationships. Efforts to restrict trade could compel obedience temporarily but might also encourage movement to rival ports.

Religious authority added another layer to these relationships. The lineage and learning of teachers at Giri, the prestige of coastal mosques, and contacts with other Islamic centers could legitimate a ruler or authorize criticism of him. Yet Muslim identity did not determine political allegiance. Religious figures might support a royal campaign, resist demands from the court, or seek accommodation under new political conditions. Sultan Agung's own religious initiatives make sense within this diverse landscape. They addressed the fact that claims to Javanese sovereignty had to speak to communities whose institutions were embedded in both inland courtly and coastal Islamic worlds.

Section 83.13

Mataram, the VOC, and the Political Struggle for Western Java

Relations between Mataram and Dutch merchants began as commercial contacts. In the early seventeenth century the Company established trading posts on Java's northern coast, including the post at Jepara in 1613, to purchase rice and other goods and to sell cloth and other imports. The Company's objectives, as examined in Chapters 77 and 79, were to dominate the trade in spices and to secure strategic ports. Mataram's objectives were to command the coast and to obtain recognition from other powers. The two governments therefore differed not only in the goods they wanted but in their conceptions of political authority.

The decisive step was the VOC's seizure of Jayakarta in 1619 and the foundation of Batavia on its site. Jayakarta had been a pepper port under the authority of Banten, and its ruler had dealings with both the Dutch and the English. After a conflict in which Company forces under Jan Pieterszoon Coen prevailed, the Company built a fortified headquarters that it named Batavia. This created a European-controlled enclave in western Java, close to Banten and to the pasisir routes connecting the west with Mataram's coastal holdings. Banten, Cirebon, and the other coastal powers were drawn into the resulting competition. Cirebon, an old coastal center in the sphere between Banten and Mataram, was increasingly connected to the latter, while Banten remained independent and hostile to the Company.

Sultan Agung regarded the Dutch presence as a challenge to his wider political ambitions. He sought recognition as the paramount ruler of Java, and a foreign fortified settlement that refused to acknowledge his authority and that dominated shipping off the coast contradicted that claim. Diplomatic exchanges between the court and the Company, in which envoys were sent and gifts exchanged, were occasions for disputes about ceremony and precedence. The Company, for its part, saw Mataram as a potential threat to its headquarters and its trading interests: a large Javanese state might close the rice trade on which Batavia depended, might ally with Banten or with European rivals, or might attack the settlement directly. The central problem was therefore more than commercial rivalry; the two sides disagreed about political sovereignty, diplomatic obligations, and control of the coastline.

These mutual suspicions were intensified by the geography of supply. Batavia depended on rice imported from other parts of Java and the archipelago, and Mataram's agricultural heartland and conquered ports were an important potential source. Mataram, for its part, needed goods and revenue from the coast. The relationship could have developed in a cooperative direction, and for a time both sides negotiated. That it moved toward war in 1628 reflects the convergence of ambition, mistrust, and the effort to establish a decisive position in the west.

Mataram and the VOC entered the conflict with different kinds of strategic depth. Sultan Agung commanded an extensive inland network of cultivators and subordinate officials, enabling large armies to assemble. The Company commanded heavily defended coastal positions and ships that could connect Batavia to markets and reinforcements elsewhere. Neither was a nation-state claiming authority through a modern system of fixed boundaries. The court regarded recognition of royal seniority as politically important; Company officials sought secure trading terms and protection from hostile attack. Their disagreements therefore involved how status, jurisdiction, and commerce should be expressed in concrete diplomatic acts.

The relationship with Banten complicated both calculations. Banten was a substantial pepper-trading sultanate in western Java, with reasons to resist both Mataram's advance and the consolidation of Dutch Batavia. Its rivalry with the Company did not make it a dependable ally of Mataram, nor should Dutch and Banten interests be assumed to coincide. The position of Cirebon, whose rulers occupied a geographically and politically sensitive location, introduced further uncertainty. A large-scale attack on Batavia thus required Mataram to understand not only the town's fortifications but also the risks of advancing through regions with distinct political loyalties.

Diplomacy before the sieges did not eliminate these contradictions. Offers concerning commercial access, demands for recognition, and the prospect of military assistance against common rivals could all appear in negotiations without creating agreement on their meaning. After Surabaya's surrender in 1625, Agung could contemplate a major western campaign from a position of greater prestige, but the ability to occupy eastern cities gave no guarantee that a distant fortified harbor could be taken by the same methods. The decision to attack in 1628 was a calculated extension of existing royal ambitions under notably different geographical and military conditions.

Cause and Effect

Why Mataram and the VOC Came into Conflict

Long-term causes

The expansion of Mataram's power from an agricultural core toward the coast, and the rise of a European corporation seeking fortified ports and monopolies, placed two ambitious powers on a collision course. Different conceptions of sovereignty, in which one demanded ceremonial submission and the other insisted on treaty-based dealing, made accommodation difficult.

Short-term causes

The conquest of Surabaya in 1625 freed Mataram's forces and increased its prestige. The Company's growing consolidation at Batavia, its competition with Banten, and disputes over trade and ceremony raised the stakes in the 1620s.

Immediate trigger or context

Mataram's decision to mount an expedition to Batavia in 1628, motivated by the court's claim to paramountcy and by the strategic importance of the western coast, in a situation in which negotiations had not yielded recognition.

Event or transformation

Two large assaults, in 1628 and 1629, that failed to take Batavia and revealed the limits of Mataram's logistical reach.

Immediate effects

Heavy losses, the withdrawal of Mataram's forces, and the consolidation of the Company's position. Mataram turned to other strategies while retaining its claims.

Long-term consequences

The VOC survived as a coastal power, and relations oscillated between hostility and trade. Later dynastic crises created openings for deeper Company involvement in Javanese affairs.

Historical complexity: The conflict cannot be reduced to commercial competition or to the personality of Sultan Agung. It arose from the interaction of agricultural kingship, coastal commerce, and corporate-state expansion, each with its own logic.

Section 83.14

The First Mataram Assault on Batavia, 1628

The first major Mataram campaign against Batavia took place in 1628. Preparations involved assembling troops from districts across the kingdom, gathering provisions, and arranging transport by land and sea. Commanders were drawn from leading nobles, and forces marched along routes to the north coast and westward. A fleet carried part of the force and supplies. The difficulties of coordinating forces arriving by different routes and at different times were considerable, and the sources, which come largely from Company records and later Javanese tradition, give only a partial picture of the planning.

Mataram's advance guard reached the vicinity of Batavia in late August 1628, and the main body followed in the weeks after. The Company had received warning and prepared its defenses. Batavia was a fortified town with a castle, walls, bastions, and artillery, and its commander, the governor-general Coen, organized the garrison and the townsmen for defense. The defenders included Company soldiers from Europe but also a diverse population: Chinese residents, whose leaders had played a role in the town's early life, Mardijkers (freed Christianized people of Asian and mixed descent who served in the militia), and other Asian communities. Company records identify several Asian groups participating in the defense, and it would be wrong to describe Batavia's defenders as exclusively Dutch.

The fighting involved assaults on outlying forts and on the town's defenses. Mataram's commanders attacked positions near the town, and the Company's artillery and fortifications inflicted losses on the attackers. Contemporary Company accounts report that the leading commander at the first stage of the siege, usually identified as Tumenggung Baureksa, was killed in the fighting, and later Javanese tradition elaborates the fates of other commanders. The assault failed to breach the defenses, supplies were strained, and by early December 1628 the Mataram forces withdrew. Precise numbers of troops and casualties are reported differently in different sources and should not be treated as established.

Historians must distinguish contemporary evidence from later stories. Dutch reports, written by participants and officials at the time, give dated observations but reflect the Company's perspective and may exaggerate or minimize. Javanese tradition recounts the campaign in the idiom of dynastic memory and in some episodes adds colorful or legendary elements. The 1628 expedition is best understood as a serious but poorly coordinated effort in which the logistics of supplying a large army far from its base proved decisive, a theme that returned more strongly in 1629. The Company emerged from the first siege without losing Batavia, but it recognized that Mataram would try again.

Contemporary Company correspondence permits a more differentiated sequence than the familiar description of one vast Javanese army arriving at the walls. An initial force led by Tumenggung Baureksa approached Batavia late in August 1628, combining vessels and troops arriving along coastal routes. A second contingent under Mandurareja and Upasanta appeared later in the same campaign. The timing mattered because the attackers could not automatically concentrate all their forces at once; the defenders could respond to successive threats and use the interval to reinforce vulnerable points. De Graaf's reconstruction, based heavily on VOC reports, identifies Baureksa's death with two sons on October 21 and the subsequent execution of Mandurareja and Upasanta in December 1628. These commanders therefore belong unequivocally to the FIRST siege, not to the expedition of 1629.

The defenders' response combined fortification with aggressive countermeasures. They protected entrances to the town, maintained batteries, and attacked positions that would have allowed the besiegers to approach closely. The urban population included people with different legal statuses and origins, some of whom performed military or transport duties under Company authority. Accounts emphasizing Dutch artillery alone fail to explain the social organization of defense: food had to be distributed, walls maintained, signals carried, and wounded people treated. Equally, the Javanese assault was more than a mass charge against masonry. Its commanders sought ways to coordinate troops, approach outworks, and weaken the defenders' capacity to resist, though Company accounts naturally foreground their opponents' failures.

Time worked against the besiegers. Maintaining an army around a fortified city required steady provisions and the ability to respond when a planned attack failed. The supply difficulties described in contemporary reports did not reflect a lack of agricultural resources in Java as a whole; they reflected the challenge of delivering food to a particular army over an extended distance. By early December 1628 the attack had failed and the remaining forces withdrew. Their departure did not resolve the strategic contest, since the court retained sufficient resources to prepare another expedition. The first siege instead revealed vulnerabilities that Mataram's leaders sought to remedy the following year.

The punishments reported after the failure are important as evidence of the pressures placed on royal commanders. If a commander risked severe sanction for defeat, he might pursue an attack beyond reasonable logistical limits. Yet the historian should avoid depicting royal punishment as the sole cause of military behavior. Campaign narratives were written by observers who had their own reasons to portray an enemy king as harsh. The strongest account identifies what the records actually report and then evaluates how fear, honor, authority, and the practical constraints of command may have interacted.

Section 83.15

The Second Assault on Batavia, 1629: Supply, Disease, and Defeat

The assault of 1629 was a distinct operation, not a continuation of the 1628 siege, and it was more carefully planned. Having learned from the first failure, Sultan Agung organized a larger and better-provisioned expedition. Preparations included the establishment of rice stores along the north coast, notably at Tegal and Cirebon, to supply the army on its march and during a prolonged siege. Forces were assembled in the interior and moved toward the coast, and artillery and equipment were prepared for the siege. The logic was to avoid the supply failures of 1628 by creating a chain of depots and by sending forces and provisions in a coordinated manner.

The Company responded decisively against this infrastructure. Learning of Mataram's preparations, Company forces attacked the coastal stores and shipping at Tegal and Cirebon, destroying provisions and vessels that had been gathered to support the army. The loss of these supplies struck at the foundation of the campaign, since the army's capacity to sustain a siege far from its agricultural base depended on them. The Company's attacks on Mataram's supply infrastructure demonstrate that the contest was fought on logistical as well as tactical ground.

The Mataram army nevertheless reached Batavia in the autumn of 1629 and besieged the town, deploying artillery and attempting to approach the defenses through trenches and assaults. The command arrangements differed from those of 1628, when Mandurareja and Upasanta had led the later-arriving contingent and were executed following that campaign. The defenders, again including Company soldiers and the town's diverse Asian populations, held their positions. During the siege Governor-General Coen died of illness in September 1629, a moment of crisis for the Company that did not result in the collapse of the defense. Hunger, exhaustion, and disease afflicted the attackers, whose supplies had been disrupted, and the effects of illness and shortage also touched the defenders. After weeks of failed attacks, Mataram's forces withdrew. Reports of royal punishments following military reverses must be dated carefully: the documented executions of Mandurareja and Upasanta followed the 1628 operation, not this later campaign.

The outcome should be evaluated without presenting numerical estimates of casualties as established facts, since contemporary sources differ. The defeat resulted from a combination of tactical, logistical, environmental, and political factors, rather than simply technological inferiority. Fortifications and artillery mattered, but so did the destruction of supplies, the difficulties of feeding a large army in a coastal environment in which the Company controlled the sea, and the effects of disease. Politically, the expedition depended on commanders and troops whose cohesion could weaken under hardship. Mataram's capacity to conduct a long campaign far from its core was limited by the structure of its mobilization and by the seasonal and logistical constraints of agrarian armies. The 1628 and 1629 campaigns should be studied as separate operations, each with its own causes and consequences.

Learning from the first failure meant concentrating on the movement of food as well as the movement of soldiers. Grain stored along the north coast could extend the time an army remained near Batavia, but depots were themselves vulnerable. They required trusted officials, transport arrangements, guards, and knowledge of when approaching units would need resupply. The distance between the interior heartland and the western coast multiplied the opportunities for interruption. An army might have sufficient manpower for an assault but not sufficient provisions to remain concentrated until defenses were breached. The 1629 preparations show the court's awareness of that problem, not its failure to plan.

Company ships could strike coastal storehouses without first defeating Mataram's main field army. De Graaf and Pigeaud specifically identify destruction of stores at Tegal as a significant blow to the expedition; Cirebon appears in broader discussions of the supply corridor, but the precise events at each harbor should not be collapsed into one verified incident. The distinction matters because supply warfare was spatially distributed. The outcome at Batavia could be influenced by operations far east of the town, carried out against rice, shipping, and warehouses rather than against besieging troops. In this respect the Company used maritime mobility to disrupt a predominantly land-based strategy.

The siege itself involved attempts to approach the defenses, organize artillery, and sustain pressure on a garrison that was also vulnerable to illness and shortages. Governor-General Jan Pieterszoon Coen died on September 21, 1629, during the crisis; his death did not break the defensive organization. Mandurareja and Upasanta had commanded a contingent in 1628 and had been executed after that failure; they therefore played no part in the second expedition. The historical reconstruction discussed by de Graaf identifies Panembahan Purbaya among the leadership associated with the second expedition, but other command details remain less secure and should be presented cautiously.

Disease and hunger should be understood as reciprocal risks of siege warfare rather than as a single dramatic explanation. Crowding, disturbed water supplies, interrupted markets, and the movement of large numbers of people could threaten defenders as well as attackers. The Company enjoyed the advantage of fortified positions and greater capacity to resupply by water, whereas Mataram could not count on secure maritime communication. Nor could artillery alone reverse the loss of food stores or replenish exhausted transport crews. Defeat resulted from interacting constraints: incomplete siege coordination, a resilient defense, loss of provisions, illness, and the impossibility of keeping pressure on Batavia indefinitely.

The wider consequences are as important as the withdrawal. Mataram did not cease to be a major power, and the Company did not suddenly acquire Java's interior. The court continued to assert prestige and to pursue eastern ambitions; the Company maintained its fortified headquarters and coastal trading relationships. For students, the two campaigns provide an unusually clear comparison between agrarian capacity and logistical reach. Resources become militarily decisive only when they can be organized and delivered, and advantages in ships, walls, or guns matter most in relation to the conditions under which each side can sustain operations.

Section 83.16

After Batavia: Diplomacy, Coastal Politics, and the Limits of Royal Ambition

The failures at Batavia did not end the relationship between Mataram and the Company, nor did Sultan Agung abandon his claims. Contacts continued through envoys, exchanges of letters and gifts, and disputes over prestige and procedure. The court maintained its assertion of superiority over Java, and the Company, which had learned that Mataram could mobilize large forces, remained wary. The relationship was neither permanent open warfare nor stable political cooperation: it fluctuated between hostility and pragmatic dealing, according to circumstances.

Agung adjusted his strategies. Having failed to take Batavia by direct assault, he turned to measures that relied less on long-distance campaigning. These included attempts to control or restrict coastal commerce, to influence the trade of ports under his authority in ways that disadvantaged the Company, and to cultivate alliances with Banten's rivals and with other powers. Efforts to restrict rice exports or to channel trade away from Batavia were part of this approach, though the evidence for their effectiveness is mixed. The political significance of Banten and Cirebon remained central: Banten's independence prevented Mataram from dominating the west, while Cirebon's connections with Mataram helped the court maintain a presence on the western coast.

Trade through Jepara and other north-coast ports continued despite official hostility. Merchants of various origins bought and sold goods, and the Company continued to obtain rice and other supplies from Javanese ports when possible. This illustrates the distinction between official political hostility and continuing economic transactions: governments might be at odds while merchants sustained commerce. For both sides, the economic benefits of exchange were substantial, and neither could entirely forgo them. Periods of tension, closure of ports, and negotiation alternated, and the relationship was shaped by shifting calculations.

The VOC survived as a coastal power despite Mataram's much larger agricultural and demographic base. Several factors help to explain this. The Company controlled the sea and could supply and reinforce Batavia by ship, while Mataram's army depended on long overland and coastal supply lines that could be disrupted. Batavia's fortifications and artillery were formidable, and the town's diverse population contributed labor and military service. The Company's finances and organization (Chapter 77) allowed it to sustain a permanent garrison and to respond rapidly. And Mataram's own political structure, reliant on negotiated loyalties and seasonal mobilization, was not well suited to prolonged operations far from home. The Company's survival was thus not simply a matter of technological superiority but of the interplay of geography, organization, and logistics. A formal peace between the court and the Company was restored in 1646, after Sultan Agung's death, in the early reign of Amangkurat I.

The years after 1629 offer an opportunity to separate political rhetoric from practical behavior. Sultan Agung continued to claim high status, and a failure to capture Batavia did not make him willing to acknowledge the Company's position as equal to his own. Yet both parties had reasons to avoid continual large-scale war. Mataram's court needed imported goods and access to maritime exchanges, while the Company relied on grain, timber, labor, and information from Javanese suppliers. Official diplomatic hostility could coexist with commerce conducted through intermediaries. A treaty was not necessary for every shipment, and a hostile royal proclamation did not imply that all economic links ceased.

Mataram explored relationships with other foreign powers, including Portuguese officials in Melaka and Goa, while also pursuing the difficult problem of asserting royal precedence over independent Javanese neighbors. Such diplomacy should not be turned into a simple anti-Dutch alliance. Each partner had separate concerns and limited capacity to act. For Mataram, the possibility of naval assistance was attractive precisely because the two Batavia campaigns had exposed the shortcomings of a force sustained mainly from land. For foreign governments, alliance with the court might promise commercial advantage but also carry the risk of expensive conflict with the VOC.

The kingdom's later eastern campaigns show a redirection of effort rather than a total abandonment of military ambition. Mataram could still exercise considerable pressure over neighboring states whose political systems were closer to its own and whose territories could sometimes be reached through established networks of tributary service. But these gains did not negate Batavia's survival. The Company and the court occupied overlapping spheres that neither could fully eliminate. Their eventual reconciliation in 1646 followed Agung's death and a change of policy at court; it did not retrospectively transform the earlier encounters into a straightforward contest with one permanent winner.

Section 83.17

Sultan Agung, Islamic Authority, and the Javanese Calendar Reform

Sultan Agung's reign is a principal moment in the interaction between Javanese court culture and Islamic identity. The rulers of Mataram were Muslims, and Agung maintained relationships with Islamic scholars, religious institutions, and pilgrimage traditions while retaining the symbols and ceremonies of Javanese kingship. He is associated with Tembayat, the burial place of a venerated early Muslim teacher, which became a site of royal pilgrimage and a place linking the dynasty to Islamic sanctity. Such practices integrated Islamic concepts into court culture rather than replacing older traditions: the court continued to honor ancestral and spiritual powers, including those represented in the stories of Nyai Roro Kidul, alongside Muslim observance.

The most celebrated expression of this synthesis is the calendar reform associated with 1633. Before the reform, the Javanese had used the Saka era, an Indian-derived lunisolar reckoning with its own numbering, while Muslims used the lunar Hijri calendar. Agung's reform retained the continuing numerical sequence of the Saka era but adopted the lunar year, so that the Javanese year count continued while the months and year length followed Islamic reckoning. This allowed religious festivals to be timed according to Islamic practice while preserving a Javanese chronological tradition. The reform is conventionally dated to 1633, and its adoption across Java was gradual. It remains a distinctive feature of Javanese culture.

In 1641, according to the usual reconstruction, Sultan Agung acquired the title sultan, following an embassy to Mecca. The title linked him to a wider Islamic world of rulership and strengthened his claims to religious as well as political authority. It also served his dealings with coastal rulers and Muslim communities, whose recognition mattered to his paramountcy. The adoption of the title did not signify a break with Javanese royal ideology; it added a dimension of legitimacy.

Students should avoid the simplistic depiction of Javanese religious life as a permanent opposition between an Islamic coast and a non-Islamic interior. Islam had reached the interior, and many inland rulers and subjects were Muslim; the coast had its own pre-Islamic and local traditions. The relationship between Islamic scholars and the court was sometimes cooperative and sometimes tense, and different figures held different views about the proper role of Islamic law and of local custom. Merle C. Ricklefs's research identifies Sultan Agung as a major figure in the development of a Javanese synthesis of court culture and Islamic identity, a synthesis that shaped later Javanese religion and politics. The sources are, again, chiefly court-centered, and the religious experience of ordinary villagers is more difficult to reconstruct.

The religious life of Mataram was shaped by institutions as well as royal symbolism. Scholars, teachers, and recognized holy places connected the court to wider Javanese Muslim communities. Pilgrimage and patronage offered rulers ways to express piety and to associate their authority with respected religious lineages. At the same time, rites connected with ancestors, dynastic origins, and the sacred landscape continued to inform court tradition. The evidence does not justify forcing all these practices into a binary choice between orthodoxy and pre-Islamic survival. Different participants might understand the same ceremony in distinct theological or political terms.

The calendar reform of 1633 was a particularly durable example of institutional synthesis. By retaining an inherited era count while adopting lunar reckoning, the court created a chronology that could carry recognizable Javanese year numbers into a system of months compatible with important Islamic observances. This did not erase every other way of reckoning time, nor did it impose identical practice on every community overnight. It demonstrated that a royal innovation could seek legitimacy through continuity while changing the practical organization of ritual and political time. Historians should distinguish the calendar's arithmetic principle from later popular explanations of the motives behind its introduction.

The assumption of the title sultan in 1641 likewise joined local political purposes to wider Islamic connections. Scholarship traces negotiations associated with an embassy to Mecca, but the political effect depended on reception in Java. A title obtained through external recognition could strengthen a court's claim to seniority without compelling rivals such as Banten or the VOC to acknowledge its authority. Religious policy was thus embedded in competition for power, yet that does not make it insincere or merely strategic. Students should consider both its devotional meaning and its political uses, allowing the evidence to show how they interacted.

The Javanese Calendar, Saka Era, and Islamic Reckoning

Definition. The Saka era is a system of year numbering of Indian origin long used in Java. The Hijri calendar is the Islamic lunar calendar, counting years from the Hijra. Historical context. Earlier Javanese kingdoms used the Saka era, as inscriptions show, while Muslim communities in the ports observed the Hijri calendar. The reform brought the court's calendar into line with Islamic ritual time while preserving the continuity of a Javanese chronology.

Why it matters. The reform symbolizes the combination of Javanese tradition and Islamic practice and illustrates the court's role in shaping religious culture.

Potential misunderstanding. The reform did not replace Islam with a Javanese religion or vice versa, and it did not instantly unify practice across Java. It was a calendrical compromise promoted by the court.

Section 83.18

Royal Culture, Historical Writing, Imogiri, and the Construction of Dynastic Memory

Mataram's rulers used architecture, historical writing, genealogy, ceremony, literature, and sacred places to express and sustain authority. The court patronized poets and scribes who produced literary works, chronicles, and religious texts, and royal ceremonies displayed the king's majesty. Genealogy connected the dynasty to venerated ancestors, including earlier Javanese kings and Islamic saints. These practices were not simply decorative; they helped to legitimize rule and to bind subordinate lords into a shared symbolic order.

The Babad Tanah Jawi tradition (Section 83.2 and the Primary Source Window above) was central to dynastic memory. Courtly accounts of Senapati and Sultan Agung represented them as heroic and sacred, and they cast religious figures as guarantors of royal legitimacy. The chronicles were revised by later courts, and the Mataram story that they preserve was shaped by the needs of successors who claimed descent from Agung. It is therefore important to distinguish contemporary royal initiatives from the later literary and ceremonial traditions that represented them.

A major material expression of dynastic memory is the royal cemetery at Imogiri, south of Kotagede, begun during Sultan Agung's reign, from approximately 1632 according to Yogyakarta's cultural heritage records. The site was laid out on a hill, and Agung himself was buried there after his death. It was subsequently expanded to receive later rulers and their families, and it remains a dynastic and religious site associated with the successor courts of Surakarta and Yogyakarta. Imogiri shows how rulers used sacred places to link themselves to ancestors and to the land, and how such places could outlast the political circumstances of their founding.

Later Javanese courts continued to interpret and preserve traditions associated with Mataram. They claimed descent from it, maintained its ceremonies, and reworked its stories, so that Mataram became a point of reference for Javanese political legitimacy long after the kingdom had fragmented. This legacy is part of why Sultan Agung is remembered in Indonesia as a major historical figure. Historians must be careful, however, not to treat the later image as a transparent window onto his reign, and not to assume that later claims of continuity corresponded to institutional continuity.

Court memory was not a passive record of past events. It selected ancestors, highlighted victories, interpreted defeats, and described relations among sacred figures and the ruling family. A chronicle that gave Senapati a supernatural mandate or represented Sultan Agung as an ideal ruler provided later dynasties with a language for defending their own claims. Recensions produced in different court settings could reorganize episodes or emphasize different genealogies. For this reason, a historian should record which version is being used, when it was copied, and how far the episode lies from the presumed date of composition. Historical meaning does not depend solely on proving that each reported event took place.

Royal architecture performed a related function in physical space. A cemetery associated with the ruling house could locate dynastic ancestry in a landscape visited and maintained over generations. Imogiri became an especially important site of this kind. Accounts of its founding and construction vary in detail; linking it to Sultan Agung is secure as a broad historical tradition, but precise building dates should not be silently imposed when sources differ. Later royal burials and ceremonies reinforced its status and sometimes changed the meanings associated with the original foundation. The site is therefore evidence for both seventeenth-century kingship and the continuing memory of that kingship.

The cultural achievements of the reign need not be treated as compensation for a failed conquest. Religious patronage, the calendar reform, architectural initiatives, and literary representations grew within an expanding political system that sought to organize society and legitimate authority. They drew on scribes, builders, religious specialists, and dependent communities whose labor is rarely highlighted in royal chronicles. A full historical account asks not simply what Sultan Agung supposedly ordered, but who implemented such projects, what audiences encountered them, and why they remained powerful reference points for later Javanese courts.

Courtly Culture and Islamic Kingship

What changed?

  • The calendar was reformed in 1633 to follow Islamic lunar reckoning while preserving the Saka-era count.
  • Pilgrimage sites such as Tembayat and the cemetery at Imogiri joined older sacred places in the royal ritual landscape.

When and how did change occur?

Change was gradual and uneven, concentrated in Agung's reign through deliberate royal initiatives but extending across generations as later courts adapted them. Court elites and religious specialists experienced change most directly; rural communities experienced greater continuity in local custom, though they observed the calendar and festivals.

Why the pattern matters: It shows that Islamization and Javanese tradition were not opposed processes but intertwined, and that rulers actively shaped religious culture.

Section 83.19

Amangkurat I, Court Conflict, and the Disintegration of Mataram, 1646–1677

Sultan Agung died in 1645 or 1646 (see the chronology note above) and was succeeded by his son, known as Amangkurat I. The new ruler made peace with the Company in 1646, ending the long period of tension, and he developed a new royal center at Plered, building a palace complex that became the focus of his court. His reign lasted until 1677 and has often been described as a period of increasing authoritarianism and conflict. Historians are cautious, however, about treating the troubles of his reign as the inevitable result of Agung's. The later crisis reflected the king's own decisions, the response of regional elites and religious figures, military developments, and the interventions of external powers.

Amangkurat I's reign is reported to have featured conflict with nobles and religious elites. Dynastic and court rivalries produced executions and purges, and the king is reported to have taken harsh measures against religious leaders whose influence he distrusted. Policy toward the coast shifted: he is reported to have restricted maritime trade and to have attempted to control shipping more tightly, measures that damaged the interests of coastal merchants and communities. The authority of regional governors was curtailed in favor of tighter court control. Such policies increased resentment among groups whose cooperation the court required, and the evidence for their severity comes from both Javanese tradition and Dutch accounts, each with its own biases.

Relations with the Company in these years were conducted through diplomatic missions, among which the embassies of Rijklof van Goens between 1648 and 1654 are especially valuable as contemporary outside observation. Van Goens described the court, the countryside, and the king's attitude toward the Company, as discussed in the source window below. The Company sought commercial privileges and security for Batavia, while the court sought recognition and, at times, restricted foreign trade. The relationship remained one of cautious negotiation rather than open war.

In the 1670s, a rebellion led by Trunajaya, a Madurese noble, drew together dissatisfied Javanese nobles and displaced Makassarese military figures who had come to Java after the Makassar War (Chapter 82). Its forces defeated royal armies and in 1677 captured the royal center at Plered, forcing Amangkurat I to flee. He died soon afterward during the flight. The collapse of the court created a crisis of authority, and his successor, Amangkurat II, turned to the Company for assistance. The VOC intervened militarily and politically, and in return obtained concessions. This marked the beginning of a deeper involvement by the Company in Javanese political affairs. The crisis thus served as the principal transition from Sultan Agung's expansive monarchy to the later history of fragmentation and Dutch intervention, which is treated in later chapters.

The new reign initially appeared to offer an opportunity for reduced conflict with Batavia. Diplomatic contacts in 1646 culminated in a peace that recognized each party's practical reasons for negotiation. Yet the settlement did not prevent disagreements over ceremony, commercial access, and the control of coastal ports. Van Goens's later visits document a court concerned with rank, protocol, and the exchange of goods, while Company officials sought predictable conditions for their merchants. The peace was therefore a framework for bargaining, not evidence that the two governments had arrived at a common understanding of sovereignty.

Amangkurat's domestic policies altered the political balance inherited from his father. His court at Plered became associated with tighter supervision and with episodes of severe repression against nobles and religious figures. Accounts of the killings differ in their details and numbers; claims about a single enormous purge should be attributed rather than presented as a securely quantified event. What is less doubtful is that repeated punishment and distrust weakened the relationships on which a court governing through provincial lords depended. Royal coercion could remove an immediate opponent, but it could also encourage others to conceal resentment or seek support outside the palace.

The coastal policy was neither a single unbroken embargo nor an unchanging programme. De Graaf and Pigeaud distinguish attempts to control the ports through pairs of supervisors in the early 1650s, a revised arrangement of senior coastal governors later in that decade, periods of tension with the VOC, and a renewed trading presence at Jepara in the 1660s. These changes reveal both an ambition to strengthen royal control and the practical limits of suppressing commerce. Port officials, merchants, provincial nobles, and Company factors sought advantage within rules that could be altered by the court. A fuller chronology makes the policy more intelligible than a simple statement that Amangkurat closed Java's ports throughout his reign.

The revolt of Trunajaya was made possible by several interacting developments rather than one inevitable reaction to authoritarian government. The Madurese leader drew support from dissatisfied nobles, while the presence of Makassarese migrants and fighters reflected the wider regional displacement described in Chapter 82. Some alliances were temporary and involved partners with different political objectives. Royal armies suffered defeats, and the loss of Plered in 1677 was a symbolic blow as well as a practical loss of the seat of government. Amangkurat I's flight and death opened a succession crisis in which his heir sought Company assistance. The VOC's stronger role in the aftermath followed bargains and military campaigns; it was not already established as direct ruler of all Java when the court collapsed.

Section 83.20

Mataram and the Contest for Java: Agricultural Power, Maritime Limits, and Political Transformation

Mataram's rise rested on the agricultural resources of Central Java, the ideology and practice of royal authority, and the capacity to mobilize soldiers and supplies through a network of nobles, officials, and village headmen. Under Sultan Agung it subordinated Surabaya, Madura, Tuban, and other rivals, and it created the largest Javanese realm since the fall of Majapahit. The conquest of Surabaya strengthened the kingdom by removing its principal eastern rival and by extending its reach to the coast. Yet that strength was uneven, since administrative capacity did not keep pace with territorial reach, and incorporation of conquered elites and communities remained incomplete.

The attacks on Batavia exposed serious limitations. Mataram's armies were mobilized through seasonal and personal ties and were sustained by rice collected from the countryside; sending them hundreds of kilometers to a fortified coastal town defended by an enemy that controlled the sea strained these arrangements. The Company's attacks on supply depots, the effects of hunger and disease, and the defenders' fortifications and artillery, together with the participation of Batavia's diverse inhabitants, produced defeat. The failure did not destroy Mataram, but it fixed the limits of its ambition in the west.

The relationship between forced political integration, court authority, regional resistance, and the later instability under Amangkurat I deserves careful interpretation. Agung's reign created tensions: conquered elites and coastal communities resented court control, and regional lords expected rewards that the ruler could not always supply. But these tensions did not mechanically produce the crisis of the 1670s. Amangkurat I's repression, his policies toward the coast and religious leaders, and the opportunities offered by displaced Makassarese warriors and discontented nobles all contributed, as did the willingness of the VOC to intervene. Contingency and choice, not inevitability, shaped the outcome.

It is instructive to compare Mataram's governing model with those of the other powers studied in Part XV. Aceh under Iskandar Muda (Chapter 80) built power around control of ports, pepper, and the straits, projecting force by sea and by expeditions against the Malay coasts. Johor-Riau (Chapter 81) was a Malay successor polity whose authority rested on the allegiance of sea peoples, dynastic prestige, and the control of trade routes. Makassar (Chapter 82) was an open commercial port whose rulers cultivated trade and a broad network of merchants. The VOC (Chapters 77 and 79) was a corporate-state that combined commercial organization with fortified bases, naval power, and financial resources. Mataram, by contrast, mobilized land, labor, and personal loyalty. Each form of power had distinctive strengths and vulnerabilities, and their interactions produced the competitive environment of the seventeenth-century archipelago.

The VOC's eventual involvement in Java's internal political struggles emerged from a long interaction with powerful Javanese states, not simply from the expansion of European military superiority. The Company's survival at Batavia, its commercial interest in Javanese rice and ports, and the vulnerabilities of Mataram's court after 1677 all created conditions for deeper intervention. That is a story for later chapters. For now, Mataram's history demonstrates that early modern Javanese power was shaped by the relationship between agricultural wealth, royal authority, military mobilization, and maritime commerce, and that Southeast Asian states were active shapers of that history rather than passive recipients of Dutch power.

The contrast between Mataram and the maritime powers of the same period should be stated in terms of resource mobilization rather than rigid categories. Gowa–Talloq drew on agricultural support as well as shipping; Aceh needed populations and land revenues alongside pepper and naval strength; Johor's royal influence reached villages and islands; and the VOC relied on Asian labor and commerce as much as its European shareholders. Mataram's distinct emphasis was its capacity to assemble an immense pool of agrarian service in the interior. The difference was one of balance and political organization, not a division between agricultural states with no maritime interests and trading states without rural foundations.

This comparison helps make sense of the uneven results of conquest. When Mataram subdued Surabaya, it benefited from repeated campaigning against a rival whose support could be isolated and whose leading family could be brought into court politics. At Batavia, it faced a fortified adversary with access to maritime reinforcement and the capacity to disrupt supply routes far from the siege itself. Both outcomes followed from the interaction of local conditions with the capabilities of a powerful court. Neither proves that an inland government was inherently inferior to a European corporation or that military success represented permanent political incorporation.

Finally, the court's later crisis should be connected to its history without being treated as predetermined. Regional resentment, tensions over revenue, political expectations among royal relatives, changes in coastal policy, and international connections all shaped opportunities for rebellion. Decisions taken by Amangkurat I and by his opponents mattered. The VOC expanded its role by entering negotiations and conflicts within an existing Javanese political system, often through alliances with local actors whose goals differed from its own. A history that centers these choices is more accurate than one that narrates European domination as the automatic endpoint of Southeast Asian rivalry.

Historical Debate

Was Mataram a Durable Imperial State or an Expansionist Monarchy with Structural Limitations?

The question: Did Sultan Agung create a durable imperial state with institutions capable of sustaining a large realm, or was Mataram an expansionist monarchy whose gains depended on the personal authority of the ruler and were vulnerable to structural limits?

Interpretation A — an expansionist monarchy with structural limits: Many historians emphasize that Mataram's authority depended on personal relationships, appanage grants, and seasonal mobilization, which made it difficult to administer distant conquests or to sustain long campaigns. The failure at Batavia, the persistence of independent powers, the incomplete integration of the east and coast, and the crisis after Agung's death are cited as evidence of these limits.
Interpretation B — a more durable and influential state than often allowed: Others stress that Mataram created a lasting political and cultural framework: the court model, ceremonial hierarchy, calendar, and dynastic memory endured in successor courts, and its subordination of rival Javanese powers reshaped the island's politics. On this view, the kingdom's influence outlasted its territorial control.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on chronicles, Company records, and heritage evidence, but differ in how they weigh institutions against personal rule and territorial control against cultural legacy. Disagreement concerns interpretation and terminology (what counts as "imperial") rather than the basic sequence of events.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand early modern Javanese statecraft, the nature of Dutch intervention, and the continuity between Mataram and its successors. It also shapes comparison with other seventeenth-century powers.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Mataram arose from the agricultural heartland of Central Java, expanded into the island's dominant political power under Sultan Agung, attempted to subordinate the coastal trading cities, and confronted the Dutch East India Company without conquering Batavia. Its central argument is that Mataram's strength and its limits came from the same source: an agrarian kingdom that could mobilize land, labor, and loyalty, but struggled to administer distant conquests or project power far from its base.

Mataram's origins lay in the later sixteenth century, when Ki Ageng Pemanahan and his son Sutawijaya, later Panembahan Senapati, transformed a grant of land near Kotagede into a regional power at the expense of Pajang. The chronology of these beginnings remains qualified, and the dynasty's own chronicle tradition, the Babad Tanah Jawi, presents them through stories of sacred ancestry and supernatural favor that historians must read critically. Under Panembahan Seda ing Krapyak, Mataram applied pressure on eastern rivals, yet Sultan Agung inherited an expanding but contested kingdom, not a unified empire.

Agung's accession in 1613 opened a period of offensive warfare. Campaigns from 1614 brought the capture of Wirasaba in 1615, the confrontation near Pajang in 1616, and the capture of Tuban in 1619. The conquest of Madura in 1624 and the capitulation of Surabaya in 1625 removed the principal eastern rival. The chapter emphasized the distinction between military defeat and political integration: conquest was followed by marriage alliances, relocation of elites, and incomplete administrative control.

Resistance and limits followed. The Pati rebellion of 1627, the religious authority of Giri, and the contested frontier of Blambangan showed why Mataram could defeat rivals without controlling all of Java. Terrain, distance, local loyalty, religious prestige, and external assistance constrained the kingdom, and Banten and Batavia remained beyond its reach.

Mataram's government rested on the court, ceremony, and a network of personal relationships, and its economy on revenue assignments (lungguh), village officials (bekel), regional governors (bupati), and the labor of rice-farming households. Royal claims to revenue exceeded what could actually be collected, and war placed heavy burdens on villages. Everyday life in the countryside combined cooperation, vulnerability, and resilience, and the agricultural surplus did not mean universal prosperity.

The pasisir remained a distinct commercial and Islamic world. Merchants of many origins, coastal rulers, and religious lineages such as Giri's shaped political outcomes, and conquest did not erase their traditions. Mataram's efforts to control shipping and revenue produced tension with coastal interests and made the Company an attractive alternative partner for some.

The VOC's seizure of Jayakarta and foundation of Batavia in 1619 created a rival center. Disagreements over sovereignty, ceremony, and coastal control led to Mataram's two assaults on Batavia. The 1628 attack failed amid logistical strain and strong defenses; the more carefully prepared 1629 attack was undermined by Company raids on supply depots at Tegal and Cirebon, by hunger and disease, and by the defenders' fortifications and artillery. Batavia's defense involved Asian communities as well as Europeans, and the defeat resulted from multiple causes rather than simple technological inferiority.

After Batavia, Agung adjusted his strategy, maintaining claims while relying on diplomacy, trade regulation, and alliances. His reign was also marked by religious and cultural creativity: the 1633 calendar reform, his association with Tembayat, the title sultan in 1641, the beginning of Imogiri, and the consolidation of dynastic memory. These show the interaction, rather than opposition, of Islamic authority and Javanese court culture.

After Agung's death in 1645 or 1646, Amangkurat I's reign saw court repression, conflict with nobles and religious leaders, and restrictions on coastal commerce. The rebellion of Trunajaya, supported by discontented Javanese nobles and displaced Makassarese warriors, captured Plered in 1677 and opened the way to deeper VOC intervention. This crisis was not an inevitable consequence of Agung's reign but resulted from later decisions and external pressures.

Comparison with Aceh, Johor-Riau, Makassar, and the VOC shows that Mataram embodied a distinctive agrarian model of state power. Its history demonstrates that Dutch involvement in Java arose from long interaction with powerful Javanese states, and that Southeast Asian rulers were active agents of their own history.

Chapter Reflection

Mataram's history matters because it shows that the early modern archipelago was not only a world of ports and ships. An inland kingdom built on rice, labor, and royal ceremony could dominate a large island and challenge a European corporation. Its history corrects any picture in which Southeast Asian societies simply reacted to Dutch commercial power.

The chapter also reveals the limits of agrarian power. The same personal ties and seasonal mobilization that built armies made them hard to sustain far from home, and the same conquests that enlarged the realm produced resentments that later rulers could not contain. The sieges of Batavia and the crisis of 1677 are two expressions of that structural problem, though neither was predetermined.

Mataram's cultural legacy proved more durable than its territorial control. The Javanese calendar, the dynastic cemetery at Imogiri, and the memory preserved in chronicles shaped the successor courts and Javanese political culture for centuries. At the same time, the crisis after 1677 opened the way for deeper Dutch involvement, a theme taken up in Chapters 93 and 100.

As the last chapter of Part XV, this chapter completes a comparison of ways of exercising power in the seventeenth-century archipelago: corporate-state, port sultanate, successor polity, open entrepôt, and agrarian kingdom. Chapter 84 now turns to the mainland, where the Toungoo kings pursued imperial expansion on a different geographic and institutional basis.
