Burmese Nationalism

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Burmese nationalism before 1941 was not the creation of one leader, one party, or one faith. It grew out of Buddhist associations, university boycotts, village committees, a rural rebellion, oilfield strikes, and parliamentary maneuvering, movements that sometimes cooperated, often quarreled, and together wore away the moral authority of British rule.

On the morning of December 5, 1920, students of the newly created Rangoon University declined to enter their classrooms. They gathered instead at the Shwedagon Pagoda, the golden shrine that dominates Rangoon's skyline and that had long served as a place of assembly as well as worship. Their grievance was a University Act that, in their view, would restrict higher education to a narrow, anglicized elite. Within days the boycott had become a national event: the students' speeches linked school fees and examination rules to the dignity of the Burmese people, and their action inspired the founding of independent "national schools." Almost sixteen years later, in February 1936, another strike began at the same university after two student leaders, Nu and Aung San, were expelled. A third moment came in 1938 and 1939, when oilfield workers from Chauk and Yenangyaung marched north and south along the Irrawaddy valley, and when students and workers faced police fire in Rangoon and Mandalay. Burmese later named this upheaval the Revolution of 1300, after the year in the Burmese calendar.

These episodes belong to a single historical story, but not to a single organization. This chapter examines how Burmese nationalism emerged between roughly 1906 and 1940 through overlapping and competing movements: Buddhist religious and cultural defense, student activism, constitutional politics, rural rebellion, labor organization, and an increasingly radical demand for independence. The chapter follows the institutional lines of that development, from the Young Men's Buddhist Association (YMBA) founded in 1906 to the General Council of Burmese Associations (GCBA) of 1920; from the village-level wunthanu associations to the Dobama Asiayone ("We Burmans Association") of 1930; from Saya San's rebellion of 1930–1932 to the strikes of 1938–1939; and from the dyarchy of 1923 to the Government of Burma Act of 1935 and the Freedom Bloc of 1939.

Three arguments run through the chapter. The first is that the movement was plural. Monks, lay associations, schoolboys, rice farmers, oilfield workers, parliamentarians, women's organizations, and minority communities each had grievances and programs, and these were not interchangeable. Saya San's rebellion and the urban Dobama movement, for example, drew on different social networks and political languages, and the relationship between them must be demonstrated rather than assumed. The second argument is that colonial legitimacy eroded not through a single dramatic defeat but cumulatively, as the British authorities repeatedly met demands for representation, educational access, and religious respect with limited reform and, at decisive moments, with prosecution and police force. The third is that Aung San, whom later memory has made the central figure of Burmese nationalism, emerged as a representative of a new generation of organizers rather than as the founder of the movement he later helped to lead.

The evidence for this history is unusually varied. It includes government reports and parliamentary papers, trial records, police and intelligence files, newspapers and periodicals in Burmese and English, speeches and pamphlets, memoirs by participants such as Ba Maw and U Nu, and an extensive scholarship that has revised earlier interpretations, especially concerning the Saya San rebellion. Each type of source reflects a perspective. Colonial reports describe rural unrest through the categories of administration and criminal law; nationalist memoirs, written after independence, often arrange events as steps toward a goal that participants did not know they would reach. The chapter therefore pays attention to who wrote each source, for what audience, and what it cannot show.

The chapter also takes care with names and categories. "Burma" is used for the colonial province and country in this period, in keeping with the usage of the sources, and the Burmese people most prominent in the nationalist organizations are often described as Bamar (Burman). Yet Burmese nationalism was not synonymous with Bamar nationalism. Karen, Shan, Kachin, Chin, Mon, Arakanese, Indian, Chinese, and other communities experienced colonial rule and the nationalist movement differently, and several had their own political organizations and aims. The chapter treats this diversity as part of the story, not as a footnote.

The chapter's chronology ends around 1940. Aung San's departure from Burma that year, the formation of the Burma Independence Army, and the Japanese occupation belong to Chapter 136, and the wartime transformation of the nationalist movement is treated there. Here the aim is to explain how, on the eve of the Second World War, Burma's nationalists had built organizational foundations, ideological vocabularies, and mass constituencies that made independence a broadly contested objective, while remaining divided over strategy, over alliances, and over what the nation was.

Historical Context

The nationalist movements of the early twentieth century developed within a colonial state that had been built in three stages. Britain annexed Arakan and Tenasserim after the First Anglo-Burmese War (1824–1826) and Lower Burma, including Pegu, after the Second (1852). Following the Third War in November 1885, Upper Burma was annexed on January 1, 1886, and King Thibaw was exiled. The conquest is treated in Chapters 106 and 107; what matters here is its institutional legacy. The Konbaung monarchy, which had been the political center of Burmese Buddhist society and the patron and regulator of the monastic order, no longer existed. Burma became a province of British India, governed first by a Chief Commissioner and then by a Lieutenant-Governor, and its legal, fiscal, and policing systems were built on Indian models.

The economy that grew within that state was oriented toward export. After 1852 the Irrawaddy delta was transformed from a thinly settled marshland into one of the world's great rice-exporting regions, as migrants from Upper Burma cleared land, supported by credit, railways, steamship connections, and the opening of the Suez Canal. Rice, teak, and petroleum tied Burma to global markets, and the labor, trade, and finance that sustained them involved large movements of people across the Bay of Bengal. Many Indian laborers, clerks, merchants, and moneylenders, including the Chettiar credit network of South India, worked in Burma, and Rangoon became a port in which people of Indian origin formed roughly half of the population by the 1930s. Chinese traders and millers, European firms, and Burmese peasants, boatmen, and officials all occupied different positions in the same system.

Politics was shaped by how that system was governed. The "Burma Proper" provinces were administered directly, while the hill and border regions, later called the Frontier Areas, were governed through local rulers and special regulations, including the Shan States and Kachin and Chin hills. Colonial administrators and missionaries also classified Burma's peoples by race and religion, and these classifications entered censuses, schooling, and recruitment. The military, for example, drew heavily on Karens, Kachins, Chins, and Indians rather than on the Bamar majority of the central plains. Representation, when it came, was narrow. The Morley-Minto reforms of 1909 and the Montagu-Chelmsford reforms of 1919 extended limited representation in India, but Burma was at first excluded from the latter, a decision that Burmese organizations took as an insult and that fed the politics of the period examined in Section 129.4.

Finally, the religious landscape changed. The monastic community, the sangha, had lost royal patronage and the supervisory office of the head of the order, and its relationship to lay Buddhists was reorganized through new lay associations, vernacular schools, and printed Buddhist literature. Chapter 125 examines religious reform across the region, and Chapter 124 treats cities, schools, and newspapers; this chapter takes up their consequences for Burmese organized politics. The reader should also keep in mind the boundary of the chapter: it studies the period to about 1940 and leaves the wartime transformation to Chapter 136.

Colonial Burma and the Problem of National Sovereignty

The first problem facing any Burmese nationalist was that the object of nationalism was not obvious. Before 1885 there had been a Burmese monarchy with a court at Mandalay, a Buddhist order supervised by the crown, and a society whose political loyalties ran through local lords, village headmen, monasteries, and ethnic communities. After the annexation, there was a colony governed from Rangoon as part of British India. The monarchy had provided a focus for loyalty, and its removal left a vacuum that different movements would fill in different ways. Early resistance to the conquest, examined in Chapter 107, was led by local notables, former officials, and monks, and was suppressed by the early 1890s. Later nationalists inherited memories of that resistance but not its institutions.

The colonial administration remade the structures of authority. The Village Act of 1887 reorganized rural administration around appointed headmen and a system of taxation that included a capitation tax, while the older hereditary offices and the web of obligations that tied them to the court lost their foundation. The legal system introduced British procedure and Indian codes, and the courts became a central stage on which political disputes would later be played out. The monastic order, for its part, had lost the king as patron and arbiter. The office of the head of the order, the thathanabaing, lapsed after the death of its last holder in 1895, and disputes over discipline had to be settled in the new courts or left unsettled. The question of who could speak for Buddhism, which had been answered in practice by the crown, was now open.

Burma's incorporation into British India meant that its governance was decided in Calcutta and Delhi, and that Indian officials, police, soldiers, and clerks were prominent in the administration. Burmese critics resented what they saw as rule by outsiders, but it matters to be precise: the structure of the empire, not the migrants themselves, produced this arrangement, and Indian laborers in Rangoon's docks and Indian peasants in the delta were often as poor as the Burmese farmers they worked beside. Section 129.11 returns to the communal tensions that this arrangement made possible.

The social order also changed. The delta's rice economy produced new landlords, tenants, creditors, and traders, and Rangoon grew into a cosmopolitan port. An English-language education created a class of Burmese lawyers, clerks, and officials who understood the colonial legal system but were excluded from its upper ranks. Many nationalist leaders of the period, including Ba Maw, U Chit Hlaing, and U Pu, came from this group, which could use British institutions against British rule. They did so, however, from a position of limited power. The Legislative Council that existed after 1897 was largely advisory, and representation before 1923 was minimal.

The political geography of the colony was also divided. Ministerial Burma, the plains and delta where Bamar Buddhists formed the majority, was distinct from the Frontier Areas of the Shan States, Kachin and Chin hills, and Karenni, which were governed through local chiefs and special regulations. The Karen population was dispersed across the delta, the Tenasserim coast, and the eastern hills, and included Buddhists, Baptists, and Catholics. This division meant that the nation the Bamar-majority nationalists claimed to represent was legally split, and that colonial policy gave minorities distinct institutional positions. The question of sovereignty was therefore double: nationalists had to argue against British rule, and also decide who would belong to the sovereign nation they sought.

The limits of sovereignty were visible in the gap between official constitutional language and everyday authority. The British monarch exercised ultimate sovereignty, but village householders met the state through tax collectors, police constables, courts, and clerks. To an urban lawyer the central problem might be the composition of a legislature; to a farmer it might be the price of rice after the annual assessment; to a monk it might be the absence of an accepted lay authority to defend the religion. These were different experiences of the same colonial order. Political organization created ways to connect them, although the resulting alliances remained unstable.

Nor was Burma's territorial unity self-evident. The direct administration of lowland districts and the special administration of border regions followed distinct military, diplomatic, and administrative histories. Shan rulers retained positions under British supervision, while other communities encountered missionaries, military recruiters, or officials with separate regulations. Where the colonial state treated such arrangements as practical exceptions, nationalists confronted a question of representation: could a legislature elected mainly within the plains claim a mandate over territories excluded from its jurisdiction? The question was not settled before the war. It was already inherent in the design of colonial institutions, and it would recur whenever nationalists used the word Burma without specifying the communities they intended to include.

The Young Men's Buddhist Association and the Defense of Burmese Culture

The Young Men's Buddhist Association was founded in Rangoon in 1906. Its name and some of its forms recalled the Young Men's Christian Association, and its founders were mainly educated, English-speaking Burmese laymen, including lawyers, teachers, and officials. They saw themselves as defending Buddhism and Burmese culture in a changed world, one in which Christian missionary schools offered the main route to modern education and in which colonial institutions treated Buddhist institutions with indifference. The YMBA's aims were cultural and educational: it organized lectures, promoted Buddhist and Pali study, supported Buddhist schools, published periodicals, and encouraged the use of Burmese language and dress. Alicia Turner's work on religion in colonial Burma stresses that these activities were not a withdrawal from politics but a response to a perceived crisis of Buddhism itself, a crisis in which laypeople took on tasks that the monastic order and the crown had once performed.

The association's growth was a notable organizational development. Local branches spread across Lower and Upper Burma, linked by a central body in Rangoon, and annual conferences brought together members from different districts. In this sense the YMBA created a national network before there was a national political party. It also trained a generation in the practices of committees, resolutions, subscriptions, and publication that would be redeployed in later organizations. The association's members were not uniformly young, and some were officials who held government posts, which constrained how openly they could oppose the authorities.

The footwear controversy of roughly 1916 to 1920 transformed the YMBA from a cultural society into a political actor. In Burmese Buddhist practice, shoes are removed before entering a pagoda's sacred precincts. Many Europeans, along with some officials and soldiers, entered pagodas wearing shoes, and some Buddhist laypeople and monks protested. The YMBA, at its conferences, passed resolutions asking that all visitors remove their footwear, and it posted notices at pagodas. The issue was not trivial. It concerned who set the rules within Burmese religious spaces, and whether colonial racial privilege, the assumption that Europeans were exempt from local custom, could extend into sacred ground. Colonial officials were divided. Some advised caution to avoid religious offense, while others argued that officials should not be told by laypeople where they could walk. Eventually the government ruled that the matter should be handled by custom and by local trustees rather than by law, and the agitation continued in different forms.

The affair also exposed a political split within the association. Some members wished to keep the YMBA within cultural and religious limits and avoid conflict with the government. Others saw the footwear question as one instance of a wider pattern of unequal treatment and wanted a political program. Their opportunity came when, after the First World War, Britain announced reforms in India and excluded Burma from them. Burmese associations, YMBA delegates among them, protested that Burma deserved similar treatment. In 1920 the YMBA's conference was succeeded by a new organization, the General Council of Burmese Associations, which carried the YMBA's network into explicit politics. The change was gradual. The YMBA continued to exist, many of its cultural projects went on, and the language of Buddhist defense remained central to the new body.

The YMBA's significance lies in what it showed about the conditions for organized nationalism. It did not replace religion with secular politics. Instead, it mobilized religious identity as a framework through which educated laymen could demand institutional control over their own affairs. This pattern, in which cultural and religious defense preceded and then merged with political claims, appeared elsewhere in colonial Asia, but in Burma its Buddhist form had distinctive features, notably the close relationship between laity and monks examined in the next section.

The printed word made religious politics more mobile. Resolutions adopted at annual meetings could be circulated to places whose inhabitants had never heard the original speakers. Newspapers reported disputes over footwear, monastic discipline, and religious education, while local committees collected subscriptions and organized gatherings. In this way, the association turned shared ritual practices into matters for public discussion and collective decision. It also helped create a Burmese political vocabulary in which preserving Buddhism could be expressed as a right of the people rather than merely a duty of a king.

Such activity contained contradictions that mattered later. Christian minorities and some non-Buddhist migrants could recognize the unfairness of colonial privilege without identifying with a program centered on Buddhist custom. Some lay activists wanted state protection for religion; others wanted greater independence for voluntary institutions. The YMBA's achievement was therefore organizational rather than an agreement about the ultimate constitution of the country. It provided committees, a printed public, and a language of dignity that subsequent organizations could use, even as new parties disputed what that dignity demanded.

Section 129.3

Buddhist Monks, Political Preaching, and Anticolonial Mobilization

The monastic community, the sangha, was not politically homogeneous, and its relationship to politics was contested within the order. Monastic discipline, as interpreted by many senior monks, discouraged involvement in worldly affairs, and monasteries had traditionally accepted the authority of whichever ruler protected them. Other monks argued that the destruction of the monarchy and the neglect of Buddhism by an alien government placed on them a duty to speak. The result was a spectrum of positions, from monks who avoided politics, through those who supported lay associations quietly, to a smaller number of politically active monks, often called pongyi politicians in colonial usage, who preached openly against British rule.

U Ottama became the best known of these. A monk who had traveled to India and Japan, he returned to Burma in the early 1920s with an interest in anticolonial politics and an eloquent public style. Colonial authorities charged him with sedition for speeches delivered in 1921, and he was imprisoned. He was again arrested in later years, and over the next decade he spent long periods in custody, becoming for many Burmese a symbol of the colonial state's willingness to punish religious figures. Some details of his career remain debated, and later nationalist accounts have tended to enlarge his role, but the importance of his imprisonment as a public event is not in doubt: his prosecutions provided occasions for demonstrations and for the circulation of his speeches in print.

U Wisara's case showed another dimension. Imprisoned in the late 1920s, he demanded the right to wear his monastic robes in jail. Prison regulations required uniforms, and the authorities refused. U Wisara began a hunger strike and died on September 19, 1929, after several months. His death provoked large public mourning, with a funeral procession in Rangoon and protests across the country. Here the issue was explicit: it concerned the right of a monk to maintain monastic identity under state power. The prison authorities saw a matter of administrative order, while Burmese Buddhists saw a violation of sacred status. The same facts could thus be read through opposed categories, and that difference was part of the political conflict.

The arrest and punishment of monks changed how political grievances were understood. Where earlier protests had involved petitions and resolutions, imprisoned monks became figures for whom ordinary people could feel direct obligations. Offerings of food to imprisoned monks, attendance at their trials, and the rituals of mourning were forms of political participation open to people who did not belong to associations or attend conferences. The monastic network, with monasteries in nearly every village, also provided speakers and meeting places, and the sangha's moral authority made it difficult for the government to treat open dissent by monks as ordinary crime.

The relationship between monks and lay leaders was not always harmonious. Lay politicians sometimes used monks as symbols while pursuing electoral strategies the monks disliked. Some monks criticized lay nationalists as insufficiently devout, while others were criticized by senior clergy for breaching monastic discipline. U Maung Maung's study of the nationalist movements of this period describes the shift "from sangha to laity" in the leadership of nationalism, a shift in which the politically active monk gave way, in the 1930s, to secular students and Thakin organizers. That change should not be overstated, since monks continued to take part in protests through 1939, but it marks a long-term change in the character of the movement.

Monastic political activism depended partly on the moral economy of donations. Lay supporters fed, clothed, and housed monks and expected them to embody Buddhist discipline. When a monk faced imprisonment for speaking against the colonial government, a sentence imposed under secular law could be understood by supporters as an affront to a religious vocation supported by the wider community. Public commemorations and alms-giving then became ways of sustaining political solidarity without requiring all participants to enroll in a party. The effect was powerful, but it could not guarantee a common program. Followers who revered a monk might favor constitutional reform, complete independence, or simply an end to a particular official abuse.

It is also necessary to distinguish two registers of authority. U Ottama made explicitly political arguments that British rule could not command Burmese moral loyalty; imprisonment turned these arguments into a demonstration of colonial coercion. U Wisara's resistance over the treatment of monastic robes addressed bodily and disciplinary respect within prison. The two episodes were linked by a political interpretation of monastic dignity but differed in their immediate causes. Neither establishes that the sangha, as an institution, adopted one political program. Monasteries and senior clerics remained divided over whether participation in worldly confrontation was compatible with their obligations.

The General Council of Burmese Associations and the Wunthanu Movement

The General Council of Burmese Associations was formed in 1920 from the YMBA network and affiliated associations. It aimed to coordinate Burmese political opinion, and its program combined demands for constitutional reform with defense of Buddhism and support for education. Leading figures included U Chit Hlaing, U Pu, U Ba Pe, and U Soe Thein, lay politicians with legal and commercial backgrounds. The GCBA's early campaigns responded to Britain's decision to exclude Burma from the Montagu-Chelmsford reforms; the government of India Act 1919 extended dyarchy to India's major provinces, and Burma's inclusion was postponed. The protest against exclusion was one of the GCBA's founding issues.

Between 1921 and 1923 the GCBA turned to noncooperation, a strategy influenced by the Indian National Congress's campaign under Gandhi, although Burmese leaders adapted it to local conditions. Noncooperation included boycotts of foreign goods, refusal to pay certain taxes, withdrawal from government schools, and eventually a boycott of elections to the new legislative council. Its most distinctive expression was the wunthanu athin, village-level patriotic associations that sprang up across Burma from about 1921. The word wunthanu refers to those who protect their own race or lineage, and the associations were part social and part political: they organized villagers to withhold the capitation tax, to build local arbitration, to boycott foreign cloth and liquor, and to establish national schools. Their success varied by district, and some collapsed under pressure from officials or from within.

The wunthanu movement's significance was that it extended nationalist organization into rural society. The YMBA had been largely urban and professional. The wunthanu associations enrolled villagers, headmen, monks, and local notables, and they gave the nationalist cause a structure that reached far beyond Rangoon. At the same time, the movement revealed difficulties. Many village associations were more concerned with taxation and local grievances than with the constitutional questions that preoccupied the GCBA leadership. When the leadership split over participation in the new Legislative Council, as examined in Section 129.7, the village associations lost a central direction, and the colonial government used prosecutions and local pressure to weaken them. By the mid-1920s the movement had faded, though its networks and memories remained available for later mobilization, including, arguably, in the rural organization that preceded the Saya San rebellion.

The relationship between Buddhist cultural protection and political demands was thus complicated. The GCBA used Buddhism's symbols, held meetings in monasteries and pagoda compounds, and counted monks among its leaders. But its leadership remained overwhelmingly lay, and its constitutional demands were framed in modern political terms. The movement did not simply fuse religion and politics; it used religious networks to carry a political program that had its own logic and its own conflicts.

A distinctive feature of the wunthanu movement was its effort to change conduct rather than merely obtain a favorable parliamentary vote. Local committees might urge households to buy Burmese products, attend meetings, refuse ceremonial cooperation with officials, or withhold social approval from persons associated with government policy. In some districts, boycotts extended to relations with village headmen and people perceived as insufficiently patriotic. These techniques allowed an association with few formal legal powers to exercise substantial social influence. They also exposed its limitations: pressure could become coercive, and its burdens fell unevenly on villagers who still depended on state employment, markets, courts, or local patrons.

The question of political scale became urgent. National leaders could announce policies in Rangoon, but enforcing them depended on locally respected people and on the willingness of households to sustain economic costs. A district committee's success in organizing a meeting was not proof that it represented everyone living in the surrounding villages. Women helped raise funds and conduct social boycotts, while some monks lent prestige without assuming organizational office. Students and schoolteachers could carry notices or speak on behalf of associations. The network gave nationalism reach, but factional disputes over whether to boycott the new constitutional institutions revealed that organizational reach did not produce ideological unity.

In practice, Buddhist-centered nationalism could marginalize Karen Christians, Muslims, and Hindus, a tension that appears repeatedly in later sections.

The Rangoon University Boycott of 1920

Higher education in Burma had been limited. Before 1920, Rangoon College was affiliated with the University of Calcutta, and Judson College was a Baptist institution; students who sought degrees took Calcutta examinations. In 1920 the government created Rangoon University through a University Act, intended to establish a residential, teaching university on a British model. The reform was presented as an advance, but many Burmese saw it as a barrier. The act favored residential study, which was costly; it raised standards of entry and imposed regulations that seemed designed for a small elite; it placed control of the new institution largely in official and European hands; and it gave limited place to Burmese language and culture. Educated Burmese, including GCBA leaders, feared that it would shut out students of modest means and impose colonial values on the next generation.

Student leaders at Rangoon College and Judson College met in late 1920 and decided to boycott the new university. The strike began on December 5, 1920, an event commemorated in Myanmar later as National Day. Students gathered at the Shwedagon Pagoda, which served as a protest center, and a strike committee organized speeches, subscriptions, and publicity. The students' demands combined practical and national points: revision of the act, wider access, a role for Burmese language and culture, and respect for the dignity of Burmese education. Support came from the GCBA and from monks, parents, and townspeople, who sent donations and joined meetings. The boycott lasted for months. The government made some concessions in later years but did not reverse the principal features of the act.

The boycott was important less for what it won immediately than for what it began. It was the first large collective action by Burmese students as students, and it created an organizational model: committees, funds, meetings at pagodas, and a vocabulary that connected education to national dignity. Aye Kyaw's study of the student movement traces how the boycott led to independent national schools and to continuing student activism, which provided the foundation for later leadership. It also showed that nationalist protest could be launched by young people from a variety of backgrounds, not only by established politicians.

The strike had limits as well. Its leaders came largely from Rangoon's educated families, and female students, though present, did not hold leading roles. Rural young people had little access to the university and hence little voice in the boycott. Some students, once the strike ended, returned to the university; others entered the national schools or took up politics. And the movement's themes, national dignity, access, and respect for Burmese culture, left open the question of what kind of education the nation needed. That question was answered differently by those who established national schools, as examined in Section 129.6.

The controversy therefore involved more than the formal wording of the University Act. Residential requirements threatened to increase the cost of study for families outside Rangoon. University governance appeared remote from the students whose education it directed, and the proposed institution seemed likely to train a limited professional elite without expanding broader access. Many students had encountered British political language about liberty and representation in their classrooms; the boycott asked why they were not to be consulted about institutions governing their own futures. When the dispute moved to the Shwedagon platform, students presented educational exclusion as a collective injury rather than a personal inconvenience.

The boycott also forced questions of organization into the open. Strike committees had to negotiate, maintain discipline, explain decisions to parents, and communicate with students who faced different examination and employment pressures. A student willing to boycott for a few days did not necessarily support an indefinite rejection of the university. Public sympathy helped turn the campaign into a national symbol, but the political meaning of that sympathy remains open to interpretation. Ko Ba U's speech of 20 December, preserved in a documentary sourcebook, called on young people and their elders to recognize a national injustice and support a national university. That appeal is evidence of the strike leadership's ambitions, not a statistical measure of unanimous popular support.

Section 129.6

National Schools, Political Education, and the Expansion of the Burmese Public

The boycott led directly to the founding of national schools, institutions run by Burmese associations rather than the colonial government. The GCBA and student supporters established a National College in Rangoon and national schools in many towns, offering instruction in Burmese, courses in Burmese history and literature, and a curriculum meant to cultivate patriotic habits. Their founders aimed to show that Burmese could build their own educational institutions without official sponsorship. This distinguishes them from the comparative developments studied in Chapter 124, since the national schools were explicitly alternatives to government-controlled education, not merely additions to it.

The schools faced serious difficulties. They lacked the government grants that allowed official institutions to pay teachers and construct buildings, and their degrees carried no official recognition, which meant graduates could not easily enter the civil service or the professions. Many relied on donations from monasteries, merchants, and village associations, and these were unreliable. The movement's strength in the early 1920s declined as noncooperation weakened and as families, needing practical credentials, returned their children to government and mission schools. By the end of the decade the national schools had shrunk in number and influence, though some survived and the idea of independent education persisted.

Their significance extended beyond their enrollment. They created networks of teachers who combined schooling with political commitment, and they helped to produce Burmese-language textbooks, readers, and periodicals. The Burmese press expanded at the same time, with newspapers and magazines reporting on political meetings, trials, and strikes. A public that read in Burmese, and discussed politics in tea shops, monasteries, and associations, grew during these years. The students of the 1930s who led the Rangoon University Students' Union read Burmese and English political literature and drew on this wider reading public.

National schools needed more than nationalist rhetoric to survive. Teachers had to be recruited and paid, classrooms found, reading materials produced, and examinations arranged for students whose parents hoped education would still lead to employment. Organizers therefore faced a tension between creating a genuinely independent curriculum and ensuring that pupils could proceed to further study or find remunerative work. A school emphasizing Burmese literature and Buddhist ethical instruction might deepen cultural confidence; it still had to teach subjects that students regarded as useful in a colonial economy. This was particularly difficult outside the largest towns, where subscription income and trained teachers were scarce.

The curriculum debate was political in itself. What counted as national education? Some advocates favored religious and literary instruction; others demanded modern science, professional training, and civic knowledge. Boys' education drew much attention, but the availability of education to girls and to linguistic minorities raised questions that the general slogan of a national school could conceal. English could be interpreted either as the language of domination or as a practical tool for arguing against colonial authority. The movement's lasting significance thus did not depend only upon how many schools survived. It familiarized families with the possibility that Burmese organizations, rather than the colonial government alone, could formulate and administer institutions of public importance.

Local national schools also acted as meeting places. Their committees linked parents, teachers, merchants, monks, and students who might disagree about formal party politics yet cooperate to support an educational institution. Fundraising tours, printed appeals, and public examinations created occasions for political speech without necessarily announcing themselves as party rallies. The educational movement became a training ground in administration as well as ideology; it showed young activists the demanding labor involved in sustaining institutions beyond the moment of protest.

Because participation was voluntary, the fortunes of individual schools varied with local support. Some remained educational institutions while others became repositories of political memory after funding or official recognition changed. Their diversity is a reminder that national education was a movement of experiments, not a single centralized school system.

The experience also made students and teachers aware that curriculum decisions were forms of political power: institutions could shape not only what pupils learned, but who had the authority to define a national public.

Everyday Life and Social History

Education and Daily Life in the National Schools

For a student in a national school, daily life differed from that of a pupil in a government or mission school. Instruction was largely in Burmese, lessons often began with Buddhist observances, and teachers frequently combined classroom work with political discussion. Classes were often held in monastery buildings, borrowed halls, or rented houses. Fees were low and some teachers worked without full salaries, motivated by patriotism or by lack of other jobs.

Families made hard choices. A national-school education expressed political commitment but offered uncertain employment, whereas a government or mission school education opened clerical and professional careers. Girls attended in smaller numbers than boys, though women's associations later promoted girls' schooling. Because most of the surviving evidence comes from nationalist leaders' memoirs and government reports, historians know less about what ordinary pupils and parents thought, and must read these sources for indirect evidence.

Section 129.7

Dyarchy, Legislative Reform, and Divisions within the Nationalist Movement

The British government responded to Burmese protests in 1921 by extending a version of dyarchy to Burma. Under the Government of Burma Act of 1921 and the constitution that began operating in January 1923, a Legislative Council with an elected majority was created, and certain departments, called "transferred" subjects, such as education, public health, and local government, were placed under ministers responsible to the council. Other subjects, "reserved" to the Governor and his executive council, included finance, law and order, and land revenue. The Governor retained wide powers, and the system was criticized, in India and Burma alike, because ministers had responsibility without control of money or the police. The franchise was limited by property, tax, and residence qualifications.

The reforms divided the GCBA. Some leaders held that participation was a way to gain experience and to use the council as a platform, and that boycotting would hand the field to collaborators. Others argued that dyarchy was a sham, that participation legitimated colonial control, and that noncooperation should continue. The 1922 elections were boycotted by sections of the GCBA, and turnout in many districts was low, but the council's seats were filled by candidates from other groups. A faction led by U Ba Pe and others organized as the "Twenty-One Party" or as a Home Rule group to contest elections, and the GCBA split into factions led by figures such as U Chit Hlaing, U Soe Thein, and U Pu. Personal rivalries, regional bases, and disagreements over strategy all contributed to the fragmentation.

The councils of the later 1920s were not powerless. Ministers could shape education and public health policy, and council debates provided a place to criticize the government. Yet the system placed Burmese politicians in a difficult position: they were accountable to voters but constrained by British power, and they were often accused of cooperation by those outside. By the end of the decade the political scene was marked by personal factions and shifting alliances more than by a single national party.

Dyarchy divided subjects of government into departments supervised by officials and departments for which ministers answerable to a restricted legislature might bear responsibility. This distinction was intended to make reform gradual and safe from the imperial standpoint. To critics, it placed politicians in the uncomfortable position of being blamed for services without controlling the coercive and financial mechanisms that conditioned them. Supporters of electoral participation countered that even limited authority could provide experience, public budgets, and an arena in which officials could be questioned. Neither argument was wholly abstract: elected office carried salaries, opportunities for patronage, and exposure to voters' demands, while boycott deprived nationalists of influence over decisions that would still be made.

The resulting splits in the GCBA involved both principle and social position. Lawyers and professionals accustomed to institutional negotiation might value elected office; village activists who had promoted noncooperation might regard such a turn as abandonment. Some Buddhist leaders favored keeping political conflict outside the monasteries, whereas other figures saw participation as a concession that would diminish public enthusiasm. The controversy also raised the question of whether the electoral framework could represent frontier populations and religious minorities on terms acceptable to them. Colonial officials described constitutional reform as evidence of progress; nationalist critics measured it against political authority actually surrendered. By those different standards, the same statute could be interpreted as an advance and as a mechanism for preserving rule.

Even those who entered politics did not form one coherent pro-government camp. Constitutionalists could criticize taxation and administration while cooperating with individual officials on educational or public-health initiatives. Boycotters likewise differed in how permanent a boycott should be and what alternative structures might replace electoral councils. A useful historical assessment must distinguish strategic preferences from moral labels such as patriot or collaborator: the record contains repeated shifts as opportunities changed and coalitions fractured.

Reform was consequently a field of negotiation, not a simple binary choice.

Constitutional Cooperation versus Political Boycott

The question: Was participation in the dyarchy legislature a practical way to advance Burmese interests, or did it weaken the nationalist movement by dividing it and legitimating colonial authority?

Interpretation A — participation was practical: Supporters argued that elected representatives could influence education and local policy, use the council to expose official actions, and gain experience of government. Boycotts, they held, ceded the arena to others and produced no concrete gains.

Section 129.8

Rural Society, Land, Debt, and the Growth of Agrarian Resistance

Most people in Burma were farmers, and rural conditions shaped nationalist politics. In the delta, rice cultivation was organized around smallholders, tenants, landlords, and creditors. Cultivators borrowed to clear land, buy seed and bullocks, and pay taxes; creditors included Burmese moneylenders, Chinese and Indian traders, and above all the Chettiars of South India, whose banking firms provided much of the capital for the rice economy. Michael Adas's study of the delta describes a frontier in which rapid expansion brought growth along with debt and insecurity. Rents and interest rates were high, tenancy increased over time, and farmers depended on world rice prices that they could not influence. Taxation, including the capitation and land taxes, added to the burden.

The dry zone presented different conditions: smaller holdings, drought risk, and an older social order based on village and monastery. The Burmese press, village associations, and monks were important channels of communication. In both zones, rural people experienced colonial rule through headmen, police, tax collectors, and courts, and through the loss of the patronage and mediation that the monarchy had provided. They also had traditions of resistance and of millenarian expectation, in which a righteous king or a holy man might restore justice.

These conditions explain why rural grievances were widespread, but they do not by themselves explain why rebellion occurred when and where it did. Structural grievances, debt, tenancy, taxes, and falling prices after 1929, existed across Lower Burma, yet organized revolt broke out in particular districts and through particular networks. The wunthanu associations and Buddhist networks provided organizational precedents, and local leaders, healers, and monks supplied the leadership. The rural story must therefore be told in two parts: material conditions that made protest likely, and organizational and ideological choices that made one form of protest possible. The Great Depression's wider effects are treated in Chapter 133; here it is enough to note that falling rice prices around 1929–1930 sharpened existing strains.

Debt relationships were structured by time. A cultivator needed credit before the rice crop could be harvested, often to purchase seed, pay taxes, finance transport, or meet household obligations. Repayment depended on a price that the farmer did not control. When prices fell, selling the same quantity of rice could leave a household unable to meet an obligation contracted under more favorable conditions. Creditors might then acquire land through legal foreclosure, and tenants could find themselves cultivating fields they had previously owned. Such processes were not uniform across districts; location, soil, access to transport, and the availability of relatives or patrons affected vulnerability.

Indian Chettiar financiers became prominent in the colonial rice-credit system, but they were not the only lenders, and they operated within property and legal institutions established or upheld by the colonial state. The politics of indebtedness cannot be reduced to ethnic antagonism. Burmese creditors and landholders also participated in rural markets, while Indian laborers and petty traders possessed no uniform interest in foreclosures. A farmer's complaint might be directed against a tax collector, a particular mortgage contract, a merchant, an employer, or village authorities. Nationalist rhetoric could combine these into an indictment of colonial rule, but the historian should preserve the distinct mechanisms at work.

Land grievances did not produce rebellion automatically. Households often negotiated payment extensions, shifted labor within the family, migrated temporarily, or sought assistance through village relationships. Only in particular districts and circumstances did discontent become organized resistance. Speakers, associations, circulating rumors, and police action could transform scattered grievances into a shared confrontation. The point matters for understanding Saya San: a political narrative that makes declining rice prices sufficient to explain the uprising ignores why the timing, organization, and symbols of protest varied among communities exposed to economic distress.

Seasonal mobility complicated collective action. Workers who migrated for harvest employment had fewer durable ties to village committees, while established cultivators sometimes feared losing credit or access to land if they joined protests. Differences within the countryside shaped both participation and restraint.

Section 129.9 · Division III — Saya San's Rebellion and the Rise of the Dobama Movement

Saya San and the Outbreak of the Rebellion of 1930–1932

Saya San, also written Hsaya San, was born around 1876 in the Shwebo area of Upper Burma and had a varied career. He was for a time a monk, then a practitioner of traditional medicine and astrology, and he became involved in the GCBA, in one of its factions, during the 1920s. In that capacity he traveled in Lower Burma and, according to several accounts, helped to investigate agrarian grievances. The title "Saya" indicates a teacher or master, in this case in medicine and esoteric knowledge. His connections to village associations, Buddhist networks, and healing traditions gave him a base in rural society, especially in Tharrawaddy district, a rice-growing area at the northern edge of the delta with a record of earlier unrest.

In 1930, local organizers, among them members of associations called Galon associations after the mythical garuda that was a symbol of royal power, began to organize in the Tharrawaddy region. Saya San was proclaimed king at a ceremony in October 1930 at a forest site near Tharrawaddy, taking a royal title associated with the Galon; this ceremony, with its use of royal regalia and a hall or palace built for the purpose, is the most strongly emphasized part of colonial accounts. The rebellion began on December 22, 1930, with attacks on police posts and other targets in Tharrawaddy. Insurgents used swords, spears, and some firearms, and many carried amulets and tattoos thought to protect against bullets.

The uprising spread rapidly through the Pegu region and neighboring districts, including Insein and Henzada, and then to other areas, including Prome and Tavoy on the Tenasserim coast, and parts of the Shan States. The colonial authorities responded with police, military units, and special measures, and by 1931 the rebellion had become a sustained counterinsurgency. Saya San was captured in 1931, tried before a special tribunal in which Ba Maw served as his defense counsel, and executed in November 1931. Local fighting continued into 1932.

Two features need emphasis. The first is that the rebellion was geographically uneven: it was concentrated in parts of Lower Burma with certain organizational histories and was not a national uprising. The second is that it combined several kinds of grievance and symbolism. Participants objected to taxes, to local officials, and to the economic hardship of the early depression years. They also drew on religious ideas about kingship, protective magic, and the restoration of a righteous order. The relative weight of these elements has been debated, and no single motive explains all participants. The rebellion should not be reduced to a spontaneous economic protest, nor to a purely religious fantasy; it must be understood as a movement in which material pressures, local organization, and religious imagination were combined.

The sequence of the first outbreaks matters for evaluating claims about leadership. The official government's 1931 report placed the initial violence on the night of 22 December 1930 in the southeastern part of Tharrawaddy district, near the Rangoon–Prome railway. It noted the existence of village associations and political speeches, including opposition to the capitation tax. These observations locate the conflict within identifiable local political relationships. Yet the same report interpreted the district as inherently troublesome and associated political agitation with criminal propensity. Its administrative vocabulary therefore tells historians something about colonial expectations as well as about the conditions of the rebellion.

Rebel mobilization used material and symbolic resources available in villages. Amulets, ritual protection, flags, and stories of legitimate kingship could make collective action intelligible to participants who had not enrolled in a modern party. Their presence does not imply that all insurgents believed they were invulnerable to bullets or that a single religious leader directed every encounter. Some men joined groups through kinship or local pressure; others were motivated by tax claims or anger at police intervention. The military capacity of these groups also differed substantially. Raids against police posts were not equivalent to the possession of a disciplined army able to confront colonial forces on open ground.

The rebellion spread beyond its initial district in uneven episodes. Local leaders could imitate or adopt the Galon label without being in direct contact with Saya San. A historian must therefore reconstruct connections case by case instead of drawing lines of command from the later trial back to every band described by police as a participant. The question is not whether Saya San mattered—he plainly became central to the legal and commemorative history—but how much the complex field of rural resistance can reasonably be explained through one individual.

Section 129.10

The Suppression of the Rebellion and the Historical Debate over Its Meaning

The British response combined military action, police operations, and legal proceedings. Troops, including Indian and Burmese units and reinforcements brought from India, were deployed alongside the military police. Villages suspected of supporting rebels were searched, and many were punished. Special tribunals tried captured insurgents, and large numbers were convicted. Official statements at the time gave figures for those killed, arrested, and sentenced, but the numbers are hard to compare. Government totals of rebels killed run to the order of a thousand or more, arrests are generally given in the thousands, and executions in the low hundreds, but nationalist accounts have claimed higher casualties, and civilian suffering and property destruction cannot be reduced to a single figure. Students should treat all totals as estimates and ask who produced them and why.

The trials were important beyond their immediate results. They produced a body of testimony, police reports, and legal findings that shaped how the rebellion was understood. Colonial officials described a coordinated conspiracy directed by Saya San, who was said to have styled himself king and recruited followers through secret associations, magic, and promises of royal restoration. The official account placed emphasis on millenarian belief, loyalty to a pretender, and criminal conspiracy. Such an account had political advantages: it presented the uprising as a manifestation of backward superstition and minimized the role of economic distress and administrative failures.

Later scholarship has interrogated this account. Earlier historians, writing in the decades after independence, stressed traditional kingship, Buddhist millenarianism, and peasant resentment as causes, and nationalist memory honored Saya San as a patriot who began the armed struggle. Patricia Herbert's reappraisal and Maitrii Aung-Thwin's later book, The Return of the Galon King, challenged the assumption that colonial records provide a straightforward window on the rebellion. Aung-Thwin argues that the legal process, through trials, evidence rules, and official inquiries, helped construct the narrative of a royalist conspiracy centered on Saya San, and that this narrative then entered both colonial and nationalist history. Whether or not one accepts every part of this argument, the central methodological point is widely accepted: documents produced for prosecution and administration must be read for what they reveal about officials' assumptions as well as about rural events.

The result is a more complicated picture. Rural communities participated for varied reasons, including local disputes, taxation, and faith in protective practices. The degree of central direction is uncertain, and the role of Saya San as supreme commander versus symbolic figure is debated. Meanwhile nationalist memory has used the rebellion in different ways: some Dobama leaders honored the rebels, while others criticized the lack of modern organization. The relationship of the rebellion to the urban Dobama movement, founded a few months earlier, was one of coexistence rather than command; the two shared a rejection of colonial rule but not a leadership or a program.

The colonial government's need to explain the uprising shaped the archive it created. Reports passed from district officials to higher authorities, and testimony obtained by police or presented in court could be selected to support charges of conspiracy. When prosecutors linked separate local events into a single organized rebellion, they supplied a model that later textbook narratives sometimes repeated without examining how it had been constructed. At the same time, the official archive preserves valuable evidence: dates, places, the names of defendants, and descriptions of tax arguments. Its usefulness does not depend on accepting every claim of intention or political command.

Legal proceedings also affected the later memory of resistance. The conviction and execution of a prominent defendant offered colonial authorities a visible end to a case, but could give nationalist activists a martyr whose life represented a wider grievance. These interpretations differed not merely in sentiment. The government asked who had committed crimes under existing law; nationalist writers asked why those laws claimed authority over Burmese people and what injustices made resistance plausible. Maitrii Aung-Thwin's analysis shows how the rebellion's received story was shaped by both colonial legal practice and later nationalist remembrance. His argument should be assessed alongside, not substituted for, the testimony of participants and evidence for localized violence.

Statistics must be used carefully. A running military report might count reported rebels killed in particular operations; a judicial register might count convicted persons; a nationalist account might estimate total suffering across villages. The categories, geographical coverage, and time intervals differ. Combining them into one apparent certainty would create a number that no source actually provides. The civilian consequences also included damaged property, disrupted cultivation, family displacement, and fear of collective reprisal—costs that rarely appear in simple counts of combatants. A rigorous narrative therefore discusses repression as a social process, not only as the outcome of a trial or the movement of military units.

Section 129.11

Migration, Communal Tensions, and the Limits of Burmese National Unity

The nationalist movement developed alongside communal violence, and any account of Burmese nationalism must deal with it directly. Migration from India to Burma was large and varied. Seasonal laborers came to work in the docks, rice mills, and fields; clerks and professionals joined the administration; Chettiar bankers financed agriculture; and Muslim and Hindu traders and shopkeepers settled in towns. In Rangoon in the 1930s people of Indian origin were a very large share of the population, and Indian workers dominated dock labor. Chinese merchants and millers, European firms, and Burmese cultivators, traders, and workers made up the rest of an economy in which different communities occupied different niches.

In May 1930, a strike by Indian dockworkers in Rangoon led employers to hire Burmese replacements. When the strike ended, Indian workers returned, and the Burmese who were displaced resented the loss of their jobs. Violence broke out in the city in the last week of May, with Indians attacked in the streets and docks and counter-violence in some areas. Official figures recorded well over a hundred dead and many hundreds injured, though totals are uncertain. Later episodes occurred in 1938, when riots in Rangoon and other places targeted Indian Muslims after a controversy over a book considered insulting to Buddhism, and these are discussed in Section 129.16.

Explaining these events requires attention to structure and to politics. Colonial economic arrangements pitted workers against each other for scarce employment, and employers used ethnic division to manage labor. Colonial classification, which counted populations by race and religion, strengthened the sense that communities were fixed and opposed. Nationalist rhetoric sometimes encouraged hostility, presenting Indian migrants as symbols of colonial exploitation, though others, including some labor organizers and Muslim and Hindu Burmese, rejected such rhetoric. Indian moneylenders were a conspicuous target in the rural debt crisis, but they were one part of a lending system that rested on bank credit, colonial law, and export markets, and most Indians in Burma were laborers, not creditors.

Differences among Indians also mattered: Hindus, Muslims, Sikhs, Tamils, Telugus, and Bengalis had different occupations and ties, and some Indian Muslims intermarried with Burmese and were identified as Burmese Muslims. The violence thus fell on groups with heterogeneous interests. For nationalism, the consequences were mixed. Communal anger could mobilize crowds, but it divided potential allies, strained relations with Indian nationalists, who might otherwise have been partners against British rule, and showed that a Buddhist-centered, Bamar-centered nation excluded significant parts of the colonial society.

To interpret the dock confrontation of 1930, it is necessary to separate the employment dispute from the violence that followed. Indian dockworkers, Burmese workers, contractors, and European employers were not equivalent categories of political actors. Changes in the labor force during a strike could intensify resentment over hiring and wages, but the decision to attack people identified by ethnicity was another act requiring explanation. Rumor, public speeches, colonial policing, and the concentration of workers in specific streets and neighborhoods influenced the scale of the disorder. Official casualty statistics, drawn from particular reporting periods, cannot measure every injury or the longer fear experienced by families.

Colonial categories of race could make economic antagonisms appear natural. Census labels grouped communities whose languages, occupations, legal positions, and religious affiliations differed. Indian people in Burma included landless laborers, clerks, professionals, merchants, police personnel, Muslims, Hindus, and others, while Burmese communities likewise contained employers, creditors, tenants, and wage laborers. Anti-Indian violence did not follow inevitably from immigration. Political actors sometimes promoted the false simplification that all migrants benefited equally from the imperial economy. Other activists tried to build labor solidarity across ethnic lines, a project made difficult by separate hiring networks and the racialized structure of work.

The outbreaks of 1930 and 1938 should not be treated as one continuous episode. The earlier riots followed an identifiable employment dispute; the later disturbances unfolded amid religious controversy, organized protests, and broader parliamentary tensions. Each had distinct immediate triggers, leadership, and patterns of violence. Their comparison nevertheless reveals a recurring weakness in claims to represent a single Burmese nation: religious and ethnic outsiders could be subjected to intimidation or exclusion even when they shared some economic grievances with the nationalist majority. This contradiction shaped minority responses to subsequent demands for a sovereign Burma.

Section 129.12

The Founding of Dobama Asiayone and the Politics of the Thakins

The Dobama Asiayone, "We Burmans Association," was founded on May 30, 1930, in Rangoon, within days of the riots of that month. It was established through the efforts of young activists around Ba Thaung; subsequent leading figures included Ba Sein and Tun Oke, whose prominence should not be retrospectively conflated with the original founding circle. The movement attracted the older writer Thakin Kodaw Hmaing, whose poetry and prestige gave it cultural authority. The founders were mostly educated in Burmese or English, some of them former students, journalists, or teachers. They were dissatisfied with the older GCBA leadership, whom they regarded as divided, cautious, and tied to legal and parliamentary careers.

The association's ideas centered on a few slogans. It insisted that Burma, its literature, and its language belonged to the Burmese, and that love for them was a duty. It rejected colonial subordination and affirmed the aim of national independence, a more radical position than the home-rule demands common in the 1920s. It also adopted a distinctive form of address: members called themselves thakin. In Burmese usage the word means "master" or "lord," and under colonial rule it was the form of address used by Burmese toward Europeans and in some cases toward officials. By taking the title for themselves, the members claimed the position of masters in their own country. The act was both humorous and defiant: it challenged the colonial hierarchy of speech and status.

The Dobama was a new kind of organization because of its style. It emphasized youth, activism, and discipline, held meetings and songs that spread its slogans, wore Burmese dress, and produced pamphlets and newspapers. It recruited not only educated urban men but, over time, workers, villagers, and students. Khin Yi's study of the movement describes how it developed in several stages and how the thakins worked to reach audiences beyond Rangoon. It should not be described as simply a youth wing of the GCBA, nor as the organizational heir of Saya San's rebellion: it began with its own founders, language, and social base.

The movement was also never homogeneous. From early on, members disagreed about whether to work with parliamentary politicians, how to respond to communal tensions, and how far to adopt socialist ideas. These disagreements became more serious in the later 1930s and are the subject of Section 129.13. The founding moment, however, established a vocabulary and an attitude that outlasted the movement's early divisions: a demand for independence, an assertion of cultural pride, and a refusal of colonial deference.

Specialist research by Khin Yi identifies Thakin Ba Thaung as a foundational organizer of Dobama Asiayone. Early associates included other young political writers and activists, but the leadership that became visible during the mid-1930s should not be projected unchanged onto the meeting of May 1930. In particular, Ba Sein and Tun Oke were important in the organization's later political growth and factional divisions. Separating founding personnel from subsequent leaders is necessary for explaining how a small group acquired a broader following rather than assuming it was always a large movement with established national figures.

The decision to call members thakins reversed a daily linguistic relationship. In settings where Europeans and other persons claiming superior status might receive the address, Burmese activists used it to assert that the country's inhabitants were rightful masters of their own land. That choice was symbolic but not purely rhetorical. Members publicly used the title, joined in collective performances, promoted vernacular writing, and criticized customs regarded as signs of inferiority. The association's insistence on language and national products could make anticolonial politics visible in ordinary behavior and not merely in parliamentary discussion.

The word Burman also presented a persistent ambiguity. Some speakers intended an inclusive political identity embracing all residents of the country; others invoked the specific cultural position of the Bamar majority. Slogans about Burma belonging to the Burmese could be read as a demand for independence from Britain, but might also alarm communities uncertain whether they would enjoy equal standing under Bamar-led rule. The later growth of Dobama did not resolve this difficulty. Indeed, the more confidently its leaders spoke for the nation, the more urgently minority and migrant organizations asked whose political claims were being advanced.

Historical Vocabulary II
Thakin, Dobama, nationalism, and political self-definition

Thakin means "master" or "lord." Under colonial rule it was a polite or deferential title used for Europeans and officials. Dobama members adopted it as a name for themselves, turning a mark of subordination into a claim of ownership. Dobama means "we Burmans" (or "our Burma"); the word bama was used by the movement to indicate the people and country, and later usage and translation vary. Nationalism, in the Dobama sense, was the claim that the Burmese people had a right to govern themselves and to define their culture, and its content was disputed among Burmese and was contested by minority communities.

Potential misunderstanding: Thakin was not a class designation, and the movement was not made up of landlords or aristocrats. The title's force lay in its deliberate appropriation as a challenge to colonial hierarchy. Likewise, "Burman" in the Dobama's name should not be read as identical with all citizens of Burma, a distinction that becomes important in Section 129.18.

Dobama Organization, Youth Politics, and the New Nationalist Generation

In the first half of the 1930s the Dobama Asiayone grew slowly and unevenly. It held meetings and annual conferences, published periodicals and pamphlets, and sent organizers to districts outside Rangoon. Its message of independence, Burmese pride, and refusal of deference attracted young teachers, journalists, students, and some workers. Like other nationalist organizations, it was watched by police, and its members were occasionally prosecuted for speeches and writings. The Dobama also developed rituals of solidarity: a distinctive song, slogans, uniforms and dress, and the use of Burmese rather than English in meetings. These practices created a sense of membership among people who might never have met.

Factionalism was persistent. In the mid-1930s the movement divided over whether to take part in elections under the constitution that was being introduced, and over relations with parliamentary politicians. Some leaders, associated with Ba Sein and Tun Oke, were prepared to work through elected bodies, while others insisted on keeping the movement outside the legislature. Personal rivalries, regional bases, and differences over finance and newspapers also played a part. These divisions showed that the Dobama was less a single party than a loose, changing association of groups using the same name and title.

From about 1936 the movement was reshaped by the arrival of university-trained activists. Students who had led the strike of that year, Nu and Aung San among them, took part in Dobama activities, and from 1938 Aung San served as general secretary. Left-wing literature also circulated. The Nagani ("Red Dragon") Book Club, begun in 1937, published translations and original works on socialism, anti-imperialism, and nationalist movements elsewhere, and its books were widely read by young activists. Marxist ideas, together with accounts of Irish, Indian, and Chinese nationalism, gave the thakins new frameworks, and a group of them, including Thakin Soe and Thakin Than Tun, moved toward communism. The movement's expansion beyond its original membership meant that, by 1938, it included people with very different ideas about class, religion, and the role of Burmese culture in politics.

This variety should not be overlooked, since later memory sometimes presents the Dobama as a unified vanguard. The movement included cultural nationalists, socialists, communists, constitutionalists, and those whose primary interests were local. What united them was a shared repertoire: the title thakin, the slogan of independence, and the habit of challenging colonial hierarchies. How far these elements amounted to a common program was a question the movement itself would face repeatedly.

The movement's internal politics became more complicated as it expanded. A district branch concerned with local prices or educational access did not necessarily share the priorities of student intellectuals reading socialist literature in Rangoon. Organizers disagreed about the value of elections, alliances with constitutional politicians, the proper use of street demonstrations, and the tone of speeches about British and Indian residents. Such differences could be accommodated temporarily by a common nationalist vocabulary, but became sharper when leaders had to choose candidates, authorize strikes, or negotiate with the government.

Newspapers, songs, pamphlets, and public ceremonies helped convert scattered members into a recognizable association. Political language could travel beyond the membership roll: a school student might call himself a thakin before joining any central committee, and a villager could adopt a boycott without understanding all the disputes within the movement. The same circulation made discipline difficult. A central leadership could claim to speak for branches while local speakers acted independently. A modern political movement therefore had to solve questions of representation that earlier cultural associations had encountered on a smaller scale.

The approach to workers and peasants was especially important. Some leaders initially emphasized cultural pride and the restoration of dignity; others sought a program concerned with class inequality, wages, and land. These emphases were not mutually exclusive, but they required different forms of organization. Socialist reading circles did not automatically become industrial unions, and public nationalist gatherings did not by themselves make village committees durable. Khin Yi's account of later factional division helps show why the movement's rapid growth was accompanied by recurrent disputes over its identity. Such tensions should not be interpreted as evidence that nationalism lacked seriousness: they were consequences of bringing distinct constituencies into one political arena.

The Student Strike of 1936 and the Emergence of Aung San and U Nu

By the mid-1930s Rangoon University had a lively student culture, with a Students' Union, hostels, debating societies, and magazines. Students came from varied backgrounds, and many of those active in politics were from provincial towns and modest families. The university's publications included Oway ("Peacock's Call"), the Students' Union magazine, edited in 1936 by Aung San. In early 1936 the magazine published a satirical article attacking a senior university official, titled in English "Hell Hound at Large," that the university considered insulting. When the university demanded to know who the author was, the union leaders refused to disclose the name.

The university expelled two student leaders, Nu, who was president of the Students' Union, and Aung San, the magazine's editor. The expulsions came in early 1936 and prompted a strike beginning in late February, when students walked out of classes and gathered, as in 1920, at the Shwedagon Pagoda. The strike spread to Mandalay University College and to schools, and public figures, including politicians and monks, supported it. The authorities, wary of a repeat of 1920, negotiated; the expelled students were allowed to return, and a committee investigated the university's administration. The outcome was a partial victory for the students, and the strike brought Nu and Aung San to national attention.

What was new in 1936 was the organization and the generation. The students who led the strike were not seeking a place within the existing political order. They had read widely, formed networks across colleges, and created an All Burma Students' Union that linked schools and colleges nationwide. Aung San became president of the Rangoon University Students' Union and a prominent figure in student politics. Nu, a writer as well as a student leader, helped to link student politics to Burmese-language literature. Both soon became involved in the Dobama movement, and by 1938 they were active thakins.

The strike should be seen within the longer history of student politics rather than as the beginning of Aung San's career alone. Its leaders could draw on the model of 1920, on the associations and newspapers of earlier years, and on a Burmese-language public that had grown since the boycott. Aung San was one of a cohort, including Nu, Kyaw Nyein, Ba Swe, Than Tun, and others, whose experiences of university politics shaped their later roles. His ability as an organizer and speaker was real, but his prominence depended on the networks and traditions he joined.

The episode centered on the magazine Oway revealed the political possibilities of student print culture. A satirical article with the English title Hell Hound at Large attacked university authority; the decision to hold an editor responsible made the issue one of institutional control over student speech. Aung San's refusal to identify the contributor defended editorial responsibility and the solidarity of the union, although students did not all interpret his action in the same way. U Nu's disciplinary dispute arose from his own confrontations with the university authorities and should not simply be described as punishment for writing the article. The expulsions brought separate grievances into a common protest.

The strike widened students' political education by demanding organizational labor. Committees had to communicate with colleges beyond Rangoon, arrange meetings, respond to parents, and explain demands to the general public. This created a network that proved valuable when student leaders moved into Dobama or labor politics. The university was simultaneously a colonial institution through which a restricted group acquired professional advantages and a site where that group learned to challenge authority. The paradox resembles the 1920 boycott, but the political environment had changed: by 1936, socialist ideas, nationalist newspapers, and more developed associations offered students additional paths into public life.

Aung San and Nu did not become national leaders because their expulsion automatically made them popular. They acquired influence through arguments, organizational positions, contacts with fellow activists, and subsequent choices. Contemporary student accounts and later biographies should therefore be compared. Retrospective histories often organize the strike as the first scene in Aung San's rise, but the union contained multiple speakers and dissenting views. The event mattered most because it institutionalized the idea that students could act collectively on matters extending well beyond examinations and campus discipline.

Section 129.15

The Oilfield Strikes and the Rise of Organized Labor, 1938–1939

Burma's petroleum industry, centered at Yenangyaung and Chauk in the dry zone and dominated by the Burmah Oil Company, employed thousands of workers in drilling, maintenance, and transport. Skilled and supervisory positions were often held by Europeans and Indians, and many Burmese workers held lower-paid positions and worked under difficult conditions. Housing, wages, hours, and safety were frequent grievances, and the company town structure gave employers considerable control over daily life. Labor organization before 1938 was weak, but in the later 1930s there was growing interest in unions, aided by thakins who saw labor as a base for nationalism.

The prolonged strike began at Chauk in January 1938, then spread and intensified before the late-year march toward Rangoon. Thakin Po Hla Gyi and other organizers helped to form strike committees and to articulate demands, which included higher wages and improved working conditions and housing, as well as recognition of workers' organization. The strikers also received support from nearby villagers and from students and nationalists. Some workers set out on foot on a march toward Rangoon, going down the Irrawaddy valley, receiving food and shelter in towns and villages en route. The march gave the strike a public dimension: workers appeared as a visible national force. The police arrested leaders and attempted to disperse marchers, and in some places there were confrontations.

The oilfield strike mattered because it connected industrial labor to nationalist politics. Earlier nationalist movements had been mostly urban, professional, or rural. Here was an organized industrial workforce, mixed in ethnicity but with a substantial Burmese component, making economic demands against a major foreign company and being supported by nationalists who tied those demands to national claims. The company's response and the government's attention to law and order showed that officials saw such alliances as threats. The thakins gained experience of mass organization, and labor became a new constituency, though its relations with the nationalist leadership were not without friction.

The chronological starting point requires correction: the prolonged Chauk oilfield strike began in January 1938, not at the end of that year. Its escalation and the celebrated march toward Rangoon came later. Thakin Po Hla Gyi's contemporary pamphlet The Strike War, available in a modern translation, preserves an unusually detailed list of demands. Workers asked for changes to daily and monthly pay, leave, housing conditions, medical provision, the running of company schools, and the length of the workday. One demand specified an eight-hour rather than a twelve-hour day. Others concerned the dismissal of an activist and the treatment of workers' families. These particulars show that the strike was grounded in concrete industrial relations rather than merely an abstract call for independence.

Industrial geography gave the strike national significance. The dry-zone oilfields were tied to the capital by transport routes that workers and their supporters could use to spread news and seek assistance. A marching body of workers was also a moving political event: supporters supplied food and shelter, local organizations staged receptions, and police could monitor or restrict passage. The journey linked an industrial labor dispute to villages and towns beyond the immediate workplaces. It made the workers visible to people who had no direct connection with petroleum and gave nationalist leaders a cause around which to campaign.

The pamphlet also records claims about women workers and relatives who faced arrest and physical mistreatment, reminding readers that an industrial strike was a household crisis. When men stopped earning wages, family members had to preserve food, manage debts, and participate in relief arrangements. Some women picketed or supported the association and became targets of policing. Not every worker approved of continuing the stoppage, and surviving activist texts cannot reveal the private views of non-strikers. Yet the demands show a form of political consciousness rooted in work, social welfare, and demands for respectful treatment. When such claims were connected to Dobama nationalism, a labor organization helped redefine independence as a question of social and economic authority.

The demands are therefore best interpreted through both industrial bargaining and a broader critique of colonial economic power.

Section 129.16

The Revolution of 1300 and the National Political Crisis

The upheavals of 1938–1939 are called the Revolution of 1300 because the Burmese Era year 1300 corresponds to 1938–1939 CE. The name conveys the sense that the period marked a turning point, although the events did not amount to a revolution in the sense of a seizure of state power. They involved a series of connected disturbances: the communal violence of July 1938, student protests, the oilfield strikes, and demonstrations that spread to Rangoon, Mandalay, and other towns.

The year began with the riots of July 1938, in which crowds attacked Indian Muslims in Rangoon and elsewhere after protests over a book considered insulting to Buddhism. The violence was serious, and an official inquiry examined it. Historians differ about its causes, including the role of religious feeling, economic competition, and the use of the controversy by political rivals to embarrass the Ba Maw ministry. The riots damaged the government's standing and showed the fragility of parliamentary arrangements.

In the closing months of 1938 student and labor protest intensified. In December, student demonstrators in Rangoon marched toward the Secretariat and the legislature, and police confronted them. A student, Aung Kyaw, was killed, and his death was widely mourned and remembered as a martyrdom. The government's use of force angered students and the public and gave a symbol to the movement. In early 1939 in Mandalay, police fired on demonstrators, killing and wounding a number of people, among them monks, and further protests followed. Ba Maw's government, pressed by opposition inside the legislature and demonstrations outside, lost its majority, and in February 1939 U Pu replaced him as Premier.

The consequences were mixed. The protests brought down a ministry, showed that students, workers, monks, and nationalists could act together, and strengthened the thakins' reputation. They did not, however, produce a transfer of power or constitutional change. The government retained its authority and used arrests to contain the movement; the new Premier's ministry was equally constrained. What had changed was the political atmosphere: the use of force against students and monks damaged the claim of the British administration to be a lawful, benevolent government, and the idea of independence as an immediate objective gained ground.

The relation among the protests is best understood as an alliance in motion rather than a single centralized command. University students could assemble in large groups and attract newspaper coverage; oil workers could disrupt production and bring economic pressure; monks could confer moral importance on a demonstration; and political parties could attempt to turn these actions into demands on the ministry. Their objectives sometimes converged, but each constituency retained its own interests. Attempts to create one national campaign could also expose disagreements about violence, communal solidarity, and the priority of labor reform compared with immediate independence.

When students encountered police at the Secretariat in December 1938, the resulting death of Aung Kyaw generated a powerful memorial culture. Processions, funeral rites, and speeches used his death to question the claim that the colonial state offered orderly channels for peaceful dissent. His story should be told as that of a person whose death carried consequences for others, not simply as an inevitable sacrifice on the path to independence. Some activists favored further confrontation; others feared that continued disorder would strengthen the governor's powers and weaken attempts at parliamentary compromise.

In Mandalay in February 1939, demonstrations involving monks, students, and townspeople ended with police firing on crowds and the deaths of seventeen people in accounts of the incident. Sources differ about the exact sequencing between meetings, arrests, and the shooting, so the narrative should distinguish established circumstances from disputed dates. The episode demonstrates why nationalism could draw support outside the port capital, but it also illustrates the difficulties of assigning responsibility: officials justified restrictions in terms of public order, while demonstrators regarded them as violations of political rights. The Revolution of 1300 weakened Ba Maw's ministry, yet it was not a transfer of sovereignty. The British governor and coercive apparatus remained in place.

Separation from India, the 1935 Government of Burma Act, and Parliamentary Politics

The question of whether Burma should remain part of British India was one of the main constitutional issues of the 1920s and 1930s. Burmese opinion was divided. Some argued that separation would free Burma from Indian administration and allow it to develop its own institutions. Others, fearing that a separate Burma would receive less advanced constitutional status than India or that separation was a British device to weaken nationalism by dividing Indian and Burmese opponents of the Raj, preferred to stay within India or to press for a better constitutional offer first. Minority communities and business interests, including Europeans and Indians in Burma, had their own concerns. The Burma Round Table Conference held in London in 1931–1932 brought delegates from Burma, including Daw Mya Sein, to discuss separation and constitutional safeguards. A general election in 1932 turned largely on the issue, and anti-separationists won, but the British government went ahead with plans for a separate Burma.

The Government of Burma Act of 1935, enacted on August 2, created a separate constitution that took effect on April 1, 1937. It established a bicameral legislature, a Senate and a House of Representatives, with a ministry responsible to the House in a wide range of domestic matters, and it retained a Governor with substantial reserved powers. Defence, external affairs, currency and certain financial matters, and the administration of the Frontier Areas remained under the Governor and ultimately the British Crown, and the Governor had special responsibilities for protecting minorities and the rights of the services. A separate Burma Office in London replaced the India Office's role. The franchise was extended beyond the earlier limits, though not universally.

It is essential to be clear about what this meant. Separation from India was not independence from Britain. Burma remained a British colony with a partly elected government. Ministers were responsible for matters such as education, health, and agriculture, but the Governor could override them in areas of reserved authority, and the financial and security structures were controlled from outside. Many nationalists, particularly the Dobama thakins, regarded the constitution as a device for continued control, while parliamentary politicians used it as a platform for reform.

The 1936 election was fought among competing parties and factions, among them groups descended from the GCBA, Ba Maw's Sinyetha ("Poor Man's") Party, and independents. No party won an outright majority in the House. Ba Maw, a lawyer and former minister who had defended Saya San, became the first Premier in April 1937, heading a coalition. His ministry was unstable. It had to manage factional rivalries, pressure from the thakins and students, the communal violence of 1938, and the limits imposed by the Governor's powers. In his political career before the war, Ba Maw should be distinguished from his later role during the Japanese occupation, which is treated in Chapter 136. As Premier he was a constitutional politician operating within the colonial system, and his ministry fell in February 1939 after the upheavals described in Section 129.16.

The 1935 Government of Burma Act made constitutional change both tangible and visibly limited. The legislature was bicameral, with a House of Representatives and a Senate; the governor retained significant responsibilities and special powers. The statute allocated seats through a combination of general, communal, and functional constituencies, including categories for Karens, Indians, labor, commercial interests, and Rangoon University. Such arrangements were defended as protections for distinct interests. Critics could argue that they also encouraged political communities to compete for separate recognition within an institution whose ultimate authority remained imperial. The constitutional design therefore reflected British judgments about society rather than a freely negotiated agreement among all its inhabitants.

Election campaigns exposed the relationship between political parties and local networks. A party could promise reforms but still depend on recognizable candidates, financing, district notables, and bargains with other groups. The 1936 elections produced no single majority. Ba Maw's Sinyetha organization won a minority of seats and he formed a ministry through coalition arrangements. His claim to speak for poorer people had to be tested against the actual policies his government could finance and the pressures it faced from landlords, business interests, workers, and the governor. The word premier should not obscure these dependencies; nor should the existence of an elected ministry be equated with sovereignty.

Separation in April 1937 removed Burma from the formal administrative framework of British India but retained the rule of the British Crown. For some nationalists, this represented a significant opening for indigenous political action. Others regarded it as an imperial rearrangement that left defense, international affairs, emergency authority, and major minority territories outside effective ministerial control. The merits of parliamentary participation thus changed in scale, not in kind: elected politicians could do more, but they still could not answer the central question of who possessed final political authority.

Section 129.18

Women, Ethnic Communities, and Competing Visions of the Burmese Nation

Women were active participants in colonial Burmese public life, and not only as supporters of male leaders. Burmese women had long held economic roles in trade and household management and enjoyed legal rights in marriage and property under customary law that were in some respects greater than those of women in many neighboring societies. In the early twentieth century women also entered education, journalism, and associations. Chie Ikeya's research shows that debates over women, education, marriage, and modernity were central to political and cultural argument, and that women were both subjects and participants in those debates. Women formed patriotic associations in the 1920s, joined the wunthanu movement, wrote for periodicals, and took part in strikes and demonstrations. Daw Mya Sein, educated in Britain, was a delegate to the Round Table Conference in 1931–1932, and became a teacher and writer. A recurring controversy concerned marriages between Burmese Buddhist women and foreign, especially Indian Muslim, men, which nationalists and religious leaders debated in terms of religion, property, and national identity. Women's own voices in these debates are documented unevenly, but they show that gender was an arena of political contest.

The nation's boundaries were also contested along ethnic and religious lines. The Dobama's name and rhetoric were Bamar-centered, and the movement's leadership came largely from the Bamar Buddhist majority. Other communities developed their own organizations and priorities. The Karen National Association, founded in 1881, sought representation, educational opportunities, and recognition of Karen interests, and many Karens, among them Baptists and Catholics as well as Buddhists, were wary of a government dominated by the Bamar majority. Some Karen leaders argued for reserved representation in the legislature and cooperation with British authorities as a safeguard; others wanted greater autonomy; and some Karens supported Burmese nationalism. The 1935 act provided reserved seats for Karens and other groups, and these arrangements added to the complexity of constitutional debate.

In the Frontier Areas, Shan, Kachin, Chin, and other peoples lived under hereditary leaders and special regulations, and nationalist politics in the plains had little organization there. Their leaders' views of future relations with ministerial Burma differed by area and over time. Indian, Chinese, Anglo-Burman, and European communities had their own representation and concerns. The result was that "Burmese nationalism," as expressed by the main movements studied in this chapter, represented one vision of the nation among several. Later chapters on independence and civil war, beginning with Chapter 154, show how these unresolved issues shaped postwar politics.

Women's political participation was shaped by practical institutions as well as public speeches. Women managed subscriptions, raised relief funds for strikers' families, supported national schools, joined boycott committees, and increasingly addressed meetings. These roles could be praised by male nationalist leaders as evidence of patriotic duty while still leaving women excluded from decision-making. The distinction between mobilizing women and sharing authority with them is therefore central. In Chie Ikeya's account, debates about education, dress, marriage, and professional opportunities were connected to the wider question of whether modernity would reproduce colonial and patriarchal hierarchy or enlarge personal freedom.

Daw Mya Sein's participation in the constitutional negotiations in London during 1931–1932 illustrates both opportunity and its narrow social basis. Her education and public position enabled her to address questions that would have remained inaccessible to many rural women. She should be evaluated for her arguments and political participation, not merely identified as a notable woman present among male delegates. Women engaged with constitutional issues that affected family law, education, employment, and representation, but no single spokeswoman could represent Burmese women of different classes, communities, and regions.

Among minority groups, the experience of empire could invert the mainstream nationalist story. Christian Karens, for example, sometimes viewed British educational or military institutions as sources of security or advancement, even while other Karen organizations pursued their own claims to national recognition. Shan rulers had interests in preserving regional authority, and communities in Kachin and Chin areas lived under different administrative rules from the lowlands. Such differences were not evidence that minorities lacked political awareness. They shaped different diagnoses of the risks and possibilities of independence. A national movement unable to negotiate these claims faced a problem of legitimacy that the departure of British officials alone would not solve.

Section 129.19

Aung San, Left-Wing Nationalism, and the Formation of the Freedom Bloc

By 1939 the nationalist movement had changed. The strikes and protests of the previous year had strengthened the thakins, and many of them looked to socialism and communism for analysis and strategy. Study groups and the Nagani Book Club circulated writings on Marxism, the Russian Revolution, and anticolonial movements elsewhere. In 1939 Thakin Soe, Aung San, Than Tun, and other leaders founded the Communist Party of Burma, with Aung San as its first general secretary, and within weeks a separate organization, the Burma Revolutionary Party (also called the People's Revolutionary Party), was established under the leadership of Aung San and others who wished to focus on national independence. The two groups overlapped in membership and aims but had different emphases. Aung San's political outlook combined nationalism with socialist and anti-fascist ideas, and its development was still in progress.

Aung San's role should be understood in context. He was one of several leaders of a generation formed by student politics and Dobama, and he worked alongside Nu, Than Tun, Soe, Thein Pe, Ba Hein, and others. His strengths were organizational energy and a capacity for political strategy, and by 1939 he was a leading figure among the younger thakins. He did not create the movement in which he worked, whose organizational and ideological foundations had been laid by the YMBA and GCBA, the wunthanu associations, the student strikes, and the Dobama in the preceding three decades.

Meanwhile, constitutional and radical nationalists sought cooperation. After the fall of Ba Maw's ministry in February 1939, he remained a prominent figure in the legislature and in opposition to the government. When war broke out in Europe in September 1939, the question arose of Burma's role. The Governor and the ministry supported the British war effort, but many nationalists argued that Burma had not been consulted and should not fight for Britain without a promise of freedom. In October 1939 Ba Maw's Sinyetha Party, the Dobama and its thakins, student organizations, and political monks formed the Freedom Bloc. Its demands included a declaration of Burma's right to independence after the war, and it opposed unconditional support for the war effort. The coalition brought together groups with differing outlooks: Ba Maw was a parliamentarian and the thakins were revolutionaries, and the alliance reflected tactical convergence rather than shared doctrine.

The spread of left-wing reading circles did not amount to a unified communist movement. Socialist, Marxist, and anti-imperialist literature arrived through translations, newspaper debate, study groups, and the Nagani Book Club, which began publication in 1937. The appeal of such writing lay partly in its promise to connect national subordination with the condition of tenants and wage workers. Readers could admire a socialist critique of exploitation without agreeing about whether Burma should immediately adopt a communist party, build labor unions, or organize a broad front for independence. These differences soon became institutional as activists established separate political organizations.

Aung San's emergence should be understood within those networks. His experience of the student movement supplied contacts and skills, while Dobama offered a national platform and access to political figures outside the university. In August 1939 he participated in the founding of the Communist Party of Burma; the Burma Revolutionary Party developed later that year. Neither body exhausted the range of radical nationalists, and both operated amid intense pressure from the colonial state. The appearance of these parties revealed that leaders were debating social ownership, international solidarity, labor organization, and political discipline as part of the independence question.

The Freedom Bloc offered another form of organization. With Ba Maw as a central figure, it joined elements of constitutional politics to Dobama activists and other allies in a demand that Burma's war policy should not be determined unconditionally by Britain. Cooperation was tactically valuable but vulnerable: parliamentary politicians and younger militants differed over whether to campaign for concessions, organize civil resistance, or prepare for the breakdown of the imperial order. Such disagreements became urgent after September 1939, when emergency powers narrowed the legal space for protest and increased the costs of remaining publicly associated with the movement.

Historical Vocabulary III
Freedom Bloc, socialism, communism, and constitutional nationalism

The Freedom Bloc was the coalition formed in late 1939 that united Ba Maw's Sinyetha Party, the Dobama thakins, and allied organizations in demanding independence and refusing unconditional support for Britain's war. Socialism in this context meant a family of ideas stressing economic equality, collective ownership, and opposition to imperialism, drawn from European and Asian writings available through the Nagani Book Club. Communism refers more specifically to Marxist-Leninist organization, as in the Communist Party of Burma founded in 1939. Constitutional nationalism was the strategy of pursuing self-government through legislatures, elections, and negotiation within the colonial legal order.

Potential misunderstanding: These categories overlapped in practice. Many thakins combined nationalist, socialist, and sometimes communist ideas, and constitutionalists such as Ba Maw also demanded independence. The labels describe tendencies, not fixed party memberships.

Section 129.20

World War II and the Collapse of British Colonial Legitimacy

The outbreak of war in 1939 found the Burmese political scene unsettled. The ministry headed by U Pu from February 1939, and then by U Saw from September 1940, cooperated with the Governor, while the Freedom Bloc and the thakins demanded a statement of British intentions. London was unwilling to commit to an early grant of independence or even dominion status on a fixed date. Britain's war aims were publicly framed in terms of freedom, and Burmese nationalists pointed out the contradiction between those aims and the denial of self-government to Burma. The government's response was to expand emergency powers, using the Defence of Burma Rules to restrict speech and assembly and to detain activists.

In 1940 the authorities arrested a number of nationalist leaders. Ba Maw, accused of sedition for anti-war speeches, was imprisoned, as were a number of thakins. Others went underground or abroad. Aung San left Burma in August 1940, seeking support for the nationalist cause from outside, an action that opens the story of Chapter 136 and is not discussed further here. By 1940 constitutional compromise appeared exhausted from the standpoint of the radical nationalists, although the parliamentary ministry continued to function and many Burmese, including Karens, Kachins, and others, remained willing to cooperate with the government, and the army and police were still largely reliable.

Colonial legitimacy had thus weakened, though not collapsed in the sense of an immediate loss of control. It had been undermined by the exclusion of Burma from early reforms, the narrow franchise and limited powers of dyarchy and the 1937 constitution, the prosecution of monks and the execution and imprisonment of rebels, the shooting of students and demonstrators, and the contradiction of fighting a war for freedom while denying it. At the same time, nationalism was divided over strategy, ethnicity, religion, and ideology, and the government retained military and administrative capacity. The outcome was an unresolved political situation on the eve of Japan's entry into the war in December 1941.

The chapter's concluding argument is that British rule confronted a movement increasingly capable of mobilizing different social groups, including monks, students, workers, villagers, parliamentarians, and women, though divided by strategy, ethnicity, religion, economic interest, and competing definitions of independence. The importance of the prewar nationalist movement lies not simply in the rise of Aung San but in the organizations, vocabularies, and experiences that made independence a widely contested objective. How those foundations were used and transformed under Japanese occupation is the subject of Chapter 136.

War transformed the meaning of an independence campaign. Before 1939, nationalists could debate boycotting elections, obtaining stronger safeguards, or persuading London to revise the constitutional system. Once Britain entered a global conflict, the colonial government claimed expanded powers of censorship, detention, and political restriction. Activists who treated war as an opportunity to demand immediate independence confronted the charge that their campaigns undermined imperial security. Yet they could answer that the population had not consented to the decision to join the war. This made the question of sovereignty concrete: who had the authority to commit Burma's people and resources to an international conflict?

The state's response was not uniform. Constitutional politicians could still negotiate or seek office, while radicals faced surveillance and detention. Some nationalists remained skeptical of foreign assistance and worried that opposing Britain might expose Burma to an even more coercive empire. Others began to look beyond constitutional action because the war appeared to have closed ordinary political channels. Aung San's departure from Burma in August 1940 belongs at this threshold, but it should not be narrated as proof that a Japanese alliance was predetermined or universally approved. The detailed choices and consequences of wartime organization belong in Chapter 136.

By the end of 1940 the nationalist movement possessed durable assets: networks of committees inherited from Buddhist associations, student unions capable of mobilizing schools, journalists and publishers who had cultivated a political reading public, rural and industrial activists, and leaders experienced in elections and protest. It also carried deep vulnerabilities. Communal violence had exposed the exclusionary possibilities of majoritarian rhetoric; factional arguments weakened organizations; and minority political claims remained unresolved. British constitutional reform had created limited channels for participation but had not answered the demand for final authority. That combination, rather than any inevitable collapse of empire, explains why the prewar order was under such strain.

Chapter Summary

Burmese nationalism before 1941 developed through overlapping movements rather than a single organization. The colonial conquest of 1885–1886 destroyed the Konbaung monarchy, left the Buddhist monastic community without its royal patron, and placed Burma within British India. The export rice economy, the legal and fiscal system, and the classification of peoples by race and religion created the conditions in which political associations arose.

The first durable organization was the Young Men's Buddhist Association of 1906, which began as a cultural and educational society. The footwear controversy and Britain's exclusion of Burma from the post-war reforms moved its network into politics, and in 1920 the General Council of Burmese Associations took shape. Its village associations, the wunthanu athin, carried noncooperation into rural society, although the movement was weakened by the split over the dyarchy constitution of 1923.

Buddhist monks occupied a contested place in this politics. The prosecution of U Ottama and the death of U Wisara in 1929 made imprisoned monks into symbols, but the monastic community was not politically uniform, and from the 1930s leadership passed increasingly to students and secular activists.

The Rangoon University boycott of 1920 inaugurated student nationalism and led to national schools, which were influential but financially fragile. The strike of 1936 produced a new student leadership, including Nu and Aung San, and a national student union. The comparison shows both continuity, in the use of the Shwedagon and the language of dignity, and change, in organization and ideology.

The Saya San rebellion of 1930–1932 was the largest rural uprising of the period. It arose from the structural strains of debt, tenancy, taxation, and depression-era price decline, but it needed local organization, leadership, and religious imagination to occur. Colonial investigators and courts shaped its record, and recent scholarship, notably Maitrii Aung-Thwin's, has stressed that official accounts must be read critically. The rebellion and the urban Dobama movement, founded a few months earlier, were distinct movements that shared a rejection of colonial rule.

The riots of 1930 and 1938 show how economic competition, colonial classification, and nationalist rhetoric combined in communal violence, and why simple blame of Indian migrants as a group obscures the structure of colonial capitalism and the diversity of the people affected. The Dobama's adoption of thakin challenged colonial hierarchy, but the movement was itself divided between cooperation and boycott, cultural and socialist currents, and moderate and radical leaders.

The oilfield strikes and the Revolution of 1300 in 1938–1939 brought labor, students, monks, and nationalists into direct confrontation with the colonial government, and the death of Aung Kyaw became a symbol of resistance. The protests helped to bring down the Ba Maw ministry but did not change the constitution, showing both the power and the limits of mass mobilization.

The Government of Burma Act of 1935 separated Burma from India in April 1937 and created a legislature and ministry, but retained wide reserved powers. Separation was not independence. Women's associations, Karen organizations, and the peoples of the Frontier Areas show that the nation was defined in competing ways and that Bamar-centered nationalism was not the only vision.

By 1939–1940, left-wing parties had formed, the Freedom Bloc united parliamentary and radical nationalists, and the outbreak of war posed the question of Burma's participation. The arrests of 1940 and Aung San's departure ended the period of constitutional compromise. British legitimacy had been profoundly weakened, though authority had not collapsed and the outcome was not inevitable.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows how a nationalist movement can be built from unlikely materials: a lay Buddhist association, a university dispute, village committees, a rural uprising, and a strike in the oilfields. None of these alone produced the demand for independence, and they were not always allies. Their significance lies in the accumulation of organizational experience, shared symbols, and public confrontation with a government that could grant limited reform but could not secure consent.

The chapter also demonstrates why careful reading of sources matters. Colonial reports, nationalist memoirs, and later scholarship each describe the same events through different assumptions. The history of the Saya San rebellion in particular shows how legal and administrative processes can shape the record, and why historians must ask how a narrative came to exist as well as what it claims.

The unresolved questions of this period carried forward. The relationship between Bamar nationalism and minority political aspirations, the divisions between constitutionalists and revolutionaries, and the place of religion in public life all shaped the wartime and postwar years. Chapter 136 follows the nationalist movement into the Japanese occupation, and later chapters on independence and civil war return to the questions of nation and state that were left open in 1940.

For the textbook as a whole, the chapter contributes a case in which Southeast Asians acted as historical agents in shaping the terms of colonial politics. It also offers a comparison with Indonesia and Vietnam, where nationalist movements took different organizational forms, and prepares for the study of how the Second World War altered the possibilities open to each.
