The Pyu City-States

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Behind the brick walls of Sri Ksetra, near the modern city of Pyay in central Myanmar, archaeologists have traced fortifications enclosing an urban area of roughly fourteen square kilometers, among the largest of any first-millennium settlement anywhere in Southeast Asia. No continuous, locally authored chronicle explains who organized these walls, how political authority actually worked within and among the Pyu cities, or how the city's rulers understood their place in the wider world; even a name for the city itself survives only indirectly, through a Prakrit form, Siri Khetta, preserved on an inscribed bronze ring associated with the site. Almost everything historians know about the society that built Sri Ksetra, and its sister cities at Beikthano and Halin, comes from archaeology, inscriptions, scattered Buddhist relics, and brief, often puzzled descriptions written by outsiders.

The lands historians now call the Pyu world lie along the Irrawaddy and its tributaries in the central dry zone of present-day Myanmar, a landscape of seasonal rainfall, fertile alluvial soils near the rivers, and drier plains beyond them. Between roughly the late first millennium BCE, with securely dated urban and monumental evidence extending back to about the second century BCE, and the ninth century CE, this region became home to a cluster of substantial walled cities, commonly grouped together by modern scholars under the label "Pyu city-states." These cities were not villages that gradually grew large by accident. Their builders dug moats, raised massive brick and earthen walls, laid out street patterns and monumental precincts, and organized the surrounding countryside to feed thousands of urban residents. They built Buddhist stupas and monasteries whose ruins still dominate the landscape, buried at least some of their dead in a small but historically important group of inscribed funerary urns, and left behind inscriptions in a distinctive script adapted from South Asian writing systems.

Chapter 23 examined how the Red River delta developed under a millennium of direct Chinese imperial administration, a history shaped by conquest, resistance, and eventual independent Vietnamese kingship. The Pyu world followed an entirely different trajectory. No outside empire directly annexed and administered the Irrawaddy basin during this period. Instead, communities living along the river built their own urban centers, adopted and transformed religious and administrative ideas reaching them chiefly by sea and overland routes from South Asia, and maintained relationships, sometimes tense and sometimes cooperative, with Chinese courts to their northeast and with other Southeast Asian societies. This chapter treats the Pyu world on its own terms, as a genuinely important episode in early Southeast Asian urban and religious history, not as a mere preface to the later kingdom of Pagan.

That last point deserves emphasis before the chapter proceeds further. It is tempting, looking backward from the eleventh-century kingdom of Pagan, examined in Chapters 40 and 41, to read Pyu history as though it existed only to prepare the way for a unified "Burmese" state. The evidence does not support that framing. Pyu-speaking communities built cities, adapted Buddhism, and maintained regional and international connections for the better part of a millennium, and for most of that time no evidence suggests they were moving toward, anticipating, or even imagining a future political order resembling Pagan. The relationship between the Pyu urban world and the kingdom that eventually dominated the same river valley was real, but it was neither inevitable nor simple, and this chapter is careful to describe continuity and transformation rather than destiny.

Much of what can be said about the Pyu world rests on a genuinely difficult evidentiary foundation. No continuous Pyu chronicle survives describing dynasties, wars, or successions in the way that later Burmese or Chinese court records sometimes do for other societies. What survives instead is a patchwork: excavated city walls and monuments, funerary urns bearing brief inscriptions, fragments of Buddhist scripture inscribed on gold and silver sheets, scattered references in Chinese dynastic histories written by outsiders who visited or heard about the region, and the physical remains of trade goods that traveled along Irrawaddy and overland routes. Piecing together Pyu history therefore requires the same careful, source-critical method that earlier chapters applied to Funan, Chenla, and Champa: distinguishing archaeological evidence from later textual tradition, treating outside accounts as products of their own observers' interests, and resisting the temptation to fill gaps with confident narrative where the evidence will not support it.

This chapter asks students to hold two ideas together. First, the Pyu cities were a substantial, sophisticated, and historically significant urban civilization, one of the earliest in mainland Southeast Asia to combine large fortified settlements, monumental Buddhist architecture, a working system of writing, and participation in trans-regional networks connecting South Asia, China, and the wider Southeast Asian world. Second, the Pyu cities remain only partially understood, and honest history requires acknowledging what the evidence cannot yet tell us: whether the major cities formed a single kingdom, a shifting confederation, or a set of independent centers that rose and fell in relative importance over time. The chapter that follows takes up both of these commitments across the Irrawaddy basin's earliest cities, their economy, religion, writing, and international connections, before examining the transformations of the ninth century that reshaped, but did not simply erase, the Pyu urban world.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain what archaeological and textual evidence identifies "the Pyu" as a historical population and language community in the Irrawaddy basin.
  2. Describe the physical geography of the Irrawaddy basin and central dry zone and explain how it shaped, without determining, Pyu urban settlement.
  3. Compare the major Pyu urban centers of Beikthano, Sri Ksetra, and Halin in terms of scale, fortification, chronology, and material culture.
  4. Analyze the historiographical debate over whether the Pyu cities constituted a single kingdom, a shifting hegemony, or a set of largely independent city-states.
  5. Describe Pyu city planning, including walls, gates, moats, water management, and the relationship between enclosed urban cores and surrounding agricultural populations.
  6. Explain the agricultural and economic foundations that sustained large Pyu urban populations.
  7. Trace the development of Buddhism within Pyu society and evaluate evidence for religious plurality alongside Buddhist practice.
  8. Apply a localization framework to Pyu adoption of South Asian scripts, religious ideas, and artistic forms, rather than a passive "Indianization" model.
  9. Interpret Pyu inscriptions and evaluate what epigraphic evidence can and cannot establish about Pyu political and social life.
  10. Assess the significance and limitations of Chinese textual references to the Pyu (recorded as "Piao") for reconstructing Pyu history.
  11. Evaluate competing explanations for political and demographic change in the Pyu world during the ninth century, including the role of Nanzhao.
  12. Distinguish the historical trajectory of the Pyu urban world from the later kingdom of Pagan, avoiding teleological narration.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did substantial, fortified urban centers develop in the Irrawaddy basin before the emergence of any centralized mainland Southeast Asian empire?
  2. What evidence allows historians to identify Beikthano, Sri Ksetra, and Halin as related but distinct urban centers rather than provinces of a single continuous state?
  3. How did Pyu communities select, adapt, and transform Buddhist religious practice, South Asian scripts, and other outside ideas to serve local needs?
  4. What can, and cannot, Pyu inscriptions and Chinese textual references tell historians about Pyu political organization?
  5. How did the agricultural and hydrological base of the central dry zone sustain large urban populations, and what are the limits of applying a "hydraulic state" model to the Pyu evidence?
  6. Why should the political and demographic changes of the ninth century, often associated with Nanzhao, be understood as a process rather than a single catastrophic event?
  7. In what ways does the Pyu urban world complicate any assumption that early Southeast Asian states developed along a single common political model?
  8. Why is it historically inaccurate to treat the Pyu cities primarily as a preliminary stage leading to the kingdom of Pagan?

Historical Context

The Irrawaddy basin's human history extends far deeper than the Pyu urban centers examined in this chapter. Chapters 12 through 16 traced the region's transition to agriculture, the spread of Tibeto-Burman-, Austroasiatic-, and other language-family populations across mainland Southeast Asia, and the growth of bronze and iron metallurgy and regional exchange networks that laid an economic and technological foundation for later urbanization. By the middle centuries of the first millennium BCE, communities across the middle and lower Irrawaddy valley were participating in expanding networks of exchange that moved metal goods, ceramics, and other commodities across considerable distances, evidence of increasing social complexity well before any of the great walled cities examined in this chapter had taken shape.

Chapters 17 through 19 established the wider setting within which cities like Beikthano, Sri Ksetra, and Halin emerged: expanding maritime and overland trade linking South Asia, mainland and island Southeast Asia, and China; the historiographical shift away from older "Indianization" models toward frameworks emphasizing selective adoption and localization; and the recognition that Southeast Asian communities were active participants in, rather than passive recipients of, these expanding networks. The Pyu cities offer one of the clearest mainland Southeast Asian illustrations of this localization process, since Buddhist religious practice, Indic scripts, and certain artistic and architectural forms reached the Irrawaddy basin through these networks and were then adapted into distinctly Pyu urban and religious institutions.

This chapter's place within Part V, alongside Chapters 20 through 23 on Funan, Chenla, Champa, and the Chinese-administered Red River delta, and Chapters 25 through 27 on the Mon world of the Chao Phraya basin, the early Malay Peninsula, and early Borneo and Java, illustrates a central argument of this volume: the earliest documented states of Southeast Asia did not all follow a single political template. Funan and Chenla developed around the lower Mekong under changing and only partially centralized political arrangements; the Red River delta was directly incorporated into Chinese imperial administration for roughly a millennium; and the Pyu world, as this chapter will show, developed multiple substantial urban centers whose precise political relationship to one another remains genuinely uncertain. Readers should not expect the Pyu evidence to resolve into a tidy dynastic narrative resembling later, better-documented Southeast Asian kingdoms.

Section 24.1

The Irrawaddy Basin before the Pyu Cities

Long before the specifically Pyu urban sequence began, which securely dated evidence currently places from around the second century BCE, communities across the Irrawaddy basin had already developed agriculture, bronze and iron metallurgy, and regional exchange networks over the preceding centuries, developments examined in detail in Chapters 12 and 16. This earlier settlement should be understood as pre-Pyu antecedent development rather than as already-established Pyu urbanism. Excavated burial sites from the region's Bronze and Iron Ages show evidence of increasing social differentiation, indicated by variation in grave goods, and expanding contact with exchange networks that moved metal objects, beads, and other commodities across considerable distances. These developments did not directly cause the emergence of Pyu cities in any simple, mechanical sense, but they established the demographic density, agricultural surplus, craft specialization, and exchange relationships on which later urban growth could build.

Population movement into and within the Irrawaddy basin during this period is best understood as a long, gradual, uneven process rather than a single migration event, a caution already established in Chapter 15 regarding Tibeto-Burman-, Tai-Kadai-, and Hmong-Mien-speaking populations more broadly. By the time the earliest substantial fortifications appear in the archaeological record, communities in the region were already linguistically and culturally connected to wider Tibeto-Burman-speaking networks extending toward the Himalayan foothills and southwestern China, though the precise relationship between these linguistic connections and the specific communities that built Beikthano, Sri Ksetra, and Halin remains an active area of scholarly investigation rather than a settled certainty.

This section deliberately remains brief. Prehistoric migration and language-family formation belong primarily to Chapters 12 through 16, and this chapter's task is to explain how, out of that deeper background, substantial city-centered societies emerged in the Irrawaddy basin during the first millennium BCE and CE.

Section 24.2

Who Were the Pyu?

The term "Pyu" derives from later Burmese usage and from a related term, rendered in Chinese dynastic sources as "Piao," used by Tang-era chroniclers to describe a kingdom or people encountered in the Irrawaddy region. Pyu inscriptions themselves, written in a distinctive script derived from South Asian writing systems, preserve an extinct language that modern linguists classify securely within the Sino-Tibetan language family, though its precise position within that family remains unresolved. Older scholarship often placed Pyu broadly within a "Tibeto-Burman" grouping alongside Burmese, and the two languages do appear to be related at some level within Sino-Tibetan, but specialists caution that a specific, demonstrated subgrouping connecting Pyu directly to Burmese has not been established with confidence.

Historians must resist two temptations when defining "the Pyu." The first is to treat "Pyu" as though it named a single, bounded, self-aware ethnic nation comparable to a modern national identity, uniformly conscious of shared political or cultural belonging across every city and rural settlement in the basin. The evidence does not support this: inscriptions from different Pyu sites show local variation in script, religious emphasis, and material culture, suggesting a shared linguistic and broadly cultural sphere rather than a single, centrally enforced identity. The second temptation is to treat the Pyu as a direct, uncomplicated ancestor population of the later Burmese-speaking population that built Pagan, projecting a continuous national lineage backward in time. While there is clear evidence of continuity, in urban sites, religious institutions, and possibly some population, between the Pyu world and later Irrawaddy basin society, the relationship involved substantial transformation, incorporation of other populations, and linguistic and political change, not a simple, unbroken succession of one "Burmese" people replacing itself under new names.

What can be said with reasonable confidence is this: from around the second century BCE, fortified centers conventionally assigned to the Pyu archaeological-cultural sequence developed across the middle and northern Irrawaddy basin. Direct Pyu-language inscriptional evidence appears later, with securely attested use of the sophisticated Pyu script generally placed around the third to fourth centuries CE, confirming that at least some communities within this urban tradition spoke the extinct Sino-Tibetan language now called Pyu, even though the script itself cannot be documented from the tradition's earliest, second-century-BCE phase. Modern historians and archaeologists refer to this cultural and linguistic sphere as "Pyu," while remaining alert to the gaps between the modern category and the self-understanding of the people who actually lived in Beikthano, Sri Ksetra, and Halin.

Historical Vocabulary
Pyu

Pyu is the modern scholarly and later Burmese-derived name for a population, or related set of populations, speaking an extinct Sino-Tibetan language whose precise position within that family remains uncertain, associated with a cluster of fortified urban centers in the Irrawaddy basin. The archaeological-cultural sequence conventionally assigned to the Pyu tradition begins by about the second century BCE, but direct Pyu-language inscriptional evidence, confirming the presence of Pyu speakers specifically, appears only later, with the sophisticated Pyu script generally attested from around the third to fourth centuries CE, and the tradition continues through the ninth century CE. Chinese Tang dynasty sources rendered a related name as "Piao." No surviving Pyu-language inscription uses "Pyu" as a name the inhabitants applied to a unified political community in the modern sense; the term functions today chiefly as a linguistic and archaeological classification.

Historical context: The label groups together evidence from multiple distinct urban sites, Beikthano, Sri Ksetra, Halin, and smaller associated settlements, that share script, certain religious and architectural forms, and linguistic features, without implying that these sites were necessarily governed by a single continuous political authority. Its precise place within the wider Sino-Tibetan family remains an open question for specialists.

Why it matters: Using "Pyu" carefully, as a cultural-linguistic label rather than a synonym for a unified kingdom or modern ethnic nation, helps students avoid two common errors: treating the Pyu cities as a single centralized state, and treating "Pyu" as an ancient name for a fixed ethnic group persisting unchanged into the present.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that every fortified site in the Irrawaddy basin from this period was necessarily "Pyu" in language or culture, nor that all Pyu-affiliated communities were culturally identical to one another.

Section 24.3

Geography of the Irrawaddy and Dry Zone

The Irrawaddy, fed by its major tributary the Chindwin and by smaller streams draining the surrounding hills, runs the length of present-day Myanmar from the northern highlands to the delta on the Andaman Sea coast, examined in its full mainland context in Chapter 2. The middle stretch of this river system, where Beikthano, Sri Ksetra, and other Pyu sites are located, lies within what geographers call the central dry zone: a region that receives markedly less rainfall than the surrounding highlands and the delta, shielded from the full force of the southwest monsoon by mountain ranges to the west.

This geography created both a challenge and an opportunity for the communities that settled there. Rainfall alone could not reliably support intensive agriculture across much of the dry zone, but the Irrawaddy and its tributaries, along with numerous smaller streams and seasonal watercourses, made irrigation-supported and river-adjacent cultivation possible in favorable locations. The dry zone's relatively open terrain, compared to the denser forests and steeper terrain of the surrounding uplands, also facilitated overland movement, connecting the Irrawaddy basin to routes leading northeast toward Yunnan and China, and, through mountain passes and river valleys, toward the Bay of Bengal and South Asia.

Geography did not determine that cities would rise in this particular landscape, nor did it dictate any specific political outcome. Other regions with comparable rainfall patterns did not necessarily develop comparable urban centers, and other factors, agricultural technique, social organization, religious institutions, and long-distance exchange, were equally necessary conditions. What the dry zone's geography did provide was a set of possibilities: defensible, resource-adequate locations along reliable water sources, positioned along corridors connecting South Asia, mainland Southeast Asia, and China, within which the specific historical developments examined in the following sections took place.

Section 24.4

The Emergence of Pyu Urban Centers

Archaeological evidence indicates a gradual transition, with securely dated urban and monumental evidence currently extending back to around the second century BCE and continuing over several subsequent centuries, from smaller, unfortified settlements toward larger, planned, walled centers. This transition did not occur simultaneously and uniformly across the entire Irrawaddy basin; different sites show different chronologies, and the relative sequence in which Beikthano, Sri Ksetra, and Halin developed their major fortifications remains a matter of ongoing archaeological investigation rather than settled consensus.

Scholars use the term "city-state" for these centers deliberately, rather than "kingdom" or "empire," to reflect the archaeological reality: each major Pyu site shows evidence of substantial fortification, monumental construction, and organized urban planning, but direct evidence for centralized political control extending continuously across all sites simultaneously is limited. This does not mean the cities existed in isolation from one another. Shared script, related architectural and artistic forms, and overlapping material culture indicate a broadly shared cultural sphere and very likely political and commercial interaction, alliance, and rivalry among the major centers, questions examined further in Section 24.8.

The construction of substantial defensive walls at multiple sites also indicates that these centers existed within a competitive political environment, whether that competition took the form of warfare among Pyu cities themselves, conflict with non-Pyu neighboring communities, or defense against more distant threats. The scale of investment represented by kilometers of brick and earthen fortification at sites such as Sri Ksetra implies significant organized labor, resource mobilization, and political authority capable of directing such projects, even if the precise nature of that authority, a single ruler, a council, a religious institution, or some combination, cannot be established with certainty from the archaeological record alone.

Section 24.5

Beikthano

Beikthano, located on the middle Irrawaddy plain, is among the earliest Pyu urban centers to have been extensively excavated, and its excavation in the mid-twentieth century provided much of the foundational archaeological framework for later Pyu studies. The site's name, meaning roughly "city of Vishnu" in later Burmese usage, is itself a retrospective label rather than necessarily reflecting the city's ancient name or the religious identity of its original inhabitants, a caution students should keep in mind when encountering modern place names applied to ancient sites throughout this textbook.

Excavations at Beikthano revealed a rectangular walled enclosure with gateways, moats, and a grid of internal features including what excavators interpreted as monastery-like structures, brick platforms, and burial areas. Evidence of cremation burial, in some cases associated with what may be Buddhist ritual practice, appears at the site, though the interpretation of specific structures as religious versus secular, or as evidence for a particular Buddhist tradition, remains debated among specialists working from incomplete and sometimes ambiguous archaeological remains.

Radiocarbon and stylistic dating place significant occupation and construction at Beikthano beginning by around the second century BCE, with a calibrated radiocarbon date range for one monumental architectural feature extending as early as approximately 190 BCE, making it, on current evidence, one of the earliest of the major Pyu fortified centers, though the precise chronology continues to be refined as excavation methods improve. Beikthano's relative decline, and the shift of regional prominence toward Sri Ksetra in later centuries, illustrates a broader pattern examined further in Section 24.8: political and economic importance among the Pyu cities was not fixed but shifted over time.

Section 24.6

Sri Ksetra

Sri Ksetra, near modern Pyay on the lower-middle Irrawaddy, is the largest and most thoroughly documented Pyu urban center, and much of what historians know about Pyu religion, writing, and material culture derives from finds at this single site. Its fortifications, roughly circular in plan and enclosing an area considerably larger than Beikthano's, represent one of the most substantial urban construction projects known from first-millennium mainland Southeast Asia. Multiple gates pierced the walls, and excavated sections show sophisticated brickwork, suggesting significant organized labor and technical skill.

Sri Ksetra has produced the richest concentration of Pyu inscriptions, including gold, silver, and stone inscriptions bearing Buddhist texts, dedicatory formulas, and funerary information. A small but historically important group of inscribed funerary urns, most famously four stone examples discovered near Payagyi in 1911 and supplemented by later finds including an inscribed terracotta urn and the more extensively genealogical Payahtaung urn, have been recovered from the site, containing cremated remains and bearing personal or royal names and dates calculated by regnal or era systems whose precise correlation to absolute dates remains debated. Monumental Buddhist stupas at and around Sri Ksetra, some still partially standing, indicate substantial investment in religious architecture and a well-developed monastic or ritual establishment.

The scale of Sri Ksetra, combined with the concentration of inscriptions naming individuals sometimes interpreted as rulers, has led some historians to treat Sri Ksetra as the leading or capital center of a broader Pyu political sphere, at least during certain periods. This interpretation should be treated cautiously rather than as established fact. The abundance of evidence from Sri Ksetra may partly reflect the accident of preservation and the extent of archaeological investigation at this particular site rather than proof that it dominated all other Pyu centers throughout the entire period this chapter examines. Section 24.8 returns to this problem directly.

Section 24.7

Halin

Halin, located further north than Beikthano and Sri Ksetra, in a portion of the dry zone with somewhat different agricultural conditions, represents a third major Pyu urban center whose plan and material culture both resemble and differ from its southern counterparts. Halin's fortifications enclose a substantial, though generally smaller, urban area, with evidence of gateways, internal structures, and burial practices broadly consistent with the wider Pyu cultural sphere.

Excavations at Halin have produced evidence for salt production, an economically significant resource in the region, alongside agricultural remains suggesting a mixed subsistence base of rice cultivation and other crops adapted to the drier northern conditions. Water-management features identified at and around the site, including reservoirs and channels, indicate that Halin's inhabitants, like those of the other major Pyu cities, invested considerable effort in managing an environment where rainfall alone could not reliably support the scale of agriculture needed to sustain a substantial urban population, a theme examined further in Section 24.10.

Halin's inscriptions and material culture confirm its place within the broader Pyu linguistic and cultural sphere, while also showing some distinct local features in ceramic styles and architectural details, evidence consistent with the broader pattern of regional variation within Pyu society rather than a single uniform culture replicated identically at every site.

Section 24.8

Were the Pyu Cities One Kingdom?

This chapter's title deliberately uses the plural, descriptive phrase "city-states" rather than asserting a single "Pyu kingdom" or "Pyu empire." This choice reflects a genuine and unresolved historiographical problem rather than a stylistic preference. The evidence permits several different interpretations of the political relationship among Beikthano, Sri Ksetra, Halin, and smaller associated sites, and historians have not reached consensus on which interpretation best fits the available evidence.

One interpretation holds that the major Pyu cities were largely independent polities, each governing its own urban core and agricultural hinterland, connected to one another chiefly through shared language, religion, trade, and periodic alliance or conflict, rather than through any continuous shared government. Under this model, references in Chinese sources to a single "Piao kingdom" would reflect an outside observer's simplification of a more politically fragmented reality, a pattern of misreading local plurality as unified statehood that recurs elsewhere in this textbook's treatment of Chinese descriptions of Southeast Asian polities, including Funan and Chenla in Chapters 20 and 21.

A second interpretation proposes a shifting hegemony model, in which political predominance passed from one major center to another over time, Beikthano perhaps earlier, Sri Ksetra later, without any single city permanently ruling the others, but with the leading city at a given period exercising some degree of authority, tribute collection, or ritual precedence over neighboring centers. This model draws support from the apparent decline of Beikthano's prominence alongside the apparent rise of Sri Ksetra, though the causal relationship between these two developments, and the degree of authority genuinely exercised by any dominant center, remains uncertain.

A third, more cautious position holds that the current evidence simply does not permit a confident reconstruction of Pyu interstate political relationships, and that historians should resist choosing among the first two models until further archaeological and epigraphic evidence becomes available. This textbook adopts a position close to this third view: it uses "city-states" as the most defensible general descriptor, acknowledges evidence consistent with shifting regional predominance, particularly around Sri Ksetra in later centuries, and explicitly avoids describing the Pyu world as a single centralized kingdom comparable to later, better-documented mainland states such as Pagan or Angkor.

Section 24.9

Building a Pyu City

Pyu urban planning followed recognizable, though not identical, patterns across the major sites. Cities were typically enclosed by substantial walls, built of rammed earth, brick, or a combination of both, often accompanied by external moats that served both defensive and water-management functions. Multiple gateways pierced these walls, positioned to control and monitor movement into and out of the city and, in some cases, aligned with cosmological or ritual considerations that historians infer cautiously from comparable practices elsewhere in South and Southeast Asia, rather than from direct Pyu textual explanation.

Within the walls, excavated Pyu cities show evidence of zoning: areas containing what are interpreted as monastic or religious complexes, areas of dense residential occupation, and open spaces that may have served agricultural, ceremonial, or market functions. Some sites show internal subdivisions or secondary walls, suggesting a hierarchy of protected zones within the larger urban enclosure, possibly reflecting differences in status, function, or the sequence of urban expansion over time.

Beyond the walls, Pyu cities were surrounded by agricultural land, smaller settlements, and, in some cases, additional water-management infrastructure connecting the urban core to its rural hinterland. The relationship between the fortified city and this surrounding countryside was almost certainly one of mutual dependence: the city required agricultural surplus, labor, and raw materials from the countryside, while rural populations likely depended on the city for religious institutions, craft goods, exchange opportunities, and, in times of conflict, protection within its walls. Reconstructing the precise administrative relationship between city and countryside, however, remains difficult given the absence of detailed administrative records comparable to those surviving from some contemporary Chinese or South Asian polities.

Section 24.10

Agriculture, Water, and the Urban Economy

Sustaining populations large enough to build and maintain kilometers of fortification and substantial monumental architecture required a dependable agricultural surplus. Rice cultivation formed the economic base of Pyu society, supplemented by other crops suited to the dry zone's conditions, and by livestock raising, fishing along the rivers, and craft production including ceramics, metalwork, and textiles, evidenced by tools, kiln remains, and finished goods recovered from excavations at multiple sites.

Because rainfall in the dry zone was often insufficient for reliable rice cultivation without supplementary water, Pyu communities developed reservoirs, channels, and other water-management features, some of which remain visible in the archaeological record around Halin and other sites. These features indicate a considerable degree of coordinated labor and planning, but historians should be cautious about applying an unmodified "hydraulic state" model, the theory, associated with the scholar Karl Wittfogel and long debated for other world regions, that large-scale irrigation management necessarily produced centralized despotic political control. The scale of Pyu water-management infrastructure, while significant, has not been shown to require or prove a single centralized authority directing agricultural production across the entire Irrawaddy basin; more localized, city-specific management remains equally plausible given current evidence.

Craft production at Pyu sites extended beyond subsistence needs. Evidence for specialized ceramic production, metalworking including iron and bronze objects, and possibly textile production suggests economic specialization capable of supporting artisans who did not themselves produce their own food, a hallmark of urban economic complexity. Salt production identified at Halin illustrates how particular sites could specialize in resources valuable both locally and for exchange with other Pyu centers and beyond, a theme examined further in the following section on trade.

Section 24.11

Trade and the Irrawaddy Corridor

The Pyu cities did not develop in isolation. Their position along the Irrawaddy corridor placed them within reach of overland routes leading northeast toward Yunnan and the Chinese interior, and, via tributary valleys and mountain passes, toward Bengal and other parts of South Asia. Archaeological finds at Pyu sites, including beads, glass, and other materials whose composition indicates origins outside the immediate region, provide physical evidence for these long-distance exchange relationships, complementing the more circumstantial evidence of shared religious and artistic forms discussed in later sections.

Local and regional exchange among the Pyu cities themselves, and with smaller surrounding communities, almost certainly formed the bulk of everyday economic activity, even though this local trade is harder to document archaeologically than long-distance luxury exchange. Agricultural surplus, salt, ceramics, metal goods, and craft products likely moved along the Irrawaddy and its tributaries by river transport, supplemented by overland routes connecting inland settlements to the main river corridor.

Framing the Pyu cities' trade connections requires the same caution against a passive "recipient" model that Chapter 19 applied to the wider region. Pyu communities were not simply waiting at the end of a trade route for South Asian merchants or Chinese envoys to bring them goods and ideas; they participated actively in these networks, exporting their own products, adapting imported materials and ideas into locally meaningful forms, examined further in Sections 24.12 through 24.16, and maintaining relationships that served Pyu political and economic interests rather than existing merely as a conduit between more "important" neighboring civilizations.

Section 24.12

Buddhism in the Pyu World

Buddhism occupied a central place in Pyu religious and urban life, as attested archaeologically across the major Pyu sites through monumental stupas and funerary practices, while Sri Ksetra provides by far the richest surviving epigraphic and reliquary evidence, including inscribed Buddhist texts on precious metal. Large brick stupas, some rising to considerable height and still partially standing today, represent significant investments of labor and resources, comparable in scale to the city walls themselves, and indicate that Buddhist institutions commanded substantial patronage, quite possibly from ruling elites, wealthy merchants, or organized lay communities, though the precise sources and mechanisms of this patronage cannot always be reconstructed in detail.

Among the most significant finds from Sri Ksetra is the Khin Ba mound deposit, which included a gold manuscript inscribed with Pali Buddhist canonical text, among the earliest surviving substantial examples of the Pali canon anywhere, together with an elaborate silver reliquary and other votive objects, alongside further dedicatory inscriptions associated with named individuals, some interpreted as rulers or members of a ruling elite. These finds indicate that by at least the middle centuries of the first millennium CE, Pyu Buddhist communities possessed access to, and the capacity to inscribe, formal Buddhist scriptural material, evidence of a sophisticated monastic or scholarly establishment capable of transmitting and reproducing religious texts.

Evidence also points to a degree of religious diversity within Pyu Buddhist practice itself. Material remains at various sites have been interpreted by different scholars as reflecting elements of Theravada practice, Mahayana influences, and, in some interpretations, tantric or esoteric elements, though these classifications should be applied cautiously, since later, more clearly defined sectarian categories do not always map neatly onto the more fluid religious landscape of first-millennium mainland Southeast Asia. What can be said with more confidence is that Buddhism at Pyu sites was institutionally significant, materially well resourced, and integrated into funerary practice, urban planning, and, quite likely, political legitimation.

Section 24.13

Religious Plurality and Localization

Buddhism did not simply replace all earlier or coexisting religious practice within Pyu society, nor did it arrive in a single, uniform, already-completed form from South Asia. Archaeological evidence for non-Buddhist ritual practice, including certain burial customs, artifact types, and iconographic elements that some scholars associate with Brahmanical or indigenous local traditions, suggests a religious landscape in which Buddhism coexisted with, and in some cases absorbed, other practices, rather than one in which a single imported religion displaced everything that came before it.

Applying the localization framework developed in Chapter 19, historians increasingly interpret Pyu Buddhism not as evidence of straightforward "Indianization," the passive transmission of a finished South Asian religious system into a receptive Southeast Asian vacuum, but as evidence of active selection and adaptation. Pyu communities adopted specific texts, architectural forms, and ritual practices that reached them through South Asian and Southeast Asian networks, and then integrated these elements into locally meaningful funerary customs, urban planning, and political ideology, producing religious institutions that were recognizably Buddhist while also distinctly Pyu in their specific material and ritual expression.

Scholars have sometimes interpreted aspects of Pyu royal and Buddhist patronage through the Sanskrit cakravartin (Pali cakkavattin/cakkavatti) ideal, a term for a universal or wheel-turning ruler whose legitimacy is closely associated with the support and patronage of Buddhism, drawing inferences from monumental religious architecture and associated inscriptions. This remains an interpretive model applied by modern scholars rather than a concept directly and explicitly articulated in surviving Pyu-language sources. This pattern, in which Buddhist kingship ideology and monumental religious patronage reinforce political authority, recurs across many of the mainland and maritime polities examined later in this textbook, including Pagan, Angkor, and Sukhothai, making the Pyu evidence an important early Southeast Asian example of a much broader regional pattern.

Historical Vocabulary
Cakravartin

Cakravartin (Sanskrit; Pali cakkavattin/cakkavatti), literally "wheel-turner" or "universal monarch," describes an ideal ruler whose righteous governance, closely associated with Buddhist virtue and patronage, extends his benevolent authority widely, ideally over the whole world, without necessarily requiring military conquest of every territory implied by the term.

Historical context: The concept reached Southeast Asia through the same expanding religious and commercial networks examined in Chapter 18, and it was adapted by numerous mainland and maritime rulers, including, on inferential grounds, Pyu elites, to frame political authority in Buddhist religious terms.

Why it matters: Recognizing the cakravartin ideal helps explain why substantial resources went into monumental Buddhist architecture at Pyu sites: such patronage was not merely pious devotion but also, very plausibly, a form of political self-presentation.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that every Pyu ruler necessarily used this specific term or understood it in exactly the same way South Asian Buddhist texts describe it; the concept's application to Pyu society is an informed scholarly inference rather than a directly attested textual claim.

Section 24.14

Writing, Languages, and Inscriptions

The Pyu developed a distinctive script, adapted from South Asian Brahmi-derived writing systems, used to record an extinct Sino-Tibetan language, of uncertain precise classification within that family, on stone, metal sheets, and funerary urns. This script represents one of the earliest attested writing systems in mainland Southeast Asia outside areas under direct Chinese administrative influence, and its development illustrates the broader process of selective adaptation examined throughout this chapter: Pyu scribes did not simply copy an Indian script wholesale but modified it to represent the specific sounds of the Pyu language.

Surviving Pyu inscriptions fall into several categories: funerary inscriptions on burial urns recording names, dates, and sometimes brief genealogical or commemorative information; dedicatory inscriptions associated with religious donations, including the gold and silver Buddhist texts discussed in Section 24.12; and a smaller number of inscriptions that appear to record royal or elite titles and activities. Multilingual or multiscript inscriptions at some sites, incorporating Pyu alongside Pali, Sanskrit, and, in at least one notable case, Prakrit, indicate a cosmopolitan scribal environment in which multiple languages and scripts coexisted, likely reflecting Pyu society's connections to wider South and Southeast Asian religious and commercial networks. Old Mon material, by contrast, belongs chiefly to later, Pagan-period multilingual inscriptions rather than to the core first-millennium Pyu corpus, and should not be read back into this earlier period without specific supporting evidence.

Despite these valuable finds, Pyu epigraphy remains fragmentary, and modern understanding of the Pyu language itself has depended on painstaking, ongoing decipherment work. Many inscriptions are damaged, incomplete, or difficult to date with precision, and the corpus of securely translated Pyu texts remains relatively small compared to inscriptional traditions from better-documented contemporary societies such as Angkor or Champa. Historians must therefore draw conclusions about Pyu political and social organization cautiously, aware that future discoveries or improved decipherment could substantially revise current understanding.

Epigraphy Study

Reading Pyu Inscriptions: What Survives and What It Can Tell Us

Pyu inscriptions survive on several kinds of objects, including stone funerary urns, votive gold and silver plates, seals and sealings, stone slabs, rings, and a smaller number of monumental stone inscriptions, rather than a single closed set of media. Funerary urns typically record a personal name, sometimes a title, and a date calculated according to an era system whose correlation with absolute calendar dates remains debated among specialists, since Pyu era systems have not been definitively cross-correlated with securely dated external events in every case. Dedicatory inscriptions on gold and silver sheets, especially the well-known examples from Sri Ksetra, preserve portions of Buddhist canonical text alongside donor information, indicating both religious literacy and the material wealth available for such donations. A smaller number of inscriptions associated with monumental architecture appear to record royal or elite names and activities, though interpretation of specific titles and their precise political meaning remains provisional.

What this evidence can establish: the existence of a functioning, locally adapted writing system; evidence for Buddhist textual literacy and canonical transmission; evidence for named individuals of apparent social or political importance; and evidence for multilingual scribal contact with Pali, Sanskrit, and, in at least one notable case, Prakrit. What this evidence cannot, on its own, establish: a continuous king list; a clear administrative hierarchy connecting different Pyu cities; detailed information about the lives of non-elite Pyu subjects; or a fully resolved absolute chronology for the major political and religious developments discussed in this chapter.

  4. How might future advances in decipherment or newly discovered inscriptions change current historical interpretations of Pyu society?
Section 24.15

Connections with India

Evidence for South Asian contact with the Pyu world is substantial: the Pyu script's derivation from Brahmi-based writing systems, the presence of Pali and Sanskrit religious texts and terminology, Buddhist architectural and iconographic forms showing South Asian influence, and material goods, including certain beads and other trade items, whose origins point toward South Asian exchange networks. These connections likely reached the Pyu world through a combination of maritime routes via the Bay of Bengal and overland routes through northeastern India, rather than through a single fixed channel of contact.

As Chapter 19 establishes for the region as a whole, this evidence should not be read through an older "Indianization" framework in which a passive Southeast Asian population simply received a finished South Asian civilization. Instead, the specific pattern of adaptation visible in the Pyu evidence, a locally modified script, Buddhist practice integrated into distinctly Pyu funerary and urban customs, and religious ideology adapted to serve Pyu political self-presentation, illustrates active selection and transformation. Pyu elites and religious communities chose which South Asian religious and administrative ideas to adopt, and shaped those ideas to serve local needs, rather than importing an undifferentiated package of "Indian civilization."

At the same time, historians should not overcorrect by minimizing the genuine importance of South Asian religious and intellectual contact to Pyu society. The presence of Pali canonical text, Sanskrit-derived vocabulary, and Buddhist architectural forms demonstrates real, sustained, and religiously significant contact with South Asian Buddhist traditions, contact that Pyu communities valued enough to invest substantial resources in monumental construction and scriptural transmission. The historically accurate account lies between uncritical diffusionism and an equally uncritical denial of South Asian influence: sustained contact, real influence, and active local transformation all occurred together.

Section 24.16

Connections with China and the Wider Southeast Asian World

Chinese dynastic records concerning the Tang period, several of them compiled after the Tang dynasty itself had ended, provide the most substantial outside textual description of a kingdom or people called "Piao," conventionally and plausibly identified with the Pyu cultural sphere examined in this chapter, though the exact scope of that identification has been discussed by specialists rather than treated as beyond question. These records describe a kingdom in the region with a capital city, courtly customs, musical traditions, and Buddhist religious devotion, including an account of missions reported to have reached the Tang court in 800 and 801–802 CE, the second of which is described as including thirty-five musicians, an episode examined in the Primary Source Window below. These accounts provide valuable, if limited and outsider-authored, confirmation of Pyu-associated political organization, urban life, and religious practice around the turn of the ninth century CE.

As with Chinese accounts of Funan and Chenla examined in Chapters 20 and 21, these descriptions must be read critically. Chinese chroniclers wrote from the perspective of an imperial court interested chiefly in tributary relationships, exotic curiosities, and confirmation of Chinese cultural centrality, and their accounts likely simplified, exoticized, or misunderstood aspects of Pyu political organization and religious practice. The reported musical delegation to the Tang court, for instance, tells historians that some form of diplomatic or cultural exchange occurred between a Pyu polity and the Tang empire, but it does not by itself establish the internal political structure of the Pyu cities or resolve the question, discussed in Section 24.8, of whether a single unified "Piao kingdom" actually existed as the Chinese sources imply.

Beyond China, the Pyu world maintained connections, evidenced through shared or related material culture, artistic motifs, and possibly religious exchange, with neighboring mainland Southeast Asian societies, including the Mon-associated communities of the Chao Phraya basin examined in Chapter 25. These connections should be understood within the broader pattern, established in Chapter 18, of expanding first-millennium exchange linking South Asia, mainland and maritime Southeast Asia, and China, a pattern in which the Pyu cities functioned as active participants and intermediaries rather than as an isolated or peripheral outpost.

Primary Source Window

Chinese Records of the Piao and the 802 Musical Embassy

Creator: Chinese court officials and later compilers who preserved and organized Tang-period administrative and diplomatic records into dynastic histories, most notably the Tang Huiyao (completed 961), the Old Book of Tang (completed 945), and the New Book of Tang (completed 1060) · Date: the events described occurred in 800 and 801–802 CE, during the Tang dynasty (618–907 CE); the surviving compilations that record them were assembled between several decades and roughly two and a half centuries afterward · Type: Later compiled dynastic and administrative histories incorporating earlier court records of diplomatic missions

Context: Tang-period court records, later organized into the dynastic compilations named above, describe missions from a kingdom or people called the "Piao," conventionally and plausibly identified with the Pyu cultural sphere, reaching the Tang court in 800 CE and again in 801–802 CE. The Tang Huiyao specifically dates a musical presentation to the eighteenth year of the Zhenyuan era, corresponding to 802 CE, and records twelve musical pieces performed by a delegation of thirty-five musicians.

Paraphrase / Synthesis (not a verbatim excerpt):

The compiled records describe a kingdom to the southwest possessing a capital city, a ruler surrounded by courtly ceremony, widespread Buddhist devotion among the population, and an elaborate musical and performance tradition, thirty-five musicians performing twelve distinct pieces before the Tang court in 802 CE. Because this description survives only through later Chinese compilation rather than a Pyu-authored account, and because some of the surviving compilations were assembled decades or centuries after the events they describe, the record should be treated as an outside synthesis, shaped by Chinese administrative and literary conventions and by the practices of later editorial compilation, rather than as a neutral, contemporaneous, or comprehensive record.

Analysis: This source demonstrates that a polity associated with the Pyu cultural sphere, whichever specific city or authority it represented, possessed sufficient political organization and outward-facing diplomatic capacity to organize and send a large formal delegation to the Tang court, and that Chinese observers considered Pyu-associated Buddhist devotion and musical culture notable enough to record in detail, including specific numbers of performers and pieces. At the same time, because the account was written by and for a Chinese audience and reached its surviving form through later compilation, it emphasizes diplomatically and culturally striking details rather than providing a systematic description of Pyu government, economy, or society.

Limitations: The account cannot establish whether the delegation represented all Pyu cities, a single dominant city, or merely one polity among several; it also cannot be used, on its own, to reconstruct everyday Pyu life, since it reflects a courtly and diplomatic encounter rather than ordinary experience, and it reaches modern readers through compilations assembled well after the Tang dynasty itself had ended.

Perspective: The perspective represented is that of the Tang imperial court and its later compilers; the perspective of the Piao delegation itself, and of ordinary Pyu-associated subjects, is not directly recorded.

Section 24.17

Ninth-Century Change and the Nanzhao Problem

Chinese sources record military campaigns by Nanzhao, a powerful polity centered in present-day Yunnan, extending into the Irrawaddy region during the early ninth century, with a frequently cited date of 832 CE associated with a significant Nanzhao action affecting the Pyu area. Older historical narratives, drawing heavily on this Chinese textual evidence, sometimes presented this campaign as a single catastrophic event that abruptly destroyed Pyu civilization, after which the Pyu cities were quickly abandoned and their population dispersed or absorbed.

More recent archaeological and historical scholarship treats this narrative with considerable caution, for several reasons. First, the Chinese textual evidence for the Nanzhao campaign, while informative, is itself a source requiring critical evaluation rather than straightforward acceptance: it reflects a Chinese court's interest in recording disruptions among neighboring polities, and it does not provide a detailed, verifiable account of conditions within the Pyu cities themselves before, during, or immediately after the reported campaign. Second, archaeological evidence at Pyu sites does not show uniform, simultaneous abandonment consistent with a single catastrophic destruction; some sites show evidence of continued or renewed occupation into later centuries, suggesting a more gradual, uneven process of political and demographic change rather than a sudden collapse.

A more historically defensible interpretation treats the ninth century as a period of significant transformation, in which external military pressure from Nanzhao, possible internal political strain among the Pyu cities, shifting trade patterns, and other factors that current evidence does not allow historians to fully specify, combined to reduce the political and demographic prominence of the major Pyu urban centers over an extended period, rather than in a single dramatic moment. This more cautious framing avoids the "cliff-edge" model in which Pyu civilization simply vanishes in 832 CE, replacing it with a picture of contested, uneven, and incompletely documented change extending across the ninth through eleventh centuries.

Cause and Effect

The Ninth-Century Transformation of the Pyu World

Possible long-term contributing factors

Hypothesized, but not securely established, long-term strain on Pyu political cohesion arising from the uncertain and possibly shifting relationships among Beikthano, Sri Ksetra, Halin, and other centers, combined with the demands of sustaining large fortified urban populations in a dry-zone environment. Current evidence does not securely identify the endogenous causes of Pyu political transformation, and these factors should be read as plausible hypotheses rather than demonstrated causes.

Short-term causes

Recorded ninth-century Nanzhao military activity extending into the Irrawaddy region, along with possibly related shifts in regional trade routes and political alliances that current evidence cannot fully reconstruct.

Effect

A gradual, uneven decline in the political and demographic prominence of the major Pyu urban centers over the ninth through eleventh centuries, rather than a single abrupt collapse, accompanied by the eventual redirection of population, urban traditions, and religious institutions toward new centers of authority in the Irrawaddy basin.

This textbook's response

Consistent avoidance of the older "cliff-edge" 832 CE collapse narrative; explicit acknowledgment that the ninth-century transformation remains only partially understood and that future archaeological evidence may further revise current interpretations.

Historical complexity: Treating this transformation as a process rather than an event does not deny that real, significant, and in some respects sudden disruption may have occurred at particular sites and moments; it means historians should not generalize from a single Chinese-recorded campaign to the fate of the entire Pyu cultural sphere.

Section 24.18

From Pyu Networks toward the Age of Pagan

The centuries following the ninth-century transformation discussed above saw significant, but again gradual and uneven, reorientation of political authority, population, and urban life within the Irrawaddy basin. Some Pyu urban sites appear to have continued in reduced form well beyond the ninth century, and Pyu-derived religious institutions, artistic conventions, and possibly elements of the Pyu population itself were incorporated into the new political and urban formations that eventually emerged further up the Irrawaddy, in the vicinity of what would become Pagan.

Later Burmese inscriptions and traditions, dating from the Pagan period itself, occasionally reference the Pyu, sometimes describing them as an earlier people whose religious sites and traditions preceded and, in some measure, informed the new kingdom's own religious institutions. Historians should read these later references carefully, since they were produced within Pagan's own political and religious context, examined fully in Chapters 40 and 41, and may reflect Pagan-era efforts to construct a historical lineage for its own Buddhist kingship as much as they reflect accurate memory of earlier Pyu political realities.

This chapter stops deliberately at this threshold. It has traced the rise, urban development, religious life, and gradual transformation of the Pyu cities, but it does not narrate the rise of Pagan itself, a subject reserved for Chapter 40, nor does it assume that Pagan represented an inevitable or intended culmination of Pyu history. What can be said is that the Irrawaddy basin's later political and religious development did not begin from nothing: centuries of Pyu urban planning, water management, Buddhist institution-building, and epigraphic practice established models, sites, and possibly populations upon which later Irrawaddy basin societies, including Pagan, would draw, adapt, and transform in their own distinct historical circumstances.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Pyu Cities toward Pagan

What Changed

  • Political prominence shifted away from the older Pyu urban centers of Beikthano, Sri Ksetra, and Halin toward new centers of authority further up the Irrawaddy over the ninth through eleventh centuries
  • The Pyu language and script gradually gave way to Burmese as the dominant written and spoken language of the region, a linguistic transition whose precise timeline remains only partially documented
  • Political organization moved, over an extended and uneven period, from a landscape of multiple substantial urban centers of uncertain interrelationship toward the more centralized kingdom later built at Pagan

What Stayed the Same

  • Buddhism remained central to religious and political life in the Irrawaddy basin across this transition, even as specific doctrinal emphases and institutional forms changed
  • The Irrawaddy River and the central dry zone continued to structure settlement, agriculture, and political geography
  • Patterns of monumental religious architecture as a form of political legitimation persisted from the Pyu cities into the Pagan period and beyond

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents treating Pagan as an entirely new beginning unconnected to earlier Irrawaddy basin history, while also preventing the opposite error of treating Pyu history as though it existed only to produce Pagan.

Historical Debate

City-States, Shifting Hegemony, or One Kingdom?

The question: Did Beikthano, Sri Ksetra, Halin, and other Pyu sites constitute largely independent city-states, a single kingdom whose center of gravity shifted over time, or some other political arrangement not fully captured by either model?

Interpretation A — largely independent city-states: Some historians argue that the absence of clear inscriptional or archaeological evidence for continuous, centralized administration linking all major Pyu sites supports viewing them as independent polities connected chiefly by shared language, religion, and trade, with Chinese references to a single "Piao kingdom" reflecting outside simplification rather than local political reality.
Interpretation B — a single kingdom with a shifting capital: Other historians argue that the concentration of inscriptions, monumental construction, and apparent elite titulature at Sri Ksetra during its period of greatest prominence, combined with Chinese textual references to a unified "Piao" polity, supports interpreting the Pyu world as a single, if loosely integrated, kingdom whose political center shifted from Beikthano to Sri Ksetra over time.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same archaeological and epigraphic record and the same Chinese textual sources but weigh the significance of shared culture versus direct political control differently.

Why it matters: The interpretation chosen shapes how historians describe Pyu political history, how they read Chinese accounts of the "Piao," and how they understand the relationship between the Pyu world and the later kingdom of Pagan.

Student task: Using the evidence presented in Sections 24.5 through 24.8, construct an argument for each interpretation and explain what kind of additional evidence would help historians decide between them.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary Life within and around the Walls

Most surviving Pyu evidence, inscriptions, monumental architecture, and elite funerary urns, reflects the activity and priorities of religious institutions and social elites capable of commissioning inscriptions and monumental construction. Reconstructing the experience of farmers, artisans, laborers, and other non-elite Pyu residents therefore depends heavily on indirect evidence: settlement patterns, agricultural tools, ceramic remains, and the physical scale of labor represented by city walls and water-management infrastructure, all of which imply the coordinated work of large numbers of ordinary people whose individual names and experiences went unrecorded.

The construction and maintenance of kilometers of fortification, moats, reservoirs, and irrigation channels at each major Pyu city required sustained physical labor from a substantial workforce, whether organized through corvée-style labor obligation, religious donation of labor, or other mechanisms that current evidence does not allow historians to specify with confidence. Household life within Pyu cities likely combined craft production, small-scale trade, and participation in agricultural cycles tied to the surrounding countryside, while religious observance, centered on the numerous stupas and monastic establishments documented archaeologically, would have structured much of communal and calendrical life for urban and rural populations alike.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Pyu city-states of the Irrawaddy basin as one of mainland Southeast Asia's earliest and most significant urban civilizations, building substantial fortified cities, sophisticated Buddhist religious institutions, and a locally adapted system of writing centuries before the emergence of the kingdom of Pagan in the same river valley. Rather than treating the Pyu world as a preliminary or incomplete stage on the way to a later, more familiar Burmese kingdom, this chapter has insisted on examining Pyu society on its own historical terms.

The chapter began by cautioning against treating "Pyu" as a fixed, uniform ethnic or political category, explaining instead that the term describes a Sino-Tibetan-speaking linguistic and cultural sphere, of uncertain precise internal classification, identified through shared script, related material culture, and archaeological association with a cluster of major urban sites. It then examined the physical geography of the Irrawaddy basin and central dry zone, explaining how river systems, seasonal rainfall patterns, and overland corridors created possibilities for urban settlement without determining any specific political outcome.

Sections on Beikthano, Sri Ksetra, and Halin presented the three best-documented Pyu urban centers, comparing their scale, chronology, and material culture, while a dedicated section addressed directly the unresolved historiographical question of whether these cities formed a single kingdom, a shifting hegemony, or a set of largely independent polities. This textbook has adopted the cautious position that "city-states" offers the most defensible general descriptive label for current evidence, without treating that label as proof of political independence, while acknowledging real evidence for shifting regional predominance, particularly the apparent rise of Sri Ksetra's importance in later centuries. Other reputable sources, including UNESCO's own archaeological documentation, refer to the same sites using both "Pyu Kingdoms" and "Buddhist city-states," a variation in terminology that itself illustrates why the underlying political question remains open.

The chapter examined Pyu city planning in detail, including fortification, water management, and the relationship between urban cores and agricultural hinterlands, cautioning against an unmodified "hydraulic state" model that would assume large-scale irrigation infrastructure necessarily implies centralized despotic political control. It then traced the economic foundations of Pyu urban life, including rice agriculture, craft production, salt extraction, and participation in long-distance exchange networks connecting the Irrawaddy basin to South Asia and China.

Buddhism emerged as a central theme, with substantial evidence for monumental stupa construction, inscribed canonical texts, and funerary practices integrating Buddhist ritual, alongside evidence for continuing religious plurality and the active, selective adaptation of South Asian religious ideas into distinctly Pyu forms, consistent with the localization framework introduced in Chapter 19. The chapter examined Pyu writing and epigraphy as a valuable but genuinely fragmentary evidentiary base, cautioning that inscriptions can establish certain facts about religious literacy and elite activity while leaving many questions about political structure and everyday life unanswered.

Connections with India and with China and the wider Southeast Asian world were examined as evidence of active Pyu participation in trans-regional networks, including critical assessment of Tang dynasty Chinese accounts of the "Piao," which provide valuable outsider testimony while requiring the same source-critical caution applied throughout this textbook to Chinese descriptions of other Southeast Asian polities.

The chapter's final substantive sections addressed the transformation of Pyu society during and after the ninth century, rejecting the older narrative of a single catastrophic collapse associated with the 832 CE Nanzhao campaign in favor of a more historically defensible model of gradual, uneven political and demographic change extending across several centuries. The chapter concluded by tracing, without assuming inevitability, how Pyu urban traditions, religious institutions, and possibly population were incorporated into the political formations that eventually produced the kingdom of Pagan, a subject reserved for full treatment in Chapters 40 and 41.

Understanding the Pyu city-states matters for the wider argument of this textbook because they demonstrate, alongside Funan, Chenla, Champa, and the Chinese-administered Red River delta examined in the preceding chapters, that the earliest documented states of Southeast Asia developed through genuinely different political forms rather than a single common model. The Pyu evidence, fragmentary as it remains, shows a mainland Southeast Asian society building substantial cities, adapting Buddhist civilization to local needs, and participating actively in international networks, well before any of the region's later, more thoroughly documented classical kingdoms had taken shape.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Pyu
Modern scholarly and later Burmese-derived name for a Sino-Tibetan-speaking cultural and linguistic sphere, of uncertain precise classification within that family, associated with fortified urban centers in the Irrawaddy basin, roughly the late first millennium BCE (securely attested from about the second century BCE) through the ninth century CE.
Piao
The name used in Chinese sources for a kingdom or people conventionally and plausibly identified with the Pyu cultural sphere, though the exact scope of that identification remains open to scholarly discussion.
Beikthano
An early, extensively excavated Pyu fortified urban center on the middle Irrawaddy, important for establishing the archaeological framework of Pyu studies.
Sri Ksetra
The largest and most thoroughly documented Pyu city, notable for massive fortifications, Buddhist monuments, and the richest surviving body of Pyu inscriptions.
Halin
A major Pyu fortified city in the northern dry zone, notable for salt production, water-management features, and distinct local material culture.
City-state
A conventional descriptive term for a major fortified urban center and its surrounding hinterland; as used in this chapter for the Pyu centers, the term does not by itself prove that each city was politically independent, and it is adopted for its descriptive usefulness rather than as a settled conclusion about Pyu political structure.
Sino-Tibetan languages
A large language family that includes the Pyu language and the later Burmese language, among many others across the region; Pyu's precise position within the family, and its exact relationship to Burmese, remain unresolved, examined further in Chapter 15.
Dry zone
The central Irrawaddy basin region receiving comparatively low rainfall due to surrounding mountain ranges blocking much of the southwest monsoon.
Moat and rampart
Defensive features combining an excavated water-filled or dry ditch with an adjoining raised wall, characteristic of Pyu urban fortification.
Funerary urn
A stone or ceramic/terracotta vessel used to hold cremated human remains; several important examples from Sri Ksetra bear Pyu inscriptions recording names, titles, genealogical information, and dates.
Stupa
A Buddhist monumental structure, typically dome- or bell-shaped, built to house relics or commemorate sacred sites, central to Pyu religious architecture.
Reliquary
A container, often made of precious materials, used to hold relics associated with the Buddha or other venerated figures.
Cakravartin
The Sanskrit term (Pali cakkavattin/cakkavatti) for a universal, righteous, Buddhism-supporting monarch; an interpretive model some scholars have applied to aspects of Pyu royal and Buddhist patronage, rather than a concept directly attested in surviving Pyu-language sources.
Localization
The process by which Southeast Asian societies, including the Pyu, selected and transformed outside religious and cultural elements to fit local needs, as opposed to passively receiving them; examined further in Chapter 19.
Epigraphy
The scholarly study of inscriptions, a central evidentiary source for reconstructing Pyu religious, social, and political life.
Indic script
A writing system deriving from South Asian Brahmi-based scripts, adapted by the Pyu and other Southeast Asian societies to represent local languages.
Pali
A Middle Indic liturgical language used for Theravada Buddhist scripture, found in inscriptions at Pyu sites, particularly Sri Ksetra.
Sanskrit
A classical Indian liturgical and courtly language whose vocabulary and religious concepts, including cakravartin, influenced Pyu religious and political terminology.
Theravada
A tradition of Buddhism emphasizing the Pali canon, with evidence for its presence among other Buddhist traditions at Pyu sites.
Mahayana
A broad tradition of Buddhism emphasizing the bodhisattva ideal, with possible evidence for its presence alongside Theravada elements in Pyu religious material.
Tantric Buddhism
A set of esoteric Buddhist ritual and doctrinal traditions that some scholars have tentatively identified in certain Pyu material remains, though this interpretation remains debated.
Nanzhao
A powerful polity centered in present-day Yunnan whose ninth-century military activity is recorded in Chinese sources as affecting the Pyu region.
Tang dynasty
The Chinese imperial dynasty (618–907 CE) during which missions from a kingdom or people called the "Piao," conventionally and plausibly identified with the Pyu cultural sphere though the exact scope of that identification remains open to scholarly discussion, reached the Chinese court; the surviving records of these missions were preserved in dynastic compilations assembled later, including the Tang Huiyao (961) and the New Book of Tang (1060).
Hydraulic state model
A theory, associated with Karl Wittfogel, proposing that large-scale irrigation management produces centralized despotic political control; applied cautiously, and only partially, to Pyu evidence in this chapter.
Anachronism
The error of applying a later concept, identity, or political form to an earlier period in which it did not exist or apply, a risk this chapter addresses regarding both Pyu ethnic identity and the relationship between the Pyu world and Pagan.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it demonstrates, with unusual clarity, both the achievements and the genuine evidentiary limits of early Southeast Asian historical reconstruction. The Pyu cities were substantial, sophisticated, and historically significant, capable of monumental construction, religious scholarship, and international diplomacy, and yet so much about their internal political organization remains uncertain that responsible historians must speak in terms of interpretation and probability rather than settled fact. Students who take this uncertainty seriously, rather than treating it as a failure of the historical record, are better prepared to engage honestly with the rest of this textbook's treatment of early and classical Southeast Asian states.

The Pyu world also reveals larger processes that recur throughout this textbook: the selective, active adaptation of outside religious and administrative ideas into locally meaningful institutions; the danger of reading later political outcomes backward into earlier periods as though they were inevitable; and the necessity of using multiple, differently limited kinds of evidence, archaeology, epigraphy, and outside textual accounts, together rather than relying on any single source of information. These same interpretive habits will recur when this textbook examines the Mon world of the Chao Phraya basin in Chapter 25, the early states of the Malay Peninsula in Chapter 26, and the earliest political centers of Borneo and Java in Chapter 27, each of which developed its own distinct political and religious pattern during roughly the same broad period examined in this chapter.

Finally, this chapter's careful boundary, tracing Pyu urban and religious development through its ninth-century transformation but stopping short of narrating the rise of Pagan, models an important historical discipline: resisting the pull of a later, better-documented, and more famous historical outcome when it threatens to distort the honest treatment of an earlier, less completely understood society. The Pyu cities deserve to be studied and remembered for what they were, not only for what came after them.
