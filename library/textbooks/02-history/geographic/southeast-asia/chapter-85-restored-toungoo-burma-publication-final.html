Restored Toungoo Burma

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

The Restored Toungoo kings did not rebuild Bayinnaung's empire. They built something smaller, more carefully counted, and more durable: a royal state anchored in the Irrawaddy valley, sustained by registered service populations, supervised provinces, taxed land, river commerce, and Buddhist kingship, and it lasted for roughly a century and a half.

In 1638, officials acting in the name of King Thalun undertook a survey of the kingdom's people, land, and obligations. We know of this revenue inquest not because its complete register survives, but because later administrative compilations, particularly those prepared during Bodawpaya's reign, referred back to it, treating it as a benchmark of how a Burmese king could compel his realm to be counted. The inquiry is a useful opening to this chapter for what it implies. A king does not order such a survey over a landscape of ruins, scattered refugees, and rival warlords. By the late 1630s the Toungoo monarchy, which had fragmented violently a generation earlier, once again commanded enough officials, enough literate record keepers, and enough local cooperation to ask who owed what to whom.

Chapter 84 examined the rise of the First Toungoo Empire under Tabinshwehti and Bayinnaung, its extraordinary conquests across the Irrawaddy valley, the Shan states, Lan Na, and parts of the Chao Phraya basin, and the political fragmentation that followed Bayinnaung's death in 1581 and culminated in the sack of Pegu in 1599. This chapter begins where that story of imperial breakdown ends. It asks how a monarchy that had overextended itself, exhausted its manpower, and lost its capital was reconstructed, and why the reconstructed system, though far less spectacular than its predecessor, proved considerably more durable.

The chapter treats reconstruction as an institutional and social process rather than as a sequence of royal victories. Campaigns matter, and the careers of Nyaungyan, Anaukpetlun, and Thalun supply the chronological frame, but the central subjects are the instruments through which kings turned military success into regular government: appointed and hereditary officials, a council of ministers, hereditary service registers, provincial governors, revenue inquests, control of river corridors and ports, and a carefully managed relationship with the Buddhist monastic order. Each of these was negotiated with local elites, monks, merchants, and villagers, who were participants in the restoration and not merely its subjects.

The chapter also resists a tempting simplification, namely that a smaller kingdom was a lesser or merely defensive one. The restored monarchy concentrated on the Irrawaddy valley, treated the Shan states and Lan Na as frontier relationships of differing intensity, and recovered, for a time, access to the sea. Its rulers faced limits familiar to every early modern mainland state: scarce labor, uncertain harvests, the competing claims of monasteries on land and people, and rivals along every frontier. How they handled these constraints, and why the arrangements eventually frayed, is the historical problem this chapter explains.

The evidence is uneven and must be handled with care. Burmese royal chronicles, above all U Kala's Maha Yazawin, composed in the early eighteenth century, preserve a court-centered narrative written long after many of the events they describe. Stone and metal inscriptions record donations to pagodas and monasteries and offer glimpses of local officials and landholders. Palm-leaf and parabaik (folding paper) records, where they survive, document later administrative practice. Dutch, English, and Portuguese merchants and missionaries left contemporary descriptions, though they saw chiefly the ports and courts. Each source type captures part of the picture, and the chapter repeatedly asks whose voice is present and whose is not.

The chapter's argument unfolds in five divisions. Division I traces political restoration from the 1590s to the capture of Syriam in 1613. Division II examines royal power, the central court, provincial administration, and military organization, including the consolidation under Thalun. Division III analyzes the material foundations of government: agriculture, taxation, inland commerce, and overseas trade. Division IV turns to the Buddhist Sangha, royal patronage, village life, and the diversity of the kingdom's peoples. Division V follows the system's long middle life and its crisis, ending with the fall of Ava in 1752 and a synthesis of the restored monarchy's significance.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the Toungoo monarchy was restored after 1599, distinguishing the contributions of Nyaungyan, Anaukpetlun, and Thalun.
  2. Contrast the expansive imperial strategy of the First Toungoo Empire with the more concentrated administrative strategy of the restored kingdom.
  3. Analyze the significance of Syriam, the career of Filipe de Brito, and the resettlement of Portuguese and Eurasian captives for royal power and Burmese commerce.
  4. Describe the structure of royal government, including the court, ministerial council, provincial governors, and hereditary local officials.
  5. Distinguish service populations (ahmudan) from tax-paying commoners (athi) and explain how registration of manpower supported royal authority.
  6. Evaluate what historians can and cannot establish about the 1638 revenue inquest and the evidence on which knowledge of it rests.
  7. Assess the relationship between the crown and the Buddhist Sangha, including tensions over tax-exempt religious land and manpower.
  8. Reconstruct the economic and social lives of villagers, artisans, soldiers, and women in the restored kingdom, acknowledging the limits of surviving evidence.
  9. Interpret the role of Asian and European merchants in Burmese commerce without treating European companies as the central actors.
  10. Explain the interconnected causes of the crisis of the eighteenth century, including the Mon-led restoration of Hanthawaddy in 1740 and the fall of Ava in 1752, without treating collapse as inevitable.
  11. Analyze the diversity of Bamar, Mon, Shan, Portuguese-descended, and other communities without equating language with fixed political identity.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How does a state reconstruct central authority after fragmentation, and what is the relationship between military reunification and durable government?
  2. Why might a smaller, more tightly administered kingdom prove more durable than a larger empire built mainly by conquest?
  3. What does the 1638 revenue inquest reveal about the aims of royal government, and how do the limits of the surviving evidence shape what we can claim?
  4. How did the crown negotiate with local elites, hereditary officials, monks, and merchants rather than simply commanding them?
  5. Why did control of people matter more to Burmese rulers than control of territory alone?
  6. How did royal patronage of Buddhism both strengthen the crown and drain its resources?
  7. What roles did Asian merchants, European companies, and Portuguese-descended communities play in the restored kingdom's economy and military?
  8. Why did a system that worked for generations eventually weaken, and what distinguishes a structural weakness from a cause of collapse?
  9. How do retrospective chronicles and contemporary foreign accounts complement and contradict each other as evidence for this period?

Historical Context

The restored kingdom grew from the landscape described in earlier chapters. The Irrawaddy valley had supported Burmese-centered polities since Pagan (Chapters 40 and 41), and the fragmented political world that followed Pagan's decline, with its rival centers at Ava, Pegu, Toungoo, and Prome, is examined in Chapters 59 and 60. The Mon kingdom of Hanthawaddy at Pegu commanded the commerce of the lower Irrawaddy delta and the Gulf of Martaban, while Ava dominated the dry-zone rice lands upriver. Tabinshwehti and Bayinnaung brought these worlds together in the sixteenth century, as Chapter 84 narrates. Their empire depended on military mobilization, on the personal authority of the king, and on the willingness of subordinate rulers, including the Shan sawbwas and the rulers of Lan Na, to accept Toungoo overlordship.

That empire was hard to sustain. Its campaigns drew heavily on the labor and food of the same populations whose cultivation supported the court. Capital and revenue were concentrated at Pegu, while subordinate rulers governed their territories with considerable autonomy. Royal gifts of land and people to the Buddhist monastic order, an expression of pious kingship, gradually shifted resources away from royal control. After Bayinnaung's death in 1581, his son Nanda Bayin could not hold together an empire whose subordinate rulers could now seek other allegiances. Regional rebellions, wars with Ayutthaya and Arakan, and population displacement culminated in the fall of Pegu in 1599, with Arakan, Toungoo, and other rivals each acquiring a share of the wreckage.

Students should approach the restoration with two cautions. First, it was not inevitable; at several points in the 1590s and 1600s, other outcomes, including permanent partition among Ava, Toungoo, Pegu, and Arakan-backed regimes, were plausible. Second, the restored monarchy was not simply a return to the earlier model. Historians influenced by Victor Lieberman's work on Burmese administrative cycles argue that the restored state rested on a different logic: a deliberate concentration of authority in the Irrawaddy heartland, more regular supervision of service obligations, and a more careful accounting of manpower and revenue. This chapter adopts that interpretive framework while noting, where relevant, scholarly questions about how far these innovations were new and how effectively they operated.

Section 85.1

The Political Landscape after the First Toungoo Collapse

The sack of Pegu in 1599 left the Irrawaddy world without a recognized center. The coalition that destroyed the royal capital did not replace it with a single successor. Arakan, whose rulers had expanded eastward across the Arakan Yoma and along the Bay of Bengal coast, claimed the profits of the delta ports. The Toungoo branch of the royal family held its ancestral town. Ava, on the upper river, was held by a prince of Bayinnaung's line, and the Shan states and Lan Na, whose rulers had been bound to Pegu by tribute and personal allegiance, were free to reconsider their loyalties. Ayutthaya, whose armies had pressed into Lower Burma in the final years of the sixteenth century, was a further claimant to influence in the south. For several years there was no obvious reason to expect that any Burmese king would again govern the whole valley.

The human and economic costs of the collapse were considerable, although the surviving evidence rarely permits precise quantification. Warfare, forced movement of populations, and the destruction of Pegu's urban fabric disrupted settlement and agriculture in the delta and the middle valley. People fled to other towns, to monasteries, to the hills, or into the service of local magnates who could offer protection. Royal service lists, which recorded who owed labor or military service to the crown, were disrupted; households that had been registered under one authority now owed obedience, in practice, to whoever could enforce a claim. Because the kingdom's wealth depended on people who cultivated land and rendered service, depopulation and dispersal struck directly at the capacity of any would-be king to rebuild.

Restoration nevertheless proved possible, and the reasons are important. The upper river valley, with its irrigated rice districts, retained a relatively dense population and a long tradition of royal administration inherited from Pagan and Ava. The institutions of Buddhist kingship, the literate class of officials and monks, and the habits of recording service and land survived the fall of the capital. Local elites, who had no interest in permanent chaos, were willing to cooperate with a credible overlord who promised protection and the recognition of their local standing. And the rival powers surrounding Burma were themselves constrained: Arakan could raid but not easily govern inland, while Ayutthaya, after its own wars, could not maintain forces in the Irrawaddy for long. These conditions gave an ambitious ruler at Ava an opportunity but not a guarantee.

For this chapter, the essential point is a difference in strategy. Bayinnaung had governed by conquering and attaching subordinate rulers to his person across a vast space. The restorers who emerged from the collapse had to rebuild from the center, beginning with the territory they could actually supply, administer, and defend. The sources, mainly chronicles written at later courts, tend to compress this process into heroic narratives of royal campaigns. Historians reading them alongside inscriptions, European accounts, and later administrative records see instead a gradual, uneven recovery, in which the reconstitution of officials, service households, and revenue mattered as much as victory in the field.

Section 85.2

Nyaungyan and the Reconstruction of Upper Burma

Nyaungyan, a son of Bayinnaung, established himself at Ava in the closing years of the sixteenth century. Chronicle traditions and modern reconstructions differ about the exact timing of his rise and the sequence of his early campaigns, so the dates attached to his reign, generally given as extending from the late 1590s to 1605, should be treated as approximate. What the sources agree upon is the character of his achievement: he was a builder of a base rather than a conqueror of an empire. Ava and the irrigated districts around it provided grain, labor, and a defensible river position, and the dry zone's older traditions of administration gave him officials and record keepers to draw upon.

Nyaungyan's methods were practical. He sought to reassemble the service populations scattered by the collapse, rewarding local leaders who brought their followers back into royal registers and sending armed forces against those who refused. Campaigns against Shan polities to the north and east extended his influence, reducing the independence of frontier rulers who had asserted themselves after 1599 and securing access to the resources of the upland valleys. Where he could not govern directly, he accepted negotiated subordination: a Shan sawbwa who acknowledged the king's overlordship, supplied tribute and troops, and retained local authority was more useful than one who had to be conquered and garrisoned. Nyaungyan's Shan campaigns approached the frontier of Lan Na, but the kingdom of Chiang Mai had its own succession struggles and connections with Ayutthaya; it should not be described as having submitted to Nyaungyan. The new dynasty's decisive intervention in Lan Na came under Anaukpetlun in 1614–1615, following requests from local political actors and conflict among regional rulers.

His reign mattered chiefly for what it made possible. By restoring a functioning court at Ava, reestablishing a reliable pool of manpower, and demonstrating to local elites that royal authority could again deliver order, Nyaungyan prepared the ground for a more ambitious successor. The historian should resist reading this as destiny. Nyaungyan died in 1605 leaving a kingdom of real but limited extent, with no guarantee that his son would succeed or that the rival centers in the south would yield. Success depended on the decisions, and the luck, of his successor.

The restoration began with the recovery of trust as well as soldiers. A court that could distribute protection, settle disputes, recognize landholding, and keep trade moving offered local leaders practical reasons to acknowledge it. That relationship did not eliminate coercion: the return of scattered service households could restrict their freedom to choose employers or places of residence. But it explains why the crown's expansion depended on bargains made at village and district level. The upper valley provided a supply base from which such bargains could be enforced; it was not evidence of a fully restored empire. Nyaungyan's death before the reconquest of Lower Burma also reminds us that rebuilding a capital and restoring a kingdom were different achievements.

Section 85.3

Anaukpetlun and the Reunification of the Irrawaddy Valley

Anaukpetlun succeeded his father in 1605 and spent much of his reign extending royal power down the river. Chronicle-based accounts place his capture of Prome in 1607 or 1608, followed by Toungoo in 1610 and Syriam in 1613. The difference between 1607 and 1608 for Prome illustrates the need to distinguish source chronologies rather than imply a single certain date. What the sequence demonstrates is a deliberate strategy of control over the river corridor. Prome sat at a strategic point where the valley narrows between the dry zone and the delta; Toungoo commanded the Sittang valley and routes eastward. Controlling both meant controlling the movement of troops, grain, and taxes between Upper and Lower Burma.

It would be misleading to describe the reunification as a succession of battlefield triumphs. Each town taken had to be integrated: its governor replaced or confirmed, its service households registered, its granaries and boats brought within royal supply. Strategic towns were valuable because they sat among the agricultural settlements and the pools of manpower that supported government. Capturing a town without securing the surrounding villages produced no revenue and few soldiers, while a locality that cooperated could supply both. Anaukpetlun's success rested therefore on a combination of force, negotiated surrender, appointment of loyal officials, and the use of members of the royal family and trusted commanders as regional governors.

The reunification also extended toward the frontiers. Chronicles and Northern Thai accounts describe Anaukpetlun's intervention in Lan Na in 1614–1615, including operations around Lampang and Chiang Mai amid local succession disputes. The ruler of Nan helped facilitate Burmese intervention and subsequently received authority at Chiang Mai under Burmese suzerainty. This was not yet the same as direct administration by Ava; Lan Na's political arrangements changed again later in the century. His forces also operated along the southern coast in conflict with Ayutthaya. These campaigns show that the restored monarchy still sought influence beyond the Irrawaddy valley, but the evidence indicates that the outer relationships were less secure than the core. The Irrawaddy heartland, supplied by a relatively dense population and a navigable river, could be administered with some regularity; Shan polities and Lan Na required changing arrangements with local rulers; some later came under more direct royal supervision, and their political relationships cannot be summarized as merely intermittent tribute or resistance.

Anaukpetlun also relocated the royal capital to Pegu, reflecting the importance he attached to the delta and its maritime connections. This decision responded to the strategic and commercial value of the south after Syriam's fall, and it contrasts with his successor's later return to Ava, discussed in Section 85.5. The two choices reveal a debate within the dynasty about where the kingdom's center of gravity should lie: the commercial and maritime south, or the agricultural and administrative north.

Conquest also had an important administrative afterlife. A ruler entering a recovered town needed to decide which former officials could be trusted, how demands for service would be recorded, and how neighboring cultivators would be protected from passing troops. Than Tun discusses an order issued at Anaukpetlun's accession in 1605, reassuring inhabitants that commerce should continue despite the change of king. Such an order is evidence of royal intention rather than proof that everyone felt secure, but it illustrates an important aspect of reunification: the monarchy sought to restore predictable conditions for markets and households while it assembled military force. The political language of protection was itself an instrument of restoration.

Section 85.4

Syriam, the Portuguese, and the Recovery of Maritime Access

Syriam (Thanlyin), on the Pegu River near its mouth, was one of the principal ports of the Gulf of Martaban. After the fall of Pegu in 1599, Arakan's ruler placed Filipe de Brito e Nicote, a Portuguese who had come to Asia as a young man and entered local service, in charge of the port's customs. Within a few years de Brito asserted autonomy. With support from the Portuguese authorities at Goa, who recognized his position, he fortified Syriam and governed it as a quasi-independent port. Efforts by Arakan to dislodge him failed, and he became a recognized, if resented, power at the head of the Gulf.

Historians approach de Brito with caution because most narratives, whether Portuguese missionary reports or Burmese chronicles, were written by parties with strong interests. Portuguese accounts tended to present him as a defender of Christianity; Burmese chronicles emphasize his foreign domination and the conflict with Buddhist institutions. Modern scholarship tends to see him less as a representative of Portuguese imperial power, which had limited resources in Burma, than as an adventurer exploiting a political vacuum. He depended on local alliances, on mercenaries, and on revenue from trade, and his rule was resented by Burmese and Mon populations who bore its taxes and by Buddhist communities who disliked its religious policies.

Anaukpetlun's forces besieged Syriam in 1613 and took the port after a siege conducted with the support of ships and troops. De Brito was executed. The conquest restored royal control of the principal southern port, removed a rival foreign-backed ruler, and reopened the royal court's access to the profits of maritime trade. It also demonstrated that the restored monarchy could mobilize naval and siege capacities beyond the river valley. Chapter 68 places Portuguese activity within the wider Indian Ocean context, and Chapter 70 examines the Portuguese position at Melaka and the struggle for the straits; Syriam shows how a small Portuguese presence in Lower Burma depended on local politics.

The consequences of the capture extended beyond the port. Anaukpetlun resettled many Portuguese and Eurasian captives, along with other skilled prisoners, in Upper Burma, where their descendants became the hereditary royal service communities known as bayingyi, a term derived from the word for "foreigners." Employed as gunners, musketeers, and artisans, and permitted to retain their Catholic religion, they illustrate a recurrent feature of Burmese statecraft: the incorporation of war captives and foreign specialists into the registered service population. This practice, also applied to Manipuri and other captives, supplied the crown with valuable skills and added to its manpower. Section 85.16 returns to these communities.

Section 85.5

Thalun and the Consolidation of the Restored Monarchy

Anaukpetlun's reign ended in violence. In 1628 his son Minyedeippa murdered him and claimed the throne; the resulting contest for succession ended with Thalun's victory in 1629. The episode highlights a persistent weakness of Burmese monarchy: the absence of a single binding rule of succession meant that every royal death risked a struggle among princes, each of whom could mobilize followers, officials, and service households. Thalun, who had been a regional governor with experience of administration, succeeded in ending the contest and, more importantly, used his long reign to turn the military gains of his predecessors into a more regular government.

Thalun's most visible decision was to return the capital to Ava in 1635. The move relocated the court from the maritime south to the agricultural and administrative heartland. Several reasons are suggested by historians. Ava was close to the irrigated rice districts and the densest concentrations of service households, and it lay beyond the reach of the seaborne raids and foreign pressures that had troubled Pegu. It also stood at a safer distance from Arakanese and Siamese forces. The decision did not mean abandoning the south, which remained crucial for revenue and trade and was governed through appointed officials, but it signaled a preference for control of people and grain over direct supervision of maritime commerce. The Kaunghmudaw pagoda near Sagaing, begun in 1636 and substantially completed after Thalun’s death in 1649, connected the restored capital with Buddhist merit-making and royal legitimacy. Its construction was a sustained dynastic undertaking, not an instantaneous accompaniment to the 1635 transfer.

Thalun is associated above all with attention to government: regulating the Sangha and religious landholding, reorganizing service obligations, and conducting the revenue inquest of 1638, examined in Section 85.10. Than Tun's study of the reign draws on stone and palm-leaf records and later references to reconstruct these measures. Historians should note a methodological caution: because later Burmese administrators and chroniclers admired Thalun as a model ruler, accounts of his reforms may idealize their coherence. Nonetheless, converging evidence suggests a king who understood that durable power depended on knowing and controlling the resources of his realm.

Thalun's reign was not free of military coercion or intervention beyond the Irrawaddy basin. After renewed conflict in Lan Na, his forces took Chiang Mai in 1631; Ken Kirigaya describes a period of pacification and rearrangement of government that followed. This qualifies any image of Thalun as a ruler concerned only with peace at home. The important distinction is one of strategic priority: his frontier interventions served a more concentrated kingdom whose ministers also devoted sustained attention to village order, personnel registers, religious endowments, and the legal process. His rule should be evaluated through these connected activities, not set up as a simple opposition between warfare and administration.

Section 85.6

Kingship, the Royal Court, and Central Government

The restored monarchy drew its legitimacy from several overlapping sources. The king was a patron of Buddhism whose merit, expressed through the construction of pagodas, donations to monasteries, and support for the Sangha, was believed to sustain the prosperity of the realm. He was also the heir of earlier royal traditions, claiming connections to Bayinnaung and, through royal genealogies, to the kings of Pagan. He was a military commander and the ultimate arbiter of justice, whose law drew on Buddhist legal texts known as dhammathats. Chronicles composed at court naturally emphasized these ideals, and students should remember that practical authority depended as much on the cooperation of officials and local elites as on ideology.

Central government centered on the king and a council of senior ministers, known in later usage as the hluttaw. Ministers, titled wungyi and wundauk, advised the king, conveyed royal orders, supervised revenue, and administered justice. The king appointed ministers, commanders, and provincial governors, often from among relatives and trusted followers, and could dismiss them. This appointment power was a strong instrument of control but also a source of instability, because appointed officials built their own followings and became rivals. The central court was a site of constant factional competition among princes, ministers, and prominent monks, in which the king's skill at balancing interests determined the stability of his rule.

Historians caution that the formal structure of the court is better documented for later centuries than for the seventeenth. Specific offices and procedures described in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century sources may have reflected later elaboration, and the Thalun-era court should be reconstructed from converging earlier evidence, including inscriptions and European descriptions of audiences at Ava, rather than assumed to match later practice exactly. The stronger claim supported by the evidence is that the restored court put renewed emphasis on written records, defined service obligations, and supervision of officers; the precise size of the seventeenth-century bureaucracy relative to its sixteenth-century predecessor cannot simply be inferred from later administrative manuals.

The work of royal government included legal disputes, not just military orders and taxation. Drawing on royal edicts, Than Tun describes the compilation in 1629 of a dhammathat associated with a monk of Taungthpila and the lord of Kaingza. Civil adjudication could emphasize negotiated settlement, although legal procedure also distinguished offenses against individuals from actions deemed threatening to the crown. The same evidence shows attempts to control judges' fees and the behavior of advocates. These are revealing examples of the court's desire to regulate officials who exercised authority in the king's name. They do not, however, demonstrate that rules were applied uniformly across the kingdom or that subjects enjoyed rights in a modern constitutional sense.

Historical Vocabulary
Hluttaw and Wungyi

Hluttaw is the Burmese term for the royal council or the chamber in which senior ministers met to deliberate and issue orders; wungyi means "great minister." The terms are most securely attested in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, but historians use them in discussing the seventeenth-century court because the institution appears to have older roots.

Why it matters: The council illustrates how kingship worked in practice: the king ruled through officials who could advise, delay, or implement his orders. Potential misunderstanding: The hluttaw was not a parliament or a body with independent legal authority. It operated at the king's pleasure, and its members' influence depended on royal favor and their own followings.

Section 85.7

Provincial Administration and the Limits of Central Authority

Beyond the capital, the restored monarchy governed through a layered system of towns, districts, and villages. Principal towns, known as myo, were governed by officials appointed by the crown, often titled myowun, who were responsible for collecting revenue, mustering service households, maintaining order, and judging disputes. Important provinces were entrusted to princes or high-ranking officials, who held considerable authority and, particularly in distant regions, behaved as semi-autonomous rulers. Below the town level, hereditary local headmen, known as myothugyi in later sources, managed districts and villages, linking communities to the royal administration.

The role of hereditary officials is crucial. Appointed governors were outsiders to the localities they governed, but the headmen whose families had managed districts for generations knew the land, the households, and the customs. The crown depended on their cooperation to register households, collect revenue, and recruit labor. In return, local elites received recognition of their status, a share of local revenue, and access to royal patronage. The relationship was negotiated and often tense: officials could be dismissed, but dismissing a locally embedded family could provoke resistance, and governors who overreached might find the districts under them quietly noncompliant.

Distinguishing royal aspiration from administrative capacity is therefore essential. Royal orders spoke the language of comprehensive control, but the king's actual reach depended on the loyalty of intermediaries, the speed of messengers, the season, and the willingness of communities to comply. The distance of a province from Ava, the strength of its leading families, and the proximity of rival powers all shaped how far royal authority extended. The restored system was effective in the Irrawaddy heartland, where river transport, dense settlement, and long administrative tradition supported regular government. It was less uniform in the delta, where settlement had been disrupted, and varied considerably in upland and frontier areas. Lan Na illustrates change within a single region: Anaukpetlun initially worked through local rulers, while later seventeenth-century crises led Ava to appoint governors more directly.

Village appointment practices show how local continuity could coexist with royal approval. Than Tun's account of Thalun-era orders identifies three considerations for choosing a headman: inherited entitlement, support among the inhabitants, and acceptance by superiors. The principle illustrates why an apparently centralized monarchy could not simply replace every local officeholder at will. A headman capable of identifying residents and organizing communal obligations helped make the state legible, but his local roots could also enable selective reporting. Officials sometimes expected a village to pursue offenders and answer collectively for failures of order. Such arrangements reveal the burdens placed on communities as well as the administrative reach claimed by the center.

Section 85.8

Military Organization, Manpower, and Frontier Governance

Military power in the restored kingdom rested on the registration of service households. Instead of relying on the personal followings of great commanders, as the First Toungoo Empire had done to a large extent, the crown tried to maintain lists of households obliged to provide military service, and of the officers responsible for them. When campaigns were planned, officials mustered the households of particular districts, which supplied soldiers, boatmen, porters, and provisions. Garrisons at strategic towns, particularly on the river corridor and the frontiers, provided continuing defense and regional control. Specialist groups, including gunners, cavalry, elephant handlers, and craftsmen, formed hereditary service communities whose skills were passed from generation to generation.

Firearms mattered, but they did not transform royal power by themselves. Muskets and cannon, which Portuguese and other foreign specialists helped to introduce and operate, strengthened sieges and river warfare, and the bayingyi gunners were valuable for that reason. But the crown's military strength depended mainly on its ability to feed and supply armies, which required control of grain, boats, and manpower. Campaigns were seasonal, constrained by the monsoon and the agricultural calendar, since soldiers drawn from farming households could not be kept away from their fields indefinitely without damaging the revenue base on which the kingdom depended. This constraint made the restored kings more cautious about prolonged expeditions than Bayinnaung had been.

Frontier governance varied. Within the Irrawaddy valley, authority was relatively direct: governors, garrisons, and registered households formed a continuous administrative fabric. Among the Shan states, the crown generally relied on negotiated subordination: sawbwas retained local rule but acknowledged the king's overlordship, supplied tribute and troops, and sent family members to the court. Lan Na, further to the east, initially remained under local rulers acknowledging Anaukpetlun's suzerainty after 1615. During the crisis of the early 1660s, Siamese forces occupied Chiang Mai, local forces expelled them, and Ava thereafter tightened control by appointing governors. Thus administrative arrangements changed over time rather than fitting permanently into one category. Chapter 56 examines Lan Na's own society and politics; here the point is that Burmese authority combined negotiated overlordship with episodes of direct supervision, and should not be characterized as uniformly weak or uniformly centralized. Students should distinguish three categories: direct administration, negotiated subordination, and tributary authority, and should recognize that a single region could move among them over time.

The surviving orders give this military organization unusual specificity. Than Tun describes Thalun-era arrangements separating palace, civil, and military service and assigning specialist laborers to royal establishments. Some service personnel were settled in designated quarters and given cultivable land; occupational assignments could be hereditary, and supervisors were instructed to report births, deaths, disability, ordination, and absences. These were prescriptive regulations, not complete evidence that everyone remained in the category assigned. They show why the court saw an effective muster as an accounting problem: the soldiers of a campaign were only one part of a larger service system that included boats, food preparation, transport, craft labor, and maintenance of royal institutions.

Section 85.9

Land, Agriculture, and the Agrarian Economy

The restored kingdom rested on rice. In the dry zone of Upper Burma, around Ava, Sagaing, and the districts of Kyaukse, Minbu, and the Mu valley, cultivators grew rice with the help of irrigation works, weirs, and canals that drew on tributary streams. Many of these works had been developed or maintained under earlier kingdoms, including Pagan (Chapters 40 and 41), and their upkeep required labor, coordination, and local knowledge. Irrigated districts produced relatively reliable harvests in a region of uncertain rainfall and supported dense populations, which is why they were the political and administrative center of gravity. Rain-fed fields, dry crops such as sesame, millet, pulses, and cotton, and the products of palm groves supplemented the diet and the market economy.

Lower Burma offered different advantages. The delta and the lower Irrawaddy and Sittang valleys received abundant rainfall and had large areas of fertile land, but in this period much of the delta was lightly settled, subject to flooding, and recovering from the disruptions of the late sixteenth century. Rice cultivation there was less dependent on irrigation but required clearing, drainage, and a labor force that the delta did not always have. The great expansion of delta rice cultivation belongs to a much later period under British rule (Chapter 107), and students should not project it backward. In the seventeenth century, the south's importance lay in its ports, its timber and other forest products, and its potential for agricultural recovery rather than in massive grain surpluses.

Land and people were connected. Villages, organized around fields, water sources, and shared religious buildings, were the basic units of settlement. Land could be held by cultivating households, by officials and members of the elite as service or reward, by religious institutions through donation, and, in some cases, by the crown directly. Because land was abundant relative to labor in many areas, control of people was often more important to rulers than control of territory in itself. A king who held land but lacked cultivators could collect little revenue; a king who held households could, by settling them on land, create revenue. This principle explains why royal policy emphasized registration, resettlement of captives, and prevention of flight into monasteries or the service of magnates.

An agricultural landscape was also an administrative landscape. Irrigation channels and embankments required work at particular seasons; families needed access to fields and water long before crops could be counted as revenue. Locally coordinated repair therefore mattered as much as royal decrees announcing a project. In districts where a community was recovering from war, the movement of households back onto cultivated land could involve negotiations over old holdings and labor duties. The king's interest in resettlement was not simply demographic. Cultivators sustained garrisons, markets, monasteries, and the court, and an irrigated plot without enough workers offered little immediate fiscal return.

Section 85.10

Taxation, Revenue Inquests, and Royal Finance

Royal finance in the restored kingdom drew on three kinds of claims: labor, produce, and money. The most distinctive was labor and service. A large part of the population, the households known as ahmudan, owed hereditary service to the crown, whether as soldiers, boatmen, craftsmen, or retainers attached to a particular officer or office. In return, these households were typically exempt from ordinary taxation and received land or other support. Commoner households, the athi, owed taxes in produce, in money, or in both, as well as occasional labor on public works. Because service obligations were hereditary and attached to the household, the crown's strength depended on keeping accurate lists of who belonged to which category and on preventing households from slipping out of the lists.

Revenue rules could also operate as a defense against the crown's own agents. Than Tun reproduces Thalun-era edicts insisting that collectors observe established dues and warning against unauthorized exactions. Such pronouncements were severe in tone, but their value as evidence lies chiefly in the problems they identify: an official with access to records and soldiers could demand more than was authorized, reducing the confidence of cultivators and merchants. Strict orders did not abolish extortion. They reveal that the center understood the political cost of unchecked intermediaries, and that accurate assessments mattered only if people could distinguish a legitimate obligation from an officer's private demand. The inquest and enforcement of customary dues were therefore complementary, not identical, policies.

Historical Vocabulary
Ahmudan and Athi

Ahmudan refers to hereditary royal service households, owing military or other service in return for exemptions and support. Athi refers to commoner households that owed taxes and occasional labor rather than regular hereditary service. The terms are best documented in later periods, particularly the eighteenth century, but historians use them, with care, to analyze the seventeenth-century system because the underlying distinction between service and tax-paying populations appears to have older roots.

Why it matters: The categories show that royal power depended on controlling people. Potential misunderstanding: The categories were not fixed castes. Households could move between categories through royal grant, punishment, flight, donation to monasteries, or resettlement, which is exactly why the crown sought repeated inquests to determine who belonged where.

Collecting taxes required officials and records. Town governors, hereditary headmen, and specialized clerks compiled lists of households, land, and obligations, recording them on palm leaf or on parabaik, folding paper books. Local officials had both the knowledge and the opportunity to conceal households, divert revenue, or favor their own followers. The crown therefore periodically ordered a general survey, known in Burmese as a sittan, to establish the true condition of the realm. These inquests were assertions of royal authority as much as statistical exercises: by compelling officials, headmen, and monastic managers to account for people and land, the king reasserted control over resources that had slipped into private or religious hands.

The best-known such survey in this period is Thalun's inquest of 1638. Its existence is attested through references in later royal administrative compilations, especially those from Bodawpaya's reign, as D. G. E. Hall explained, but the complete original register does not survive. This evidentiary limit matters. Historians can say with confidence that the king ordered an inquiry into households, land, and religious holdings, and that the exercise was remembered as a benchmark of effective government. They cannot reconstruct from the surviving record exact totals of households or revenue, and they should treat figures attributed to the inquest by later writers with caution. The inquest should be understood as an attested historical undertaking whose detailed results are largely lost.

The political implications were considerable. Registering households and land allowed the crown to identify tax-exempt religious lands that exceeded their legitimate donations, to restore households that had escaped service, and to adjust obligations. It also threatened the interests of officials, local elites, and monasteries accustomed to the informal use of resources. The success of the inquiry therefore depended on the king's ability to secure the cooperation or acquiescence of these groups. Where such cooperation was withheld, compliance was partial. The long-term effectiveness of the restored state turned on whether later kings could repeat Thalun's insistence on accounting.

Cause and Effect

Why Revenue and Manpower Reforms Strengthened Royal Authority

Long-term causes

Labor scarcity relative to land made control of people the key to royal power. The First Toungoo Empire's exhaustion of manpower and the gifts of land and people to monasteries had drained resources from the crown.

Short-term causes

The dislocation after 1599 had scattered registered households, while the need to supply armies and rebuild the capital made accurate records urgent for rulers from Nyaungyan to Thalun.

Immediate trigger and context

Thalun's consolidation after 1629 and the move to Ava in 1635 created an administrative center able to compel officials to account for their districts.

Immediate effects

The crown identified and restored service households, regulated religious land and ordination, and clarified obligations, increasing royal revenue and manpower in the heartland.

Long-term consequences

The practice of inquests became a model for later governments, and the stability of the restored kingdom for generations is partly attributable to this regular accounting.

Historical complexity

Compliance depended on local cooperation. Later erosion of registration, through flight, donation, and elite diversion of households, shows that reform strengthened the crown only so long as it was enforced.

Reading the pattern: The reforms strengthened royal authority by enhancing information and enforcement, not by changing the fundamental dependence on local intermediaries.

Section 85.11

Markets, River Transport, and Inland Commercial Exchange

The Irrawaddy and its tributaries formed the arteries of the kingdom. Boats carried rice, salt, fish, cotton, timber, cloth, lacquer ware, and other goods between the dry zone, the middle valley, and the delta. The river also carried soldiers, officials, royal orders, and tax produce. Because navigation depended on seasonal water levels and prevailing winds, the movement of goods followed a rhythm: downstream voyages during the high-water season, upstream voyages assisted by winds or by tracking from the bank. River towns such as Ava, Sagaing, Prome, and Pegu were therefore natural centers of markets, storage, and administration.

Inland exchange linked specialized production zones. The dry zone produced rice in irrigated districts, as well as cotton, which was an important commodity in trade with Yunnan and elsewhere. The southern regions supplied timber, forest products, salt, and fish. Upland districts contributed forest goods, metals, and, in some areas, precious stones. Local crafts, including pottery, lacquer ware, weaving, metalwork, and the making of religious images, supplied both village markets and the court. Markets operated in towns and at religious festivals, often under the supervision of local officials who collected dues and kept order.

The crown's relationship to commerce was twofold. It taxed trade through market dues, tolls, and customs, and it sometimes monopolized or reserved particular goods, including certain precious stones, for the king. At the same time, it depended on merchants, many of them local and some foreign, to move supplies and provide credit. Because most royal revenue came from land and people rather than commerce, the court was not wholly dependent on trade, but commercial activity enriched officials and local elites and provided the money needed for purchases of arms, cloth, and luxury goods. Historians should resist both the idea that Burmese kings disdained commerce and the opposite view that they ran a mercantile state; the evidence shows an agrarian monarchy that valued and taxed trade without making it the foundation of government.

The seasonal river system created points at which different forms of authority met. A merchant transporting cotton or forest products might encounter local toll officials, royal agents at larger river towns, and port authorities closer to the sea. The same journey depended on boat crews, landing places, storage facilities, and networks of traders who could extend credit or provide information about demand. No single office could command this chain in full. The monarchy benefited when officials kept routes sufficiently predictable for goods to move, but tolls and discretionary restrictions could also redirect traffic. Commercial geography thus helps explain both the state's interest in the Irrawaddy corridor and the persistent bargaining power of merchants and local authorities.

Section 85.12

Ports, Overseas Commerce, and Foreign Trading Communities

Lower Burma's ports connected the kingdom to the Bay of Bengal and the wider Indian Ocean. Pegu and Syriam, along with Martaban and other coastal towns, received ships from the Coromandel and Bengal coasts, from Southeast Asian ports, and from further afield. Indian merchants, many of them Muslim and Hindu traders from the Coromandel and Bengal coasts, were long-established participants in this commerce, importing textiles, which were in high demand, and taking away rice, timber, lac, and other goods. Mon, Burmese, Chinese, Persian, and other merchants also operated in the ports, forming cosmopolitan communities with their own leaders and customary privileges.

Europeans formed a small part of this world. After the Portuguese episode at Syriam, English and Dutch companies sought commercial access to Burma during the seventeenth century. The Dutch East India Company (Chapter 77) established trading posts in Burma in the 1630s and maintained them, with interruptions, until the late 1670s, while English traders also operated, particularly at Syriam, in the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries. These companies sought textiles-for-goods exchanges, rice, lac, and other products, and negotiated with Burmese officials for permission to trade, to maintain houses, and to receive customs concessions. Chapter 92 examines the commercial world of early modern Southeast Asia as a whole.

It is important to keep the European role in proportion. The companies operated at the king's sufferance, under rules set by Burmese officials, and within a market where Asian merchants were dominant. They did not control Burmese territory or politics and often found themselves frustrated by royal monopolies, uncertain customs, and the bargaining power of local officials. Their records are valuable because they were meticulous and contemporary, but they primarily reflect the Dutch and English view of commerce and, because they concentrated on the ports and the court, tell us less about the inland economy. Historians therefore use them with care, to illuminate commercial practices while avoiding the error of making European companies the principal actors of Burmese economic history.

Dutch records discussed by D. G. E. Hall illustrate the bargaining position of the Burmese court. In a reply preserved in Batavia's company register, Thalun complained that Dutch hostility toward Portuguese traders disrupted accustomed commerce, and he attributed some Dutch losses to competition from Indian merchants and failures to understand local business methods. The commodities he identified for export included rubies, gold, lac, tin, long pepper, wax, deer pelts, and buffalo horns. This is a reported royal position rather than a neutral market inventory: it shows the crown seeking trading partners without accepting Dutch commercial preferences as its own.

A documented episode shows the court using its commercial position actively. In Hall's account, two VOC agents, Dirck Steur and Wiert Jansen Popta, obtained a royal audience at Ava in September 1635. Hall also discusses a separate royal letter preserved in Dutch translation, in which Thalun defended Burmese trading practices and criticized Dutch efforts to exclude Portuguese merchants. These are not identical documents, and the letter's precise dating should not be inferred from the audience. Taken together, as mediated through Hall rather than a new collation of Dutch originals, they illustrate a market in which a Burmese ruler evaluated competing outsiders and sought outlets for goods on terms he regarded as advantageous.

Section 85.13

The Buddhist Sangha and Monastic Institutions

Theravada Buddhism, whose spread across the mainland is examined in Chapter 53, shaped the institutional landscape of the restored kingdom. The Sangha, the community of ordained monks, lived in monasteries (kyaung) in towns and villages across the kingdom. Monasteries were centers of worship and meditation, but also of education: boys of many social backgrounds studied there, learning to read Burmese and Pali and acquiring moral instruction. The literacy that supported administration, record keeping, and the copying of religious and legal texts depended heavily on monastic education. Monks also served as advisers, mediators, and arbiters in village life, giving them social authority beyond the monastery walls.

Monasteries held land. Donors, from kings and officials to ordinary villagers, gave fields, orchards, and servants to monasteries to earn merit, and such grants could be recorded on stone or metal inscriptions. Gifts to the Sangha were often exempt from taxation and service, meaning that the households who worked the land owed their labor to the monastery or pagoda instead of the crown. This arrangement fulfilled religious ideals, but it created a structural tension: each donation increased the wealth of religious institutions and reduced the manpower and revenue available to the state. Villagers, too, had incentives to attach themselves to monastic lands or to enter the monkhood in order to escape service.

Thalun's efforts to distinguish religious from royal property could require attention to earlier donations. Than Tun reports that authorities consulted inscriptions recording grants, tried to identify the extent of land devoted to religious establishments, and reclaimed some holdings. The distinction was politically sensitive because endowments were both pious commitments and claims over fields and labor. Religious dependents could be removed from ordinary military service; their status was not interchangeable with that of free cultivators. The court therefore sought to define where legitimate donation ended and unauthorized exemption began. That policy should not be flattened into either persecution of monks or complete royal mastery over them: it was an attempt to negotiate a difficult boundary within a Buddhist kingdom.

Historical Vocabulary
Sangha

Sangha (Pali) designates the Buddhist monastic community, comprising ordained monks living under disciplinary rules known as the Vinaya. In Burmese usage it also refers to the institutional order as a whole, including its monasteries and, by extension, its landed endowments.

Why it matters: The Sangha was simultaneously a spiritual community, an educational system, a landholder, and a political actor whose support legitimated kings. Potential misunderstanding: The Sangha was not a unified church under a single authority. Monks belonged to diverse lineages and monasteries, and the crown's efforts to regulate them were attempts to impose discipline on a decentralized order.

Thalun's regulation of the Sangha addressed this tension. Than Tun's study of the reign, based on inscriptions, palm-leaf records, and later references, shows royal concern with monastic discipline, with the accounting of religious land, and with preventing laymen from taking ordination merely to avoid obligations. The measures can be read as an effort to secure both religious purity and fiscal discipline. By checking the legitimacy of ordination, regulating donations, and investigating whether monasteries held more land and people than their grants warranted, the crown sought to restore households to royal registers without openly attacking religion.

The relationship was not simply adversarial. Monks relied on royal patronage and protection, and the crown relied on the monks' moral authority, their educational work, and their ritual services. Royal support for monks accused of laxity or for those whose lineages were favored could confer advantages, while royal disfavor could be damaging. Chronicles composed by monks and officials generally praise kings who purified the Sangha, but historians should read such praise as shaped by the interests of the writers. What can be said is that the crown continually negotiated, rather than dictated, the terms of its relationship with the monastic order.

Section 85.14

Royal Buddhist Patronage, Legitimacy, and Religious Life

Royal patronage of Buddhism served religious, political, and social purposes. Kings built and restored pagodas, endowed monasteries, sponsored the copying of scriptures, and presided over donations to monks. Such acts generated merit, understood in Buddhist thought as a moral force improving the donor's future, and displayed the king's piety to his subjects. Thalun's Kaunghmudaw pagoda near Sagaing, begun in the mid-1630s, shows how a monument could do several things at once: it commemorated the return to Ava, claimed religious legitimacy, and displayed the crown's capacity to mobilize labor and materials. Inscriptions at many sites record donations by kings, queens, officials, and ordinary people, linking them in a shared practice of merit-making.

Chronicles contributed to legitimacy. Court historians recorded royal genealogies, campaigns, and acts of piety in ways that placed each king within a continuous tradition of Buddhist kingship. U Kala's Maha Yazawin, written in the early eighteenth century, is the most important surviving chronicle for this period, and its account of the restoration shaped later historical memory. Because it was composed long after many events it describes, it must be compared with contemporary sources, including inscriptions and European accounts, to test its details. Research on the chronicle's reliability shows that it preserves valuable material, drawn in part from older records, while also shaping events to fit courtly ideals.

Court-sponsored Buddhism was only one part of religious life. In villages, people made offerings to monks and attended festivals, but also honored spirits known as nats, believed to inhabit landscapes, trees, and ancestral lineages and to influence health, harvests, and safety. Practitioners did not necessarily see this as a conflict with Buddhism; the two coexisted in daily life. Royal ceremony itself incorporated spirit veneration and astrological and Brahmanical elements inherited from earlier courts. Historians should avoid treating the doctrinally pure Buddhism of monastic texts as the whole of religion. Evidence for ordinary religious practice is thinner than for court ritual, but surviving inscriptions, later ethnographic descriptions, and the persistence of spirit cults point to a layered religious world.

Kaunghmudaw also provides a material example of how chronology and patronage must be separated. The pagoda's construction began in 1636 after the relocation of the court, and the ceremonial raising of its finial occurred in 1649 under Thalun's successor Pindale. Its association with Thalun is therefore justified, but completion was a dynastic act extending beyond his lifetime. The building's inscriptional and architectural evidence can be used alongside narrative sources to ask whose labor, resources, and ritual claims monumental projects represented. A monument tells historians that a patron wished to make an enduring religious and political statement; by itself it does not measure the prosperity or consent of the people who built it.

Section 85.15

Villages, Households, Labor, and Everyday Life

Most people in the restored kingdom lived in villages and worked the land. A household typically cultivated rice, vegetables, and other crops, kept livestock, fished, gathered forest products, and made tools, cloth, and household goods. Village communities, often organized around a headman and a local monastery or pagoda, managed shared resources such as water, grazing land, and paths, and organized festivals and religious donations. The royal administration reached the village through officials and headmen who assessed households, collected taxes, and called out service, which meant that royal reconstruction touched ordinary people in concrete ways, shaping where they could settle, what they owed, and to whom.

Obligations differed by status. Athi households paid taxes and owed labor for public works such as canal repair or pagoda building, while ahmudan households owed hereditary service, often as soldiers or specialists, in exchange for land or exemptions. Debt and bondage were present: households that could not meet obligations or that suffered harvest failure might borrow from wealthier neighbors, officials, or monasteries, and could fall into dependency. Some people moved to escape burdens, finding refuge on religious lands, in the service of magnates, or in less closely supervised districts. The crown's repeated efforts to register households can thus be read as a response to constant, quiet movement by people seeking better terms.

Households also experienced administration through marriage, age, and gender. Thalun-era orders discussed by Than Tun asked local authorities to record marriage status and age as part of efforts to maintain and reproduce service populations. The language of those orders presents people as resources for labor and military recruitment, but cannot stand in for a full account of family decisions. Women cultivated land, participated in exchange, managed household work, and appeared within systems of obligation, yet the surviving elite-centered records rarely allow historians to reconstruct their choices with the detail available for royal officials. Marriage alliances, widowhood, debt, and the movement of dependents might affect a family's obligations differently, and regional custom mattered. Students should distinguish the concerns expressed in edicts from the actual lives of the families to whom they applied.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Women, and Work

Evidence for ordinary life in this period is indirect. Inscriptions record donations by named individuals, including women, and later legal texts, the dhammathats, and nineteenth-century observers indicate that Burmese women commonly held property in their own right, took part in marketing and trade, and had significant say in marriage and divorce. Historians use these later sources cautiously for the seventeenth century, since practice may have changed, but the pattern is consistent enough to suggest that women's economic roles were considerable. Women worked in agriculture, weaving, pottery, and market trade, and some, particularly in elite families, managed property and made religious donations.

Children contributed to household labor from an early age, herding animals, fetching water, and helping in the fields, while boys might spend periods in monastery schools. Artisans, including weavers, potters, lacquer workers, metalworkers, and builders, formed hereditary or semi-hereditary groups, some attached to the court or to temples. Soldiers and their families lived in service villages and might be absent for seasonal campaigns, leaving women and children to manage fields. Slaves, including debt bondsmen and war captives, worked in households, on religious estates, and in royal service. The scarcity of direct testimony from these groups means that historians must infer their experience from administrative practice, donation records, and later legal and ethnographic sources.

For ordinary people, restoration brought mixed consequences. Renewed order meant protection from raiding and arbitrary seizure by rival warlords, and the revival of irrigation and markets supported recovery of agricultural production. But royal registration also meant fixed obligations, reduced opportunities to escape burdens, and the demands of service in campaigns and construction. Villagers were not passive; they bargained with headmen, sought the protection of monasteries, moved when they could, and sometimes resisted. The experience of the restored state therefore varied by status, place, and household circumstances.

Section 85.16

Ethnic, Linguistic, and Religious Diversity in the Restored Kingdom

The restored kingdom was linguistically and culturally diverse. Burmese-speaking communities, often called Bamar in modern usage, formed the majority in the dry zone and middle valley, while Mon-speaking populations were concentrated in Pegu and the delta. Shan-speaking communities lived in the plateau and valleys to the east and northeast, and Karen, Chin, Kachin, Arakanese, and many other groups inhabited upland and frontier districts. Language was an important marker of community but did not map neatly onto political loyalty or fixed ethnic identity. People could be bilingual, could change language over generations, and could assume identities appropriate to their service, residence, or patron.

Integration into royal service blurred boundaries. Mon, Shan, Arakanese, Manipuri, and other captives, as well as Burmese households resettled by royal order, entered the registered service population and often assimilated linguistically and culturally over time. Court culture, using Burmese language and Buddhist institutions, provided a framework within which diverse peoples could become part of the royal system. At the same time, local elites, notably Shan sawbwas and Mon aristocratic families, preserved regional traditions, and the authority of the crown varied with the degree to which local leaders were willing to cooperate. Modern national categories should therefore not be projected onto the seventeenth century: the Mon revolt of 1740 (Section 85.19) was a political movement with regional and economic roots, not the simple expression of a fixed ethnic identity.

Religious diversity existed within the Buddhist majority. The bayingyi communities, descended from Portuguese and Eurasian captives, maintained a Catholic identity, supported at times by priests, while serving the crown as gunners and specialists. Muslim and Hindu merchants and artisans resided in ports and towns, and other minority communities, including Chinese traders, lived in commercial centers. Upland peoples followed diverse local religious traditions. The state could accommodate minority religious practices within service and trading relationships, but the surviving evidence does not establish a consistent policy of toleration for all groups or periods; local conditions and royal priorities mattered. Evidence for the experience of these communities is limited and often comes from the perspective of outsiders, including missionaries, merchants, and court chroniclers, so historians must interpret it with care.

Sources on royal establishments make diversity visible in a less obvious way than campaigns and rebellions do. Than Tun identifies service and entertainment groups associated with several regions and language communities, including Shan, Arakanese, and groups named for places beyond the Irrawaddy heartland. These labels appear in administrative classifications, not in a census of self-declared identities. A household could be assigned a service designation inherited from a captive ancestor even after years of residence near Ava. This distinction helps explain why court records, while invaluable for studying mobility and coercion, cannot simply be translated into modern ethnic statistics. It also reminds us that incorporation could mean both opportunity for specialized employment and enforced hereditary attachment to an occupation.

Section 85.17

Burma and Its Neighboring Political Worlds

The restored kingdom existed within a field of neighbors whose actions constrained its choices. To the east, Ayutthaya remained a rival for influence over Lan Na and the Tenasserim coast. Chapter 57 traces Ayutthaya's rise and Chapter 86 its cosmopolitan institutions; for Burma, the essential point is that warfare and diplomacy along this frontier absorbed manpower and revenue and that control of Lan Na shifted between Burmese, Siamese, and local hands. King Narai of Ayutthaya sent expeditions into Lan Na in the early 1660s; Siamese forces occupied Chiang Mai by early 1663. Burmese relief forces could not retake the city, but local Chiang Mai forces subsequently expelled the Siamese and renewed their connection with Ava. The Burmese court then tightened supervision by appointing governors, so it would be inaccurate to describe Burmese authority after Narai as uniformly nominal. Ken Kirigaya's study of Lan Na under Burmese rule emphasizes the shifting balance among royal supervision, local agency, and regional military pressures.

To the west, Arakan, which had profited from the collapse of Pegu, remained a significant naval and commercial power on the Bay of Bengal during much of the seventeenth century, with changing ties to Portuguese and other maritime specialists. To the northwest, Manipur and its raiders posed a growing problem, especially in the eighteenth century, when attacks into the Chindwin and Irrawaddy valleys became frequent. To the north and east lay the Chinese frontier, where Yunnan authorities, border Shan states, and Burmese claims overlapped. The most dramatic episode of this period was the flight of the Ming claimant Yongli into Burma in 1659, the Qing-aligned campaign associated with Wu Sangui in 1661–1662, and the surrender of Yongli in 1662. The episode illustrates the vulnerability of a kingdom whose frontier relationships had been managed by negotiation rather than by overwhelming force.

These regional pressures worked against the restored state not as constant disasters but as persistent drains. Every campaign or border emergency demanded troops, supplies, and attention, and each failed or costly effort weakened the crown's ability to enforce registration and revenue at home. Conversely, periods of relative regional quiet allowed rulers to concentrate on internal administration. The restored kingdom's strategic position, caught between the Bay of Bengal, the Shan plateau, Siam, and China, thus influenced the pace and limits of its institutional development.

One episode especially illustrates the need to treat frontier authority as changing rather than static. Following disputes among Lan Na rulers, Thalun intervened and occupied Chiang Mai in 1631, after which Burmese authority operated through rearranged local arrangements. Decades later, the early-1660s Siamese intervention and local reaction produced another settlement, followed by stronger appointments from Ava. Kirigaya's comparison of Burmese and northern Thai chronicles challenges the older assumption that every period of Burmese influence was necessarily an undifferentiated age of devastation. His argument does not establish that warfare or extraction disappeared. It shows that provincial rivalries, trading conditions, and the interests of local elites have to be examined before describing the effect of any change in overlordship.

Section 85.18

The Later Restored Toungoo Kings and Institutional Continuity

After Thalun's death in 1648, the throne passed to his son Pindale, whose reign ended in 1661 when his brother Pye seized power during the crisis surrounding the Ming claimant's presence in Burma. Pye's reign (1661–1672) was followed by a succession of kings, including Narawara, Minrekyawdin (reigned 1673–1698), Sane, Taninganway, and Mahadhammaraza Dipati, whose reign ended in 1752. Succession disputes and palace intrigues recurred, and kings were frequently overshadowed by powerful ministers and by court factions. This pattern is sometimes summarized by describing the later kings as weaker than Thalun, but historians caution against inferring institutional collapse from the personal profiles of rulers, since the machinery of government continued to operate for long periods.

Much did persist. The court at Ava remained the seat of government, the hluttaw and ministers continued to administer revenue and justice, provincial governors and hereditary officials continued to manage localities, and royal patronage of Buddhism continued to produce pagodas, monasteries, and inscriptions. Taxation and service obligations remained the basis of royal finance, and the recording of households persisted, if unevenly. The relative longevity of the system, lasting well over a century after Thalun, shows that it fulfilled essential functions: it provided a framework for resolving disputes, mobilizing resources, and sustaining religious and cultural life.

Yet weaknesses were emerging. Gifts to the Sangha and to pagodas, which were laudable acts of merit, accumulated and removed households and land from royal registers. Hereditary offices and local authority hardened, giving provincial elites greater independence. Kings, dependent on ministers and on distant governors, found it harder to carry out the sorts of inquiry that Thalun had undertaken. The early eighteenth-century chronicler U Kala compiled the Maha Yazawin in the early eighteenth century during Taninganway’s reign, apparently as an independent scholar rather than an official court historiographer. His narrative recorded political disturbances as well as royal virtues, but it ends its sustained coverage in 1711 and cannot by itself document the ensuing decades. Historians thus describe this phase as one of persistence rather than renewal: institutions continued, but their capacity to enforce accounting and mobilize resources was eroding.

The survival of institutions should be distinguished from the survival of evidence about them. U Kala's Maha Yazawin, completed in the first quarter of the eighteenth century, provides an indispensable narrative of royal succession and conflict, but its extended account ends in 1711. Later reigns consequently depend to a greater degree on subsequent Burmese chronicles and local traditions that were compiled after the events. Victor Lieberman's analysis draws attention to this imbalance and to the need to compare the centers that had competing interests in the outcome of the crisis. Neither the silence of a court narrative nor the appearance of an orderly succession proves that tax collection, frontier defense, or service registration functioned unchanged everywhere.

Section 85.19

Fiscal Strain, Regional Rebellion, and the Crisis of the Eighteenth Century

By the early eighteenth century, the crown's control over manpower and resources had weakened in several respects. Households registered as service or tax-paying populations were increasingly diverted to religious and private patronage, reducing the effective base of royal revenue. Provincial governors and hereditary elites acted with greater autonomy, and the court, absorbed in factional rivalry, was slow to respond to emerging problems. Frontier pressures intensified. Manipuri horsemen raided deeply into the Upper Burma heartland in the 1720s and later, destroying villages and undermining confidence in the crown's ability to protect its subjects, while the Chinese frontier and the Shan states demanded continuing attention.

The decisive blow came from Lower Burma. In 1740 a revolt rooted in Lower Burma's Mon-speaking communities and supported by a wider coalition of local elites reestablished a Hanthawaddy kingdom centered on Pegu. The rebels elevated Smim Htaw Buddhaketi as king; Binnya Dala succeeded him in 1747. The revolt drew on regional grievances, including resentment of royal demands, the weak presence of royal authority in the delta, and memories of Pegu's earlier prominence, as well as on the opportunity created by the court's distraction. Its leaders did not conceive of the movement in modern ethnic terms; membership included Mon, Burmese, and other participants, and its aims combined local autonomy, control of the ports, and the claims of a restored royal line. The southern kingdom, under its later ruler Binnya Dala, gained strength, took Prome, and in 1752 captured Ava, ending the Toungoo dynasty. Chapter 60 discusses the earlier Hanthawaddy kingdom and the political significance of the delta.

The Mon victory was not the end of the story, since a Burmese resistance movement soon emerged in the Upper Burma heartland under Alaungpaya, founding the Konbaung dynasty, but that development belongs to Chapter 94, as does the Konbaung administrative system. What matters here is that the fall of Ava in 1752 was the product of interlocking pressures rather than a single cause, and that nothing in the preceding decades made the outcome inevitable. A more vigorous king, a better-timed response to the delta revolt, or a quieter frontier might have produced a different result.

A collapse of authority need not begin at the capital. Lower Burma possessed ports, fertile districts, and memories of Hanthawaddy kingship that could support an alternative center when Ava's demands lost legitimacy. Conversely, Ava's recovery would depend on the response of people and leaders in the upper valley, not simply on the outcome of one siege. Mon-language political traditions, Burmese chronicles, and European reports speak from different positions within this conflict. The historian must distinguish the grievances and alliances of the 1740 movement from later national narratives that portray it as an inevitable confrontation between timeless peoples. Regional autonomy, dynastic claims, commercial resources, and frontier disruption all helped make the contest possible.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Restored Political System Eventually Weaken?

The question: The restored Toungoo state lasted for generations and then lost its capital in 1752. How much of this should be attributed to internal structural decline and how much to external pressure and contingent events?

Interpretation A — administrative erosion: Scholars influenced by Victor Lieberman's analysis of Burmese administrative cycles stress that kings repeatedly consolidated control of people and revenue and then gradually lost it, as donations to the Sangha, the growth of hereditary local power, and elite diversion of households reduced the crown's resources. On this view, the eighteenth-century crisis was the latest downturn in a recurring pattern.
Interpretation B — frontier and regional pressure: Other approaches emphasize external and regional factors: Manipuri raiding, pressure on the Chinese and Shan frontiers, and the growing independence of the delta and its commerce. In this view, even a well-administered state might have struggled with simultaneous pressures on several fronts.
Interpretation C — political contingency: A third emphasis falls on court politics: succession disputes, powerful ministers, and kings who could not impose decisions. Here the key question is not structural decline but the failure of leadership at critical moments.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on chronicles, inscriptions recording religious donations, descriptions of raids, European accounts, and later administrative records. Each source has limitations: chronicles reflect court perspectives, inscriptions document donations rather than the overall distribution of land, and foreign accounts concentrate on ports.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns causation and weighting rather than the basic sequence of events. Historians differ over how far religious land accumulation actually diminished royal capacity, how much autonomy local elites gained, and whether the Mon revolt reflected deep structural weakness or opportunistic timing.

Why it matters: If administrative erosion was central, the restored system's strength and weakness were intertwined, and its decline was patterned. If external pressure was central, its durability emphasizes resilience. If contingency was central, the outcome appears less determined. The three views are not mutually exclusive.

Student task: Select one interpretation and identify the evidence that would most strongly support it and the evidence that would most seriously challenge it. Explain how the three might be combined into a multi-causal account.

Section 85.20

The Historical Significance of Restored Toungoo Burma

The restored Toungoo state proved more durable than its sixteenth-century predecessor because it matched its ambitions more closely to its resources. By concentrating on the Irrawaddy valley, where population, grain, river transport, and administrative tradition coincided, it created a core that could be governed with some regularity. By registering service households, supervising provinces, and conducting inquests, it enhanced the crown's knowledge of its realm. By patronizing Buddhism while regulating the Sangha, it secured religious legitimacy and, for a time, limited the loss of resources to monastic institutions. And by treating the outer frontiers as negotiated relationships rather than as provinces to be directly governed, it avoided the overextension that had undermined Bayinnaung's empire.

Durability should not be confused with stability. The restored kingdom experienced repeated succession struggles, regional revolts, and frontier crises. Its institutions depended on the continued cooperation of local elites and monks, and its dependence on hereditary service meant that registration needed constant renewal. The very practices that sustained the system, such as merit-making donations and the delegation of authority, also created long-term problems. The system's vulnerability was thus built into its strengths, a pattern that Lieberman and others describe as an administrative cycle of consolidation and erosion.

There is a further lesson in the evidence itself. Royal edicts display the ambition to classify people, keep soldiers disciplined, regulate judges, and control religious land. Inscriptions preserve selected acts of patronage, while foreign merchants described negotiations that mattered to their business. None offers an uncomplicated view of the whole population. A historian who combines these partial perspectives can explain how the restored state worked without mistaking prescription for performance or a dynastic chronicle for a village census. Its durability was not the absence of conflict: it was the recurrent capacity to recover cooperation, enforce obligations, and preserve recognizable institutions despite shocks.

Timing and experience: Change was gradual and uneven, concentrated in the first half of the seventeenth century and most visible in the heartland. Officials and service households experienced the reforms most directly, while upland and frontier communities experienced greater continuity. Why the pattern matters: The comparison shows that political success can lie in institutional fit rather than territorial size, and that continuity and reform can coexist.

The restored kingdom also left a legacy for its successors. The memory of Thalun as a model administrator, the practice of inquests, the use of hereditary service categories, and the political geography centered on the Irrawaddy valley and its ports all shaped the Konbaung monarchy that emerged after 1752. The Konbaung rulers, whose expansion and administration are examined in Chapter 94, built on these inherited patterns while adding their own innovations and military ambitions. For the students of this textbook, the restored kingdom therefore stands as an important example of mainland early modern state-building: smaller than the first Toungoo empire, but more carefully constructed, and a bridge between the sixteenth-century conquest state and the eighteenth-century dynasty that would reunify the valley once more.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the Toungoo monarchy was reconstructed after the collapse of 1599 and why the reconstructed state endured for generations. Its central argument is that the restored kings did not recreate Bayinnaung's empire. They built a smaller and more carefully administered royal state centered on the Irrawaddy valley, in which control of people, revenue, and religious institutions mattered more than territorial extent.

Restoration began at Ava. Nyaungyan established a supplied and administered base in Upper Burma, reducing the independence of neighboring Shan polities and reassembling service populations scattered by the collapse. His son Anaukpetlun extended royal power down the river, taking Prome and Toungoo and, in 1613, capturing Syriam, ending the quasi-independent rule of Filipe de Brito and restoring access to the sea. The resettlement of Portuguese and Eurasian captives as hereditary royal specialists illustrated the crown's practice of absorbing skilled captives into its service population.

The reunification was fragile. Anaukpetlun's death in 1628 amid a family conflict exposed the lack of a binding rule of succession, a weakness that persisted throughout the dynasty. Thalun's accession in 1629 and his long reign marked the decisive consolidation. His return of the capital to Ava in 1635, his attention to monastic regulation, and the revenue inquest of 1638 expressed a concern with knowing and controlling the resources of the realm. Our knowledge of the inquest rests on later references rather than a surviving register, which limits what can be claimed.

Royal government operated through the king, a council of ministers, appointed governors, and hereditary local officials. Authority was strongest in the heartland and weaker in the delta and the frontiers, where it depended on negotiated recognition. The crown's military strength rested on registered service households, seasonal mobilization, and the supply of grain and boats, with firearms supplementing but not replacing these foundations.

The material foundations of the state were agricultural and fiscal. Irrigated rice in the dry zone supported the densest populations and the administrative center, while the delta's importance lay in its ports and its potential. Revenue came from service, produce, and money, and the distinction between ahmudan service households and athi tax-paying households structured obligations. Inland river commerce and overseas trade through Pegu and Syriam enriched officials and merchants and supplied the court, though most royal revenue came from land and people. European companies operated at the sufferance of the court and were secondary to Asian merchants.

Buddhism shaped legitimacy, education, and society. The Sangha was both a partner and a competitor of the crown, since donations earned merit but removed land and people from royal registers. Thalun's measures sought to regulate monastic land and ordination without attacking religion. Royal patronage, as at the Kaunghmudaw pagoda, displayed piety and power, while village religion combined Buddhist practice with spirit veneration.

Ordinary people experienced restoration in different ways. Renewed order and revived irrigation supported recovery, but registration fixed obligations and limited escape. Women's economic roles, the hereditary status of artisans and soldiers, and the presence of debt and bondage all shaped daily life, though evidence is indirect. The kingdom included Bamar, Mon, Shan, Arakanese, Manipuri, Portuguese-descended, and other communities whose identities were fluid and whose integration into royal service blurred ethnic and linguistic boundaries.

After Thalun, the system persisted under his successors, and many institutions continued to function for over a century. But the accumulation of religious endowments, the hardening of hereditary local power, factional court politics, Manipuri raiding, and pressures on the Chinese and Shan frontiers eroded the crown's capacity. The Mon-led revolt of 1740 restored Hanthawaddy at Pegu, and the capture of Ava in 1752 ended Toungoo rule, though the outcome was not inevitable.

The restored Toungoo state matters because it shows that durability can come from institutional fit rather than conquest. Its strengths, registration, supervision, and religious legitimacy, were also the sources of its vulnerability, since they depended on continuous enforcement and local cooperation. Its legacy shaped the Konbaung monarchy examined in Chapter 94 and remains an important case in the comparative study of mainland early modern states.

Source and Editorial Note

How This Chapter Uses Historical Evidence

The chapter combines retrospective Burmese chronicles with published studies of edicts, inscriptions, and seventeenth-century commercial correspondence. Institutional rules are discussed as evidence of government intentions; their practical enforcement varied. The 1638 revenue inquest is known through later references, not through an extant complete original register. The VOC source window uses an attributed paraphrase of documents discussed by D. G. E. Hall; it is not a facsimile-verified translation. The Lan Na discussion uses Ken Kirigaya's comparison of Burmese and northern Thai historical traditions and distinguishes local agency from royal claims.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that political success in early modern mainland Southeast Asia could be measured by institutional fit as well as by conquest. The Restored Toungoo state controlled far less territory than Bayinnaung's empire, yet it governed more effectively over the area it could supply, register, and defend. That contrast invites students to reconsider the common association of historical importance with territorial size.

The chapter also reveals larger processes shared across early modern mainland states: the centrality of manpower, the tension between royal power and religious endowment, the reliance on local intermediaries, and the recurring cycle of consolidation and erosion. These patterns reappear in Ayutthaya (Chapter 86), in the Vietnamese states (Chapter 87), and in the Lao kingdoms (Chapter 90), and comparing them helps distinguish what was specific to Burma from what was common to the region.

The restored monarchy shaped what followed. Its practices of registration, its memory of Thalun as an administrator, and its political geography were inherited by the Konbaung dynasty (Chapter 94), and its fall in 1752 set the stage for the wars with Siam examined in Chapter 95. At the same time, the Mon revival of 1740 is a reminder that the delta retained its own political life and that Burmese history cannot be told solely from the perspective of Ava.

Finally, the chapter illustrates how historians reason from imperfect evidence. A revenue inquest known only through later references, a chronicle written generations after the events, and merchants' reports written for distant employers each illuminate part of the past while concealing others. Understanding the restored kingdom means learning to combine these sources, to notice whose voices are missing, and to resist the temptation to read the outcome of 1752 back into the preceding century.
