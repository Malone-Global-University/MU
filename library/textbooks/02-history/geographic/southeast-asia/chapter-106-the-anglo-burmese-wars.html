The Anglo-Burmese Wars

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

In the second week of May 1824, a British expeditionary force sailed up the Rangoon River and landed at the principal port of the Burmese kingdom. The commanders had expected a populous commercial city, a hostile garrison, and perhaps a rising by Mon communities against Konbaung rule. What they found, according to contemporary British narratives, was a largely emptied town: its inhabitants dispersed, its food stores removed, and its defenders withdrawn to stockades inland. The occupation of Rangoon, proclaimed as a decisive blow, was the beginning rather than the end of a difficult war. Sixty-one years later, in November 1885, a British force again moved up a Burmese river, this time the Irrawaddy, aboard steamers, and reached the royal capital at Mandalay in less than two weeks. Between those two river journeys lay the arc of this chapter: the progressive loss of Burmese sovereignty.

The three Anglo-Burmese Wars of 1824–1826, 1852–1853, and 1885–1886 are often narrated as a single story of British advance and Burmese decline. This chapter argues that they should be understood as three related but distinct imperial conflicts. The first grew from competing claims along a contested frontier among Burma, the East India Company, and the small states and displaced communities of Assam, Cachar, Jaintia, Manipur, and Arakan. The second arose from a commercial and diplomatic quarrel at Rangoon that Lord Dalhousie, the Governor-General of India, turned into a war for territory. The third resulted from a mixture of commercial pressure, British fears of French influence, and an ultimatum whose terms would have placed Burma’s foreign relations under British direction. Each war ended differently: with a negotiated treaty, with a unilateral annexation and no treaty, and with the destruction of the monarchy.

The Konbaung kingdom that confronted Britain was not a weak polity waiting to be absorbed. It had defeated Qing invasions in the 1760s, destroyed Ayutthaya in 1767, conquered Arakan in 1784–1785, and extended its influence over Manipur and Assam (Chapters 94 and 95). Its rulers commanded large levies, an extensive system of royal service, a Buddhist court culture, and a revenue system centered on the Irrawaddy valley. The Burmese court’s reading of its own position was not irrational: it had real military successes behind it, and it misjudged less the justice of its claims than the logistical and financial reach of the Company state and, later, of the British Indian empire.

The chapter consistently distinguishes three things that older narratives often merge: a military victory, a territorial annexation, and the effective establishment of colonial government. British forces took Rangoon in 1824, Pegu in 1852, and Mandalay in 1885, but in each case holding the countryside, collecting revenue, and persuading populations to accept the new order took far longer than the capture of a city. After 1885 in particular, the fall of the capital opened a decade of armed resistance that British officials labeled “pacification.”

The evidence for these wars is unevenly distributed. British military narratives, parliamentary papers, treaty collections, and the correspondence of Governors-General survive in abundance. Burmese sources include the royal chronicles, notably the *Hmannan Yazawin* compiled in the early nineteenth century, court orders and administrative records, some of which are published in the multivolume *Royal Orders of Burma*, and later Burmese-language narratives. Mon, Rakhine, Shan, Karen, Kachin, Chin, Assamese, and Manipuri materials add further perspectives, but fewer have been translated. Missionary and merchant accounts, including those of men imprisoned at Ava during the first war, provide additional testimony. Students should ask throughout whose voice a source preserves and whose is missing.

The human cost of the wars is central to the chapter. British and Indian soldiers died overwhelmingly from disease rather than battle; Burmese soldiers and civilians suffered the effects of levies, requisition, stockade fighting, displacement, and later pacification. Reliable casualty figures are scarce, and the chapter distinguishes documented numbers from later estimates. It also gives attention to communities who experienced the wars differently from the Burmese court, including Mon, Karen, Rakhine, Shan, Kachin, and Chin peoples, and to the Indian soldiers whose labor, as sepoys and later military police, made British conquest possible.

This chapter belongs to Part XVIII, which examines Dutch and British expansion in maritime and mainland Southeast Asia. It follows Chapter 105 on the Straits Settlements and precedes Chapter 107, which studies British colonial Burma. Its responsibility is the wars themselves, the political decisions behind them, their territorial settlements, and the immediate resistance that followed annexation. What British rule became after conquest, in administration, rice agriculture, migration, and social change, belongs to Chapter 107.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the structure of Konbaung royal authority and why the kingdom was a significant regional power rather than a weak state awaiting conquest.
  2. Trace how Burmese expansion into Arakan, Manipur, and Assam and British expansion in northeastern India produced a contested frontier before 1824.
  3. Analyze the diplomatic breakdown of 1819–1824 and assess whether diplomacy could have prevented the first war.
  4. Describe the Rangoon campaign, Maha Bandula’s resistance, and the roles of disease, logistics, and Indian soldiers in the first war.
  5. Distinguish the provisions of the Treaty of Yandabo, separating the cession of Arakan and Tenasserim from Burma’s renunciation of claims in Assam, Cachar, Jaintia, and Manipur.
  6. Compare the causes of the second war with those of the first, evaluating British commercial grievances against Dalhousie’s territorial aims.
  7. Explain why the second war ended in unilateral annexation of Pegu without a peace treaty.
  8. Evaluate King Mindon’s reforms and challenge the view that Upper Burma stagnated between 1853 and 1878.
  9. Interpret the 1885 ultimatum, the Bombay Burmah Trading Corporation dispute, and the role of French diplomacy in the decision for the third war.
  10. Assess the role of river steamers and the Irrawaddy in the rapid British advance on Mandalay in 1885.
  11. Analyze post-annexation resistance, the colonial category “dacoit,” and the difference between conquering a capital and governing a country.
  12. Synthesize the three wars as stages in the destruction of Burmese sovereignty, comparing causes, methods, outcomes, and the varied experiences of Burma’s peoples.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. In what sense was the Konbaung monarchy a regional power, and how did its own conceptions of authority differ from British conceptions of sovereignty?
  2. How did the movement of refugees, rulers, and armies across the Arakan, Manipur, and Assam frontiers help cause the first war?

Historical Context

By the late eighteenth century the Konbaung dynasty, founded by Alaungpaya in 1752 and examined in Chapter 94, ruled the largest political system in the western mainland. It rested on the Irrawaddy valley, with its rice lands, river transport, and royal capital, and extended through a graduated set of relationships: districts governed directly by royal appointees; hereditary local headmen and service groups; tributary Shan principalities; and frontier peoples whose allegiance varied with the strength of the court. The kingdom had absorbed the Mon kingdom of Hanthawaddy (Chapter 60), repelled Qing armies, and reduced Ayutthaya. Its warfare was an expression of a political order in which kings measured their standing by the tribute and submission of neighbors.

The East India Company, by contrast, was a commercial corporation that had become a territorial state. After Plassey (1757) and the grant of the diwani of Bengal (1765), it expanded rapidly, and by the 1810s its armies, largely composed of Indian sepoys under British officers, had defeated the Marathas and Nepal and made the Company the dominant power in much of the subcontinent. Its financial base was Indian revenue; its soldiers were recruited from Indian communities; its governors-general, from Wellesley to Amherst, balanced strategic ambition against pressure from London to limit costs. In northeastern India its frontier touched small kingdoms such as Cachar, Jaintia, Assam, and Manipur, and the coast of Chittagong, which adjoined Arakan.

When the two states met, they did so across regions that neither fully controlled. Arakan had only recently been conquered by Burma. Assam’s Ahom kingdom was riven by factional strife. Manipur’s princes alternated between resistance, flight, and alliance. Cachar and Jaintia were small but strategically located. Refugees, raiders, merchants, and mercenaries crossed these borders. A modern map showing a line between “Burma” and “British India” misleads: the frontier in 1819 was a zone of overlapping claims, and the first war began there.

Later British writing sometimes explained the wars as the natural result of an “aggressive” Burmese court meeting “civilizing” Britain, or alternatively of a stagnant monarchy incapable of reform. This chapter rejects both frames. The Burmese court acted according to its own political logic and made real choices; British officials acted from a mixture of commercial, strategic, and ideological motives that changed across the century. The three wars share a pattern of escalation, but not a single cause.

Section 106.1 · Division I — The Konbaung Empire and the Road to War, 1784–1824

The Konbaung Dynasty and the Political Geography of Burma

At the end of the eighteenth century the Konbaung kingdom was the largest and most militarily capable state in mainland Southeast Asia’s western half. Founded by Alaungpaya in the 1750s on the ruins of the Restored Toungoo order, which Mon forces had overthrown in 1752, and after defeating the Mon-led Restored Hanthawaddy kingdom (Chapters 85 and 94), the dynasty built its power on the Irrawaddy valley. The river was both highway and fiscal artery: it carried rice, cotton, salt, teak, troops, and royal officials between the dry-zone heartland around Ava, Sagaing, Amarapura, and Mandalay and the delta ports of Rangoon, Bassein, and Syriam. Control of the river meant control of the kingdom’s revenues and mobility.

Royal authority was exercised through a layered system. The king, understood as a Buddhist monarch whose merit and patronage sustained the religion and the realm, ruled with the advice of the Hlutdaw, a council of senior ministers. Provinces were assigned to governors (*myowun*) who were often members of the court elite and who collected revenue, raised levies, and administered justice. Beneath them stood hereditary local headmen (*myothugyi*) whose standing rested on local lineage and who mediated between villages and the court. Population was classified roughly into royal servicemen (*ahmudan*), who owed hereditary service as soldiers, boatmen, artisans, or retainers in exchange for land and exemption from some taxes, and commoners (*athi*), who paid taxes in cash or kind. This arrangement allowed kings to mobilize large armies quickly, though at the cost of repeatedly removing men from agricultural labor. Bodawpaya’s revenue inquest of 1783 and the later royal inventories (*sittan*) are examples of the court’s effort to know and control its resources.

The kingdom’s peripheries were governed differently. Shan principalities, ruled by hereditary *sawbwas*, acknowledged Burmese kings through tribute and marriage alliances while retaining substantial local autonomy. Kachin, Chin, and Naga upland communities had looser, more situational relations with the court. Mon and Karen populations in the south and east were at times integrated, at times resistant, and at times targets of punitive campaigns. Arakan, after 1784, was held as a conquered province. It is therefore misleading to speak of a single Burmese “territory” with fixed lines. Territory under direct royal administration shaded into tributary regions and then into areas of occasional influence, a pattern historians often describe with the mandala model introduced in Chapter 1.

Konbaung rulers drew legitimacy from conquest and from religious patronage. Bodawpaya’s construction of pagodas, including the unfinished colossal Mingun temple, his support for monastic reform, and his seizure of the Mahamuni Buddha image from Arakan all expressed a conception of kingship in which sacred power and military success reinforced each other. The court at Amarapura, founded in 1783, and later at Ava (restored as capital under Bagyidaw in 1823), was therefore not merely an administrative center but a ritual one.

These features explain why Britain did not face a decrepit state. The Konbaung monarchy had defeated Qing invasions, outlasted several Siamese wars, and demonstrated an ability to raise large forces. What it lacked was not strength in general but certain specific capacities that would matter in the wars to come: naval power, financial reserves comparable to those of the Company, and an artillery and logistics system able to defeat a seaborne enemy at the mouth of its principal river. The wars exposed these limits; they did not reveal inherent weakness.

It is important to distinguish the resources that a king could theoretically command from the resources he could actually mobilize in a particular campaign. A royal decree might summon hereditary service groups and provincial contingents, but the speed of their arrival depended on harvest cycles, waterways, local headmen, and the willingness of officeholders to enforce obligations. Military elephants, river craft, gun crews, and laborers required food as well as orders. The court's impressive capacity for regional warfare rested on a labor system that could be stretched to breaking point when a distant campaign lasted longer than expected. A king who stripped fields of cultivators risked losing the rice and revenue that made warfare possible.

The relationship between Buddhism and political power also deserves more than a ceremonial description. Monasteries preserved literacy, mediated disputes, educated boys, and connected villages to centers of religious authority. Court patronage helped kings establish moral legitimacy, but monks and local donors did not become mere government employees. During war, religious institutions could shelter displaced families, preserve accounts of suffering, and convey judgments about the conduct of rulers. The destruction of a palace later threatened this elaborate set of relationships without erasing them. The British tendency to identify sovereignty with the king's person therefore obscured the resilience of community institutions that survived royal defeat.

Burmese Expansion into Arakan, Manipur, and Assam

Arakan, a Buddhist kingdom on the eastern shore of the Bay of Bengal with its own long history centered on Mrauk-U, fell to Bodawpaya’s forces in 1784–1785. A Burmese army commanded in part by the king’s son and heir marched overland through the Arakan mountains and by sea, exploiting divisions within the Arakanese court. The conquest brought the Mahamuni Buddha image, one of Arakan’s most venerated religious objects, to the Burmese capital, an act of both plunder and royal religious assertion. Large numbers of Arakanese (Rakhine) were deported to central Burma, where they were resettled as laborers and servicemen, and many others fled northwest into British Chittagong.

The conquest created the first durable point of friction with the East India Company. Refugees camped in Chittagong, and Arakanese resistance leaders used the British side of the border as a refuge from which to raid. Burmese authorities demanded the return of fugitives and sometimes pursued them across the frontier. A series of British envoys, including Michael Symes (1795 and 1802) and Hiram Cox (1798), visited Burma to discuss these matters and commercial prospects. Cox’s attempt to resettle refugees gave his name to Cox’s Bazar, but his mission also showed how difficult it was for the Company to reconcile humanitarian pressure, border security, and diplomatic relations with Ava. A major Arakanese rising led by Khyen-bran (Chin Byan) between about 1811 and 1815 again sent Burmese demands across the frontier and strained relations.

To the north, Manipur and Assam became the next theater. Manipur’s princes, divided among themselves, had alternately been tributaries, rivals, and victims of Burmese pressure. In 1819 a Burmese army invaded Manipur, and the prince Marjit Singh fled; his brother Gambhir Singh and other members of the royal family took refuge in Cachar and British territory. In Assam, the Ahom monarchy was fractured by faction. In 1817 one party invited Burmese forces to assist it against rivals, and by 1819 the Burmese had effectively become the arbiters of Ahom politics. Over the next few years Burmese commanders installed and deposed Ahom rulers, garrisoned the upper Brahmaputra valley, and by about 1822 administered much of Assam directly. The campaigns brought heavy suffering to Assamese villages, and many fled westward into British-held Bengal.

The Burmese court understood these campaigns within its own political order. Kings of Ava saw rulers on their frontiers as potential tributaries, and the arrival of a ruler’s representatives to seek help was routinely interpreted as submission. Intervention in Assam and Manipur was, in this view, the legitimate extension of royal protection and authority over neighboring princes who had acknowledged or could be compelled to acknowledge Burmese overlordship. The refusal of the Company to return refugees or its sheltering of defeated princes could appear to Ava as protection of rebels. The difference in understanding is crucial: for the court, frontiers were defined by loyalties of rulers; for the Company, increasingly, by territorial boundaries that governments could be asked to respect.

By 1822 the Burmese frontier stood on the Brahmaputra, touched Cachar and Jaintia, and bordered Bengal. Population displacement, competing claims to tributary authority, and the movement of armed parties had produced precisely the kind of overlapping jurisdictions in which a minor incident could become a casus belli. Expansion had been successful and, from Ava’s perspective, cheap. Few at court foresaw how it would look to Calcutta.

The experiences of Arakanese communities illustrate how conquest could transform a frontier into a persistent political problem. Flight did not necessarily imply that every refugee wished to support the East India Company. Some hoped to return home, others feared deportation, and still others organized armed attacks against Burmese officials. British administrators at Chittagong confronted demands to control a refugee population whose obligations and allegiances were not theirs to dictate. On the Burmese side, regional governors regarded raids from across the frontier as evidence that British protection encouraged insurrection. The same movements of people could consequently be described as humanitarian refuge, unlawful sanctuary, or political resistance, depending on the observer.

Nor was the struggle for Assam a simple invasion of a unified state. Factions among the Ahom political elite had invited outside intervention before Burmese commanders attempted to impose direct control. Local officials who initially viewed Burmese soldiers as allies sometimes came to see them as occupiers; others cooperated for reasons of family advantage or security. Those changes mattered to British intelligence and to the later recruitment of allies. By 1824 war was not only a clash between Ava and Calcutta but a competition for the support of frontier populations. The displacement caused by earlier campaigns made promises of order attractive while leaving bitter memories that would not be resolved by the Treaty of Yandabo.

British India, Frontier Politics, and Imperial Rivalry

The East India Company’s northeastern frontier before 1820 was thinly governed and strategically peripheral. Chittagong had been ceded by the Nawab of Bengal in 1760. Sylhet and the Garo and Khasi hills were being drawn into Bengal’s administrative orbit. Beyond lay semi-independent states whose relationship to the Company was undefined. Governors-General from Cornwallis to Hastings preferred to avoid costly expansion there; the Company’s main concerns were the Marathas, Nepal, and the Bengal revenue. The Nepal War (1814–1816) and the Third Maratha War (1817–1818) had left the Company dominant in India and financially strained.

This history matters because the Burmese advance coincided with a moment of British confidence. By 1823, under Governor-General Lord Amherst, the Company could no longer treat Assam, Cachar, and Manipur as irrelevant. Cachar’s ruler, Govinda Chandra, faced a Burmese threat and sought British protection; the Company eventually extended a protectorate over Cachar in 1823–1824. Jaintia was similarly caught between Burma and Bengal. Manipuri exiles, Ahom pretenders, and Arakanese fugitives sought Company assistance, and each had interests distinct from those of the Company. Gambhir Singh, in particular, would raise a Manipur Levy with British support and become an ally who pursued his own restoration. Treating this frontier as an arena for two centralized states obscures the agency of the local rulers and communities who pursued their own survival and advantage.

British motives combined strategy, commerce, and security. The Company valued Burma’s teak, which was important for shipbuilding at Calcutta and Bombay, and its trade in cotton, rice, and other commodities. It also looked to the Bay of Bengal as a corridor to Southeast Asia and, in some visions, to an overland route between India and Yunnan. Officials worried about Burmese ambitions. Rumors circulated that the court dreamed of marching on Bengal, and some Burmese statements about Chittagong and Dhaka gave them support; the evidence for any serious plan is thin, but the perception shaped British decisions.

Regional rivalry also mattered. Siam, long Burma’s enemy, drew closer to the Company during the 1820s, and the British considered it a potential partner. French and other European interest in the region had diminished after the Napoleonic wars, but the Company remained alert to Anglo-French competition throughout Asia. In 1824 the Company’s authorities in Calcutta did not expect a long war. They assumed that a naval blow at Rangoon would force Ava to the table. That assumption proved costly.

A striking feature of British frontier policy was its mixture of hesitation and assertiveness. Officials in Bengal sometimes instructed local officers to avoid entanglement, then accepted commitments to rulers whose restoration required force. The word 'protection' could signify diplomatic reassurance, armed assistance, or a claim to supervise a principality's foreign relations; the precise meaning varied by document and by circumstance. It was therefore easy for Burmese commanders to perceive the Company's involvement as territorial encroachment, even when officials in Calcutta denied seeking further land. Political agents and military officers made decisions before their superiors possessed complete information, and reports written after an incident often supplied the case for escalation.

The choice to attack Rangoon reveals the Company's imperial reach, but also its ignorance. Commanders could assemble troops, ships, guns, and supplies from Bengal and Madras, yet they depended on imperfect charts and merchant reports for knowledge of Burmese politics. An amphibious invasion offered the possibility of avoiding the mountains separating the two states. It also bypassed the frontier allies whose grievances had furnished the initial justification for war. This created a strategic contradiction: the expedition struck a central port to resolve disputes on the western frontier, assuming that damage to commerce would compel the royal court to yield. A campaign that did not bring rapid negotiation risked trapping the invaders in unfamiliar territory, far from established supply depots.

Section 106.4 · Division I — The Konbaung Empire and the Road to War, 1784–1824

The Diplomatic Breakdown and Outbreak of the First Anglo-Burmese War

Between about 1819 and 1824 Anglo-Burmese relations deteriorated through a sequence of frontier crises. After the Burmese invasion of Manipur and the consolidation of Burmese authority in Assam, Burmese commanders wrote to Company officials demanding the surrender of fugitives and asserting claims over Cachar and Jaintia, which they regarded as tributary to Assam and therefore to Ava. The Company, for its part, protested against Burmese incursions into territory it considered under its protection and gave shelter to refugees. Each party’s demands appeared to the other as threats.

The immediate crisis centered on Shapuree (Shahpuri) Island, a small, low-lying island at the mouth of the Naaf River, the border estuary between Chittagong and Arakan. Both sides claimed it. In September 1823 the Company stationed a small guard there; within days Burmese troops attacked and expelled it with casualties. In the same period, Burmese forces moved against Cachar. Calcutta reacted to what it saw as both an insult and a threat to the Bengal frontier. After an unsatisfactory exchange of messages, in which the court did not offer the apology and withdrawal the Company demanded, the Governor-General in Council issued a declaration of war on 5 March 1824.

Open warfare began on several fronts at once. In the northeast, British-supported forces moved to contest Cachar and Assam. In Arakan, Burmese forces led by Maha Bandula advanced into Chittagong and defeated a small British detachment at Ramu in May 1824, causing alarm in Calcutta. The Company’s main strategic decision, however, was to attack Burma at its commercial center by sea, an expedition that was already being organized and is the subject of Section 106.5.

The two governments understood sovereignty differently. For Ava, the king’s authority extended to those who acknowledged his overlordship, and overlordship over Assam, Manipur, and neighboring princes legitimated Burmese campaigns. For Calcutta, territorial boundaries ought to be fixed, protected principalities were under Company guardianship, and violation of that protection was a casus belli. Both positions had internal logic, and neither side recognized the other’s as legitimate. Burmese officials could not easily concede claims they regarded as sacred obligations of kingship; British officials could not easily accept a protectorate-less zone on Bengal’s border.

Could diplomacy have prevented the war? The evidence suggests that opportunities existed but that both governments underestimated costs. The Company could have accepted a negotiated settlement of the Shapuree and Cachar disputes; the Burmese court could have withdrawn from Cachar and sought arbitration. Neither did. Ava believed it could defeat a force sent against Arakan and Cachar and may not have expected an invasion of its heartland. Calcutta believed that Burmese power was brittle and that a quick victory would be profitable. The Company’s decision-makers, including Amherst, were also influenced by officers on the frontier whose reports emphasized Burmese arrogance. In retrospect both governments were wrong, and the war that followed lasted over two years and cost the Company far more than anticipated.

Before the war, individual confrontations accumulated into a record of mutually reinforcing suspicion. British political agents described Burmese demands as evidence of aggressive designs; Burmese officials understood the sheltering of exiled rulers as interference in tributary affairs. The incident at Shapuree Island became especially useful because it supplied a bounded place, a recognizable injury, and a simple narrative of occupation and expulsion. Yet the island alone cannot explain the enormous scale of the campaign mounted afterward. A reader who follows only the official war declaration might miss the decisions concerning Cachar, Ahom factions, and the proposed relief of Manipur that had already broadened the dispute.

The experiences of people living along the Naaf and Barak waterways expose another weakness in state-centered explanations. Local boats carried goods, relatives, fugitives, and military intelligence across spaces that officials wished to classify as fixed borders. When patrols and garrisons followed, trade and daily movement could become suspicious activities. For frontier households, the approaching conflict meant requisitioned food, uncertain protection, and the risk that an ally today would be a target tomorrow. The resulting insecurity encouraged still more movement across the frontier. A fuller explanation of 1824 therefore requires both diplomatic correspondence and the geography of villages, routes, and regional claims.

Historical Vocabulary
Sovereignty and Tributary Authority

Definition. Sovereignty is, in modern usage, the exclusive right of a state to govern a territory with defined borders. Tributary authority describes a relationship in which a ruler acknowledges the superior standing of another, typically through gifts, ritual submission, or military service, while often retaining local rule.

Historical context. Konbaung kings regarded Shan sawbwas, and claimed to regard rulers of Manipur, Assam, Cachar, and Jaintia, as tributaries. The Company, by contrast, was increasingly using treaties and protectorates that defined territorial limits and obligations in written form.

Why it matters. The war began partly because the two governments had irreconcilable understandings of what a frontier was and who could act on it. Tributary claims could be genuine, flexible, and overlapping; territorial claims were meant to be exclusive.

Potential misunderstanding. It is wrong to say that Burma had no concept of authority over territory; it had a different one. It is equally wrong to treat Britain’s concept as simply “modern” and Burma’s as outdated: the Company also used protection and overlordship strategically, and its “boundaries” were often asserted rather than established.

The Burmese expulsion of the British guard from Shapuree Island (September 1823) and the threat to Cachar (early 1824).

Section 106.5 · Division II — The First Anglo-Burmese War and the Treaty of Yandabo, 1824–1826

The British Invasion of Rangoon, May 1824

The Company’s principal operation was an expedition under Major-General Sir Archibald Campbell, assembled at Calcutta and Madras and carried by sea to Rangoon. Planners reasoned that Rangoon, the kingdom’s main port, collected customs revenue, supplied rice and teak, and sat at the head of a delta from which the Irrawaddy led toward Ava. A blow there, it was hoped, would force the court to negotiate. The Company also expected local help: Mon communities in the delta had suffered under Konbaung rule, and British officials assumed that many would welcome an invader.

The expedition reached Rangoon on 10 May 1824. The Burmese governor’s forces fired from the riverside batteries, but the British landing met little sustained resistance, and troops occupied the town. Contemporary accounts agree that Rangoon was nearly empty. Burmese authorities had ordered inhabitants to withdraw, had removed provisions and boats, and had left only token defenses. This was not disorganization but a recognizable strategy of denying an invader supplies and drawing him inland while the main army was recalled. Britain’s plan, which depended on local provisioning, immediately encountered shortage.

The invading force consisted of several thousand troops, including King’s (British Army) regiments, units of the East India Company’s European forces, and a large contingent of Indian sepoys, chiefly from the Madras Army, supported by Royal Navy and Bengal Marine vessels. The substantial role of soldiers recruited in India is easy to overlook in narratives that speak of “British” victories. Sepoys marched, fought, built, and died in Burma in large numbers, and the Company’s ability to wage this war depended on Indian revenues and Indian manpower. A small steamer, the *Diana*, was also used on the river, an early sign of what steam would later mean.

The occupation of Rangoon did not mean the British had secured the countryside or a route to Ava. The monsoon began soon after the landing. Roads became channels of mud, the troops lacked transport animals and boats, and intelligence about the interior was poor. The expected Mon rising did not occur on the scale hoped for. Some Mon communities aided the British; others fled or remained loyal to Burmese authority, and in time many Mon would suffer retribution. Burmese stockades ringed the occupied town, and the British, camped in the abandoned buildings and the compounds of the Shwedagon pagoda, soon found themselves besieged by weather and sickness as much as by an enemy. Rangoon was a foothold, not a conquest.

The landing also exposed a misconception about the port itself. Rangoon was a royal customs center and an entrance to the Irrawaddy system, but its merchants and inhabitants could evacuate, and the court could withdraw supplies from the invader's reach. A flag over warehouses could not ensure access to the rice needed by thousands of soldiers. Burmese commanders avoided giving the expedition exactly the battle it expected. Instead, they drew their forces toward defensive works and threatened communications between the landing area and the countryside. The emptiness of the city denied the British an easy source of provisions and intelligence.

The expedition was not culturally or militarily homogeneous. British regular regiments served alongside East India Company units and Indian sepoys drawn from different presidencies, supported by sailors, gunners, transport workers, and medical personnel. Their officers imposed different expectations of health, diet, discipline, and camp organization. Supply failures were felt unevenly: European soldiers could be especially vulnerable to diseases against which they had little acquired immunity, while Indian troops faced their own environmental and logistical burdens. Civilian carriers and camp followers rarely feature in battle narratives despite sustaining the operation. Their experiences should be reconstructed through muster returns and logistical records rather than assumed from the general's dispatches. The campaign's first months consequently illustrate the difference between arriving with a large force and maintaining that force where no reliable supply base existed.

Section 106.6 · Division II — The First Anglo-Burmese War and the Treaty of Yandabo, 1824–1826

Maha Bandula, Burmese Resistance, and the Campaigns of 1824–1825

Maha Bandula was the most prominent Burmese commander of the war. He had served in the campaigns in Assam and Arakan and, after the British landing at Rangoon, was recalled to lead the defense of the heartland. His army, raised from royal servicemen and fresh levies, was assembled over several months and reached the Rangoon area in late 1824. British accounts give inflated figures, sometimes tens of thousands; the numbers cannot be verified, but the army was large by regional standards.

Burmese military practice combined mass infantry, muskets, jingals (light swivel guns), cannon, and above all fortified positions. Stockades of teak and earthwork, built with rapidity and often supported by trenches, were designed to blunt assaults and to protect men from artillery. This was a rational response to the Burmese environment, where dense vegetation, flooded fields, and rivers favored defensive works. The Burmese also used war boats on the river and fire rafts against British ships. But their artillery was less mobile and less accurate than that of the Company, and they lacked the ships and engineers needed to dislodge an enemy sheltering under naval guns.

Bandula’s attacks against Rangoon in the first week of December 1824, which included an assault on British lines at Kokine, were repulsed with heavy casualties. British artillery, Congreve rockets, and disciplined infantry volleys broke the Burmese advance. Bandula withdrew to Danubyu on the Irrawaddy, a fortified position that controlled the route upriver. Burmese forces remained dangerous: they inflicted losses on British detachments, interrupted British communications, and sustained resistance for months.

Campbell’s forces moved against Danubyu in early 1825. Bandula was killed at Danubyu in early April 1825 (commonly dated 2 April), reportedly struck by a shell, and his army dissolved. The British took Danubyu the next day and advanced to Prome by late April. The loss of Bandula mattered not only because of his skill but because it was a symbol: the court had invested its prestige in his army.

Meanwhile, secondary operations went forward. British and allied forces cleared Burmese troops from Assam, Cachar, and Manipur by 1825, with Gambhir Singh’s levy playing a role in recovering Manipur. A British column occupied Mergui, Tavoy, and Ye on the Tenasserim coast, and a force advanced into Arakan, taking the town of Arakan in the spring of 1825. Each of these operations altered the territorial stakes for the final settlement. Yet Burmese resistance continued beyond Bandula’s death. It would be a mistake to treat the campaign as a march from victory to victory. The British were repeatedly delayed by supply problems, disease, and the necessity of detaching troops, and the Burmese court kept fresh forces in the field into 1826.

Burmese defensive methods were rational adaptations to local conditions. Timber and earth stockades could obstruct approaches, channel attackers into narrow lanes, and exploit knowledge of flooded ground; they were less effective when exposed to concentrated naval artillery or flanking movements. Bandula's challenge was to coordinate dispersed forces while sustaining food supplies and morale. The movement of troops from the western frontier was itself an impressive undertaking, but its success depended upon river transport and local provisioning. A large army that gathered to besiege Rangoon also became a demanding concentration of mouths to feed. British bombardment and repeated attacks tested the defenses without eliminating the terrain advantages of Burmese forces.

The death of Bandula should be treated as a major loss of leadership, not the magical end of Burmese fighting capacity. Commanders and garrisons continued to defend positions farther upstream, while troops along other frontiers operated within their own political circumstances. At Danubyu, a field commander's loss coincided with the superior firepower of the advancing army and the growing difficulty of assembling replacements. Burmese court officials faced a dilemma: retreat invited further British advances, but repeated concentrations risked more destructive defeats. Local communities meanwhile had to supply soldiers and refugees from both sides. This combination of military pressure and fiscal exhaustion helps explain why a negotiated settlement became increasingly likely even though the royal capital had not yet fallen.

Section 106.7 · Division II — The First Anglo-Burmese War and the Treaty of Yandabo, 1824–1826

Disease, Logistics, and the Human Cost of the First War

The first war demonstrated that military superiority does not erase environmental and logistical constraints. The British expedition was far from established bases, depended on sea transport, and arrived in the monsoon. Dysentery, malaria, cholera, scurvy, and fevers swept the camps. In some units a large majority of men were sick at once, and hospitals and medical supplies were inadequate. The Arakan column suffered especially heavily after occupying low, humid ground, losing many troops to disease in 1825. A widely repeated historical estimate places total deaths among European and Indian soldiers at roughly 15,000, from a force whose cumulative strength is sometimes given near 40,000; the underlying rolls and categories differ, and more restrictive military returns yield lower totals. In every accounting, disease exacted a disproportionate toll compared with combat. These are estimates assembled by later historians from contemporary returns, and they should be treated as indicative rather than exact.

Logistics proved as dangerous as disease. The Company’s armies depended on baggage animals and carts that did not function in the delta, on river boats that were scarce, and on food that had to be imported or purchased from a countryside whose population had fled or been ordered away. Burmese denial of supplies, the destruction of boats, and the unwillingness or inability of the population to sell provisions to the invader were forms of resistance. The strain also had political consequences within the Company army. In November 1824, at Barrackpore near Calcutta, the 47th Bengal Native Infantry refused to march to Burma, citing shortages of carriage and costs they would have to bear and, in some accounts, concerns about caste and sea travel; the mutiny was suppressed by force, with executions. The episode shows that Indian soldiers were not passive instruments and that the cost of the war was borne in part by their grievances.

Burmese soldiers and civilians also suffered. The court’s levies pulled men from farms, and thousands died in battle, from disease, and from privation. Villages in the delta and the Tenasserim and Arakan regions were disrupted by armies, requisition, and flight. British prisoners, including American Baptist missionary Adoniram Judson and merchants such as Henry Gouger, were held in harsh conditions at Ava and nearby, leaving narratives that became a major source of British perceptions, which had their own biases. Burmese voices are harder to recover, though court records and later Burmese accounts preserve elements of the experience.

The financial cost to the Company was also severe. Estimates of the total cost run to several million pounds, many times the indemnity eventually exacted, and the debt contributed to financial strain in India. The war thus had a deterrent effect on British appetite for further Burmese adventures, until commercial and strategic circumstances changed.

The familiar claim that roughly fifteen thousand British and Indian soldiers died in the first war is found in influential histories, but it is not a self-interpreting statistic. Regimental returns, cumulative recruitment totals, hospital admissions, missing men, and attached personnel have not always been counted on the same basis. A contemporary aggregate might exclude transport workers and camp followers, while a later synthesis may include losses across several theaters. Students should therefore ask which units and dates a total covers and whether death from wounds is classified with disease or combat. The firm conclusion is that sickness and inadequate sanitation imposed a devastating toll; a single unqualified number is less defensible.

The distribution of suffering also varied by season. During rains, flooded ground restricted movement and contributed to illness, while interrupted river traffic and the exhaustion of nearby food stocks complicated resupply. A village that provided rice to Burmese soldiers could be punished by invaders; one that sold goods to the British could be accused of aiding the enemy. These pressures do not produce a neat casualty register. They leave traces in petitions, administrative correspondence, descriptions of abandoned fields, and reports of migration. The Company's often-cited cost of approximately five million pounds is likewise an estimate tied to particular accounting conventions. To assess the financial burden, historians must distinguish immediate campaign expenditure, subsequent occupation costs, and the indemnity promised by Burma but not instantly collected.

Section 106.8 · Division II — The First Anglo-Burmese War and the Treaty of Yandabo, 1824–1826

The Treaty of Yandabo, February 24, 1826, and the First Territorial Settlement

After Prome fell, an armistice and negotiations followed in the autumn of 1825. They failed, in part because the Burmese court refused to cede territory and balked at the indemnity demanded. Fighting resumed. Burmese forces under newly appointed commanders attacked British positions near Prome in late 1825; they were beaten, and Campbell advanced north. In January 1826 the British defeated Burmese forces at Malun and moved on Pagan, reaching a point only a short distance from Ava. At this point, with its field armies destroyed and the Company’s army close to the capital, the court agreed to terms.

The treaty was signed at Yandabo on 24 February 1826 by Campbell and a Burmese delegation acting for King Bagyidaw. Its provisions combined several distinct kinds of settlement, and treating them as a single “cession” obscures its structure. The king ceded to the Company the coastal provinces of Arakan and its dependencies and Tenasserim (Tanintharyi), which were transferred as territory. These were direct territorial cessions. By contrast, Assam, Cachar, and Jaintia were handled through Burma’s renunciation of claims and its agreement not to interfere, and Manipur’s status was addressed by Burma’s acknowledgment of Gambhir Singh as its raja. The Company did not annex these regions immediately; their later histories diverged. Cachar was incorporated into British India in 1832 after the death of its raja, Assam was gradually brought under British administration, and Manipur remained a princely state.

The treaty also required Burma to pay an indemnity of one crore of rupees, then roughly one million pounds, in installments, and Company forces were to remain at Rangoon until payment advanced. It provided for the exchange of diplomatic representatives, including a British Resident at Ava, the release of prisoners, and the negotiation of a commercial agreement. For the Burmese court, the indemnity was a burden because it drained the treasury and, with the loss of coastal revenues, compounded financial strain. The commercial treaty, pursued by John Crawfurd’s mission in 1826, yielded modest results and disappointed the Company.

The settlement weakened Burmese sovereignty without destroying the kingdom. The monarchy retained its capital, the Irrawaddy heartland, and Rangoon (returned after payment of the indemnity), but lost its Bay of Bengal outlets in Arakan and Tenasserim and its northwestern claims. The loss of prestige was considerable: Ava had not previously ceded provinces to a foreign power. And yet the court did not regard the treaty as final. Later kings sought to revise or evade its provisions, setting the stage for renewed conflict.

The treaty's precise grammar matters. Article II renounced Burmese claims upon Assam, Cachar, and Jaintia and provided for recognition of Gambhir Singh should he return to Manipur; it did not simply 'cede' those territories to Britain. Articles III and IV dealt with Arakan and the coastal districts of Ye, Tavoy, Mergui, and Tenasserim, naming territorial areas and boundaries. Article V set the indemnity at one crore of rupees. Article VII provided for accredited diplomatic ministers and a future commercial agreement, while Article XI addressed ratification and the return of prisoners. Treating all these articles as though they were identical measures of annexation conceals the very different positions of the peoples concerned.

The act of signing also did not settle practical questions of authority. British officers had to decide how to organize the former Burmese provinces, whether to rely on existing officials, and how to collect customs and land revenues. Arakanese communities sought relief from forms of rule associated with the conquest of 1784, but British taxation and policing brought new grievances. In Tenasserim, merchants and inhabitants confronted an administration whose borders were clearer on paper than on the ground. At Ava, the court had to balance the prestige of retaining independence against the budgetary demands of the indemnity. The exchange of diplomatic representatives offered communication but also exposed the inequalities created by defeat. Yandabo thus ended one war while institutionalizing the circumstances from which later disputes would grow.

Historical Vocabulary
Indemnity and Territorial Cession

Definition. An indemnity is a payment demanded from a defeated state to compensate the victor for the costs of war. A cession is a formal transfer of territory from one government to another by treaty.

Historical context. At Yandabo both appeared: Burma ceded Arakan and Tenasserim, and was to pay a large indemnity. In the northeast, Burma agreed to renounce claims or abstain from interference rather than to cede in the same sense.

Why it matters. The two instruments worked differently. Cession permanently altered the map; indemnity reduced a kingdom’s financial capacity and created a pretext for continued British presence until payment was complete.

Potential misunderstanding. Students sometimes speak of the treaty as giving the British “Assam and Manipur.” That is inaccurate. Burma gave up its influence there, but the political status of each region changed by separate processes.

Section 106.9 · Division III — The Second Anglo-Burmese War and the Loss of Lower Burma, 1826–1853

Burma after Yandabo: Political Crisis, Trade, and Anglo-Burmese Relations

The generation after Yandabo was one of strain and recalibration. King Bagyidaw, shaken by the defeat, withdrew from active rule, and by the 1830s power was contested at court. In 1837 his brother Tharrawaddy seized the throne, placing Bagyidaw under confinement; Bagyidaw died in 1846. The loss of Arakan and Tenasserim reduced royal revenues from customs and from the rich rice and fishing economies of the coasts. The indemnity had been a heavy charge, and the court, burdened with debt, raised taxes and pressed on the countryside.

Relations with British India fluctuated. The Resident installed under the treaty, Henry Burney (from 1830), cultivated the court, but his position was ambiguous: the king resented the Resident’s status, and Burmese officials resisted British claims to precedence. Tharrawaddy, who came to power in a time of resentment, expressed hostility to the Company, questioned the permanency of Yandabo, and sought to evade some of its terms. The Resident was withdrawn in 1840 after Burmese officials declined to receive him on British terms, and thereafter relations were conducted through correspondence and occasional missions. Tharrawaddy’s reign saw unrest in the delta, where Mon and Burmese communities were affected by taxation and local disorder.

Meanwhile British-controlled coastal territories were growing in commercial weight. Moulmein, founded as the capital of the Tenasserim provinces after 1826, became a major teak-exporting port, and Akyab in Arakan developed as a center for rice exports. These ports drew migrants, including Burmese, Mon, Karen, Indian, and Chinese traders, and they showed how British administration could attract labor and capital. Rangoon, still under Burmese rule, remained the kingdom’s major port, but its governors and merchants were increasingly entangled with British commerce.

The reign of Pagan Min, who succeeded Tharrawaddy in 1846 after a bitter succession, was marked by intrigue and by a series of commercial disputes. Royal officials in Rangoon were accused by British merchants of extortion, arbitrary fines, and denial of redress. Burmese authorities, for their part, saw foreign traders as claiming privileges inconsistent with the kingdom’s laws. The structure of the Yandabo settlement—a Burmese kingdom with its main port but without the coasts that had once supplied revenue and security—ensured that disputes at Rangoon had large political stakes. By 1851 the relationship had become volatile enough that a dispute involving a few merchants could trigger a major crisis.

The kingdom's reduced coastline had immediate consequences for its administrative imagination. Court officials could no longer assume that military orders or commercial permissions issued from Ava would be recognized at the lower river ports. Traders could obtain imported manufactures and sell forest products through British territory, creating incentives to bypass royal controls. The British Resident at the court was meant to ease communication, but questions of ceremonial precedence and the treatment of envoys turned diplomatic exchanges into arguments about whether the king remained sovereign. These difficulties were not mere matters of etiquette: public reception, seating, escort, and access to the king signaled political standing.

After Bagyidaw's displacement in 1837, the court's uncertainty about what relations with British India should look like continued. Tharrawaddy sought to recover royal prestige and resisted concessions that seemed to transform an independent monarchy into a subordinate one. Yet practical commerce continued across the boundary, and commercial disagreements did not automatically entail war. Officials in the Company's coastal territories likewise disagreed about whether trade should be encouraged through accommodation or protected through stronger political pressure. When Pagan Min came to power, this unresolved mixture of diplomatic uncertainty, imperial suspicion, and commercial opportunity created the setting in which individual grievances at Rangoon could acquire disproportionate significance. The causal chain from Yandabo to 1852 was not automatic; it depended on choices made on both sides.

The Rangoon Commercial Disputes and Commodore Lambert’s Intervention

In 1851 complaints reached Calcutta from British merchants at Rangoon, notably Captain Sheppard and a man named Lewis, who alleged that the Burmese governor had fined them on false charges, had mistreated them, and had refused redress. The sums involved were small, on the order of a few hundred to a thousand pounds, and Dalhousie, who had been Governor-General since 1848 and was a vigorous proponent of expansion, saw an opportunity. He sent Commodore George Lambert in HMS *Fox* to present demands and seek compensation.

Lambert’s mission began in late 1851 and was complicated by his own conduct. He arrived with a naval force, delivered a letter demanding redress and the removal of the governor, and engaged in a confrontation in which the Burmese governor’s evasions, a new governor’s unwillingness to receive British officers on terms Lambert regarded as respectful, and British demands for apologies escalated tension. In January 1852 Lambert, claiming insult to British officers who had been denied an interview, seized a royal vessel in the harbor and declared a blockade; Burmese batteries fired on HMS *Fox*, and the British shelled the stockades. Dalhousie, who had in effect authorized pressure, privately criticized Lambert’s precipitate action while using it to justify further demands.

In the next stage Dalhousie’s demands widened. He sent an ultimatum requiring compensation for the merchants plus an apology and, in February 1852, a larger indemnity, said to be about ten lakhs of rupees (£100,000) to cover the costs of the naval expedition, payable by a fixed date. The Burmese court tried to offer concessions, including removal of the governor and some compensation, but did not accept demands it regarded as an insult to its honor, and the British deadline lapsed. A British expedition was assembled.

The sequence reveals how an original grievance was transformed. The merchants’ complaints were real but limited; the ultimatum replaced redress with punitive demands, and by early 1852 the aim was less compensation than military pressure. Historians debate how far Dalhousie planned annexation from the start, a question taken up in the Historical Debate that follows Section 106.12. What is clear is that the commercial grievances were a pretext and a lever for a broader strategic and territorial ambition: control of the Irrawaddy delta and its commerce, which had been severed from Burmese power in 1826 only in part.

The origins of the 1852 conflict must be read against the mechanisms by which merchants sought assistance. Shipping masters and agency houses could petition resident officials or the Governor-General, turning a dispute over customs treatment into an issue of British honor. Such petitions served immediate private interests and should not be accepted as impartial descriptions of Burmese administration. Nor should allegations of mistreatment be dismissed simply because British expansion followed. An accurate account asks what the original grievances were, how Burmese authorities responded, and which new demands appeared after the naval expedition arrived. These were different stages of escalation.

Lambert's actions are central because he had both armed ships and a diplomatic assignment. The Burmese government removed a local official whose behavior had been criticized, an action that might have opened negotiations. Instead, further British demands, the seizure of a royal vessel, and a blockade changed the bargaining environment. A royal vessel carried political symbolism that a commercial cargo did not; its capture communicated that British force could humiliate the court. The ensuing exchange of fire strengthened those in Calcutta who believed that annexation was justified. Dalhousie could then represent wider military action as the continuation of an effort to secure redress. The chronology shows how an ostensibly limited attempt to resolve a dispute could create the very confrontation cited as the reason for a territorial war.

The Campaign of 1852 and the British Annexation of Pegu

British forces were commanded by General Henry Godwin, with a naval squadron cooperating, and included British and Indian regiments drawn from Bengal and Madras. On 5 April 1852 they captured Martaban on the Salween estuary; by mid-April they stormed and occupied Rangoon, including the Shwedagon pagoda area, in operations that, compared with 1824, met stronger prepared resistance but were assisted by steam vessels, better medical preparation, and the lessons of the earlier war. Bassein fell in May, and Pegu, the old Mon capital, was taken in the summer and again later when Burmese forces retook it. Prome was occupied in October 1852 after fighting.

The decisive decision was political. Dalhousie, who did not wish to repeat the long, costly march of 1825–1826 to Ava, concluded that a limited war would suffice if it delivered territory. He determined to annex Pegu, the province that joined Arakan and Tenasserim and gave Britain the entire coast from Arakan to Tenasserim. Dalhousie notified the Burmese court of British annexation in December 1852, while published accounts distinguish December readings of the annexation claim from a formal public proclamation dated 20 January 1853. The decision proclaimed a new British province without the king’s consent and threatened further intervention if resistance continued. Formal administrative steps, including the appointment of Arthur Phayre as Commissioner and the organization of the province, followed in early 1853.

Pegu’s importance lay in the delta. The Irrawaddy’s rice-growing plains, the ports at Rangoon and Bassein, and the teak-producing hills offered commercial returns far beyond the cost of the war. Control of the coast meant control of Burma’s access to the sea, which gave Britain extraordinary leverage over the interior. For the Burmese kingdom, the loss of Pegu meant the loss of customs revenue, access to maritime trade, and the fertile region that was becoming the kingdom’s main rice surplus zone.

The Second Anglo-Burmese War ended without a bilateral peace treaty. King Pagan Min refused to sign any document ceding Pegu, and Dalhousie declined to continue operations to compel one. The British simply declared annexation and held the territory, while fighting continued in some districts as local resisters opposed the occupation. Burmese forces led by figures such as Myat Htun resisted in Pegu’s forests and villages into 1853–1854, and British pacification of the province required additional troops and some harsh methods. The war was therefore a military victory achieved quickly, an annexation declared unilaterally, and an administration established only gradually.

The campaign can be reconstructed more precisely than a broad statement that the coastal ports fell in 1852. The attack on Martaban occurred on 5 April; British forces landed at Rangoon on 12 April and took its principal defenses after two days of fighting. Bassein fell on 19 May, Pegu on 3 June, and Prome on 9 October, according to the National Army Museum's chronology. These dates demonstrate how amphibious movement and river access compressed the campaigning season. They do not imply that each surrounding district immediately accepted British supervision. British forces still had to guard communications and meet resistance after each capture.

The political end of the campaign requires two distinct documentary milestones rather than one unqualified date. Accounts identify a declaration or public reading of annexation at Rangoon in December 1852, while the National Army Museum places the formal public proclamation on 20 January 1853. Different records use 'decision,' 'reading,' and 'proclamation' for events in this sequence. The finished chapter should state the December decision and notification, followed by the January public formalization, and explain the evidentiary difference. Because Pagan Min did not sign a treaty of cession, British authority ultimately rested on possession and unilateral declaration. For the inhabitants of Pegu, this meant a rapidly changing relationship to armed force, courts, tax officials, and local leaders rather than a clear constitutional transition recognized by everyone at once.

Historical Vocabulary
Annexation and Gunboat Diplomacy

Definition. Annexation is the unilateral incorporation of territory into a state without the consent of the government that previously held it. Gunboat diplomacy is the use of naval power, or the threat of it, to obtain concessions from a weaker government.

Historical context. Lambert’s ship arrived at Rangoon to press merchants’ claims; his seizure of a royal vessel and the blockade used naval force to enforce demands. In December 1852 Dalhousie annexed Pegu by proclamation, without a negotiated cession.

Why it matters. The distinction between a treaty cession (as at Yandabo) and annexation (as in 1852) is crucial: the latter had no Burmese signature, and the court never formally conceded the loss.

Potential misunderstanding. Gunboat diplomacy is sometimes imagined as a quick, effortless exercise of power. In Burma, even in 1852, it required army operations, sustained occupation, and counterinsurgency.

King Mindon, the Survival of Upper Burma, and Royal Reform after 1853

In February 1853 Pagan Min was deposed by his half-brother Mindon, with the support of Mindon’s full brother Kanaung. Mindon’s accession ended the war in practice. The new king refused to sign a treaty acknowledging the loss of Pegu but did not renew hostilities, and the British, content to retain the province, did not press. This tacit settlement allowed an uneasy peace, regularized later through commercial agreements in 1862 and 1867, which gave British traders access to Upper Burma and reduced royal monopolies.

Mindon inherited a kingdom that was landlocked, poorer, and strategically exposed. It had lost the delta rice lands, ports, and customs, and it faced a neighbor controlling the sea. Yet it retained the monarchy, the dry zone, the upper Irrawaddy and Chindwin valleys, the Shan states’ tributary ties, and a rich Buddhist heritage. The challenge was to adapt without conceding sovereignty.

Mindon’s reforms were substantial. He sought to replace some forms of hereditary revenue assignment with salaried officials, introduced a household tax (*thathameda*) in the early 1860s to rationalize revenue, and minted a standard coinage. He established state-owned factories and arsenals, imported machinery and technical advisers from Europe, built steamers, and extended the telegraph. He sent students and missions abroad, and Kinwun Mingyi led an embassy to Europe in 1872 that visited Britain, France, and Italy. Mindon also cultivated Buddhist institutions: he convened the Fifth Buddhist Council in 1871 and sponsored the inscription of the Tipitaka on marble slabs at the Kuthodaw pagoda in Mandalay.

Mandalay was founded in 1857 and the court moved there in 1859, in part for religious reasons, in part because it was farther from the British frontier and a stronger center for the dry zone. The new city, with its palace, moat, and monasteries, was meant to renew the kingdom’s ritual power after the defeat of 1852.

Mindon’s reign thus challenges the interpretation that Upper Burma stagnated between 1853 and 1878. Modern scholarship emphasizes his administrative and economic adaptation. The limits were also real. Reform strained the court and provoked opposition: in 1866 two of Mindon’s sons rebelled and killed the heir apparent Kanaung, a major reformer, an event that deepened the kingdom’s succession problem. Revenue was constrained by the loss of Lower Burma. Britain, meanwhile, watched with suspicion. Mindon’s efforts to build alliances with France and Italy and to import arms were seen in Calcutta as possible signs of independence that Britain would not tolerate. His success in preserving the kingdom for twenty-five years was real, but it depended on conditions that his death in 1878 would remove.

Reform under Mindon combined political adaptation with the demands of Buddhist kingship. The monarchy encouraged more systematic revenue assessment, experimented with taxation in cash, and supported workshops and the importation of technical knowledge. The court's diplomatic envoys sought to understand the outside world as well as to secure recognition of its independence. The royal project was not a simple imitation of Europe: reforms had to fit local institutions, elite interests, religious patronage, and the need to preserve court legitimacy. Kanaung, Mindon's brother, became a particularly important sponsor of technical and military experiments before his death in the failed palace revolt of 1866.

The transfer of the royal center to Mandalay symbolized this combination of renewal and continuity. A new capital could express religious merit and dynastic permanence while offering a setting for reorganized departments and workshops. Yet an independent Upper Burma remained economically vulnerable. Much overseas trade passed through British-controlled Lower Burma, so taxation, transport charges, and relations with Rangoon firms affected the court's revenues. The persistent border also divided families, merchants, and monastic networks. Mindon could improve administration without removing these structural constraints. Historians should therefore evaluate his reign by the reforms attempted, the support and opposition they encountered, and their effects on state capacity—not by asking only whether they succeeded in preventing British annexation after his death.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Second Anglo-Burmese War Occur?

The question: Was the second war caused chiefly by a genuine commercial dispute that escalated, or by a British decision, led by Dalhousie, to annex Lower Burma?

Interpretation A — Escalation from a dispute: Some historians stress the genuine grievances of British merchants, the intransigence of the Rangoon authorities, and Lambert’s impulsiveness, arguing that the crisis spiraled beyond intention and that annexation was decided only when military success made it possible.
Interpretation B — Expansion by design: Others point to Dalhousie’s record as a proponent of annexation elsewhere in India, the growing size of British demands, and the strategic and commercial value of the delta, arguing that the dispute was a pretext for a war already preferred.

Evidence: Dalhousie’s correspondence, Lambert’s reports, merchants’ petitions, Burmese diplomatic responses, and the timing and content of the 1852 ultimatum.

Points of disagreement: Chiefly causation and intention: how much was planned, and when the aim of annexation crystallized.

Why it matters: If the war was largely accidental, it is a story of misjudgment; if designed, it exemplifies a pattern of imperial expansion through manufactured pretexts. Most scholars see elements of both.

The Last Konbaung Court: Mindon’s Death and Thibaw’s Accession

Mindon died in October 1878 without having named a clear successor, and the question of who should rule was fought out inside the palace. Thibaw, a young prince who had studied in the monastic system and was married to a daughter of the king, was elevated with the support of powerful figures, including the queen mother Hsinbyumashin and ministers such as Kinwun Mingyi’s rivals and allies. The court was divided between those who favored accommodation with British India and those who sought to assert independence. In February 1879 a number of royal princes and princesses were killed in what was officially presented as a measure to secure the throne; the number is often given as roughly eighty, though sources vary. The massacre shocked British opinion and was widely cited by colonial writers as proof of oriental despotism.

Historians now treat that framing critically. Royal succession by elimination of rivals was a long-standing Konbaung practice, and Mindon’s own accession had involved violence; the 1879 killings were severe but not unique to Burma, and British reports amplified them for political purposes. What the violence did show was the fragility of the court’s legitimacy after Mindon’s long reign, and it weakened the group of reformers who might have negotiated with Britain.

Thibaw’s government, led in practice by the Hlutdaw, the queen mother, and Queen Supayalat, retained the administrative structure Mindon had built: salaried officials, the household tax, factories, and a Buddhist establishment. It was also economically exposed: Lower Burma’s rice trade, which was growing under British rule, left Upper Burma dependent on a shrinking set of resources and on trade through Rangoon. The kingdom’s diplomatic position was fragile. Britain asked that the Resident be received with honors the court regarded as contrary to royal dignity, such as shoe-removal before the king, and the court resisted. In 1879 the Resident, Shaw, was withdrawn from Mandalay, and the formal channel of diplomacy collapsed.

For the following years, communication relied on unofficial contacts, and each side interpreted the other’s actions with suspicion. Britain saw a young and unstable court; the court saw a power that had taken Lower Burma and might take more. The withdrawal of the Residency did not itself make war inevitable, but it removed a stabilizing institution just as new pressures were gathering.

Thibaw inherited a court with more than one political agenda. Kinwun Mingyi and allied ministers had proposed the reorganization of departments and limitations on arbitrary royal expenditure; supporters of a stronger palace household preferred direct control over revenue and military appointments. The accession crisis sharpened these disagreements. In February 1879 royal princes, princesses, and other court prisoners were killed. Contemporary and later accounts differ about totals and responsibility. Thant Myint-U's reconstruction identifies thirty-one of Mindon's sons and nine daughters among the victims, while broader estimates include other members of royal households. Thibaw's personal complicity cannot be established simply by repeating hostile colonial press accounts.

The subsequent withdrawal of the British Resident in 1879 further reduced the possibility of routine diplomatic negotiation. It is tempting to see every court maneuver as evidence of chaos, but the record includes ambitious policy proposals, disputes over ministerial authority, and attempts to coordinate relations with foreign powers. Kinwun Mingyi's advisers drew upon both Burmese political thought and knowledge of European institutions. At the same time the royal couple and military allies sought to preserve prerogatives they regarded as essential to monarchy. These internal struggles weakened the capacity to respond consistently to British demands, yet they did not make British annexation legitimate or unavoidable. An explanation of 1885 should begin with this divided but politically active court rather than a caricature of a passive or irrational king.

Teak, Trade, French Diplomacy, and British Imperial Anxiety

Upper Burma’s teak forests, in the Chindwin and Ningyan regions, were among the most valuable in Asia, and in the 1870s and 1880s they were leased to firms that cut and floated timber to Rangoon. The largest of these was the Bombay Burmah Trading Corporation, a Scottish-Indian firm that held extensive concessions. Concession terms involved royalties and rules on the size of trees that could be cut, and disputes with royal foresters and officials were frequent. Royal officials suspected that the Corporation was extracting more than permitted and underpaying royalties; the Corporation accused officials of extortion and of demanding payments beyond contract.

Trade was growing along the Irrawaddy. British merchants at Rangoon and in Britain wanted greater access to Upper Burma and, beyond it, to southwestern China. Chambers of commerce lobbied for intervention, hoping for a route through Bhamo to Yunnan that would open an immense market. These hopes were more speculative than the trade statistics warranted, but they shaped political pressure.

French expansion added strategic anxiety. In 1883–1885 France established protectorates over Annam and Tonkin (Chapter 110), and its agents were active in Cambodia and Laos. Thibaw’s court, aware of Britain’s intentions, sought outside support. Burmese missions visited Paris and Italy, and in January 1885 the court signed a commercial agreement with France. Rumors circulated of a French loan, a bank, and a railway from Tonkin to Mandalay, along with arms deliveries. British officials worried that France might gain a foothold on the upper Irrawaddy, flank Burma’s east, and threaten India’s northeastern frontier. In 1885 Lord Randolph Churchill, as Secretary of State for India, was sympathetic to those who urged action.

Historians distinguish genuine strategic concerns from pretexts. French activity was real, but its actual substance was limited: the agreement of 1885 was a commercial treaty, and French power in Indochina was stretched. Britain was at the same time pursuing wider imperial goals, and many officials had long desired to complete the incorporation of Burma. The French question served both as cause and as justification, and the evidence points to an interplay of long-standing ambitions and immediate fears rather than any single trigger.

The teak business required detailed local arrangements: contracts specified forests, extraction rights, royalties, and obligations to employees or local authorities. The government alleged that the Bombay Burmah Trading Corporation had exceeded permitted cutting and manipulated its accounts. British representatives disputed the penalties and argued that the firm's case should not be settled without an acceptable review. The widely reported assessment of roughly twenty-three lakh rupees must be described as a contested financial penalty, not as proof that the company was either guilty or innocent. The significance of the case lay partly in the question of who possessed legal authority to judge a firm operating under Burmese concessions.

French connections intensified this dispute because they offered Mandalay a possible counterweight to British domination. French missionaries, merchants, and diplomats had contacts with Upper Burma, and colonial expansion in mainland Southeast Asia made such contacts sensitive in British eyes. Yet a proposal for commercial cooperation is not the same as a secret alliance guaranteeing French military intervention. The court's effort to diversify its relationships can be understood as a strategy of sovereignty, even if British officials interpreted it as an imminent danger. Merchants in Rangoon simultaneously lobbied for predictable access to inland timber and transport routes. The convergence of private commercial demands, imperial anxiety, and public diplomatic pressure helped convert a litigation dispute into an ultimatum about foreign relations.

The competing versions of the logging case are therefore themselves evidence. Company correspondence is likely to emphasize the security of contracts and the risk of arbitrary penalties; Mandalay's documents emphasize the right to supervise royal forests and enforce concession terms. A strong textbook must ask whether either side supplied an independent account of the trees cut, wages paid, or duties assessed. Without that comparison the dispute becomes merely a prelude to invasion rather than a controversy about sovereignty and economic resources.

The British Ultimatum of October 1885 and the Decision for War

The immediate crisis arose from the Bombay Burmah Trading Corporation’s dispute with the court. In 1885 the Hlutdaw, accusing the company of extracting more teak than reported and of bribing foresters, imposed a substantial fine, commonly reported at about 23 lakh (2.3 million) rupees; sources differ on details. The firm refused to pay and appealed to British officials, and British authorities responded by seeking intervention. The Government of India, led by Viceroy Lord Dufferin, took the opportunity to press a broader agenda.

On 22 October 1885 Dufferin’s ultimatum was transmitted to Mandalay. It demanded that the court suspend proceedings against the Corporation until a British envoy arrived to arbitrate, that the envoy be received at the capital with an escort, that a permanent British Resident be established, that the Government of India be allowed to supervise Burma’s relations with foreign states, and that facilities be given for the opening of trade with China. It required a reply within a short deadline. The final demand effectively placed Burma’s foreign affairs under British control, which would have reduced the kingdom to a protectorate.

The Burmese court’s reply, received at Rangoon on 9 November (after deliberations in Mandalay), was judged inadequate by British officials. Reports disagree about how far the court was prepared to negotiate the commercial dispute, but the demand for British direction of foreign relations threatened independent authority; the official British interpretation of the reply should not be mistaken for a neutral account of the Burmese position. British officials declared the response unsatisfactory, and Dufferin authorized the expedition. The decision for war was thus made in Calcutta and London, not forced by Burmese refusal alone. Accepting British supervision of external relations would have extinguished a core attribute of sovereignty, and a court that had struggled to maintain independence for thirty years could hardly have conceded it.

The teak dispute alone did not cause the war. It supplied an occasion, but the ultimatum’s scope far exceeded the dispute. Commercial lobbying, French fears, and Britain’s long-standing wish to finish what had begun in 1824 and 1852 shaped the decision. Equally, Burmese choices mattered: the court’s attempt to leverage the Corporation’s dispute, to cultivate France, and to resist British demands was a gamble that failed.

The chronology of the ultimatum should be stated without conflating composition, delivery, and receipt. The British government transmitted its demands on 22 October 1885 and required a satisfactory response by 10 November. Burmese deliberations took place during the following weeks; contemporary records place receipt of the formal reply at Rangoon on 9 November. The reply's degree of compromise is itself interpreted differently in British narratives and later scholarship. It should not be said to have been delivered in late October. The court sought to preserve the authority to judge commercial disputes and manage its foreign relations even while indicating possible channels for discussion.

The most consequential clauses were not about teak accounts. A British Resident with privileged access to the king and British supervision of foreign policy would have redefined Upper Burma's international status. Concessions for trade toward China would have affected routes through territories not uniformly controlled from Mandalay. In this context the ultimatum functioned less as a neutral invitation to arbitration than as a proposed transformation of the kingdom's sovereignty. The fact that British troops had already been mobilized is relevant to evaluating the sincerity of diplomacy, although plans for war alone do not prove that every official rejected compromise. Students should examine the sequence of documents and ask whether the demands left a politically viable path to acceptance for an independent monarch.

The Hlutdaw's institutional role should also be visible. Decisions were issued through ministers and court councils, not simply through an isolated king reacting impulsively to a British threat. Diplomats worked with differing assumptions about protocol and the political meaning of a Resident. The British deadline compressed the time available for consultation and transmission, while the threat of armed intervention altered every option. This matters in evaluating claims that the Burmese government 'refused' a reasonable offer: refusal of foreign supervision and refusal of commercial arbitration were not the same thing.

Historical Vocabulary
Ultimatum and Protectorate

Definition. An ultimatum is a final demand backed by a threat of force if not accepted by a deadline. A protectorate is a relationship in which a stronger state controls the foreign affairs, and often other aspects, of a weaker state that retains its own ruler and nominal internal government.

Historical context. The 1885 ultimatum asked Burma to place its external relations under British direction while keeping the monarchy, which is the form a protectorate often took. Protectorates were used elsewhere, for example in Malay states and in Annam and Tonkin.

Why it matters. It helps students see that the war was fought over the terms of sovereignty. Burma could have accepted a protectorate; its refusal, and Britain’s unwillingness to accept less, led to annexation.

Potential misunderstanding. A protectorate was not necessarily a milder form of rule; it often became a step toward direct control.

Section 106.16 · Division IV — The Third Anglo-Burmese War and the Fall of Mandalay, 1878–1885

The Irrawaddy Campaign and the Fall of Mandalay, November 1885

The Burma Field Force, commanded by Major-General Harry Prendergast, assembled at Thayetmyo on the frontier of British Burma in November 1885. It numbered around nine to ten thousand men, British and Indian, with artillery, and was transported up the Irrawaddy in a large fleet of steamers and flats, many provided by the Irrawaddy Flotilla Company, the private firm that had built a river transport network for the Burmese trade. The campaign was planned as a rapid river advance.

Operations began on 14 November. The principal obstacle was the Burmese fort at Minhla, which defended the river approach. After a brief bombardment and assault on 17 November, the fort fell with few British casualties; Burmese defenders, hampered by poor command and obsolete preparations, were overrun. The expedition then advanced steadily. Gaps in Burmese defenses, the collapse of morale, and the king’s failure to mount a coordinated defense helped. A last Burmese attempt to negotiate failed.

On 28 November British troops reached Mandalay and occupied the city. On 29 November Thibaw surrendered, and a political officer, Colonel Edward Sladen, helped arrange the transfer of the king and queens to a steamer that took them downriver. The palace was occupied and later converted into Fort Dufferin; royal treasures were taken, and looting, official and unofficial, followed.

The campaign was much shorter than the first war for several reasons. Steam navigation allowed rapid movement along the kingdom’s main artery, overcoming the transport problems that had crippled earlier British operations. British forces were better equipped and better prepared for tropical conditions. Burmese forces, divided by faction, failed to use the strategic depth that had hindered earlier invaders. Court ministers, including Kinwun Mingyi, opted for submission to avoid destruction of the capital. The speed of the campaign, however, did not represent the consent of Burmese society. The collapse of royal command removed a center of authority, but it did not remove the will of many Burmese officials, soldiers, monks, and villagers to resist, as Division V shows.

The river campaign had a precise sequence that reveals the importance of logistics. Around fifty shallow-draft steamers and associated barges carried soldiers, weapons, ammunition, and supplies along the Irrawaddy. On 17 November British forces overcame river defenses around Minhla; troops landed to attack fortified positions while naval fire helped suppress guns covering the channel. Further positions at Nyaung-U, Pakokku, and Myingyan were neutralized as the flotilla continued upstream. The vessels were not a technological shortcut that acted alone: pilots, crews, Indian soldiers, engineers, and medical staff maintained an operation that required close coordination between landings and river movement.

The advance also succeeded because Burmese royal command was fragmented. Some commanders wished to resist, others awaited instructions, and some local leaders doubted whether the court could supply reinforcements. Thibaw ordered surrender on 26 November after the British threatened the old royal center at Ava. Mandalay was occupied on 28 November, and the king's personal submission and deposition followed in the ensuing days. Those distinctions correct any impression that the city fell in a single dramatic encounter. British propaganda suggesting that the operation would replace Thibaw rather than annex the kingdom may have influenced some decisions not to fight. The speed of the march therefore arose from political uncertainty as well as military mobility. Its success remained geographically narrow: troops held the royal center and river corridor, but much of the countryside was still beyond their effective control.

The Royal Court's experience of the advance was quite different from that of the invading commanders. News arrived through messengers, local officers, merchants, and observers whose reports could be delayed or contradictory. A king considering continued resistance had to assess whether regional troops would obey orders after the river defenses fell. British reports celebrate the efficiency of the march; Burmese sources can illuminate uncertainty within the court and the choices available to local commanders. The conquest's speed should be established by dated operational records rather than presented as evidence that the society welcomed the invading force.

Section 106.17 · Division V — Annexation, Resistance, and the Destruction of Burmese Sovereignty, 1886–1895

Thibaw’s Deposition, Exile, and the Annexation of Upper Burma

Thibaw and Supayalat were taken from Mandalay on 29 November 1885 by steamer, escorted to Rangoon, and shipped to India. After a period at Madras, they were settled at Ratnagiri on the Konkan coast, where Thibaw lived under surveillance until his death in 1916. Supayalat later returned to Rangoon, where she died in 1925. The exile of a reigning king was a deliberate act: the British did not install a puppet or a protectorate arrangement but removed the dynasty. Hsinbyumashin and other royal women were also separated from Mandalay.

The monarchy was not merely a government; it was the ritual center of the kingdom. The king’s patronage sustained monasteries, his court conferred titles, his ceremonies tied the realm to Buddhist cosmology, and his officials linked villages to the capital. Removing him disrupted these relationships. Local officials lost superiors; monasteries lost patrons; royal servicemen lost their obligations and livelihoods. The political legitimacy of any successor, Burmese or British, was thus open to question.

Britain announced the annexation of Upper Burma in a proclamation dated 1 January 1886. The territory was incorporated into the Empire and attached administratively to British India. The decision to annex rather than set up a protectorate reflected the view of Dufferin and officials that a cheap, indirect arrangement would not secure British interests, together with the experience of the previous decade’s uncertainties. Some officials expected the people of Upper Burma to accept the change with little trouble; they were soon proved wrong.

The proclamation did not itself create administration. The British had few officials in the interior, little knowledge of local institutions, and a limited police presence. Many local officials waited to see which authority would prevail, and in the vacuum some made accommodations while others began to raise forces. In practical terms, the annexation was a declaration; the establishment of government was yet to come.

The end of the royal court affected institutions whose significance cannot be measured by territorial maps. The king had sponsored religious ceremonies, supported important monastic establishments, confirmed titles, appointed officials, and mediated disputes among provincial elites. Removing him did not automatically transfer these relationships to British officers. Many Burmese officials initially remained in place or tried to interpret the new commands through old procedures. Others lost their offices, refused cooperation, or joined resistance movements. Court families had to negotiate the loss of income, rank, and ritual status, while the population faced uncertainty about taxes and law.

Thibaw's exile to Ratnagiri also became a powerful symbol, though the circumstances of his removal must be reconstructed from evidence rather than later patriotic retelling alone. The colonial state could remove the monarch's body from Burma, but it could not erase the meanings of Buddhist kingship or the loyalties that local people attached to his dynasty. The proclamation of 1 January 1886 placed Upper Burma under British rule on paper. Its enforcement required more officials, intelligence about villages, policing, revenue collection, and the suppression or accommodation of armed opponents. Decisions about how to classify the Shan principalities and other frontier areas introduced further complications. The destruction of the monarchy was a decisive constitutional event; the extension of effective state power was a slower and more violent process.

The exile also raised questions about what it meant to rule a Buddhist country without a royal center. Monasteries continued to receive donations, but they could no longer depend on the same relationship with a reigning king. In districts where village headmen had administered royal obligations, new officers sought to translate customary practices into written regulations. That translation could preserve existing institutions or alter them substantially. For ordinary households the political rupture was experienced through changes in demands for labor, taxes, legal authority, and military obedience, often before any stable new administrative framework existed.

Section 106.18 · Division V — Annexation, Resistance, and the Destruction of Burmese Sovereignty, 1886–1895

Burmese Resistance and the Prolonged Pacification Campaigns, 1886–1895

Armed resistance began almost immediately and spread across Upper Burma and into Lower Burma. Leaders included former royal officials and soldiers, local headmen, members of the royal family and pretenders, and Buddhist monks. Resistance took forms ranging from large bands attacking garrisons to small groups raiding supply lines and villages. Beyond the Irrawaddy heartland, Shan states, Kachin hills, and Chin hills became arenas of contention. A prince known as the Limbin prince attempted to organize a rival authority in the Shan states, and several sawbwas wavered between submission and resistance.

British officials called the resistance “dacoity,” a term derived from the Hindi *dakait*, used in India for gang robbery. In Burma the label covered political resistance, rebellion, banditry, and ordinary crime, and it allowed authorities to present opposition as criminality rather than politics. The term must be used with caution. Some armed bands were indeed predatory; many were led by men who regarded themselves as defenders of religion and king and who commanded local loyalty. Burmese terms such as *bo* (officer or leader) reflect a different understanding.

The British response was a campaign of military and police operations that officials called pacification. Troops, many of them soldiers recruited in India, including units from the Madras Army and later large forces of military police raised in the Punjab and elsewhere, occupied posts and conducted columns through the countryside. They were supported by intelligence gathering, the building of roads and telegraph lines, and the reorganization of local administration under village headmen, who were held responsible for order. Punitive measures included burning of villages, collective fines, forced relocation, and executions. Later regulations, often dated to 1887, gave legal form to village responsibility.

The campaign was long. Tens of thousands of troops and police were deployed, far more than had taken Mandalay. Operations continued through the late 1880s and into the early 1890s, with major efforts toward the mid-1890s, as recent military-historical research such as Ian F. W. Beckett’s study of the pacification demonstrates. British commanders found the work frustrating and costly in disease and casualties. The suppression of resistance in the Shan, Kachin, and Chin regions involved further expeditions, including operations in the Chin Hills around 1889–1890, and reached into areas that had never been fully controlled by the court.

The pacification shows the gap between military victory, annexation, and government. The capital was taken in 1885; the monarchy was abolished on 1 January 1886; yet control of the countryside took nearly a decade. The costs fell heavily on villages, and the experience left a legacy of distrust that would shape Burmese politics for generations (Chapters 107 and 129).

The term 'dacoit' was administratively convenient because it made diverse armed opponents appear as a single criminal problem. British officials did encounter theft, banditry, and feuds, but the same category was also applied to former soldiers, villagers defending local autonomy, and men claiming allegiance to a displaced royal order. Colonial reports must therefore be read alongside the geography of recruitment, the identities of leaders, and the demands made by insurgent groups. Resistance did not form a unified national army. It ranged from local fortified groups to mobile bands and networks receiving supplies from sympathetic villages. Such diversity helped some groups survive but limited coordination between regions.

British countermeasures combined new military posts, village intelligence, pursuit columns, disarmament, control of movement, and the recruitment of local policemen or informants. Punitive measures and requisitioning could produce grievances that sustained opposition even when a particular leader was captured. At the frontiers, the incorporation of Shan, Chin, Kachin, and other territories introduced negotiations and campaigns distinct from operations in the central valley. Ian Beckett's study of 1885–1895 stresses the lengthy, varied character of the process conventionally called pacification. It is essential to avoid treating the end of large-scale fighting in one district as proof of general acceptance throughout Burma. Nor should every act of later social resistance be absorbed into this chapter: Chapter 107 will trace the longer-term colonial order that emerged from these campaigns.

The Human and Political Consequences of the Three Wars

The cumulative consequences of the wars were uneven. Direct military casualties were high for the Company’s forces in 1824–1826, mostly from disease, and the Burmese suffered losses in battle and from requisition and displacement. The second war was shorter and less costly for the British, but it produced resistance in Pegu and the transfer of a major rice-growing region. The third war was brief in its campaign phase but led to long pacification with heavy civilian cost. Reliable figures are scarce for each stage, and the chapter has avoided inventing precise totals.

Communities experienced these events differently. The Bamar (Burmese) of the Irrawaddy heartland were the core of the Konbaung polity and bore the burden of levies and then of pacification. The Rakhine of Arakan saw their land pass from Burmese to British rule in 1826, ending Konbaung domination but beginning a new regime. Mon communities, long marginalized under Konbaung rule, saw opportunities and disappointments: some cooperated with the British in 1824 and 1852, and the Tenasserim and Pegu provinces became Mon-inhabited parts of British Burma, but cooperation did not guarantee autonomy. Karen communities, divided between hill and delta, included Christian converts who would later find a role under British rule, though not all Karen aligned with the British. Shan sawbwas faced a choice between accommodating British power to preserve their positions and resisting. Kachin and Chin communities encountered British military expeditions on terms that varied from valley to valley. It is a mistake to treat any of these as uniform political blocs or to assume that relations with the monarchy or with Britain were identical.

Economically, the wars reoriented Burma’s trade. British control of the coast made Rangoon, Moulmein, Akyab, and Bassein centers of an export economy linked to India and Britain; the Burmese interior was cut off from the sea until 1885. The loss of customs revenue and the indemnity of 1826 weighed on the monarchy; Mindon’s reforms were a response. After 1886 the whole country was drawn into a single colonial economy, whose character is the subject of Chapter 107.

Politically, the wars destroyed institutions: the monarchy, the royal service system, and the court as arbiter. They also produced a division between areas incorporated into British rule and frontier polities whose authority remained contested; the Shan states, Kachin and Chin hills, and other peripheries were integrated by different methods and on different timetables. The legacy of that differentiation would affect Burma’s politics long after independence (Chapters 154 and 230).

The coastal territories acquired in 1826 and 1852 did not all undergo the same transformations. Arakan's relations with Chittagong, the ports of Tenasserim, and the growing rice economy of the Irrawaddy Delta connected different environments and populations to different markets. A family displaced by military operations might later find employment in a port or on a delta farm, but such movement should not be mistaken for a universally beneficial outcome. Government reports tended to celebrate expanding exports while measuring social costs less consistently. Colonial trade statistics record transactions that officials could tax or count; they say far less about lost customary rights or the disruption of local authority.

The political effects of conquest likewise extended beyond the Bamar heartland without producing a single experience among frontier peoples. Some local rulers negotiated with British officers against rivals; some resisted new garrisons and taxes; others found that earlier agreements with Ava had little meaning to officials appointed from India. Religious communities were affected by the loss of court patronage and by the creation of new channels of donation and schooling. For historians, these varied responses complicate accounts that describe Burma either as uniformly resisting or universally welcoming British government. The wars dismantled a particular monarchy, but the colonial borders and administrative categories that followed were constructed unevenly and at considerable human cost.

Everyday Life and Social History

Wartime Displacement and Ordinary Experience

Official narratives of the wars concentrate on generals, ministers, and treaties, but the wars were also experienced by farmers, boatmen, traders, women, and monks. In 1824 the inhabitants of Rangoon were ordered or chose to leave, abandoning houses, shops, and stores; in the following months, villages in the delta and along the Irrawaddy were disrupted by armies seeking food and labor. Royal levies drew men from fields, leaving women and children to manage harvests. Requisition and the movement of troops strained food supplies, and refugees from fighting crowded into towns or fled to the forests.

In 1852 the war again disrupted the delta, and in Pegu villagers faced both British occupation and the exactions of those resisting it. After 1885, pacification columns entered villages searching for fighters and weapons; headmen were called on to provide guides, supplies, and information; villages suspected of aiding resisters could be fined or burned. Monks, whose monasteries were sources of education and moral authority, were sometimes suspected of sheltering resisters. People adapted by moving, negotiating, hiding stores, and shifting loyalties, strategies that left few written traces.

The sources for these experiences are indirect: missionary letters, officers’ narratives, court orders, village records, and later memories. They show that war did not merely destroy; it also moved populations, created new market connections, and shaped how people thought about authority. Historians must read official sources critically to recover the lives of ordinary people.

Historical Synthesis: Imperial Warfare and the End of the Konbaung Kingdom

The three wars may be compared along several dimensions. The first arose from frontier competition and incompatible claims to authority in Arakan, Manipur, Assam, and Cachar; it was fought largely by seaborne invasion and ended in a negotiated treaty that took coastal provinces and imposed an indemnity. The second grew from a commercial dispute turned into a campaign for territory, was won rapidly with naval and army forces, and ended in annexation of Pegu without a treaty. The third resulted from commercial pressure, French fears, and an ultimatum that threatened sovereignty, was won in two weeks by river steamers, and ended with the destruction of the monarchy.

Several factors explain the different outcomes. Naval power gave Britain the ability to strike at Burma’s ports; Indian military resources provided manpower and revenue; commercial pressure pushed the Government of India toward action; river transport made the 1885 advance possible; and political decisions in Calcutta and London determined whether to seek a treaty, annex, or abolish the monarchy. On the Burmese side, differences in military organization, political cohesion, and leadership mattered: Bagyidaw’s court in 1824 had a mobilized army and a respected commander; Thibaw’s court in 1885 was divided and its army less capable of using the kingdom’s depth. None of these outcomes was inevitable; each depended on decisions that could have been different.

The disappearance of Burmese independence in 1886 did not mean the end of resistance or the establishment of uncontested colonial authority. The decade that followed shows that governing Burma would be a different and harder task than conquering it, which is the subject of Chapter 107.

The first war emerged from an expanding frontier in which Burmese, British, and smaller regional rulers claimed overlapping rights. Its outcome was an unequal treaty, not the removal of the monarchy. The second began with merchant complaints but escalated through naval intervention into a unilateral seizure of Lower Burma; its lack of a bilateral peace settlement was itself a statement about the balance of power. The third transformed a contested commercial case and diplomatic anxiety into an ultimatum requiring the abandonment of independent foreign relations. When British forces occupied Mandalay, the monarchy ceased to rule, but resistance continued. These distinctions are essential if students are to explain outcomes instead of merely memorizing three dates.

The military comparison is equally revealing. Britain depended on Indian revenue and soldiers in all three wars, but the operational advantages changed. In 1824 an amphibious force reached Rangoon swiftly and then struggled with disease, supply, and stubborn defense. In 1852 improved naval coordination permitted rapid coastal acquisitions while Dalhousie deliberately avoided another costly advance on the royal capital. In 1885 the Irrawaddy flotilla carried troops toward Mandalay at exceptional speed while the court confronted divisions and uncertainty. Superior firepower mattered, but it worked within logistics, diplomacy, propaganda, and the strategies of local actors.

Finally, the historical meaning of 'conquest' requires attention to evidence and perspective. British victory proclamations document state claims; treaties record unequal negotiations; military returns illuminate costs but exclude many civilians; Burmese royal and monastic sources preserve political and religious languages absent from colonial reports. A defensible history holds these records in tension. It recognizes Burmese decisions and reforms without romanticizing royal government, acknowledges the varied positions of Mon, Rakhine, Shan, Karen, Chin, and Kachin communities, and examines colonial violence without reducing every encounter to the same experience. The result was the destruction of Konbaung sovereignty and the beginning of a contested colonial system. Chapter 107 follows that system after the wars, when questions of land, labor, migration, education, religion, and resistance acquired new institutional forms.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 106 has argued that the three Anglo-Burmese Wars were related but distinct imperial conflicts through which British expansion progressively restricted, fragmented, and finally extinguished the sovereignty of the Konbaung monarchy. The kingdom that confronted Britain in 1824 was a significant regional power with a mobilized population, a Buddhist court culture, and a record of conquest in Arakan, Manipur, and Assam.

The first war grew out of a contested frontier. Burmese expansion after 1784 produced refugee flows, overlapping tributary claims, and friction with the East India Company, whose own frontier in Cachar, Jaintia, and Chittagong was unstable. The Burmese court and the Company held incompatible conceptions of sovereignty, and both underestimated the costs of war after the Shapuree Island incident and the declaration of 5 March 1824.

The British invasion of Rangoon in May 1824 captured a nearly empty port and a foothold rather than a victory. Maha Bandula’s resistance, stockade defenses, and the denial of supplies imposed severe difficulties, and disease, monsoon weather, and logistics killed far more British and Indian soldiers than battle did. Bandula’s death at Danubyu in April 1825 and the advance toward the capital brought the court to the Treaty of Yandabo on 24 February 1826.

Yandabo should be read provision by provision. Arakan and Tenasserim were ceded; claims in Assam, Cachar, and Jaintia were renounced; Manipur’s raja was recognized; an indemnity of one crore of rupees was imposed; and diplomatic and commercial arrangements were made. The treaty weakened Burmese sovereignty without destroying the monarchy, and left the court seeking to revise its terms.

The second war arose from commercial disputes at Rangoon, which Dalhousie and Commodore Lambert escalated into an ultimatum and then a campaign. The capture of Martaban, Rangoon, Bassein, Pegu, and Prome was followed by the annexation of Pegu by proclamation in December 1852. No treaty was signed, and Burma became landlocked. Historians debate whether the annexation was planned or emerged from the course of the war.

Mindon, who took the throne in 1853, rebuilt the kingdom through administrative reform, a new revenue system, coinage, factories, diplomacy, and the founding of Mandalay. His reign challenges the view of Upper Burma as stagnant, even though the kingdom remained strategically disadvantaged and politically vulnerable to succession crises.

After Mindon’s death in 1878, Thibaw’s court faced a violent succession, the withdrawal of the British Residency in 1879, a dispute involving the Bombay Burmah Trading Corporation, French diplomacy, and the ultimatum of 22 October 1885. The ultimatum’s demand for British direction of Burma’s foreign relations would have placed the kingdom under a protectorate, and the court refused.

The campaign of November 1885, carried out on river steamers under General Prendergast, took Minhla and Mandalay in about two weeks and ended with Thibaw’s submission, exile to Ratnagiri, and the proclamation of annexation on 1 January 1886. The speed of this victory reflected steam transport, divisions in the Burmese court, and the loss of Lower Burma, but it did not reflect the consent of Burmese society.

Resistance to annexation continued for roughly a decade. The British label “dacoity” grouped political rebellion with crime, and pacification involved large forces of troops and military police, many recruited in India, and coercive measures against villages. Mon, Karen, Rakhine, Shan, Kachin, Chin, and Bamar communities experienced the wars differently, and no single narrative of collaboration or resistance fits them all.

The wars therefore marked not merely the expansion of British territory but the prolonged dismantling of Burmese sovereignty and the beginning of a contested colonial order. The distinction among military victory, annexation, and effective government, which runs through the chapter, introduces Chapter 107’s study of what British rule became after conquest.

Chapter Reflection

The Anglo-Burmese Wars matter because they show how sovereignty can be dismantled in stages rather than lost in a single catastrophe. Each war left a state that could still act and still hope to recover, which is one reason Burmese rulers kept seeking revision, reform, and foreign partners. Understanding these stages helps explain why the end of the monarchy in 1885 was both sudden and long prepared.

The chapter also shows how much depended on infrastructure and logistics: ports, rivers, steamers, disease environments, and the labor of Indian soldiers. British power was real but conditional, and the war of 1824 cost far more than its architects expected. The later speed of 1885 owed much to technology and to the geography created by the settlements of 1826 and 1852.

These events shaped later Burmese history in lasting ways. The distinction between Lower and Upper Burma, the experience of pacification, the position of frontier peoples, and the memory of the monarchy all fed into the colonial order of Chapter 107 and the nationalist movements discussed in Chapter 129. The wars also belong to a wider story of imperial expansion in Southeast Asia, alongside the Dutch campaigns of Chapters 101 to 104 and the French conquests of Chapter 110.

Finally, the chapter invites students to keep asking whose evidence they are reading. Treaties and proclamations record decisions by governments; the experiences of villagers, monks, soldiers, and frontier communities must be inferred from fragments. Southeast Asian societies were historical actors throughout these wars, even when circumstances limited their choices.
