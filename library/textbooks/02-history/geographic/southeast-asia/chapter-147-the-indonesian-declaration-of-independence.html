The Indonesian Declaration of Independence

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

The document read aloud in front of a Jakarta house on the morning of August 17, 1945 is only a few lines long. The state it announced took years to build, and its survival was not settled that year.

The text proclaimed on that Friday morning in 1945 is among the shortest founding documents of any modern state. In two sentences, issued in the name of the Indonesian nation and signed by Sukarno and Mohammad Hatta, it declared Indonesian independence and stated that matters concerning the transfer of power would be handled carefully and as quickly as possible. It was read at Sukarno's residence at Pegangsaan Timur 56 in Jakarta before a modest assembly of nationalists, youth activists, and neighbors. The Japanese army that still held the city had not authorized it, and no Allied government, Dutch or otherwise, had been consulted.

The contrast between the brevity of that text and the complexity of what followed is the central subject of this chapter. A proclamation can be written in a night and read in minutes. A state needs a constitution, a government, officials who obey it, a military, a currency, a means of communicating with its citizens, and recognition from other states. In August 1945 Indonesia's leaders possessed almost none of these. What they had was a nationalist movement with roots reaching back decades, wartime institutions that had trained Indonesians in administration and arms, a set of constitutional debates conducted in the preceding months, and a moment of opportunity created by Japan's sudden surrender.

This chapter follows the whole sequence. It begins with the nationalist inheritance and the two leaders, Sukarno and Hatta, who became the Republic's president and vice president. It examines the Japanese announcement of future independence in 1944, the committees through which Indonesians debated the foundations of a state in 1945, and the formulation of Pancasila. It then treats the August crisis in detail: the surrender, the confrontation between older leaders and younger activists, the drafting of the proclamation, and the proclamation itself. The central divisions of the chapter turn to the adoption of the 1945 Constitution, the controversial removal of seven words from the Jakarta Charter, and the creation of the presidency, cabinet, and national committee.

The proclamation did not by itself create effective authority. Its meaning was made in the following weeks as news of independence spread by radio, newspaper, and word of mouth, as local committees, youth groups, officials, sultans, and workers decided whether and how to act on it, and as the Republic's leaders struggled to build security forces. The chapter therefore gives sustained attention to Java and to the islands beyond it, and to what merdeka meant to peasants, workers, and women as well as to politicians in Jakarta. The proclamation's authors also faced the return of British, Indian, and Dutch forces from September 1945, the first major confrontation at Surabaya, and a rapid transformation of the Republic's own political arrangements, culminating in the cabinet of Sutan Sjahrir in November.

The evidence is unusually rich and unusually contested. Contemporary documents survive: the proclamation drafts, constitutional texts, cabinet records, newspapers, and diplomatic correspondence. Participants also wrote memoirs, notably Hatta's, that shaped later commemoration, and the dates, speakers, and motives in those retrospective accounts do not always agree. Historians such as George McTurnan Kahin, Benedict Anderson, Anthony Reid, and R. E. Elson have built interpretations on this mixture of sources, and this chapter distinguishes among contemporary record, retrospective testimony, and scholarly reconstruction wherever the distinction affects understanding.

The chapter's central argument is that Indonesian independence was neither a Japanese gift nor the automatic result of Japan's defeat. Japanese policy created institutions and opportunities, and Japan's collapse created the moment, but the act of proclaiming sovereignty was an Indonesian political decision, taken against Japanese military wishes and in a spirit of competing strategies among Indonesians themselves. The harder task that followed was converting the proclamation into a legitimate, functioning, and internationally recognized state. This chapter ends in December 1945, when that task had barely begun. The struggle to complete it belongs to Chapter 148.

  2. Why did Sukarno and Hatta, who sat on Japanese-sponsored committees, nevertheless proclaim independence in a way the Japanese army had not approved?

Historical Context

By 1945 the lands the Dutch called the Netherlands East Indies had been governed by the Netherlands, directly or through local rulers, for generations in some regions and for only a few decades in others. A national movement had developed in the twentieth century, expressed through the organizations discussed in Chapter 126: Budi Utomo, Sarekat Islam, the Indonesian Communist Party, Sukarno's Indonesian National Party, and the youth movements that adopted the Youth Pledge in 1928 declaring one homeland, one nation, and one language of unity. Dutch repression in the late 1920s and 1930s broke most organized movements and sent leaders to prison or exile. When Japan invaded in early 1942 the colonial state collapsed quickly (Chapter 138), and the nationalist leaders were in internal exile or marginalized.

The Japanese occupation (Chapters 138, 141, 142) divided the archipelago among three commands: the Sixteenth Army in Java, the Twenty-Fifth Army in Sumatra, and the navy in Borneo, Sulawesi, the Moluccas, and the Lesser Sundas. This division matters for 1945 because nationalist institutions, communications, and Japanese attitudes differed between the army and navy zones. In Java the occupiers mobilized Indonesians through organizations such as Putera, Jawa Hokokai, and the military auxiliary PETA, and they used Indonesian officials extensively. Occupation also brought forced labor, requisitioning, and hunger. Indonesian nationalism under occupation was therefore shaped by both opportunity and suffering, and nationalists who cooperated with the Japanese to gain organizational space did so under constraints and with differing intentions, as Chapter 142 explains.

As the war turned against Japan in 1944, Tokyo's concern shifted from exploiting Indonesia to preparing it, for strategic reasons, to hold out. The announcement of future independence and the establishment of committees to prepare for it were products of this calculation. They gave Indonesian leaders a public forum and a legitimate reason to debate the nature of an independent state, even as Japanese authorities kept ultimate control. When Japan surrendered in August 1945 (Chapter 144), Allied planners assigned the reoccupation of Indonesia to the South East Asia Command under Lord Louis Mountbatten, with Dutch authorities expecting to resume colonial rule. Chapter 146 compared the revolutionary opportunities of that moment across Southeast Asia. This chapter now examines the Indonesian case in detail.

Two historiographical cautions apply throughout. First, "Indonesia" as a political idea predates the proclamation, but the territory it covered was contested: some nationalists thought of the former Dutch colony, others of a larger Malay world. Second, Java dominated both Japanese administration and the early Republic, but the archipelago's diversity means that the story of the Republic's founding cannot be written from Jakarta alone.

Division I

Nationalist Leadership and the Preparations for Independence

The political, ideological, and institutional foundations of Indonesian independence.

Section 147.1

Indonesian Nationalism and the Political Inheritance of Japanese Occupation

Indonesian nationalism was not a Japanese creation. By 1942 it had a history of three decades. Early associations such as Budi Utomo (1908) and Sarekat Islam (1912) brought indigenous elites and mass Muslim traders and peasants into modern organization. Socialist and communist currents followed, and the Communist Party's failed uprisings of 1926–1927 provoked a harsh Dutch response. In 1927 Sukarno and others founded the Indonesian National Party (PNI) around the explicit goal of an independent Indonesia. The Second Youth Congress of October 1928 adopted what became known as the Youth Pledge, affirming one homeland, one people, and one language, Indonesian, a formulation that gave the national idea a linguistic and symbolic form. Islamic organizations, including the modernist Muhammadiyah and the traditionalist Nahdlatul Ulama, ran schools and welfare institutions that reached far beyond political circles and carried their own conceptions of community and nation.

Dutch colonial authorities responded to this activity by surveillance, arrest, and exile. After Sukarno’s arrest in 1929 and mounting colonial repression, the original PNI was dissolved by its remaining leadership in 1931. Partindo and Pendidikan Nasional Indonesia (New PNI) emerged as distinct successors. Hatta and Sjahrir, who had returned from study in the Netherlands, led the latter, until they too were arrested in 1934 and sent to Boven Digoel and then Banda Neira. By the late 1930s moderate cooperation through the Volksraad and groups like Gerindo and the Indonesian Political Federation (GAPI) was the main legal avenue, and GAPI's 1939–1940 demand for an Indonesian parliament was rebuffed. The nationalists thus entered the war era with ideas, organizational memory, and a reputation, but with few institutions and little power.

The Japanese occupation altered the setting. Japanese authorities released the exiled leaders, banned Dutch-language institutions, promoted Indonesian as a public language, and, after initially prohibiting political activity, created mass organizations under their own control. Putera (Pusat Tenaga Rakyat, 1943) was led by Sukarno, Hatta, Ki Hajar Dewantara, and K. H. Mas Mansur, and was dissolved on February 29, 1944, and succeeded on March 1 by the more tightly supervised Jawa Hokokai. PETA (Pembela Tanah Air, October 1943) trained Indonesian officers and soldiers, who numbered tens of thousands by 1945; the Heiho served as auxiliaries, and in Sumatra the Giyūgun played a similar role. Indonesians also entered administrative posts left vacant by interned Dutch officials, and the Japanese authorities allowed Islamic organizations to be consolidated in Masyumi. The Japanese pursued these measures for their own war aims: manpower, rice, labor, and morale.

The consequence was that Indonesian nationalists gained something they had been denied under the Dutch: organizational reach, public platforms, military training, administrative experience, and access to the population through radio and mass meetings. They also bore the costs of association with a harsh occupation, notably the labor mobilization examined in Chapter 141. Some, such as Sjahrir, kept deliberately apart from Japanese institutions and maintained clandestine contact among students and professionals; others, including Sukarno and Hatta, believed that cooperation could extract concessions. Chapter 142 explains why this was not a simple binary of collaboration and resistance. The point here is the combined effect: Japan changed what nationalists could do, but it did not supply the idea of Indonesia, nor did it determine how they would use their new capabilities.

The movement's organizational diversity mattered as much as its common aspiration. The colonial government had governed through a legally differentiated population, a hierarchy of European and indigenous officials, and locally established aristocracies. In the cities, schools, associations, printing presses, and a growing Indonesian-language readership made it possible to imagine political solidarity across provincial boundaries. An Indonesian speaker did not need to share the same ancestral language as another participant to take part in the national movement. The Youth Pledge consequently represented an attempt to build a political community rather than a description of an already homogeneous population. The Indonesian Political Federation's demand for a parliament showed that even constitutional reformers could press the colonial state for a substantial change in political authority.

The occupation did not distribute its new opportunities equally. The Japanese administration of Java concentrated national publicity around a small number of leaders, while different military and naval authorities governed Sumatra and the eastern islands. Local Islamic schools and associations, existing administrative hierarchies, and occupational organizations also varied greatly. This helps explain why the Republic's founding institutions were concentrated in Java even though their national claims encompassed much more territory. The historian must therefore trace the connections between these nationally visible organizations and local political experience, rather than assume that an organization based in Jakarta reached every village or island. The political inheritance of occupation consisted of resources and networks, not a completed national administration.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Indonesian Nationalist Capacity before and after Japanese Occupation

What Changed

  • Mass organization, supervised by Japan, reached villages and towns on a scale the Dutch had not permitted.
  • Tens of thousands of Indonesians received military training in PETA and related formations.
  • Indonesians filled administrative posts previously held by Dutch officials.
  • Indonesian became more firmly established as a language of administration, schooling, and public life.
  • Japanese authorities themselves promised future independence and created committees to prepare it.

What Stayed the Same

  • The idea of an Indonesian nation and its core symbols, language, and aspirations predated the occupation.
  • Leading figures such as Sukarno and Hatta were products of the prewar movement.
  • Divisions among secular nationalists, Islamic groups, socialists, and communists persisted.
  • Colonial-era administrative geography and personnel largely continued to structure government.

Why the pattern matters: The occupation was an accelerant, not an origin. Treating it as the source of Indonesian nationalism would erase decades of Indonesian political work; ignoring it would explain neither the speed of events in 1945 nor the existence of armed, organized supporters of the Republic.

Section 147.2

Sukarno and Mohammad Hatta: Leadership, Ideology, and Political Strategy

Sukarno (born 1901) was educated in Surabaya and at the technical college in Bandung, where he studied engineering. A gifted orator, he built a nationalism that sought to unite Islam, secular nationalism, and Marxist-influenced social critique, an effort he expressed in 1926 and in his 1930 defense speech at Bandung, "Indonesia Accuses." Imprisoned from 1929, then exiled to Flores in 1934 and to Bengkulu in 1938, he emerged under the Japanese as the nationalist with the greatest popular appeal. His method was mass mobilization through symbolism and speech, and his instinct was to work with whatever institutions were available.

Mohammad Hatta (born 1902) came from a Minangkabau background in Sumatra and studied economics in Rotterdam in the 1920s. As chairman of Perhimpunan Indonesia he helped shape the organization into an anticolonial body committed to independence, and in 1928 he was tried in the Netherlands, famously delivering a defense advocating a free Indonesia before being acquitted. His political thought favored representative institutions, cooperative economics, and careful organization over charismatic mobilization. He believed independence required trained cadres, and he was wary of populism. Hatta and Sukarno disagreed in the 1930s over how to build a movement, and these disagreements, between a leader of the masses and an advocate of cadres, did not disappear in 1945.

Under the Japanese both men cooperated with the occupation, Sukarno with greater public visibility. Hatta, as an adviser to the military administration, tried to use the position to protect Indonesian interests and to maintain contacts with other nationalists. Their relations with younger and more radical figures differed, as did their personal links to Japanese officers. Sutan Sjahrir, once Hatta's close colleague, declined to take part in Japanese institutions and kept a network of contacts among students. Ahmad Subardjo, with long ties to Hatta from the Dutch period, worked with the Japanese navy's liaison bureau under Rear Admiral Tadashi Maeda, and provided a bridge between older nationalists and the navy.

The two men proclaimed independence together, but their political approaches were not identical. Sukarno brought public authority and the ability to make a gesture legible to crowds. Hatta brought organizational discipline and constitutional caution. Each needed the other: the joint signature in the proclamation expressed the practical alliance on which the early Republic depended, and historians generally read the later divergences between them, culminating in Hatta's resignation as vice president in 1956, as latent in these differences.

The contrast between the two leaders was shaped partly by the opportunities available to educated Indonesians under colonial rule. Sukarno's engineering education in Bandung placed him among a small professional elite capable of addressing audiences in the language of modern political organization. Hatta's years in the Netherlands exposed him to international anticolonial activism and debates about political economy. The two paths demonstrate that nationalism was not confined to a particular place of origin or to one type of educational institution. Both leaders understood that the colonial order could be challenged through political organization, but they placed different weight on the means by which a disciplined movement could be developed.

Their respective writings illuminate these differences more effectively than later characterizations of one as emotional and the other as rational. Sukarno's effort to connect nationalist, Islamic, and Marxist traditions sought a broad coalition capable of overcoming division. Hatta's emphasis on education and cooperatives connected political independence to the capacity of citizens to participate in economic and representative institutions. Neither approach was merely a personal temperament. Each offered an answer to the problem of creating a nation out of a population divided by class, region, language, and colonial legal status. Their differences about organization therefore anticipated important constitutional choices without predetermining the precise arrangements made in 1945.

The joint leadership also had a practical significance for the immediate transition. Neither leader alone controlled all the nationalists who had emerged under occupation. Their combined prestige helped make an independence declaration acceptable to participants with different backgrounds, while their contacts with officials and political activists facilitated the conversion of preparatory work into governmental decisions. Their cooperation did not erase differences within their own networks, including Hatta's long association with Sjahrir and Sukarno's ties to mass organizations. The signature of the two men provided a recognizable center around which supporters could organize. It was not an electoral mandate or a guarantee that all local authorities would obey them. Their relationship should be understood in the immediate circumstances of the founding, rather than interpreted solely through their eventual disagreement in the 1950s.

Section 147.3

Japanese Independence Promises and the Creation of the BPUPK

On September 7, 1944, Prime Minister Kuniaki Koiso announced in the Japanese Diet that the East Indies would be permitted independence in the future. The statement came after the fall of Saipan and the collapse of Tōjō's cabinet, when Japan's strategic position was deteriorating. It gave no date, no territorial definition, and no account of what independence would mean, and it left military authorities free to control the process. Nevertheless, it altered the political climate: Indonesian leaders could now discuss independence publicly, and Japanese authorities allowed the Indonesian flag and anthem, previously prohibited, to be shown on specified occasions.

The practical vehicle for preparation came in March 1945, when the Sixteenth Army in Java announced the creation of the Investigating Committee for Preparatory Work for Independence, Badan Penyelidik Usaha-Usaha Persiapan Kemerdekaan, usually called the BPUPK or BPUPKI. Its chairman was Dr. Radjiman Wedyodiningrat, a Javanese physician and elder statesman of the nationalist movement. The committee included Indonesian participants from nationalist, Islamic, regional, aristocratic, and professional circles, with Japanese representatives attached to the occupation-era body. Membership totals vary with the inclusion of Japanese representatives, leadership positions, and later additions; the chapter does not treat these different counts as interchangeable. The BPUPK was formally inaugurated on May 28, 1945, but its first substantive deliberations took place from May 29 to June 1, a distinction confirmed in the published documentary record of the Indonesian state archives and the Pancasila-ideology agency BPIP.

The committee's limits were considerable. It was established under Japanese auspices, it covered Java rather than the whole archipelago (the army in Sumatra and the navy in the east had their own arrangements), and its authority was investigative: it could debate and propose, not govern. The Japanese authorities probably envisioned a controlled transition in which Indonesia's independence would serve Japan's interests. But the participants used the committee for their own purposes. They debated what an Indonesian state should be, in a formal deliberative forum under occupation supervision, using Indonesian. By the end of the second session in July, they had produced a draft constitution, a declaration that later became the preamble, and a statement of territorial aspirations.

Koiso's announcement should be situated within the changing political economy of the Japanese empire. The worsening military situation increased the value to Japanese authorities of obtaining Indonesian cooperation, but independence remained undefined in institutional and international terms. For Indonesian nationalists, a public commitment provided a point around which further political claims could be organized. The same statement could therefore have quite different meanings for its issuer and its recipients. The promise was neither a treaty establishing Indonesia as a state nor an irreversible transfer of authority. Its importance was that it changed the range of political activities that occupation authorities were prepared to tolerate.

The BPUPK also differed fundamentally from a constituent assembly elected under conditions of independence. Its participants were appointed, its sessions took place within the limits imposed by military rule, and it could not implement its proposals through an administration of its own. Nonetheless, its members brought diverse professional and political experience to the deliberations. Lawyers could translate political principles into constitutional rules; religious figures could identify problems of communal representation; nationalists could articulate territorial and ideological claims; and administrators could consider the machinery through which a new government might actually function. These contributions explain why the committee's work remained useful after the circumstances of its creation had disappeared.

The significance of the meetings is visible in the surviving records assembled in the BPIP–ANRI documentary series. The first volume concerns the initial deliberations about the state's foundation; the second concerns the intervening period and the July discussions of territory and constitutional arrangements. They should be read as historical records of competing proposals, not simply as a retrospective account of a predetermined consensus. A proposal advanced by a member, a committee draft, and a final decision are different kinds of evidence. Moreover, later recollections and edited collections may reproduce proceedings differently. The institutional achievement was to create a body of constitutional material from which a new government could work in August, not to establish a fully authorized Indonesian state under the Japanese administration.

Section 147.4

Pancasila, the Jakarta Charter, and Constitutional Debates

The first session opened with speeches on the philosophical basis of the future state. Mohammad Yamin participated in the May 29 deliberations; the detailed speech attributed to him in later publications should not be equated uncritically with the surviving contemporary record. On May 31 Supomo, a legal scholar trained in Dutch law, argued for an "integralist" state in which ruler and people were unified rather than divided by individual rights, and he cautioned against both Western liberalism and an explicitly Islamic state. On June 1 Sukarno spoke. He had been asked by the chair to address the question of the foundation, in Dutch-era phrasing the Weltanschauung, of the state, and he proposed five principles: nationalism (kebangsaan), internationalism or humanity, consensus or democracy (mufakat), social welfare, and belief in God. He named the set Pancasila, "five principles," and indicated that it could be compressed into three or into one, gotong royong, mutual cooperation. The speech was a work of synthesis intended to be broad enough for Muslims, Christians, secular nationalists, and socialists to accept.

After the first session, a small committee reviewed the proposals, and on June 22 an informal Committee of Nine, which included Sukarno, Hatta, Subardjo, Yamin, A. A. Maramis (a Christian), Abikusno Tjokrosujoso, Abdul Kahar Muzakir, Agus Salim, and Wahid Hasyim, produced a draft statement of principles. This text, named the Jakarta Charter, contained the five principles in a changed order and wording, with belief in God first, followed in the same sentence by an obligation for Muslims to follow Islamic law ("dengan kewajiban menjalankan syariat Islam bagi pemeluk-pemeluknya"), seven words in Indonesian. It was a compromise between Islamic leaders seeking explicit recognition of Islam and secular nationalists, including Christians, who feared a confessional state.

The second session met July 10–17. It discussed territory, the form of state, and the draft constitution. The committee voted on the territorial question in favor of a large Indonesia that would include Malaya, northern Borneo, and Portuguese Timor in addition to the Dutch East Indies, a result that reflected one current of nationalist thinking and that events later overtook; the competing proposals did not establish the new Republic’s international boundaries. The members adopted the principle of a republic rather than a monarchy and, after debate over federal versus unitary structure, endorsed a unitary state. The Jakarta Charter was approved as the basis for the preamble, and a drafting committee under Supomo prepared a constitution of a few dozen articles. Debates over rights, over the role of the presidency, and over religion continued, with Ki Bagus Hadikusumo and others pressing for explicit Islamic provisions and some Christian and regional members expressing concern for minorities.

Students should distinguish three formulations that are often conflated: Sukarno's speech of June 1, the Jakarta Charter of June 22, and the final constitutional preamble of August 18. The order of principles, the place of belief in God, and the presence or absence of the seven words all changed, and the national philosophy now known as Pancasila is the final form, not the speech. R. E. Elson's scholarship on the Jakarta Charter controversy is central to understanding how contested the memory of this process became.

The question of territory reveals the difficulty of turning an imagined nation into a defined state. Some participants understood the former Dutch colonial territory as the practical basis for independence. Others envisaged a wider political community embracing neighboring Malay-speaking territories. Such proposals reflected different conceptions of national identity, not the consent of the populations or governments concerned. They must not be confused with the geographic limits of the Republic actually proclaimed in August. The borders of neighboring colonial territories were not altered by the committee's deliberations.

There was also a constitutional disagreement about how to understand national unity. Supomo's account of the relationship between society and the state placed emphasis on integration, and Hatta's concerns about representative institutions and freedoms reflected the possibility that a powerful state might itself become oppressive. Neither the eventual short constitution nor the Pancasila formulation eliminated these differences. The founding framework instead gave participants with different constitutional philosophies enough common ground to create a government. Reading the June speech against the June Charter and August preamble shows how an abstract set of principles acquired a different ordering and more specific legal implications through negotiation. It is particularly important not to infer agreement on every constitutional question from agreement on the eventual preamble.

The later authority of Pancasila as a national philosophy should not obscure the openness of these deliberations. In 1945 the speakers were preparing a political order whose institutions had yet to exist. The circumstances of occupation limited their representativeness, but the competing proposals reveal that the members were not simply executing a Japanese constitutional design.

Section 147.5

The PPKI and the Final Japanese Preparations for Independence

By July 1945 the BPUPK had finished its work, and Japanese authorities in Southeast Asia moved toward a new body. The Preparatory Committee for Indonesian Independence, Panitia Persiapan Kemerdekaan Indonesia (PPKI), was announced in early August, with Sukarno as chairman and Hatta as vice chairman. It was meant to be broader than the BPUPK in geographic terms, with members from Java, Sumatra, Sulawesi, Borneo, the Lesser Sundas, and the Moluccas, and it was designed to carry preparations into implementation. Its initial membership is generally recorded as twenty-one, with additional members subsequently admitted by Indonesian initiative. The enlarged body was not an elected constituent assembly.

The PPKI's authority derived from Japan's Southern Army, headed by Field Marshal Hisaichi Terauchi, who received the delegation at Dalat in southern Indochina. Sukarno, Hatta, and Radjiman were flown there and met Terauchi on August 11 or 12, according to differing accounts. Accounts of what was said differ in detail. The consistent core is that Terauchi told them they would head the PPKI and that preparations for independence could proceed; Indonesian accounts record that the Japanese expected independence to follow within a short period, and a date in later August is sometimes mentioned, but the exact wording and timetable are reported differently in participant recollections. The delegation returned to Jakarta on August 14, the day before Japan announced its surrender.

The difference between Japanese plans and Indonesian aims was fundamental, though not at first visible. Japan envisaged a managed transfer, with a nominally independent Indonesia whose institutions were prepared under Japanese guidance and which would remain tied to Japan's war aims. Indonesian nationalists wanted a sovereign state. For some, including Sukarno and Hatta, accepting Japanese sponsorship was a means to that end. For others, notably the underground networks around Sjahrir, any independence granted by Japan would be compromised in the eyes of the Allies. The surrender made this difference urgent. Students should also separate two tasks: the BPUPK had prepared constitutional ideas, while the PPKI was to adopt a constitution and create initial institutions, a task it carried out, in changed circumstances, on August 18.

The transition between committees also changed the nature of representation. The investigative body had drawn predominantly on the political environment of Java, whereas the preparatory committee was intended to include representatives associated with several occupation zones. That geographic composition mattered because an Indonesian state claiming the whole archipelago needed a credible means of acknowledging its regional diversity. The presence of members from other islands, however, did not mean that they had been elected by their respective populations or that they could guarantee compliance from local governments. The committee was an instrument for initiating state formation, not a permanent representative legislature.

The Dalat discussions should be understood within the hierarchy of Japanese authority. The Southern Army was dealing with a regional strategic problem, while the authorities governing individual islands faced distinct administrative conditions. The Indonesian delegation could negotiate with senior military leadership about preparations, but it could not conclude an international settlement on behalf of Japan, nor did the meeting itself confer control over the occupation's institutions. The Ministry of Culture encyclopedia, drawing on Anderson, places the meeting on August 11 and associates it with the intended first PPKI session on August 18. Accounts describing August 12 and a later August date for independence should be treated as distinct recollections rather than compressed into an apparently certain timetable.

After the surrender, the committee presented Indonesian leaders with a political dilemma. Abandoning it completely could destroy a useful institutional mechanism and make it harder to create a government quickly. Using it to proclaim independence could give opponents an opportunity to characterize the new state as a Japanese creation. The sequence that followed separated these two functions: the proclamation was issued in the name of the nation, outside a formal committee session, and the enlarged PPKI adopted the constitutional arrangements afterward. That distinction is central to the Republic's own account of its legitimacy. It does not erase the institutional origins of the committee; rather, it explains how an organization created under one authority could be appropriated for a different political purpose. The added members and decisions made after surrender were expressions of Indonesian political initiative, not evidence of a continuing Japanese mandate.

Section 147.6

Japanese Surrender and the Crisis of Nationalist Strategy

News of Japan's impending surrender reached Indonesians through clandestine radio listening, since Japanese authorities had sealed radios to receive only official stations. Sutan Sjahrir and his circle, with access to foreign broadcasts, learned of the surrender in mid-August. According to participant recollections, Sjahrir urged Hatta around August 14–15 to proclaim independence immediately and to do so independently of the PPKI, so that Indonesia could not be portrayed as a Japanese creation. The formal announcement by Emperor Hirohito on August 15 turned the matter from a possibility into a practical problem.

The leaders faced different considerations. Sukarno and Hatta had just returned from Dalat believing independence was imminent under Japanese auspices, and they were cautious about acting without Japanese cooperation, fearing bloodshed if the large Japanese forces still in Java resisted. They also wanted to hold the PPKI meeting, since it provided an institutional frame and a body capable of electing a president. Sjahrir and the younger activists worried that a PPKI proclamation would look like Japan's act and weaken Indonesia's claim with the Allies, who would soon appear. Japanese officials, for their part, were bound by surrender terms requiring them to maintain order and the status quo pending Allied arrival, and local commanders had been told not to permit changes. Japanese policy toward independence therefore changed from sponsorship to prohibition within days.

Rumors, meetings, and confusion filled August 15 and 16. Hatta later recounted that Sukarno was reluctant to act at once, that he and Sukarno wished to confirm the surrender's authenticity and to consult the PPKI, and that younger activists denounced them as slow. Participant memoirs differ on tone and sequence, and students should treat them as testimony rather than neutral record. What is clear is that the question was not whether Indonesia should be independent but how, when, and under whose authority the proclamation would be made.

The uncertainty of August 1945 was partly an information problem. News about the international war reached Jakarta through official broadcasts, foreign-language broadcasts, rumors, and personal contacts. Indonesian participants did not all have the same access to those channels, and the occupation's censorship made it difficult to distinguish a reliable report from a rumor. The difference between learning that Tokyo was preparing to surrender and obtaining confirmation that the surrender had been announced was therefore politically consequential. A historical reconstruction must avoid treating information available to later historians as information simultaneously available to everyone in Jakarta.

The surrender also had different meanings at different institutional levels. Japan's announcement signaled that its war would end, but the occupation administration continued to exist and had substantial coercive power. The formal instrument was signed on September 2. In the interim, Japanese officials received instructions intended to preserve order and facilitate the Allied takeover. They could therefore reject a proposed change of sovereignty even while their country's military defeat was unmistakable. The opportunity for independence was not the total disappearance of Japanese power; it was the increasingly uncertain legitimacy and future of that power.

The disagreements among nationalists reflected these circumstances. Established leaders had to consider whether the organizations they had spent months developing could still be used to give the future government institutional continuity. Younger activists emphasized that the legitimacy of an independent Indonesia would be damaged if its creation appeared to follow orders from a defeated empire. Sjahrir's position combined anticolonial principle with attention to the international consequences of the proclamation. The respective choices were not wholly incompatible, as the eventual separation of the declaration from the PPKI's constitutional meeting demonstrates. The historian should be cautious about turning later memoirs into direct access to individual motives: participants had reasons to emphasize their own foresight or to explain their disagreements after independence had become a national founding story. What can be established with greater confidence is the sequence of the political decisions and the institutional difference between an independent declaration and the subsequent use of preparatory bodies.

Section 147.7

The Pemuda and the Rengasdengklok Incident

The term pemuda, "youth," refers to a generation of mostly young, urban, educated Indonesians politicized by the occupation. Benedict Anderson's study of Java in 1944–1946 emphasized the pemuda as a distinctive revolutionary force with its own culture, values, and sense of generational mission. They were not a single organization. Students and former students, PETA officers, journalists, workers, and members of dormitory-based groups such as the Menteng 31 circle, associated with Wikana, Sukarni, Chaerul Saleh, and others, differed in ideology and connections. Some had ties to the Japanese navy, some to Sjahrir's network, some to neither.

On the night of August 15 several of these figures met and decided that Sukarno and Hatta must proclaim independence immediately. Wikana and Darwis went to Sukarno's house and demanded action, reportedly warning that bloodshed would follow if Sukarno did not proclaim by the next day. Sukarno answered sharply that if they wanted to kill him they could do so at once. Because the sources for this confrontation are largely later recollections, details of the words used should be treated cautiously.

Early on August 16 a group of activists took Sukarno, his wife Fatmawati, their infant son Guntur, and Hatta from Jakarta to Rengasdengklok, a small town east of the capital, where a PETA unit was stationed. The purpose was to remove the leaders from Japanese influence and from the perceived pressure of conservative advisers in Jakarta, and to compel a decision. It was not an attempt to oppose independence, and the leaders were not held as prisoners in any conventional sense, but they were taken against their wishes.

In Jakarta, Ahmad Subardjo negotiated with Wikana and other activists and reportedly guaranteed that the proclamation would be made within a day. Accompanied by a young activist, Yusuf Kunto, he traveled to Rengasdengklok and brought Sukarno and Hatta back to Jakarta that evening. The incident thus ended in a compromise: the proclamation would be made promptly, but the leaders, not the pemuda, would write and announce it, and the venue would be arranged in cooperation with Admiral Maeda, whose house offered a degree of protection.

Historians read Rengasdengklok as a conflict about strategy, timing, legitimacy, and leadership. The pemuda believed that delay would permit Japanese or Allied interference and that a proclamation by the PPKI would appear compromised; the older leaders believed that haste risked a Japanese military response and that an institutional framework mattered. Both sides wanted independence. The incident also illustrates a recurring feature of the revolution: younger activists would repeatedly push the established leadership, and the leadership would repeatedly try to channel and contain them.

The youth movement must be situated in the institutions that connected young Indonesians during the occupation. Schools, training establishments, employment in offices and newspapers, and political discussion groups offered forms of association that differed from the disciplined party structures favored by some older nationalists. Wartime mobilization gave many younger people experience of acting collectively and of hearing nationalist language in public, while the shortages and coercion of the occupation undermined the authority of adults who appeared to have accepted it. Not all young people shared those circumstances or the same interpretation of them, and the word pemuda should not be used as if it designated a single party or social class.

The significance of Rengasdengklok lies in the capacity of politically organized young people to challenge the control of the independence process by senior leaders. Their intervention created a crisis of authority within the nationalist movement, exposing the absence of any settled mechanism for determining who could speak for the future nation. Established leaders could invoke their longstanding public standing and their constitutional preparations; younger activists could argue that decisive action independent of Japanese auspices was the more authentic expression of national sovereignty. Neither claim alone supplied a fully representative political mandate.

The subsequent agreement illustrates the need for coordination across these groups. The younger activists could accelerate political decision-making but lacked the nationally recognized leadership and preparatory institutions available to Sukarno and Hatta. The senior leaders could proclaim a national decision with greater public visibility, but their authority depended on the cooperation of activists who would communicate it and organize support. This mutual dependence helps explain why the founding act combined revolutionary urgency with institutional continuity. Anderson's study of youth politics provides a framework for investigating that relationship. It should not be used to imply that every regional youth movement followed the Jakarta experience. In the weeks after independence, local activists often interpreted national instructions in light of their own political circumstances, a pattern examined in Division IV.

Section 147.8

The Drafting of the Proclamation

Back in Jakarta on the night of August 16, Sukarno and Hatta first spoke with Major General Nishimura, a senior Japanese military-administration official, who told them the army could not permit a proclamation because the surrender terms required the status quo. The leaders concluded that the Japanese army would not cooperate and went to the residence of Rear Admiral Tadashi Maeda, head of the navy's liaison office. Maeda, who had sympathy for Indonesian aspirations and had cultivated nationalist contacts, provided his house as a meeting place and, in practice, protection from army interference. His role should be described precisely: he provided facilities and a degree of security, but this was not a formal Japanese authorization of Indonesian independence, and he was himself bound by surrender conditions.

In the early hours of August 17 a group including Sukarno, Hatta, Ahmad Subardjo, Sukarni, Sayuti Melik, and others gathered in Maeda's house. Hatta and Subardjo contributed to the wording; Sukarno wrote the text in his own hand. The first sentence declared independence in the name of the Indonesian nation; the second concerned the transfer of power. An early idea of beginning with a longer statement drawn from the Jakarta Charter preamble was set aside in favor of brevity and speed. A dispute arose over who should sign. Some of the older leaders suggested that a larger body of representatives sign. Sukarni proposed that Sukarno and Hatta sign alone on behalf of the Indonesian people, and this was accepted.

Sayuti Melik, a journalist and nationalist, typed the final version. He made several changes from Sukarno's handwritten draft: the closing signature line was changed from "representatives of the Indonesian people" to "on behalf of the Indonesian people," the spelling tempoh was revised to tempo, and the date was expanded from the numeric day and month to a written formula, retaining the abbreviated imperial year 2605. The surviving documents therefore show a handwritten draft with corrections and a typed final version, and conflating them is a common error. The date's imperial-year form, "'05," is itself historically revealing: the document used the dating system of the occupation to assert independence from it.

The proclamation's brevity was itself a political choice. A longer declaration might have required agreement on the philosophical basis of government, the future disposition of colonial institutions, and the nature of the proposed state. These issues had already occupied months of constitutional discussion. A short document could assert a common objective without resolving all these matters on the night of its preparation. In this respect the proclamation differed from the constitutional preamble adopted the following day. The former established the claim of independence; the latter connected that claim to a more detailed account of the state's purposes and governing institutions.

The archival comparison offers a useful lesson in textual criticism. The handwritten document is material evidence that wording was considered and revised before the final text was produced. The typescript, by contrast, presents the settled wording intended for public circulation. Its more formal closing formula expresses the relationship between the two signatories and the nation on whose behalf they claimed to act. The change from tempoh to tempo is a spelling revision, while the expansion of the numeric date into a written formula is a documentary formatting change. Neither should be mistaken for a change in the historical date or a different calendar. The imperial-year notation survived both versions. It illustrates how new political purposes could be expressed using administrative conventions inherited from the regime being displaced.

The documents do not, by themselves, settle every historical controversy surrounding their preparation. The handwritten draft establishes that certain changes were made; it does not necessarily identify the speaker who proposed each alteration. Accounts of the discussions by Hatta, Subardjo, and other participants are a different kind of evidence, and their value depends on proximity to the event, later political circumstances, and corroboration. Students should distinguish the evidence of a textual change from evidence about the motivation behind it. The latter requires more than simply comparing the two documents.

Finally, Maeda's role should be understood without extending it beyond the documented circumstances. Cooperation by particular Japanese officers did not amount to a decision by the Japanese government to recognize the Republic. Local officials had different institutional connections and attitudes toward Indonesian nationalism, and the assistance of one official could coexist with the refusal of another. The location of the drafting meeting therefore illuminates the political ambiguity of the transition, while the proclamation's authority rested on its independent claim to speak in the name of the Indonesian nation.

Section 147.9

August 17, 1945: The Proclamation of Indonesian Independence

On the morning of Friday, August 17, 1945 (the ninth of Ramadan in the Islamic calendar), a small crowd gathered outside Sukarno's residence at Pegangsaan Timur 56. Accounts describe a relatively small gathering; precise attendance estimates vary. Sukarno, accompanied by Hatta, read the proclamation, having also delivered introductory remarks. Soon after, the red-and-white flag, sewn according to tradition by Fatmawati, was raised on a bamboo pole by Latief Hendraningrat, a PETA officer, and Suhud, a young aide, while those present sang "Indonesia Raya." The ceremony was short and plain.

The text's wording carries significant weight. "We, the Indonesian nation, hereby declare the independence of Indonesia" is a sovereign act claimed by the nation itself, not granted by Japan or any other power. The phrase "on behalf of the Indonesian nation" identifies Sukarno and Hatta as speaking for the nation without being formally elected to do so. The second sentence, referring to the transfer of power, is more ambiguous and reflects the circumstances: it acknowledges that practical authority still rested elsewhere, with the Japanese administration and its institutions, and promises that the matter would be settled by Indonesians "carefully and as quickly as possible." In practice, "transfer" turned out to mean not an orderly handover but a contest, with some officials and units transferring allegiance and others resisting or waiting.

The proclamation did not itself establish effective control. In Jakarta, Japanese military police remained, Japanese troops held key installations, and the surrender terms required Japan to maintain order for the Allies. Outside Java the news spread slowly or not at all in the first days. The proclamation was a claim of sovereignty rather than the exercise of it, and its authority depended on what followed in the days and weeks ahead.

A widely known audio recording of Sukarno reading the proclamation exists, but it is not a recording made at the 1945 ceremony; it was recreated later. Radio Republik Indonesia dates the recording to 1951, while an ANRI archival commentary describes it as made in 1950. Students should therefore not treat the recording as contemporary evidence of the event itself. The surviving 1945 evidence consists of the documents, photographs taken that morning, and the accounts of participants.

The proclamation also had a ceremonial dimension that distinguished it from the production of a constitutional draft. A written declaration could be copied and distributed, but its public reading made the claim of independence an event witnessed by others. The flag, anthem, and national language connected the act to symbols that had circulated in the nationalist movement before the war. Their public use represented a claim that these symbols now belonged to a sovereign political community rather than to an association operating under colonial or occupation restrictions. The ceremony did not itself confer an electoral mandate, but it provided an identifiable founding event around which the new state could construct public memory.

Fatmawati's contribution illustrates the importance of broadening the historical record beyond the two signatories. The flag associated with the founding ceremony became a national object of remembrance, and its production connected domestic work to the public symbolism of the state. Other participants contributed through organizing, reporting, photographing, and communicating the event. A chapter that focuses exclusively on the political speeches would miss the social relationships through which a short text became a widely recognizable declaration. Conversely, later commemorative accounts should not be treated as contemporaneous evidence for every detail of the ceremony. Photographs, documentary texts, and oral recollections have different evidentiary strengths.

The proclamation's language also suggests that sovereignty and government had to be considered separately. Its first statement asserted national independence without identifying a particular colonial authority that had granted it. Its second recognized the need to resolve the transfer of governmental power. The document did not specify the identity of every officeholder, the boundaries of every province, the organization of the army, or the precise procedures for constitutional government. This restraint allowed a declaration of common political purpose to precede institutional decisions. It also left unresolved the meaning of the proposed transfer where Japanese officials, returning colonial authorities, and local organizations disagreed.

The subsequent national commemoration of August 17 should not be confused with the date on which all consequences of independence were achieved. A state can commemorate the moment of its founding while recognizing that the consolidation of authority took considerably longer. The 1945 documents and photographs belong to the immediate event; subsequent commemorations and the recreated recording belong to its history of remembrance. Studying both makes it possible to understand the proclamation as a political act and as the foundation of a durable national historical narrative without treating later representations as if they were contemporary records.

Section 147.10

The Adoption of the 1945 Constitution

The Constitution was not adopted on August 17. On the morning of August 18 the PPKI met in Jakarta for its first full session. It had been enlarged by additional members, and it worked from the BPUPK's draft, made brief and quickly revised. Within hours it adopted the Constitution of the Republic of Indonesia, in a form that is now called the "original" or "1945" Constitution, and elected Sukarno president and Hatta vice president, by acclamation. The speed was both a product of the earlier BPUPK work and a response to urgency: the committee wished to complete formal founding before Allied forces arrived.

Students must take care that the text they use is the original. The present-day Indonesian Constitution is the 1945 text as amended in four rounds between 1999 and 2002, and many web pages that call themselves "the 1945 Constitution" present the amended version. The discussion below uses the original text of August 18, 1945, which comprised a preamble, thirty-seven articles in sixteen chapters, four transitional articles, and two additional provisions, and which also appeared with explanatory elucidations (penjelasan) published in 1946.

The preamble declares that independence is the right of all nations, that the struggle for independence had reached its moment, and that the independence was attained by the grace of God and the desire of the people; it then sets out national purposes (protecting the people, promoting public welfare, educating the nation, and contributing to world order based on independence, lasting peace, and social justice) and states the five principles of the state in their final order. Article 1 declares Indonesia a unitary state in the form of a republic, with sovereignty in the hands of the people and fully exercised by the People's Consultative Assembly (MPR). The MPR, composed of members of the People's Representative Council (DPR) together with regional and group delegates, was to elect the president and vice president and determine the broad outlines of state policy (Articles 2–3).

The president held governmental power (Article 4), with the vice president assisting. He held lawmaking power with the approval of the DPR (Article 5) and appointed and dismissed ministers, who were his assistants (Article 17). Article 6 required that the president be an "indigenous" Indonesian, a provision that reflected its time and was later reinterpreted. Article 7 provided a five-year term with eligibility for re-election, without a numerical limit. Article 18 provided for regional government, recognizing the "special character" of existing self-governing territories and traditional communities. Articles 19–22 dealt with the DPR and legislation, including emergency regulations. Articles 26–28 concerned citizenship, equality before the law, and rights of association, assembly, and expression, which were to be regulated by law and were stated in brief terms. Article 29 concerned religion, and Article 33 outlined an economy organized as a collective effort on the basis of kinship, with the state controlling branches of production important to the public and natural resources. Articles 35 and 36 established the red-and-white flag and Indonesian as the national language.

The Constitution was short and, by design, provisional. Its framers, including Sukarno, described it as a "lightning" or temporary constitution, to be replaced when conditions allowed. Its transitional provisions mattered more than its permanent ones in 1945. They stated that the PPKI would arrange the transfer of government, that existing institutions and regulations would continue until replaced, that the first president and vice president would be elected by the PPKI, and that until the MPR, DPR, and Supreme Advisory Council were formed, all powers would be exercised by the president assisted by a national committee. This last provision explains the origin of the KNIP and the extraordinary concentration of power in the presidency during the first months.

The institutional logic of the original document deserves particular attention. Article 5 placed lawmaking power in the president, subject to DPR approval; Article 20 required that bills obtain the agreement of that council. This arrangement differs from the present amended Constitution's allocation of legislative power. Article 22 provided for emergency regulations with subsequent legislative consideration. Taken together, these provisions combined a powerful executive with a contemplated representative role in legislation. Their practical operation depended, however, upon the existence of representative institutions that had not yet been established in August 1945.

The founding text also addressed responsibilities beyond the immediate transfer of political authority. Article 23 required a budget and established rules for taxation, currency, and public financial accountability. A constitutional provision concerning the budget could not itself create a functioning treasury, yet it indicated that governmental resources were to be organized under public rules rather than treated as personal possessions of officeholders. Article 24 provided for judicial power exercised by the Supreme Court and other judicial bodies established by law. Article 25 left the qualifications and removal of judges to statutory regulation. These provisions illustrate the distance between constitutional design and practical state capacity: the new Republic required functioning courts, trained personnel, reliable records, and resources before the proposed order could operate across the archipelago.

Citizenship also posed a particularly difficult problem for a national movement formed within a colonial system of unequal legal classifications. Article 26 distinguished indigenous Indonesians from other persons admitted as citizens by law. Article 27 stated equality before the law and government for citizens and recognized the right to work and a livelihood consistent with human dignity. Article 6's original presidential eligibility requirement, often translated as requiring an indigenous Indonesian, shows that the founding text did not abolish every distinction inherited from colonial-era categories. These provisions require historically sensitive analysis rather than the assumption that contemporary understandings of citizenship and equal eligibility were already fully established.

The economy provisions connected independence with social purpose. Article 33 addressed cooperative economic organization and public control of important productive sectors and natural resources; Article 34 placed responsibility for impoverished persons and neglected children on the state. The Constitution thus claimed more than territorial independence. Its framers envisaged a government with responsibilities for welfare and education as well as political authority. In the emergency of late 1945, these objectives remained largely aspirational, and the gap between them and the resources of the actual government became an enduring political problem. The original transitional rules, not the later amended text, explain how the initial institutional framework was intended to bridge that gap.

Section 147.11

The Jakarta Charter Compromise and the Foundations of National Unity

Before the PPKI adopted the Constitution on August 18, a change was made to the text derived from the Jakarta Charter. The seven words imposing on Muslims the obligation to follow Islamic law were removed from the preamble. Article 29 was revised accordingly, and the draft requirement that the president be a Muslim was dropped. The first principle took the form Ketuhanan Yang Maha Esa, belief in the One Almighty God, a formula intended to accommodate different religious constituencies without the draft’s specific Islamic-law obligation.

The change was reached in a brief, informal consultation before the plenary meeting. Hatta's account, given in his memoirs, is that on the evening of August 17 a Japanese naval officer brought a message that Christian-majority regions in eastern Indonesia would not join a state whose preamble contained the seven words. In his retrospective account, Hatta described consultations with several leaders, including Ki Bagus Hadikusumo, Wahid Hasyim, Kasman Singodimedjo, and Teuku Mohammad Hasan. The exact participants, their presence and the nature of their consent remain disputed. The sources for this episode are partly memoirs, and later scholars, including R. E. Elson, have questioned details: the identity of the Japanese officer, the nature of the message, whether protests from eastern Indonesia were as specific as Hatta described, and how the Muslim leaders understood their consent.

The compromise should be read neither as the elimination of religion from government nor as the settlement of the issue. The new formulation put belief in God first and so gave religion constitutional prominence, while declining to give any one religion legal supremacy. For many Muslim leaders it was a painful concession, and some regarded it as temporary pending a later, elected constituent assembly. The debate returned in the 1950s Constituent Assembly, in the Darul Islam rebellions, and in later arguments over the status of the Charter, and is not resolved in this chapter. The compromise permitted adoption of the revised text, but its necessity and longer-term political effects remain debated.

The controversy should be examined as a constitutional question as well as an episode of political negotiation. A proposed obligation in a constitutional preamble could have had consequences for later legislation, administrative practice, and the relationship between public authority and religious institutions. The requirement concerning presidential eligibility would have made the religious distinction directly relevant to access to the highest office. Removing those provisions therefore changed specific features of the proposed constitutional order. It did not make the Constitution a document from which religion was absent, because the preamble and Article 29 retained an explicit theological foundation and the latter protected religious observance.

The distinction between the text and the circumstances of its revision is critical. The successive documents establish that the Islamic-law language was removed. They are less conclusive about the exact consultations through which agreement was secured. Hatta's retrospective account presents the decision as an urgent response to the danger that communities in eastern Indonesia would reject the new state. The account has become influential in national remembrance. Yet recollections written after a constitutional controversy has acquired great political significance must be compared with contemporary records and other testimony, rather than treated as an unmediated record of every conversation.

Elson's 2009 article challenges the conventional narrative more directly than a simple dispute over dates or identities. Its published abstract argues that later controversy misunderstood both the Charter and its political origins, and questions whether Hatta's approach to securing the amendments was necessary or politically beneficial. This provides a substantive alternative to the familiar interpretation of the revisions as indispensable statesmanship. The disagreement is not about whether the final constitutional text differs from the June text, but about the circumstances, necessity, and political effects of the decision. These questions cannot be answered simply by observing that the Republic subsequently survived.

The compromise also illustrates the difference between national unity as an objective and unanimity as an historical condition. Muslim organizations, Christian communities, regional nationalists, secular political movements, and other religious communities were internally diverse. The preferences of a small group of political leaders cannot be assumed to exhaust those of the populations with which they were associated. The constitutional formulation established a shared legal framework for the new government, but religious and political disagreement continued. Students should therefore see the founding compromise as a consequential constitutional choice made under extraordinary pressure, not as the permanent closure of a fundamental debate concerning the Indonesian state.

Section 147.12

The Presidency, Cabinet, and National Committee

After electing Sukarno and Hatta on August 18, the PPKI met again on August 19 and 22. On August 19 it decided to divide the country into eight provinces: Sumatra, Borneo (Kalimantan), Sulawesi, the Moluccas (Maluku), the Lesser Sundas (Sunda Kecil), West Java, Central Java, and East Java. Governors were designated for each, among them Teuku Mohammad Hasan for Sumatra, Pangeran Mohammad Noor for Borneo, Sam Ratulangi for Sulawesi, Johannes Latuharhary for the Moluccas, and I Gusti Ketut Pudja for the Lesser Sundas. It also determined that the government would have a set of ministries and that existing departments would continue. On August 22 it decided to create the Central Indonesian National Committee (KNIP), a National Party, and a security body, the People's Security Agency (BKR). The one-party proposal was abandoned within a few weeks.

The Cabinet Secretariat’s consolidated chronology dates the presidential cabinet from September 2 to November 14, 1945; its individual cabinet record instead gives August 19 as the starting date. The August 19 ministerial decisions and the subsequent announcement of cabinet personnel are distinguishable. Its members included Ahmad Subardjo as foreign minister, Supomo as justice minister, and Amir Sjarifuddin as minister of information. The cabinet was responsible to the president under Article 17, not to a parliament.

The KNIP was inaugurated on August 29. It was appointed, not elected, drew on members of the PPKI and other nationalists, regions, and groups, and under the transitional provisions was to assist the president until permanent institutions were formed. It was therefore not an elected parliament, and treating it as one is a common error. Kasman Singodimedjo was its first chairman. Its early role was consultative; its transformation is discussed in Section 147.19.

The Republic inherited administrative personnel and institutions from earlier governments, as the transitional provisions explicitly allowed. Indonesian civil servants who had served the Dutch and then the Japanese continued to work, and the Republic took over many Japanese-era offices, buildings, and records, especially in Java. Yet the government claimed a new source of authority, the proclamation and the sovereign will of the nation, a combination of institutional continuity and political rupture that characterizes the early Republic.

The creation of a ministry was not equivalent to the creation of a national administrative service. Ministries required personnel, buildings, communications, records, and the means to pay officials. Many of these resources had existed under Dutch rule and had been reorganized during the occupation. The Republic sought to retain useful administrative capacity while replacing the source of legitimate authority. Officials who had worked for earlier governments therefore faced a choice about whether to recognize new appointments and continue their duties under republican authority. That process varied from office to office and was particularly difficult where Japanese or Allied personnel remained in control.

The presidential cabinet's official chronology illustrates the distinction between institutional decision and implementation. The Cabinet Secretariat's consolidated historical list places its commencement on September 2, whereas the individual cabinet page uses August 19. The latter corresponds to the early decision concerning ministerial organization. These sources should be cited separately, rather than treating the two dates as identical events. Among the portfolios established in this period were foreign affairs, interior, justice, finance, education, information, and security. Ahmad Subardjo's foreign affairs responsibilities, Supomo's justice portfolio, and Amir Sjarifuddin's information responsibilities connected the political founding to practical governmental work. The cabinet roster also reveals the importance of specialized personnel in a government frequently remembered through its two most visible leaders.

Provincial appointments similarly expressed a constitutional and administrative intention. A governor appointed in Jakarta possessed a republican claim to administer a province, but implementation required cooperation from local officials and the ability to communicate with the center. The extensive geographic reach of the eight initial provinces should therefore be read as an institutional program rather than a map of actual control. In some regions the new authorities could draw upon local nationalist organizations and continuity of civil administration; in others Dutch restoration or rival authorities limited their activities.

The Central Indonesian National Committee, inaugurated on August 29, occupied a different position from that of the cabinet. It was an appointed national body intended to assist the president during the transitional period, not the permanent elected legislature envisaged in the Constitution. Its subsequent expansion of authority in October was therefore a major institutional development. The early government should be represented as a provisional system operating under exceptional circumstances, in which the president, ministers, national committee, and provincial officials performed overlapping tasks without the settled distribution of responsibilities that a functioning permanent constitutional system would require.

Division IV

The Spread of Independence and the Formation of Republican Authority

How a declaration made in Jakarta became a national revolution across a diverse archipelago.

Section 147.13

Communicating Independence: Radio, Newspapers, and National Mobilization

A proclamation matters politically only if it is heard. In August 1945 the means of communication, radio stations, telegraph lines, newspaper presses, and railways, were largely controlled by Japanese authorities, but they were operated by Indonesians. Indonesian staff of the Domei news agency and the Japanese-run radio network took the lead in circulating the news. Accounts say that Indonesian employees at Domei prepared and transmitted the text on August 17, despite Japanese attempts to stop or suppress it, and that Indonesian newspapers published it within days. Telegraph and rail workers passed the news across Java, and word of mouth did the rest. Details of who transmitted what and when come largely from later recollection, and the precise sequence should be taken as established only where contemporary documents confirm it.

Broadcasting soon acquired an explicit republican character. Radio Republik Indonesia (RRI) was established on September 11, 1945, by a group of broadcasters who had worked for the Japanese-run Hōsō Kyoku, and it became the Republic's primary voice. Newspapers in Jakarta, Yogyakarta, and other cities, along with posters, wall slogans, and pamphlets, carried the message of independence, and the slogan "Merdeka!" appeared on trains and buildings. The flag, "Indonesia Raya," and Indonesian as the national language served as visible and audible markers of the new nation, and their meaning was reinforced by constant public use.

The largest early demonstration of republican support was the mass meeting at Ikada Square (Lapangan Ikada) in Jakarta on September 19, 1945. The Republic's leaders had called a rally, and the Japanese military, concerned about disorder, ringed the area with troops and attempted to prevent it. A large crowd gathered; precise attendance estimates differ among historical accounts. Sukarno spoke only briefly, urging calm and discipline and asking the crowd to disperse peacefully, which it did. The episode demonstrated public support for the Republic and the leaders' ability to control a crowd, and also showed the Japanese troops' reluctance to act.

Circulation of news is not the same as governmental authority. Hearing that independence had been proclaimed did not mean that tax collectors, police, or village heads changed their behavior; that required decisions by local actors, discussed in the next sections. Communication created a shared political event; it did not by itself create an administration.

Communication was one of the Republic's first administrative problems. The declaration had to be transmitted across a territory in which communications infrastructure had developed primarily for the needs of colonial administration and commercial exchange. Telegraph services and newspapers connected many urban centers, but access varied with geography, literacy, available transport, and the continued presence of occupation officials. A resident of an outlying island might receive news through travelers or local political networks rather than directly from Jakarta. Thus even knowledge of the founding act was geographically uneven.

Institutional accounts published by Radio Republik Indonesia identify the journalist Syahruddin and broadcaster Jusuf Ronodipuro among the figures involved in conveying the text and announcing the news. These retrospective institutional accounts should be distinguished from a complete contemporary broadcast log. Their historical importance lies in showing that personnel already working within occupation-era communications organizations could redirect their skills toward the Republic. The establishment of RRI on September 11 provided an institutional basis for republican broadcasting, rather than simply extending a single proclamation announcement. Broadcasting became a means through which leaders could address supporters, publicize governmental decisions, and represent the Republic to wider audiences.

The September 19 gathering at Ikada Square represented a different kind of communication: a visible demonstration of the relationship between political leadership and an assembled public. Estimates of the number of participants vary and are unnecessary for establishing its importance. The meeting showed that the proclamation had become a basis for collective action and that republican leaders could address significant groups of supporters under conditions in which authority remained disputed. It did not establish that the entire population had consented to every policy or that the government possessed undisputed control of the city.

National symbols worked across these different communications settings. A flag or anthem could express allegiance without requiring detailed knowledge of constitutional arrangements. Indonesian-language newspapers and public speeches could connect these symbols to claims about independence and public responsibility. Their wide circulation helped create a shared vocabulary of political change, while local organizations continued to give that vocabulary particular meanings. The Republic's communications effort consequently contributed to legitimacy and coordination, but its effectiveness depended on local reception and could not substitute for governmental capacity.

Section 147.14

Local Revolutions in Java and the Transformation of Political Authority

In the weeks after August 17, the Republic took form in Java less through orders from Jakarta than through local initiatives. In towns and districts, residents formed national committees (Komite Nasional Indonesia), took over offices, railway stations, and radio stations, seized Japanese weapons or demanded their surrender, and raised the flag. Participants included civil servants, teachers, former PETA and Heiho personnel, workers, religious leaders, and youth groups. The picture was one of many local revolutions, shaped by local personalities and conditions, loosely connected by the shared language of independence. Some Japanese units handed over weapons or stood aside; others resisted, and violence occurred, notably in October.

Yogyakarta offered an influential example of traditional authority aligning with the Republic. On September 5, 1945, Sultan Hamengkubuwono IX and Paku Alam VIII issued declarations placing their territories within the Republic of Indonesia and affirming loyalty to President Sukarno. Under the Dutch the sultanate had been a self-governing territory, and Article 18 of the Constitution recognized such territories' special character. The sultan, a young ruler educated partly in Dutch institutions, also provided financial and material support, and Yogyakarta would later serve as the Republic's capital. John Monfries and other historians have noted how this alignment lent the Republic legitimacy among Javanese and showed that revolution did not necessarily mean the end of monarchy.

Elsewhere, social tensions emerged. In parts of Java, villagers and local radicals challenged officials, aristocrats, and village heads seen as having collaborated with the Dutch or Japanese or as having exploited the population. Such pressures produced violence against local elites in some places. The details of those social revolutions, notably the Three Regions Affair in northern Central Java in late 1945 and early 1946, belong to Chapter 148. The point here is that the Republic's local foundation was plural and sometimes contradictory: it needed the cooperation of established officials and aristocrats, while revolutionary supporters sometimes sought to displace them.

Local revolutionary action frequently involved the existing administrative structure rather than its complete destruction. Colonial officials had governed through residency, district, and village-level authorities, and Japanese rule had modified many of these arrangements. Republican organizers inherited a population that depended on local offices for basic administrative functions. Replacing officials without ensuring continuity of records, supplies, and public services could undermine the authority of the movement that sought to govern. Conversely, retaining officials associated with earlier regimes could provoke opposition among people who understood independence as an opportunity to change local power relationships. The two problems often occurred together.

West Java's towns and rural districts contained different configurations of nationalist organizations, Islamic associations, officials, and young activists. Central Java included areas in which the authority of longstanding local elites was sharply challenged. In the Three Regions area, conflict over local authority and social inequality developed into a social revolutionary crisis during the final months of 1945. Its emergence in late 1945 demonstrates the distinct social dimensions of the revolution; Chapter 148 develops its subsequent history. East Java presented a particularly visible example of republican institutions confronting returning Allied authority; the political significance of Surabaya cannot be reduced to the decisions of national leaders in Jakarta.

Yogyakarta demonstrates that the revolution did not necessarily require the abolition of traditional rulership. Hamengkubuwono IX and Paku Alam VIII declared their alignment with the Republic on September 5, and their territories became important to the emerging national government. The relationship drew upon the special position of existing self-governing territories and the new constitutional framework for regional administration. The declarations represented a political choice by rulers whose authority had historical roots independent of the republican movement. They also created an opportunity for the Republic to benefit from an existing administrative structure rather than construct one entirely anew. The significance of Yogyakarta therefore lay not simply in symbolic loyalty but in the possibility of practical institutional cooperation.

These contrasting examples show why local revolutionary history is indispensable to understanding state formation. A committee appointed at the national level could not determine all the outcomes of disputes among local officials, youth organizations, workers, peasants, and traditional elites. The degree to which republican institutions could integrate these groups depended on local legitimacy and administrative capacity. National independence provided a common language through which they could articulate demands; it did not determine the distribution of power within every locality. The regional histories of the revolution must consequently examine both vertical relationships with Jakarta and horizontal conflicts within communities.

Section 147.15

Sumatra, Sulawesi, Borneo, Bali, and the Eastern Islands

The proclamation was received very differently outside Java. Distance, poor transportation, the Japanese division into army and navy zones, and variable nationalist organization shaped events. Scholarship such as the Regional Dynamics volume edited by Audrey Kahin and the more recent Revolutionary Worlds collection stresses that the revolution was multiple, with regions developing distinct trajectories.

In Sumatra, under the Twenty-Fifth Army, Teuku Mohammad Hasan, an Acehnese and member of the PPKI, was designated governor and returned to organize republican administration. Republican committees formed in Medan, Palembang, Padang, and other cities, often with the participation of former Giyūgun officers and local Islamic and political leaders. Sumatra's social structure, with its coastal sultanates, Minangkabau and Batak communities, and plantation belt, produced revolutionary dynamics different from Java's; Anthony Reid's study of northern Sumatra, The Blood of the People, documents violent social upheaval against the aristocracy there.

In Sulawesi, G. S. S. J. (Sam) Ratulangi, a Christian Minahasan intellectual and PPKI member, was named governor and sought to establish republican authority in Makassar. In the navy-controlled east, however, Japanese naval authorities were less accommodating, and Australian forces arrived in late 1945 to accept the Japanese surrender, followed by restored Dutch authority. Republican supporters in Makassar and elsewhere organized, but the Republic's reach was thin. In Kalimantan, Pangeran Mohammad Noor was designated governor, and nationalist organizations formed in Banjarmasin and elsewhere, but Dutch administrative return came early in parts of the island.

Bali, in the navy zone, had a distinct situation. Local rulers and aristocrats retained strong authority, nationalist youth formed organizations, and I Gusti Ketut Pudja, governor of the Lesser Sundas, tried to establish republican structures. Allied and Dutch forces arrived in 1946, and local republican resistance developed afterward. Maluku and the Lesser Sundas, with J. Latuharhary as designated governor of Maluku, presented further complications: a long history of Dutch colonial ties among Christian Ambonese communities, varied nationalist networks and relatively early Allied and Dutch reoccupation. In parts of eastern Indonesia, Dutch administration was restored before republican authorities could establish durable provincial government, but nationalist mobilization and resistance persisted.

Two distinctions follow. First, there is a difference between territorial claim and effective administration. The Republic claimed the whole former Netherlands East Indies, and its eight provinces covered the archipelago on paper, but in late 1945 it effectively administered, with varying degrees of control, only parts of Java and Sumatra. Second, the creation of Indonesia was not exclusively Javanese. Leaders and supporters from Sumatra, Sulawesi, Maluku, and other regions were central to the project, and regional political cultures shaped the revolution in distinct ways.

Sumatra's appointment of Teuku Mohammad Hasan as governor represented an effort to organize a large and internally diverse island under republican authority. It had been administered separately from Java during the war. In Aceh, Islamic scholars and their associated networks possessed political resources that differed from those of the urban organizations of Jakarta. In the plantation districts of northern Sumatra, political questions were inseparable from the position of workers, plantation interests, local rulers, and colonial-era hierarchies. West Sumatra's Minangkabau networks linked education, migration, religious organization, and nationalist leadership. Palembang and the surrounding regions of southern Sumatra possessed commercial and economic importance that made administrative authority particularly consequential. These differences meant that a single appointment could not resolve the questions of local representation and legitimacy throughout Sumatra. Anthony Reid's regional work and Audrey Kahin's edited studies provide routes for investigating these differences further; their later revolutionary histories should not be compressed into the events of August alone.

In Sulawesi, Sam Ratulangi's appointment expressed the Republic's territorial claim, while local political activists faced a very different configuration of returning power from that confronting their counterparts in Java. The Japanese naval administration had shaped wartime political opportunities differently. Republican sympathizers could form political associations and communicate a claim of independence without being able to establish a continuous provincial administration. Dutch authority and the subsequent political development of eastern Indonesia would therefore challenge any assumption that the national proclamation immediately produced a single effective state. The appointment of a governor and the existence of nationalist support must be assessed separately.

A similar distinction applies to Kalimantan, where Mohammad Noor was designated governor. The island's extensive river systems, scattered settlements, port centers, and diverse political communities made unified administration difficult even before the Japanese occupation. The Australian-led recovery of Borneo and Dutch civil administrative restoration altered the political conditions available to local nationalists. A lack of republican control in a particular district cannot be taken as proof that its residents rejected independence. Conversely, expressions of sympathy for the proclamation cannot be converted into a claim that the Republic was already administering the district.

Bali and the other Lesser Sunda Islands were grouped within the provincial arrangements under I Gusti Ketut Pudja. Local rulers, village institutions, and educated nationalist networks operated in a setting shaped by Japanese naval civilian administration and military defense arrangements. Their relationships with the Republic varied, and administrative boundaries spanning multiple islands imposed serious communications problems. The history of political mobilization in Bali should not be reduced to later military confrontation. The early question was how national claims could be translated into local organizations amid existing institutions and the return of colonial authority.

Maluku, for which Johannes Latuharhary was appointed governor, also illustrates the difference between nationally asserted territory and local political conditions. The islands contained diverse religious communities, longstanding connections to colonial administrative and military employment, and nationalist networks that did not share identical objectives. The presence of different political loyalties did not follow automatically from religious identity. In the eastern archipelago generally, the return of Dutch authorities created constraints distinct from those experienced in Java and much of Sumatra. The comparative framework of the open-access collection Revolutionary Worlds emphasizes the value of studying these regional and local experiences independently. For 1945 the principal conclusion is that Indonesia's territorial unity was a constitutional claim and a political project, not an already achieved administrative reality.

The regional leadership also had a history of its own before the national appointments. The Ministry of Culture’s biographical entry on Johannes Latuharhary identifies his contribution to discussion of Maluku’s liberation at a nationalist congress in Surabaya in 1932 and his later gubernatorial appointment in August 1945. This trajectory illustrates how activism connected the islands to political centers in Java long before the proclamation. The Buleleng cultural administration’s biography of I Gusti Ketut Pudja likewise identifies his participation in the preparation of the proclamation and his subsequent appointment in the Lesser Sundas. It dates his personal appointment to August 22, illustrating why the August 19 provincial decision should be distinguished from particular appointment dates. Neither account establishes that the governor immediately controlled all the territory he claimed to represent.

Section 147.16

Workers, Peasants, Women, and the Social Meaning of Merdeka

For most Indonesians independence was less a constitutional event than a change in daily expectations. After years of requisitioned rice, forced labor, and hunger under the Japanese, many peasants hoped that merdeka would mean relief from compulsory deliveries and the return of land used by plantations or officials. Workers on railways, in ports, and on plantations saw independence as a chance to control their workplaces and wages, and railway workers in particular played a prominent role in taking over Japanese-held installations. Students and youth associated it with education and opportunity; religious leaders saw an opening for institutions of faith. The Republic's leaders, by contrast, often wanted order and gradualism.

Women participated in many ways. Fatmawati, who sewed the flag and stood at the ceremony, is remembered chiefly in ceremonial terms, but women also took part as journalists, organizers, and relief workers. S. K. Trimurti, a journalist and labor activist who had been imprisoned by the Dutch, was an important figure in women's political organization and republican propaganda and later became minister of labor. Women's associations organized kitchens and first-aid posts, supported fighters, and formed women's auxiliary units. Research on Yogyakarta shows women's roles in humanitarian and community mobilization that earlier male-centered accounts overlooked. Evidence is uneven, since women appear less in official records, and historians draw on interviews, memoirs, and local sources.

The national revolution and the social transformations hoped for by different groups were not identical. A peasant expecting land redistribution, a worker demanding a union, and a minister building an administration might all shout "Merdeka!" while disagreeing profoundly about its content. These tensions explain why social upheaval accompanied national independence and why the Republic's leaders sometimes found themselves opposing the radical wing of their own revolution.

National independence acquired immediate social significance because the population had emerged from years of deprivation. The occupation had imposed compulsory labor, requisitions, and disruptions of trade and transportation. Families whose members had been displaced or whose livelihoods had been damaged were concerned with access to food, employment, and safety. A government claiming to represent the people inherited expectations that it would address these problems. Its resources, however, were limited by war damage and administrative fragmentation. The distance between expectations and institutional capacity was itself a source of political tension.

Workers in railway services, ports, offices, and other workplaces possessed forms of organization that were important to the practical life of the Republic. Continuing essential services under republican authority could have political significance even without participation in formal governmental institutions. Labor organizations might understand independence as an opportunity to demand better working conditions or greater control over workplace decisions. Peasant communities could interpret the end of colonial rule in relation to land tenure, taxation, or local authority. These interpretations were shaped by particular circumstances; it would be inaccurate to assume that a common slogan expressed a uniform social program.

Women's participation likewise extended beyond the most visible ceremonial contributions. Fatmawati's association with the national flag provides one documented example of the relationship between household labor and national symbolism. The journalist S. K. Trimurti represents another dimension of participation, connecting public communication, labor activism, and women's political organization. Women also contributed to community welfare and care for displaced or injured persons, activities often less visible in formal institutional records. The historian should distinguish these contributions from a claim that independence immediately eliminated gender inequalities. The short original Constitution offered a general promise of citizens' equality but did not by itself change everyday conditions of access to education, employment, or public office.

The revolution therefore brought several meanings of freedom into contact. The new national government sought the recognition of sovereign authority. Citizens could expect that the end of foreign rule would change their relationship with employers, local officials, landlords, or household institutions. These objectives could reinforce one another, but they could also conflict. The history of 1945 must recognize those differences as constitutive of the revolution rather than treat social change as an incidental consequence of elite political decisions.

Section 147.17

The BKR, TKR, and the Formation of Republican Military Institutions

The Republic had no army on August 17. The Japanese had armed and trained Indonesians in PETA, the Heiho, and, in Sumatra, the Giyūgun, but the Japanese disbanded these units around August 18–19 as part of surrender arrangements, and men returned home, often keeping some weapons. Many former KNIL (Royal Netherlands East Indies Army) officers were Dutch-trained and had a different professional background. Alongside these were youth militias, party-linked groups, religious fighting organizations, and local bands (laskar) formed independently of the government.

The government's first response was cautious. The PPKI decided on August 22 to create a People's Security Agency, Badan Keamanan Rakyat (BKR), a body for maintaining order composed of local volunteers rather than a national army. The government wished to avoid provoking the Allies by announcing a formal army. As the British landed and clashes with Dutch and Allied forces grew, this approach was abandoned. On October 5, 1945, the People's Security Army, Tentara Keamanan Rakyat (TKR), was announced. Urip Sumohardjo, a former KNIL major, was appointed chief of staff and began organizing a command structure. In November, a conference of regional commanders in Yogyakarta chose Sudirman, a former PETA battalion commander, as commander in chief, a choice discussed further in Chapter 148.

The decision to create a military institution is not the same as achieving national command. In late 1945 the TKR was a mixture of locally recruited units led by officers with diverse backgrounds and loyalties, and many armed groups operated outside it. The tension between former PETA officers, former KNIL officers, and independent militias, and between military and civilian leaders, would shape the revolution. Detailed campaigns and the later development of the armed forces belong to Chapters 148 and 149.

The formation of military institutions posed a constitutional as well as an administrative problem. A sovereign government normally claims the authority to organize the forces responsible for public security and national defense. In August 1945, the Republic possessed neither an established national military command nor the resources necessary to exercise such authority everywhere. Wartime military training had created experienced personnel, but experience acquired under Japanese or Dutch authority did not automatically translate into acceptance of a single republican command. Former servicemen brought different training, organizational cultures, and relationships with local political leaders.

The August decision concerning the BKR reflected the difficulty of announcing a national military organization at a moment when Japanese forces remained present and Allied authorities had not accepted republican sovereignty. Local security organizations provided a means of asserting responsibility for public order while the new government was still developing its institutions. Their local recruitment and organization meant that they did not constitute a unified standing army. The October decision to create the TKR was a more explicit assertion of the government's responsibility for national defense, but a decree could not immediately provide a common system of administration or discipline.

Urip Sumohardjo and Sudirman illustrate the importance of different military backgrounds within the emerging institutions. Urip's professional experience in the colonial army and Sudirman's experience in PETA offered distinct resources for the republican military leadership. The selection of commanders by regional military representatives also reflected the political importance of local formations. Relations between military leaders and the civilian cabinet were still being established. The institutional challenge was to transform a variety of formations and experienced personnel into a national organization answerable to a republican government, while protecting the population and maintaining public order.

The persistence of independent revolutionary groups demonstrates why the formal establishment of the TKR was not the end of military institution-building. Some groups were closely connected to political parties or religious associations, while others drew their legitimacy from local communities and youth movements. Their relationship to the government varied and could change with political circumstances. The difficulties of integration would influence both the subsequent revolution and the organization of the independent state. At the end of 1945 the Republic had taken a decisive institutional step, but the existence of a national military organization remained different from the effective exercise of unified command.

Section 147.18

The Return of Allied and Dutch Authorities

General Order No. 1 assigned much of the East Indies to South East Asia Command, with Borneo assigned to Australian Military Forces; subsequent arrangements gave Australia practical responsibilities in other eastern areas. In Java, responsibility fell to South East Asia Command under Admiral Lord Louis Mountbatten, with Allied Forces Netherlands East Indies (AFNEI) under Lieutenant-General Sir Philip Christison subsequently organizing the principal British-led command. A British cruiser carrying an advance party reached Jakarta in mid-September, and the main force of British-Indian troops arrived at the end of the month. Their tasks were to accept the Japanese surrender, disarm and repatriate Japanese personnel, and evacuate Allied prisoners of war and civilian internees. Their immediate military and humanitarian responsibilities were distinct from the Dutch claim to sovereignty; nevertheless, British cooperation with Dutch authorities formed part of the wider Allied political arrangements. Dutch officials, including the Netherlands Indies Civil Administration (NICA), prepared in Australia during the war, and Lieutenant Governor-General Hubertus van Mook, wanted to restore Dutch authority, and they arrived with or behind the British.

The result was a three-way contest. The British dealt with Republican officials as a practical matter in Java, requested their help with the evacuation of internees, and refused to recognize the Republic formally. The Dutch regarded the Republic as a Japanese-sponsored creation led by collaborators and sought to avoid negotiating with Sukarno. The Republic wanted recognition and insisted that the Allies deal with it as a government. Indonesian reactions to the returning Dutch and to the arrival of British-Indian troops were wary and often hostile, and the evacuation of internees was accompanied by outbreaks of violence against Dutch, Eurasian, Chinese, and other civilians from October, a period often called Bersiap whose scale, organization, and interpretation remain debated.

The confrontation at Surabaya in late 1945 exposed the failure to reconcile Allied military responsibilities with Indonesian claims to sovereignty. Armed confrontation caused widespread civilian suffering and urban destruction. The battle became a powerful symbol of resistance in Indonesian public memory, and November 10 is commemorated as Heroes’ Day. Its importance here lies in the political consequences: the Republic’s existence was contested, Allied authorities exercised military power, and a peaceful transition had not been secured. Chapter 148 develops the broader history of the revolutionary war.

Contemporary diplomatic records, such as American reports published in the Foreign Relations of the United States, show how British and Dutch positions diverged: the British sought to extricate themselves and pressed the Dutch to negotiate, while the Dutch sought to reassert control.

The international controversy arose because the Allied surrender arrangements did not settle the question of Indonesian sovereignty. Allied military authorities were responsible for the consequences of Japanese defeat, including prisoner recovery, disarmament, and civilian protection. The Dutch government understood the transition as the opportunity to restore its prewar legal authority, whereas the Republic asserted that the Indonesian nation had established a new political order. British officials had to deal with these conflicting positions while carrying out military and humanitarian responsibilities. Cooperation with Dutch officials and practical dealings with republican authorities could occur simultaneously, even though their implications were contested.

The geographical distribution of Allied responsibilities also affected the political transition. Chapter 144 established the distinction between the formal surrender allocations and practical arrangements under which Australian forces administered significant parts of eastern Indonesia. Australian responsibility for Borneo and subsequent regional assignments did not themselves confer Dutch sovereignty or determine the legitimacy of republican claims. The reappearance of Dutch civil administration under Allied protection nevertheless placed considerable limits on the ability of republican officials to organize government in those regions.

The conflict also affected populations whose political objectives were not identical to those of the principal governments. Released prisoners, civilian internees, displaced families, and people associated with colonial institutions faced considerable insecurity. The Republic's claim to represent the nation imposed responsibilities for civilian protection that could not always be fulfilled in circumstances of fragmented authority. Attacks on civilians and retaliatory violence should be described in terms of documented responsibility, rather than attributed collectively to ethnic communities or all members of the independence movement. The disagreement over the term Bersiap reflects differing historical memories and the need to distinguish the experience of victims from generalized political accusations. Surabaya's enduring political significance should likewise be understood alongside its civilian consequences and the failure of an agreed transition, rather than through a detailed account of military operations.

Section 147.19

The October–November Political Reorganization and Sjahrir's Government

Within three months the Republic's governmental practice changed radically. Under the Constitution's transitional provisions, the KNIP was to assist the president. But pressure for a more representative body grew, from socialist and youth leaders who thought a presidency dominated by Sukarno resembled a Japanese-style authoritarian arrangement, and from the need to present a more democratic image to the Allies. On October 16, 1945, Vice President Hatta issued Vice-Presidential Edict No. X, which gave the KNIP legislative powers and a role in determining the broad outlines of state policy until the MPR and DPR could be formed, and authorized a working committee (Badan Pekerja) to act on its behalf. Sjahrir was chosen to lead the working body.

On November 3 the government issued a statement encouraging the formation of political parties, aimed at enabling Indonesians to channel political energy into legitimate organization before elections planned for early 1946 (which did not take place). Parties formed or revived in the following weeks and months, including the Muslim political party Masyumi in November, socialist and communist organizations, Christian and Catholic parties, and subsequently the reorganized PNI.

On November 14, 1945, a new cabinet was established under Sutan Sjahrir as prime minister, who also held foreign affairs and interior. This cabinet was accountable in parliamentary practice to the KNIP, whose working body conducted its continuing business, rather than solely to the president, a parliamentary arrangement. Sukarno remained president, and the constitutional text was not amended. The change was made by edict and practice, and its constitutional status was questioned by some at the time. Students must therefore distinguish carefully between constitutional text, which provided presidential government, and governmental practice, which from November 1945 was parliamentary.

Sjahrir's selection was strategic. He had stayed apart from Japanese-sponsored institutions, spoke fluent English and Dutch, had a reputation as a democratic socialist, and was acceptable to many Allied observers. His pamphlet Our Struggle (Perdjoeangan Kita), issued in late 1945, criticized collaboration with Japan and argued for a democratic republic. His government sought negotiation with the Dutch and aimed to demonstrate that the Republic was not a fascist creation. Many among the pemuda and others opposed negotiation, and the tension between diplomacy and armed struggle would shape 1946.

The October edict changed the institutional position of the KNIP in two related ways. It gave the committee legislative responsibilities before the creation of the permanent constitutional bodies and a role in determining the general direction of state policy. It also provided for a working body to conduct its continuing business. That working body was chosen from the committee and was responsible to it, a distinction relevant to the accountability of the new parliamentary arrangements. The edict was issued by Hatta in his capacity as vice president; it did not transform the appointed KNIP into an elected representative assembly.

The November announcement encouraging the formation of political parties addressed another problem: how the differing political currents of the revolutionary movement could participate in public life. Religious, nationalist, socialist, and communist organizations represented different priorities and constituencies, and an organized party system offered a possible framework for political competition. The announcement should not be equated with the immediate establishment of every party or with the holding of elections. Wartime political organizations were reorganized, prewar parties were revived, and new affiliations developed over subsequent months. Elections envisaged in the announcement did not take place on the proposed timetable.

Sjahrir's appointment introduced a change in cabinet responsibility rather than a new constitution. The original constitutional text vested governmental power in the president and provided for ministers appointed by him. Parliamentary practice made the prime minister and cabinet accountable to the provisional representative institution, while Sukarno and Hatta retained their offices. The official cabinet roster records Sjahrir's simultaneous responsibilities for foreign affairs and interior. This concentration of responsibilities reflected the importance of diplomacy and administration to a government seeking to demonstrate its capacity to govern.

The reorganization can be interpreted in both domestic and international terms. Domestically, it provided greater institutional space for political groups outside the presidency and responded to concerns about the concentration of authority under the transitional provisions. Internationally, it offered the Republic a leadership less closely associated with Japanese public institutions. Neither consideration eliminated disagreement about the appropriate relationship between civilian government, military organizations, and revolutionary activists. The new arrangements provided a mechanism for political negotiation within the Republic, but their provisional constitutional basis and the absence of an elected national legislature meant that their legitimacy remained a matter of practice and political agreement. That tension belongs to the subsequent institutional history as well as the founding moment.

Section 147.20

The Republic at the End of 1945: From Declaration to National Revolution

By the end of 1945 the Republic had achieved a good deal. It had asserted national sovereignty by a proclamation not authorized by Japan, adopted a constitution, elected a president and vice president, created ministries and provinces, formed a provisional national committee, built a voice in radio and press, established a nominal national army, seen the emergence of political parties, and, from November, adopted parliamentary practice with a government led by a respected socialist. In Java, and parts of Sumatra, it had real local support and some effective administration.

Much remained unresolved. The Republic's control was limited and uneven, and large parts of the archipelago were beyond its reach. Its military institutions were weak, and armed groups acted independently. Leaders disagreed on negotiation versus resistance, on the role of religion, on social reform, and on the relationship between the center and regions. Allied and Dutch forces had returned and held major ports and cities. No state had formally recognized the Republic.

International support took modest forms. In Australia, Indonesian seafarers and political exiles, together with Australian maritime unions, organized a boycott of Dutch shipping beginning in September 1945, in the movement often called the "Black Armada," documented in the Australian National University archives on maritime unions. On December 25, 1945, Sjahrir sent a message to U.S. President Harry S. Truman, preserved in the Foreign Relations of the United States, presenting the Republic's case and seeking American support; the United States did not recognize the Republic at the time. These efforts show the leadership's awareness that survival depended on international opinion.

The proclamation was therefore a decisive founding event rather than the completion of independence. It established a claim, an ideology, a leadership, and an institutional skeleton. Turning that claim into a sovereign state recognized by others took four more years and is the subject of Chapter 148.

The achievements of late 1945 can be evaluated by distinguishing the dimensions of state formation. The Republic had articulated a claim to sovereignty, created a constitutional order, established nationally recognizable political leadership, appointed regional officials, and initiated administrative and military institutions. These were concrete achievements even where their implementation remained incomplete. Its capacity to provide public services, enforce decisions, and represent the different communities of the archipelago was uneven, however, and the distribution of effective power remained contested. Independence was a historical reality in the sense of a proclaimed political order and an organized national movement; it was not yet an uncontested international settlement.

Sjahrir's Christmas Day communication to Truman provides a contemporary example of the relationship between institutional survival and diplomacy. In the letter preserved in the American diplomatic record, Sjahrir presented the Republic's origins as a response to the wishes of Indonesians, criticized the consequences of Allied cooperation with Dutch authorities, and appealed for American influence to help end the conflict. His argument connected political legitimacy to the practical problems of economic recovery and regional peace. The document is evidence of the Republic's diplomatic strategy, not independent confirmation of every assertion made by its prime minister. Nor does its preservation in American records establish American recognition of the Republic.

These developments also demonstrate why the origins of independence cannot be reduced to one explanatory factor. Long-term nationalist work supplied the concepts of nationhood and political leadership; the Japanese occupation supplied institutional experience under coercive conditions; the collapse of Japanese imperial authority opened an unusual opportunity; and the actions of local organizations and civilians gave the declaration practical significance. Without governmental institutions, that popular action could remain fragmented. Without local support, the decisions of the founding leaders could remain largely declaratory. Without a change in international circumstances, both would confront a colonial state capable of restricting their activities. Their interaction explains the emergence of the Republic, while the question of its survival leads directly into the next chapter. The history of the founding should therefore conclude not with an assumption of inevitable victory, but with the political and institutional problems that Indonesians still faced at the end of 1945.

Historical Debate

Was Indonesian Independence Achieved through Nationalist Leadership, Popular Revolution, or Imperial Collapse?

The question: What best explains the emergence of the Republic in 1945?

Nationalist leadership: Emphasizes the work of Sukarno, Hatta, Sjahrir, and others, the constitutional preparations, and the decision to proclaim. This view is supported by the documentary record and by older nationalist historiography, but can underplay popular actors.
Popular revolution: Emphasizes pemuda, workers, peasants, local committees, and women whose actions turned a declaration into a movement, as in the work of Benedict Anderson and Anthony Reid. This view stresses local agency but can understate the role of leaders and institutions.
Imperial collapse: Emphasizes Japan's defeat, the weakness of Dutch power in 1945, and the Allied decision to occupy only partially. This view explains the opportunity but not the response.

Evidence: Documentary records, memoirs, regional studies, and diplomatic archives. Most scholars combine the explanations, and the disagreement concerns relative weight and emphasis.

Why it matters: The answer shapes whether the Republic is remembered as the achievement of a leadership, of a people, or of circumstance, and anticipates questions in Chapter 148 about how the Republic survived.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Indonesian independence was an assertion of national sovereignty by Indonesian political actors, made possible but not determined by Japanese policy and defeat, and followed by the more difficult work of building a legitimate and effective state. The proclamation of August 17, 1945 was the founding act, but the state it announced was constructed in the months that followed.

Indonesian nationalism predated the occupation. Organizations from Budi Utomo and Sarekat Islam to the PNI and the Youth Pledge of 1928 had defined the idea of Indonesia, and Dutch repression had scattered but not eliminated its leaders. The Japanese occupation changed capabilities: it gave nationalists mass organization, administrative experience, military training, and public platforms through Putera, Jawa Hokokai, PETA, and expanded Indonesian officialdom, while imposing heavy burdens on the population.

Sukarno and Hatta became the principal founders. Sukarno brought oratory, symbolism, and mass appeal; Hatta brought organizational discipline, representative-institution thinking, and a capacity for compromise. They worked together but were not politically identical, and their different emphases shaped the founding. Sjahrir and Subardjo represented alternative strategies and connections.

Japan's announcement of future independence in September 1944 led to the BPUPK, inaugurated May 28, 1945, whose substantive sessions ran May 29–June 1 and July 10–17. Participants used this occupation-sponsored body to develop their own ideas. Sukarno's June 1 speech presented Pancasila, the Committee of Nine produced the Jakarta Charter on June 22, and the second session debated territory, the republican form, and a draft constitution.

The PPKI, formed in August, was a different body with a different function: to adopt a constitution and create initial institutions. The surrender of August 15 turned a managed Japanese timetable into a crisis of strategy. Sukarno and Hatta weighed caution and institutional framing; the pemuda and Sjahrir's circle pressed for an immediate and independent proclamation. The Rengasdengklok incident on August 16 was a quarrel over timing, legitimacy, and leadership, not over independence itself.

The proclamation was drafted at Admiral Maeda's house, whose role was to provide facilities and shelter, not authorization. The handwritten and typed versions differ, notably in the signatory formula, and both used the imperial year '05. It was read on August 17 at Pegangsaan Timur 56, without effective control of the state apparatus behind it.

On August 18 the PPKI adopted the original 1945 Constitution, elected Sukarno and Hatta, and, in a rapid consultation, replaced the Jakarta Charter's seven words with belief in the One Almighty God. The compromise preserved unity at a critical moment, though historians differ about its details and Islamic leaders did not regard the issue as settled. The Constitution's transitional provisions concentrated power in the president assisted by a national committee, since the MPR and DPR did not yet exist.

The Republic's institutions were created in stages: provinces and governors on August 19, the KNIP and BKR decided on August 22, the presidential cabinet from September 2. News spread through radio, newspapers, and the Ikada rally. In Java, local committees, officials, youth, workers, and traditional rulers such as the Yogyakarta sultan and Paku Alam VIII created authority from below; in Sumatra, Sulawesi, Borneo, Bali, and the eastern islands, outcomes varied with geography, occupation zone, and local politics.

Different groups understood merdeka differently: peasants sought relief and land, workers sought control and wages, women organized and mobilized in many roles, and officials sought order. These expectations produced social tensions that Chapter 148 will examine.

From late September British-Indian forces and Dutch officials returned, the TKR was created on October 5, Surabaya became the first great confrontation, and in October–November the Republic moved to parliamentary practice under Sjahrir without amending its Constitution. By December it lacked recognition and full control but had a constitution, government, leadership, and international appeals. The proclamation was a decisive beginning, not a completion.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how a state begins. The founding of Indonesia combined an inherited idea of nation, wartime capacities, a short window of opportunity, and a set of choices made under extreme pressure. It shows that a declaration, a constitution, and a government are different things, and that a new state's legitimacy has to be built and defended after its proclamation, not merely announced.

The chapter also reveals recurring tensions that outlived 1945: between leaders and youth, between constitutional text and political practice, between unity and diversity, between Java and the outer islands, and between religion and the state. The Pancasila compromise, the transitional concentration of power in the presidency, the appointed KNIP, and the readiness to change governmental practice without amending the text all shaped Indonesian politics for decades, from the revolution of Chapter 148 through the constitutional struggles of Chapter 149 and later.

For the wider textbook, the Indonesian case is a clear example of Southeast Asian societies acting as historical agents. Indonesians used institutions created by an occupier to pursue their own ends, then acted against that occupier's wishes. Chapter 148 takes up the next question: how a Republic with limited control and no recognition survived the return of colonial power, and what it cost.
