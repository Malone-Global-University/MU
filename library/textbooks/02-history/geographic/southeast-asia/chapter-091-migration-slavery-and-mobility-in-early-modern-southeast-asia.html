Migration, Slavery, and Mobility in Early Modern Southeast Asia

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Between about 1500 and 1750, people moved across Southeast Asia in extraordinary numbers and by very different means: merchants following the monsoon, soldiers hired or captured, families resettled by kings, refugees fleeing lost wars, and men, women, and children sold, seized, or pledged for debt. They sometimes shared the same ships and roads, but they did not share the same freedom.

In 1621 a Dutch force under Jan Pieterszoon Coen conquered the Banda Islands, the overwhelmingly predominant source of traded nutmeg and mace in this period. Most of the Bandanese population was killed, died, or fled, and a number of survivors were shipped to Batavia as captives. The Company then divided the nutmeg groves into plantation lots and set enslaved workers brought from elsewhere to work them. A few decades later, after the fall of Gowa in South Sulawesi, Makassarese and Bugis fighters, merchants, and exiled families left their homeland and settled in Java, Sumatra, Borneo, the Malay Peninsula, and even Siam. In Ayutthaya, Japanese and Chinese merchants lived in assigned quarters beside Persians, Portuguese, Malays, and Dutch, while the king drew his armies from commoners whose labor was owed to the crown and from captives taken in war.

These episodes belong to a single historical landscape. Early modern Southeast Asia was a region in which population was a form of wealth. Land was abundant in many areas, and rulers who commanded people could grow rice, build temples and fortifications, man ships and armies, and carry on trade. The competition for people therefore shaped warfare, diplomacy, law, and commerce as strongly as the competition for territory or commodities. Chapters 78 through 90 followed kingdoms, wars, and colonial enterprises one by one. This chapter reverses the angle of view and follows the people who moved among them.

The central distinction of the chapter is between movement and freedom. A Hokkien merchant at Hội An, a Wajorese trader at Palembang, a Siamese commoner carried off to Burma, and an enslaved woman sold at Batavia all crossed distances, but their choices, legal protections, and prospects differed enormously. Voluntary and compelled movement often overlapped: a family might flee famine, taxation, or an abusive patron and still be said to have "chosen" to move. This chapter therefore treats slavery, debt bondage, corvée, military service, and forced relocation as distinct institutions that must be defined carefully rather than collapsed into one category.

The chapter also insists that migrants and displaced people were historical actors. Merchants negotiated privileges, soldiers bargained for land and rank, women organized households and trade, and enslaved people fled, petitioned, purchased freedom where law allowed, and built families under severe constraint. Their agency operated within very unequal limits, and the chapter will not suggest that new cultures or commercial opportunities compensated for violence and dispossession.

The evidence is uneven. Court chronicles record the capture of populations in the language of royal triumph; Dutch East India Company (VOC) registers count enslaved people in ports; law codes, debt contracts, inscriptions, and traveler accounts add fragments. Ordinary migrants, women, children, and the enslaved appear mainly through the records of those who governed, taxed, or bought them. Section 91.4 explains how historians read these sources with caution.

The chapter draws on foundational comparative scholarship, including the essays collected by Anthony Reid and Jennifer Brewster on slavery, bondage, and dependency; Kerry Ward's study of VOC forced migration; Barbara Watson Andaya's work on women and households; and detailed studies of South Sulawesi and the Wajorese diaspora. It does not retell the military history of Banda (Chapter 78), the institutions of Batavia (Chapter 79), the Makassar War (Chapter 82), the politics of Johor-Riau (Chapter 81), or the Nguyễn frontier (Chapter 88). It compares their human dimensions and prepares the regional synthesis of Chapter 92.

Historical Context

The sixteenth and seventeenth centuries are often called an age of commerce in Southeast Asia. Pepper, cloves, nutmeg, rice, tin, deerskins, sappanwood, and Chinese and Indian manufactures moved through ports from Pegu and Ayutthaya to Melaka, Banten, Makassar, Manila, and Hội An. Chapters 67 through 92 trace the political and commercial responses: the Portuguese seizure of Melaka in 1511 (Chapter 69), the rise of Aceh, Johor, Makassar, and Mataram, the creation of the VOC (Chapter 77), and the formation of large mainland states under Toungoo Burma, Ayutthaya, the Nguyễn and Trịnh lords, and Lan Xang.

This commercial and political expansion increased demand for labor. Many societies had relatively low population density compared with land, so rulers, nobles, and merchants competed to attract, capture, or retain followers. Scholars have long argued that control over dependents was a principal measure of status. People entered dependency through birth, debt, capture, punishment, sale in time of hardship, or voluntary patronage, and their position could improve or deteriorate over a lifetime.

Warfare and political change repeatedly displaced populations. Burmese and Siamese armies carried off captives; the fall of Gowa dispersed South Sulawesi communities; Ming-Qing turmoil sent Chinese merchants and soldiers southward; the VOC deported exiles and imported laborers. At the same time, commerce drew voluntary migrants, who formed durable communities in ports. This chapter separates these streams while showing how often they intersected. Mainland and maritime states differed in their institutions, but both depended on people who could be moved, counted, and put to work.

Yet the mechanisms controlling people did not develop uniformly. Ayutthaya linked many subjects to recognized service patrons; Burmese rulers supported hereditary service communities; the Nguyễn used military-administrative settlements along contested frontiers. In island markets people might circulate through household slavery, shipboard work, commercial apprenticeship, or the networks of regional rulers. The VOC overlaid some of these arrangements with a corporate system of shipping, punishment, plantation production, and forced exile. A comparative history must ask how obligations were established and enforced in each setting rather than treating this variety as one empire-wide institution.

The region's social geography was equally uneven. Port quarters could contain wealthy foreign merchants beside poor sailors, enslaved servants, and migrant women whose work kept markets operating. Settled agricultural regions were dependent on family households, water access, and seasonal labor. Rivers and sea lanes connected these environments, but a person's ability to use the network depended on patronage, legal status, money, and the presence of kin. This chapter uses the same corridors to compare sharply different experiences and keeps the distinction between voluntary settlement, constrained choice, and forcible transfer central throughout.

Research into migration and bondage is limited by the sources that remain. Official chronicles glorify victory and may exaggerate the number of captives; company records list property, workers, and departures according to administrative categories; court laws express rules that may not have been obeyed. Family histories, inscriptions, archaeology, and surviving customary law can complicate the official picture. The chapter therefore treats numerical claims cautiously, identifies distinctions in legal status, and uses scholars' interpretations as explanations to examine rather than as substitutes for contemporary evidence.

Division I — A Region in Motion: Geography, Migration, and Human Status
Division I · Section 91.1

Southeast Asia as a World of Movement, c. 1500–1750

Movement was not an exceptional event in early modern Southeast Asia; it was a routine condition of political and economic life. Fishing and trading communities worked the coasts and straits, rice-growing households shifted along river valleys, upland groups crossed mountain corridors, and rulers moved populations to new capitals or frontiers. Commercial growth after 1500 intensified these patterns. Port cities such as Melaka before 1511, then Aceh, Banten, Makassar, Ayutthaya, Manila, and Batavia, drew merchants, craftsmen, sailors, and servants from across Asia.

The principal moving populations were varied. Traders and shipowners traveled seasonally or settled. Soldiers served kings, companies, and local lords. Farming households were resettled by royal order or left voluntarily when taxes, warfare, or hunger made home untenable. Court dependents, artisans, and entertainers were transferred as gifts or spoils. Religious teachers carried texts and ordination lineages. Refugees fled defeated courts. Enslaved people were captured, purchased, and sold, sometimes across very long distances.

Mobility did not uniformly increase liberty. A growing commercial economy created opportunities for merchants and skilled specialists, but it also raised demand for rowers, porters, domestic servants, plantation workers, and soldiers. Historians have argued that expanding trade in some regions increased the market for captives and dependent labor; others stress that the effects varied by place and period (see the Historical Debate in Section 91.20). The chapter therefore asks of every movement: who decided, who benefited, and who bore the costs?

The section also warns against two simplifications. One treats Southeast Asian societies as passive recipients of foreign merchants and companies. In fact local rulers frequently invited, taxed, protected, or regulated migrants, and sometimes expelled them. The other treats movement as a modern phenomenon. The period 1500–1750 already contained long-distance forced migration, multiethnic ports, and diasporas whose institutions persisted for generations.

It is important to distinguish a journey from a migration. Sailors working a monsoon circuit could spend months in a port and then return to a home settlement; shipowners might maintain agents in several places without leaving their principal households. Other travelers settled permanently, established marriage ties, or sought protection from a new ruler. A refugee might initially hope to return but eventually raise children in a new community. The historical question is not simply whether somebody crossed water or a mountain pass, but how a new relationship to residence, authority, and labor developed. Those relationships could be transformed more than once within a person's life.

Regional governments helped shape those choices. A port ruler who reduced customs duties or promised security could attract traders and craftsmen; a ruler who required service might encourage subjects to flee. Courts welcomed specialists able to cast cannon, build vessels, produce textiles, or conduct diplomacy, although their status ranged from rewarded advisers to coerced dependents. Monasteries and mosques also received travelers and facilitated introductions. These institutions made mobility possible without implying that every individual could travel freely. People without wealth, protectors, documents, or a recognized community faced different risks from established commercial families.

Movement connected societies that were not under the same sovereign. A Bugis navigator could serve one court, trade under another ruler's protection, and use kin in a third port to obtain credit. By contrast, a person sold through a VOC market might be transferred between settlements by an owner whose legal and economic power followed them. Both routes linked distant places, but their institutional foundations were opposite. This chapter uses repeated comparisons of agency, legal standing, destination, work, and possibility of return to avoid turning the word mobility into an empty generalization.

Commercial growth also altered existing patterns rather than creating movement from nothing. Island and mainland communities had exchanged products and married across settlements for centuries. The more decisive early modern changes included new concentrations of demand, greater competition among states for labor and maritime expertise, and companies that organized transfers over unprecedented administrative distances. The same century might show voluntary merchants moving toward a profitable port and conquered populations being removed from the very territories in which that profitable commodity originated.

Division I · Section 91.2

Seas, Rivers, Mountain Passes, and the Geography of Mobility

Maritime routes ran through the Strait of Malacca, the Sunda Strait, the Java Sea, the Makassar Strait, the routes among the Maluku Islands, and the South China Sea. The monsoon system, examined in Chapter 4, shaped timing: ships generally waited for seasonal winds, which produced long stays in ports and encouraged merchants to marry, rent houses, and form lasting ties. Transshipment ports such as Melaka, Banten, Makassar, and Batavia gathered goods and people from several wind systems.

On the mainland, the Irrawaddy, Chao Phraya, Mekong, and Red River carried boats, armies, and migrants, while mountain passes and portages linked basins. Routes between Burma and Siam crossed difficult ranges; others joined Lan Xang and Cambodia along the Mekong, and Vietnamese settlers moved along the coast and into delta lands. Rapids, seasonal floods, and political frontiers shaped what could be moved and by whom.

Political boundaries often did not coincide with social networks. A Bugis settlement in Borneo or Sumatra could remain tied by kinship and law to Wajo; a Chinese merchant at Ayutthaya could depend on kin at Zhangzhou or Nagasaki; upland communities might trade with several lowland courts. Historians therefore trace corridors and nodes rather than the borders of modern states.

The difference between maritime and overland routes mattered to those being moved. A trader preparing a voyage might choose a shipping season, assess the available cargo space, and negotiate freight. Captives moved by ship depended on transport arranged by those controlling them; long journeys could separate families and undermine the possibility of escape or return. Overland travel could offer opportunities to seek refuge, but mountains and forests also exposed travelers to disease, hunger, and danger. Neither environment was uniformly more open. Its possibilities varied with local allies, military patrols, river stages, winds, and access to boats.

Ports were not simply endpoints. Melaka connected Indian Ocean arrivals with vessels bound for island Southeast Asia and the South China Sea. Makassar functioned as an eastern redistribution center before its political position was transformed by war. Batavia served both as a collecting port for commercial goods and a hub through which the VOC redirected prisoners, enslaved people, and servants to other possessions. Smaller ports and river landings were equally important to local families: they provided the practical links between plantation fields, inland villages, and ships. A map showing only great harbors would conceal much of the movement that made those harbors function.

River corridors require similar attention to direction and difficulty. Navigable stretches of the Chao Phraya facilitated access to Ayutthaya's court; the lower Mekong opened toward Cambodian and Vietnamese markets but contained seasonal obstacles farther upstream. Portage routes joined adjoining basins, and upland communities often possessed essential knowledge of paths and trading seasons. Many journeys thus combined several modes of transport: a caravan to a riverside town, a boat to a delta harbor, and a larger ship for the sea crossing. Control over a landing place or portage could translate into authority over people and fees, even without control of an entire geographic region.

Geographical scale must also be matched to sources. Shipping lists may record a vessel departing Batavia without showing its passengers' ultimate destinations; a royal chronicle may describe captives brought to a capital without locating their assigned villages. The routes drawn in a student atlas should therefore be labeled as documented corridors, likely connections, or illustrative reconstructions. The distinction matters particularly where the surviving evidence represents commercial elites far better than poor migrants, women traveling in family groups, or people transported against their will.

Division I · Section 91.3

Freedom, Dependence, Slavery, and the Meaning of Migration

Describing early modern movement requires precise categories. Even so, these categories were not fixed, and different societies drew the boundaries differently. A person's status could change during a lifetime: a war captive might become a household servant, be married into a family, and acquire a different legal position for her children.

These definitions require care because the legal categories of modern states do not translate perfectly into earlier Southeast Asian law. The designation of a person as a dependent might refer to an obligation of labor, a household affiliation, a debt, a birth status, or a relationship created by warfare. Some dependents were entitled to retain possessions or earn outside income; others were subject to sale, corporal punishment, or transfer without consent. Where a law recognized release upon repayment, the debtor still needed sufficient resources and an authority willing to enforce the rule. The existence of a formal exit does not by itself demonstrate that exit was accessible.

Comparative analysis should attend to five concrete questions. Could a person leave an employer or owner? Could the obligation be inherited by children? Could the person be sold independently of land or household? Who could hear a complaint? What happened if the dependent escaped? Answers reveal differences that the English term slave may obscure. They also prevent the opposite error of minimizing coercion by placing people under a less severe-sounding label such as servant or client. Administrative categories describe claims of authority, not necessarily the full reality of work, family, punishment, or resistance.

Nor was freedom a simple two-part status. An established merchant might possess considerable latitude to travel yet owe duties to a patron or sovereign. A registered commoner might have a recognized household and farming rights but remain liable for corvée and mobilization. An enslaved skilled worker might negotiate responsibilities inside a workshop while remaining legally owned. Gender, age, wealth, religion, and the capacity to obtain community protection shaped the practical meaning of every category. The spectrum of constraint is useful only if it does not conceal the fundamental distinction between those who could refuse a transfer and those who could not.

The chapter accordingly uses the term voluntary comparatively rather than absolutely. Movement in response to a failing harvest differed from the direct seizure of a household by soldiers, even if both situations involved restricted choices. The historian's task is to identify the institution that produced the movement and the person's capacity to accept, refuse, negotiate, or reverse it. This makes it possible to compare forced migration under Asian rulers with VOC transfers without presuming either that they were identical or that the institutions of one society were inherently less coercive.

Historical Vocabulary
Migration, Slavery, Debt Bondage, Corvée, and Captivity

Definition. Migration here means a durable change of residence, whether chosen, induced, or compelled. A war captive is a person seized in conflict, often to be resettled or put to work. Chattel slavery means a status in which a person could be bought, sold, and inherited as property. Debt bondage means labor service pledged to a creditor, in some societies in lieu of interest and in some inherited. Corvée means labor owed to a ruler or lord as a tax or service obligation, normally for limited periods and without sale of the person.

Historical context. Southeast Asian vocabularies for dependence often differed from European usage, and European observers sometimes labeled all dependents as slaves. Comparative scholarship stresses that the lived conditions of people in these categories overlapped but were not identical.

Why it matters. The categories determine what rights a person had, whether children inherited status, and whether redemption was possible.

Potential misunderstanding. The presence of legal protections or paths to manumission does not mean enslavement was benign or voluntary. Equally, imposing a single definition of slavery on every society erases real institutional differences.

Apparently voluntary movement also needs scrutiny. A family might leave a district because of famine, taxation, warfare, loss of land, or an oppressive patron. Such migrants were choosing among bad options, yet their movement differed from forced relocation. Conversely, a soldier recruited for pay, a captive, and a conscripted villager might march in the same column with very different legal positions. The chapter uses a spectrum, from free migration through constrained or induced movement to captivity and sale, and notes at each stage who held the power to decide.

Division I · Section 91.4

Reconstructing Movement: Chronicles, Shipping Records, Laws, and Personal Histories

Historians draw on several kinds of source, each with limits. Royal chronicles in Burmese, Thai, Vietnamese, Malay, Javanese, and Bugis traditions record the capture and resettlement of populations, usually as evidence of a ruler's power; they seldom report the experiences of the moved and may exaggerate numbers. Law codes and royal edicts describe categories of dependents and obligations, but laws state norms rather than practice. Debt contracts, court proceedings, and inscriptions show how obligations were created and enforced in particular cases.

The VOC produced extensive registers, resolutions, and correspondence, including records of slave purchases, arrivals, burghers, and Chinese residents. These are rich but written by a company that classified people for its own purposes. Genealogies, religious records, travelers' accounts, and mission reports add further perspectives, and archaeology contributes evidence of foreign quarters and settlements.

Several problems recur. Records are incomplete, and recorded arrivals do not equal the number of migrants, since many moved without registration or left no trace. Ethnic labels are inconsistent: "Bugis" or "Chinese" might cover several communities, and "Malay" could mean a language, a culture, or a Muslim trader. Personal names change with conversion or enslavement. Women, children, and enslaved people are underrepresented, often appearing only as counted units. For these reasons this chapter avoids unsupported totals and, where numbers are cited, identifies them as estimates or later reports.

Company documents can be exceptionally detailed and still misleading if read as complete censuses. A shipping record might identify the number of transported enslaved people at departure, another document the number delivered, and a third the number assigned to a plantation. These figures answer different questions; losses, transfers, omissions, and classification changes must be investigated before calculating a population total. The VOC's categories of origin could also combine a port of purchase with an asserted ethnic identity. A person described as coming from one trading town may have been seized or born somewhere else. Historians must distinguish a registration label from an independently established biography.

Comparative studies help reveal what any single archive hides. Malay and Bugis legal traditions describe commercial duties and the authority of migrant leaders; European trading letters often view such people through taxes and contracts. Missionary sources illuminate conversion and family networks but can frame local religions as obstacles. Royal chronicles highlight dynastic achievements, while Buddhist or Islamic writings may emphasize exemplary piety. Material remains, language practices, and later community memories provide different evidence again. Disagreement between records is not merely an obstacle: it can show the priorities of competing institutions.

Writing women's and children's history requires particular caution. A shipping manifest recording a dependent woman by age and price is evidence of an administrative practice, not an account of her hopes, relationships, or intentions. Marriage records may be more informative about legally recognized unions than about the many households that left none. Researchers can reconstruct patterns of law, labor, and residence, but should not invent a person's inner thoughts to make a scene vivid. When the original testimony is missing, a classroom perspective exercise must be labeled as an analytical reconstruction rather than passed off as an authentic voice.

Numbers are especially susceptible to misleading comparison. A chronicle's description of thousands carried away after a siege cannot be weighed as though it were identical to a company register with names and transport dates. Even company records can overlook illicit trade and informal migration. Where numbers are uncertain, the chapter emphasizes mechanisms and securely dated events; where quantified evidence is available, it identifies the population counted, the document's purpose, and the scope of what can be inferred. This evidentiary discipline is particularly important for the 1621 Banda conquest, the 1679 Chinese refugee migration, and the killings of Chinese residents at Batavia in 1740.

Division II — Coercion, Captivity, and Systems of Labor
Division II · Section 91.5

Warfare, Captivity, and the Forced Relocation of Populations

Warfare was one of the most important causes of involuntary movement. Burmese kings under Toungoo and later rulers brought people from conquered districts to the royal heartland, and Siamese kings carried off populations from Cambodia, Laos, and Burmese borderlands. The capture of Ayutthaya in 1569 under Bayinnaung resulted in the removal of members of the royal family and others to Burma (Chapter 84). Vietnamese lords likewise took captives in frontier campaigns, and Cambodian and Lao rulers participated in this competition for people.

Rulers sought control of people as well as territory because captives could supply agricultural labor, soldiers, servants, artisans, and the population of new settlements. Skilled workers such as weavers, potters, metalworkers, and entertainers were often valued highly. Military captives might be integrated into armies as units; civilian households could be settled in villages assigned to their captors. Women and children faced distinct risks, including separation from relatives and sexual coercion, discussed in Section 91.16.

The experience of forced movement differed. Elite captives might be held hostage or married into the host court; skilled workers might receive land and exemptions; ordinary households might be placed under overseers. The chronicles' totals for captives are generally unreliable, and this chapter does not offer migration figures that the sources cannot support.

Captivity was linked to the structure of lowland political economies. A ruler could seize a capital and obtain temporary submission, but controlling cultivators, boatmen, metalworkers, and military households furnished a more durable source of resources. Captives were therefore sometimes distributed among nobles, settled near a royal city, assigned to agricultural communities, or incorporated into military service groups. The exact distribution depended on the campaign and the captives' status. Elite hostages might help secure a political settlement, while common households performed work with little say in their destination. These different uses of captives should not be collapsed into a single model of conquest.

States also moved populations as a means of securing territory. Relocating cultivators into an exposed district could make a garrison sustainable and reduce an opponent's ability to recruit. Conversely, removing residents from a frontier could deprive a rival court of revenue and military support. Such policies could be described in chronicles as the gathering of people under righteous rule even when the experience involved forced marches, separation, and loss of homes. Some captives were settled in identifiable ethnic or occupational groups; others became difficult to trace as generations intermarried and court labels changed. Cultural continuity and involuntary displacement could exist simultaneously.

The movement of artisans reveals another dimension. A skilled person capable of producing firearms, constructing boats, weaving valued textiles, or performing court rituals might receive privileges that another captive lacked. Yet a higher position in the host court did not automatically mean the original relocation had been voluntary. Expertise could improve a person's bargaining position without removing dependence. Comparing a captured royal relative, a skilled craft worker, and a farming family allows students to see how gender, social rank, and occupation shaped the effects of warfare. The contrast also explains why chronicles often name prominent captives while leaving ordinary people anonymous.

Across mainland Asia, war could create secondary migration long after a formal peace. Dependents who escaped a new overlord might settle near another border; a court could reward soldiers with households or land; families displaced by taxes and uncertainty might move independently. These developments blur any easy division between a single episode of capture and later voluntary resettlement. They also remind us that the consequences extended to communities left behind, where fields, irrigation systems, and local institutions might lack the people required to maintain them. The historical measure of conquest thus includes the reorganization of human communities, not merely a change of dynastic sovereignty.

Cause and Effect

Warfare, Capture, Displacement, and Labor Demand

Long-term causes

Relatively abundant land and limited population made labor and followers key sources of power; rulers and nobles competed for dependents.

Short-term causes

Dynastic competition, tribute disputes, and campaigns against rival centers such as Ayutthaya, Cambodia, and Lan Xang.

Military victory or siege, after which commanders selected captives for removal.

Historical complexity: Capture was not an automatic result of conquest. Rulers could instead demand tribute, install clients, or negotiate. Chronicle claims of triumph may overstate numbers, and the same source rarely reveals what captives experienced.

Division II · Section 91.6

Slavery, Debt Bondage, Patronage, and Household Dependence

Across Southeast Asia, dependency took many forms. Debt bondage was widespread: a person or family pledged labor to a creditor, sometimes with the debtor's service counted as interest, sometimes as repayment. People entered bondage because of loans taken for funerals, bride-price, crop failure, or legal fines, and obligations could be inherited. Hereditary slavery, the sale and transfer of enslaved people, and domestic service coexisted with patron-client relationships in which followers owed loyalty and labor in return for protection.

Legal rules often distinguished these statuses, for example by whether a person could be sold, whether children inherited status, and how obligations might be discharged. In practice the distinctions could be fragile. A debtor who could not repay might be transferred to another master; a redemption price might rise; a dependent without kin protection had little recourse. Legal redemption did not always mean practical freedom.

Enslaved and bonded people worked in households, royal establishments, workshops, ports, boats, and fields. Many served as domestic servants, concubines, or retainers; others performed agricultural labor, mining, porterage, or craft production. Status could also carry social value for owners, since large retinues signified rank.

Debt bondage requires attention to the economy of households. Borrowing might help finance cultivation, meet a court obligation, or address a family emergency. If repayment failed, a debtor's labor—or that of relatives—could become the creditor's security. The resulting work relationship might include food and residence supplied by the patron, yet also involve restrictions on movement and the risk that the obligation would be transferred. Some traditions distinguished debts that could be redeemed from inherited slave status; other rules blurred them. The distinction mattered especially when a dependent sought release or when children reached adulthood and the question arose whether they inherited an obligation.

Household dependence was not confined to plantations or fields. Urban elites employed servants, porters, cooks, artisans, attendants, and people responsible for transporting goods. A large retinue could advertise a patron's influence, just as military followers demonstrated a ruler's power. These circumstances make it misleading to measure a society's dependence on unfree labor solely through the number of plantation workers. At the same time, the prominence of household slavery should not lead readers to describe it as harmless. People serving inside elite residences could experience confinement, transfer, punishment, forced intimacy, and separation from their families.

Legal distinctions also affect how historians compare VOC slavery with arrangements under Asian courts. The company recorded many enslaved people as movable property and circulated them through markets spanning the Indian Ocean. Elsewhere status and obligation could be expressed through allegiance to a patron, debt repayment, or inherited service to a ruler. The economic uses and human consequences sometimes overlapped, but their legal organization differed. Anthony Reid and Jennifer Brewster's comparative collection remains valuable precisely because it resists assuming that a single European category can describe every regional institution. Students should ask who possessed an enforceable claim over a dependent and what could make that claim end.

Release, when attainable, did not guarantee social equality. A formerly enslaved person might need a new employer, residence, patron, or official recognition to protect freedom. An individual freed from a debt might still owe customary service to a local ruler. Kinship and marriage offered networks of support but could also reproduce dependence. The appropriate interpretation is neither that status was permanent for everyone nor that the possibility of mobility within a hierarchy diminished its coercion. Historical analysis must weigh the formal law, the actual available options, and the testimony—or silence—of those subject to the institution.

Historical Vocabulary
Manumission, Redemption, Patronage, and Hereditary Slavery

Definition. Manumission is the formal release of an enslaved person by an owner; redemption is repayment of a debt or purchase price by which a bonded person recovers freedom; patronage is a relationship of mutual obligation between a more powerful person and followers; hereditary slavery means slave status passed to children.

Historical context. Societies differed in which of these were recognized, who could grant them, and whether religious or customary law encouraged release, including as a pious act.

Why it matters. The presence of redemption or manumission shows legal pathways, but those pathways were often narrow and dependent on owners' consent or on resources dependents lacked.

Potential misunderstanding. Patronage was not always benign or voluntary, and it was not the same as enslavement; the lines between them could blur.

Division II · Section 91.7

Corvée, Military Service, and the Competition for Manpower

Mainland kingdoms organized labor through service obligations. In Ayutthaya, commoners known as phrai were registered under officials and nobles and owed periods of labor and military service to the crown or to their lords. In Toungoo and later Burma, service groups known as ahmudan owed hereditary service (military, craft, or other) to the crown or to lords, while athi households paid taxes. In the Lao lands, mueang (principalities) organized obligations through local rulers, and in Cambodia dependent labor supported royal and temple establishments. In the Nguyễn lands, military-agricultural settlements combined garrison duty and farming.

These obligations differed from slavery. Corvée was a tax paid in labor, normally limited and attached to a status rather than a sale; service to a royal household differed from obligations to a local patron; and permanent enslavement meant property in a person. But burdens could be heavy, and people sometimes sold themselves or fell into debt to escape them.

Rulers valued population over acreage because people supplied armies, rice, and construction. Evasion was therefore common: people fled to forests or to rival jurisdictions, became dependents of exempt lords or monasteries, or negotiated exemptions. When communities moved beyond a ruler's reach, kings lost labor and revenue, and registration, tattooing, and census efforts were attempts to prevent this. The history of state power thus included a continuing struggle over who counted as whose people.

The Siamese phrai system illustrates the difference between personal dependence and a simple tax paid in money. Registered commoners were attached to officials or the crown, and the service demanded of them was mobilized through those relationships. Royal labor might be used for building, provisioning, or campaigns, while obligations to a particular patron created competing claims on the same household's time. Regulations varied across reigns, and later nineteenth-century forms should not automatically be projected back onto seventeenth-century Ayutthaya. The institution nevertheless demonstrates how a state lacking a large permanent salaried workforce could draw on households distributed across its territory.

Burmese service categories provide a useful contrast. Hereditary ahmudan groups might furnish particular military or technical services, while households outside such groups were subjected to other fiscal obligations. The distinction was not equivalent to the division between slave and free in modern legal language. Status depended on registration, service assignment, and relationships to royal authorities. Comparing Burmese service communities with Siamese commoners and Lao mueang obligations shows that rulers confronted similar manpower problems but devised different institutional answers. It would be inaccurate to assume that each kingdom operated an interchangeable corvée system.

Population control also generated avoidance and negotiation. Households might seek service under a monastery, elite patron, or distant local ruler to obtain more favorable obligations. Frontier mobility allowed some people to evade registration, while for others the dangers of travel or loss of land made departure impractical. Officials might respond by revising registers, enforcing returns, or granting exemptions to valued specialists. Tattooing and increasingly systematic registration are associated with later state-building reforms in some polities; the chapter should not present their fully developed later forms as uniform seventeenth-century practice.

These manpower systems influenced military capacity. A mobilized army depended not only on weapons and generals but on village harvest cycles, transport animals, supply workers, and the cooperation of local lords. A large campaign could remove cultivators from fields and thereby reduce the food available to sustain subsequent campaigns. Rulers often needed to bargain over mobilization even when law described service as compulsory. The resulting tension between royal command and local household survival helps explain why migration, desertion, patronage, and forced recruitment remained recurring features of mainland politics.

Division II · Section 91.8

The VOC Slave Trade, Batavia, and the Banda Plantation System

Principal research: Miske (2026); Ward (2009).

The VOC became a major participant in the capture, purchase, transport, and deployment of enslaved labor in maritime Asia. It acquired people from South Asia, Southeast Asian ports, eastern Indonesia, and beyond, and moved them among its settlements, including Batavia, Ambon, Banda, Ceylon, and the Cape. Kerry Ward's research on VOC forced migration shows that this network also transported convicts and political exiles. Among them was Shaykh Yusuf of Makassar, exiled first to Ceylon and later to the Cape, where his presence is remembered in the history of the Cape's Muslim community.

The conquest of the Banda Islands in 1621 is the central case. The VOC sought a monopoly on nutmeg and mace, which grew only on Banda. After Coen's expedition, many Bandanese were killed or died, others fled to nearby islands, and a number of survivors were shipped as captives to Batavia. Dutch troops, with Japanese mercenaries, executed a group of Bandanese leaders (orangkaya). Estimates of Banda's pre-conquest population are uncertain, and the chapter does not rely on a single figure; Chapter 78 examines the conquest itself.

The Company then divided the groves into perken, plantation lots granted to Company servants and settlers known as perkeniers, who were obliged to sell nutmeg to the VOC at set prices. The perkeniers depended on enslaved workers brought from elsewhere. Recent archival research by S. J. Miske, which examines company records on plantation organization, labor, and ecology, cautions against the older assumption that the plantation system was immediately and fully successful. Labor shortages, mortality, and administrative problems shaped how the system actually operated.

Banda connected with Batavia and wider VOC networks. Batavia drew migrants from many places, and enslaved people formed a very large share of its inhabitants, with some estimates suggesting at times a majority. They worked as domestic servants, in workshops, in construction, on ships, and in the sugar economy of the hinterland. Imperial commerce therefore required moving labor, not merely commodities. Chapter 79 examines Batavian institutions; here the emphasis is on the dependence of commercial empire on coerced and migrant labor.

Miske's recent reconstruction makes it possible to examine the plantation system at a more precise institutional scale. The VOC began allocating nutmeg land on Pulau Ai in 1621; a May 5 act recorded an intention to lease plots to settlers, and related correspondence shows that the early distribution also involved Mardijkers, formerly enslaved Christians. The division of Banda Besar proceeded more slowly, and a system later represented as thoroughly ordered took years to establish. The term perkenier therefore designates a position within a developing arrangement, not a uniform class of secure and prosperous plantation owners from the moment of conquest. The company's objectives and the practical outcome must be kept distinct.

The company's fiscal claims compounded its control over the crop. Planters owed rents or dues, had to sell the harvest to the VOC at fixed prices, and relied on the company to supply enslaved workers. Miske identifies early arrangements involving levies and price restrictions that could leave planters heavily indebted. From the perspective of the enslaved, these commercial disputes among owners did not mean less compulsion. The company and plantation holders had powerful incentives to extract work even where the organization of production was inefficient. This system joined coercion on the plantation to monopoly bargaining in the port and to maritime routes supplying laborers.

Surviving counts also complicate assumptions about the population after the conquest. A 1631 census identified 797 enslaved Bandanese, 1,164 enslaved people designated as from Malabar, and 410 recorded under other origins. These are document-specific categories, not a reliable census of everyone who had lived in Banda before 1621 or of all people deported elsewhere. The count nevertheless directly disproves a simple picture in which all Bandanese knowledge and labor disappeared and plantation cultivation was performed entirely by newcomers. It also shows the scale on which distant communities were incorporated into the nutmeg economy by force.

Nutmeg trees required environmental knowledge: fruit matured unevenly, young trees needed protection, and processing demanded skill. Miske argues that experienced workers, including surviving Bandanese, retained expertise vital to the harvest. His account also notes escape and other forms of resistance, alongside the problem of labor shortages. These details reveal why the conquest did not produce an immediate revolution in productivity. Control of land, people, and prices could secure monopoly profits without making cultivation technically superior. For students comparing labor systems, Banda offers unusually concrete evidence that capital investment, administrative violence, ecological limits, and worker knowledge shaped production together.

Division III — Maritime Diasporas, Commercial Migration, and New Communities
Division III · Section 91.9

The Makassar War and the Dispersal of Bugis and Makassarese Communities

The war of 1666–1669, in which the VOC and its allies, including Arung Palakka of Bone, defeated Gowa, is narrated in Chapter 82. Here the interest is its human consequences. After the Treaty of Bungaya (1667) and the fall of Gowa's fortifications in 1669, Gowa lost its regional predominance, and the VOC and Bone gained power. Many Makassarese and other South Sulawesi people left, among them displaced nobles, merchants, soldiers, and seafarers. Leonard Andaya's study of Arung Palakka examines refugees and the political consequences of their movement.

The migrants were not a single population. Makassarese from Gowa and Talloq, Bugis from Bone, Soppeng, and other states, and Wajorese from Wajo had different political loyalties and economic roles. Some left because they were defeated, others because they refused Company control of trade, and others as part of a long-standing tradition of seafaring. They established themselves in Banten, Java, Sumatra, Borneo, the Malay Peninsula, Siam, and Cambodia. In Ayutthaya, a group of Makassarese became involved in the 1686 rising during King Narai's reign, which illustrates both their military role and their vulnerability in a foreign court.

Displacement after the Makassar War was not a single ethnic exodus following one event. The contest involved the sultanate of Gowa, the Dutch East India Company, the kingdom of Bone, and other South Sulawesi actors whose interests did not coincide. The Treaty of Bungaya of 1667 and subsequent fighting in 1669 changed the political terms on which merchants and fighters could operate. Some Gowa-associated followers lost access to old patronage networks; others adapted to the new regional order. The rise of Arung Palakka was especially consequential for relationships among Bone, Wajo, and Makassar. A historian must distinguish refugee flight from the reorganization of maritime commerce that continued thereafter.

People left for different reasons. A defeated noble could seek a court willing to recognize his rank; seafarers could relocate to a port with better commercial protection; soldiers could enter service abroad; households could accompany relatives or patrons. These categories were connected but not identical. Some migrants possessed vessels, weapons, and business experience, while others depended on the generosity or political fortunes of leaders. Moving from Sulawesi to a Malay port could create commercial opportunity and also make a migrant vulnerable to the host court's succession disputes. The politics of diaspora therefore began at the moment of departure and continued at the destination.

Other South Sulawesi groups did not simply follow Gowa's fate. Wajorese networks rested partly on traditions of commercial organization and on communities already present outside their homeland. Some Bugis-speaking merchants prospered within new political arrangements in South Sulawesi and elsewhere. Distinguishing Makassarese from Bugis, and Wajorese from other Bugis-speaking groups, is essential because each label points to different political affiliations and social histories. Leonard Andaya's study of Arung Palakka provides the broader context in which war, displaced people, and changing patterns of allegiance can be considered together.

A comparative viewpoint helps avoid treating war refugees as permanently marginal. Some South Sulawesi migrants later acquired titles or military influence in receiving states, but success depended on armed support, recognized leadership, marriage alliances, and local sponsorship. Others remained insecure and could be expelled when a host ruler no longer welcomed them. The Makassarese rising at Ayutthaya in 1686, examined later in the chapter, demonstrates how the same mobile military expertise that made foreign soldiers desirable could become a source of distrust. Mobility could reshape political hierarchies in both favorable and dangerous ways.

Division III · Section 91.10

Bugis and Wajorese Diasporas: Trade, Law, and Political Influence

Principal research: Wellen (2014); Andaya (1981).

South Sulawesi migrants built commercial and political networks across the archipelago. Bugis participated in maritime trade, armed service, and settlement, forming alliances in Borneo, Sumatra, the Malay Peninsula, and the Riau region. Their leaders used kin ties and marriage to gain standing with local rulers, and armed followers made them valuable allies in dynastic disputes.

The Wajorese were especially notable for commercial organization. Kathryn Anderson Wellen's study of early modern Wajorese statecraft and diaspora describes a network in which customary law, cooperation, and relationships across ports supported trade, and overseas settlements retained ties to the homeland. Migrant leadership structures, contracts, and shared navigation knowledge helped traders manage risk.

Wajorese commercial organization shows why the term diaspora should imply more than a scattering of individuals. Merchant communities maintained relationships across ports, and leaders known as matoa could represent their interests and coordinate the terms of trade. Kathryn Anderson Wellen demonstrates that a commercial-law tradition, codified under a matoa in Makassar between the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, addressed credit, partnerships, inheritance, liability, and losses. Its significance was not that merchants everywhere behaved identically, but that a shared framework helped them transact across different political jurisdictions.

Such law mattered because maritime trade created persistent problems of trust. A merchant might advance capital to a partner before a voyage, deliver goods through several intermediaries, or suffer loss at sea. Creditors needed to know who was responsible for damaged cargo and how a deceased merchant's assets would be handled. Community leadership could mediate disputes and identify reputable counterparts. These practices reduced uncertainty and allowed newcomers to enter an existing commercial network. They also demonstrate that commercial institutions in Southeast Asia were not simply borrowed from the VOC or other European companies.

The Wajorese case should not be made to represent all Bugis mobility. Some people traded under community law; others entered royal armies or attached themselves to leading Bugis families seeking influence at Malay courts. In Johor–Riau the early eighteenth-century conflict over succession enabled Bugis armed leaders to support Sultan Sulaiman. The settlement of 1722 institutionalized a Bugis political role through the office of Yang Dipertuan Muda, with Daeng Marewa commonly identified as its first holder. The office illustrates how military alliance could become recognized governmental authority, but it did not mean that every Bugis trader in the region belonged to a single political faction.

Diaspora organization also transformed the places of origin. Commercial communities abroad retained kin ties and could transmit news, capital, and prestige back to Wajo and South Sulawesi. The relationship was not simply a one-way flow of emigrants. A trader's authority might depend on the ability to call upon a homeland custom while receiving recognition from an overseas ruler. This dual embeddedness helps explain both the durability and the limits of regional diasporas. Protection was negotiated across multiple political orders; it could be withdrawn, contested, or outweighed by larger conflicts.

Historical Vocabulary
Diaspora, Matoa, and Merchant Network

Definition. A diaspora is a population dispersed from a homeland that maintains ties across settlements. Matoa (also spelled matowa) refers to a leader's title in South Sulawesi, including the head of the Wajo polity, and the term is also associated with leaders of migrant communities. A merchant network is a set of traders linked by kinship, trust, shared law, and credit.

Historical context. Overseas settlements gained influence through trade, service, and marriage.

Why it matters. The concept explains how migrants combined local integration with long-distance connection.

Potential misunderstanding. A diaspora was not uniformly unified or permanently loyal to a homeland; communities competed, and ties changed over time.

The political result is clearest in Johor-Riau. In the early eighteenth century, after contest over the Johor throne and the seizure of power by Raja Kecil, Bugis leaders supported Sultan Sulaiman's restoration, and the settlement of 1722 gave Bugis an important position through the office of Yang Dipertuan Muda (also rendered Yamtuan Muda), with Daeng Marewa as its first holder. Their later influence is the subject of Chapter 81 and the following period; this section does not advance that story, but notes that migration created the conditions for such political power.

Division III · Section 91.11

Chinese Merchants, Refugees, Settlers, and Commercial Communities

Chinese merchants, many from Fujian, were present in Hội An, Ayutthaya, Batavia, Manila, Banten, and Melaka. Historians distinguish short-term residence by seasonal traders from permanent settlement; many men lived abroad for years, formed households with local women, and raised children who combined cultures. Kinship, associations, shipping networks, and recognition by rulers supported these communities, which often specialized in trade, tax farming, crafts, and agriculture.

Conditions of arrival varied. In 1679, Chinese military refugees opposed to the new Qing order, led by commanders known in Vietnamese sources as Dương Ngạn Địch and Trần Thượng Xuyên, sought refuge with the Nguyễn lord, who allowed them to settle in the southern Mekong region. Later accounts give the number of men and ships, but these figures are not firmly established. Their settlement contributed to commercial and agricultural development in the delta area (see Chapter 88).

Positions differed under different authorities. Under the VOC, Chinese merchants had commercial opportunity and a recognized kapitan, but also taxation and restrictions. In Manila, Chinese residents were essential to trade but suffered violence in 1603 and 1639. In Ayutthaya and Hội An, they received protection and officially recognized leaders and played important roles in foreign trade.

Chinese communities differed in origin and historical circumstance. Hokkien-speaking traders from Fujian were prominent in several ports, but the broad label Chinese encompassed people with different languages, kin networks, professional roles, and legal statuses. Some arrived as seasonal merchants with established capital; others worked as artisans, boatmen, or agricultural settlers. Officials might use one category for taxation or residence even when local families distinguished themselves by homeland, lineage, or occupation. The growth of a Chinese community was therefore not necessarily a single organized migration, nor did its members always share political interests.

Maritime commercial connections allowed families to maintain relationships across several ports. Brokers and merchants needed information about prices, creditworthiness, shipments, and the intentions of rulers. Kin and native-place ties could provide trust, while marriage and residence with local partners made some communities part of the receiving society over generations. In Hội An Chinese intermediaries were important to the Nguyễn trading economy; at Ayutthaya Chinese merchants participated in a court-centered but cosmopolitan commercial system. Under the VOC, company officials sought to classify and tax residents through recognized leaders, although such arrangements reflected colonial administrative interests as much as community wishes.

The events of 1679 provide a more overtly political case. Vietnamese histories identify Dương Ngạn Địch and Trần Thượng Xuyên as commanders arriving with followers who sought acceptance under the Nguyễn rulers. Traditional narratives describe several thousand people, but the figures should remain attributed to these sources, not presented as a modern enumeration. Nguyễn authorities directed groups toward the Đồng Nai and lower Mekong frontier, where settlement and commerce intersected with questions of Khmer jurisdiction. This was not simply the transfer of a neutral merchant colony into unused land. Soldiers, families, local communities, and Nguyễn officials all participated in the changing politics of the region.

Commercial opportunity did not guarantee security. Chinese communities could enjoy privileges because rulers needed their trade and then face exceptional taxes, suspicion, or violence during political crises. The massacres in Manila in 1603 and 1639 and the Batavia violence of 1740 demonstrate distinct colonial contexts of extreme insecurity; they should not be portrayed as interchangeable incidents. The comparative question is how governments could simultaneously depend on migrant expertise and treat migrant residents as politically vulnerable outsiders. A well-developed network offered resources but could not always protect people from an armed state.

Division III · Section 91.12

Japanese, Indian, Malay, and Other Migrant Communities in Regional Ports

Japanese merchants traveled on red-seal ships licensed by the Tokugawa government in the early seventeenth century and settled in Ayutthaya, Hội An, and other ports. In Ayutthaya, a prominent Japanese figure, Yamada Nagamasa, held court status in the 1620s. After the 1630s, when Japan restricted overseas travel and shipping, Japanese communities lost reinforcement, and many gradually assimilated or declined.

Indian merchants and maritime laborers, including Gujarati, Tamil, Telugu, and Bengali traders and sailors, were prominent across ports. Malay-speaking and Javanese traders, Persians, Arabs, and others contributed to the cosmopolitan culture of ports such as Melaka, Aceh, Banten, and Ayutthaya. Many communities retained ties to places of origin while forming households, mosques, temples, and relationships with local rulers.

The comparison is institutional. Communities differed in how they organized residence (separate quarters or mixed neighborhoods), trade (kin firms, guild-like associations, or state-sponsored monopolies), family life (intermarriage and the status of children), and political protection (recognized headmen, royal patronage, or company charters). These differences, not simply ethnic labels, explain why some communities flourished and others declined.

Japanese commercial movement illustrates a change in the legal conditions of overseas travel. Merchants had sailed to Southeast Asian ports before the Tokugawa government consolidated its licensing practices. From 1604 the red-seal system provided authorized traders with official credentials; overseas voyages expanded commercial ties with Hội An, Ayutthaya, Manila, and other ports. Merchants carried metals and manufactured goods and acquired silk and tropical products. These ventures involved crew members, brokers, interpreters, and residents who did not necessarily return on the same vessel. The movement of traders could therefore create settled Japanese neighborhoods as well as temporary shipping circuits.

Receiving rulers made different uses of such communities. At Ayutthaya foreign residents might receive positions as commercial intermediaries or members of military retinues. Yamada Nagamasa, prominent during the 1620s, shows that a Japanese migrant could become influential within the Siamese court, though his career should not be generalized to every Japanese inhabitant. At Hội An merchants formed ties with local suppliers and administrators. The growing restrictions on Japanese overseas travel in the 1630s reduced fresh departures from Japan and affected existing communities, but the lives of people who had already settled abroad did not end with the licensing system.

Indian Ocean traders followed different commercial traditions. Gujarati, Tamil, Telugu, Bengali, and other merchants and sailors reached Southeast Asian ports under changing political conditions, sometimes as independent operators and sometimes as part of larger commercial partnerships. Malay-speaking networks connected islands and coastal settlements through language, Islamic scholarship, and trading practice without corresponding to a single kingdom. Indian and Malay identities also changed through intermarriage, conversion, and occupational association. Describing these people as one undifferentiated foreign community would conceal the varied institutions supporting their movement.

Comparison shows both similarities and differences. A trusted merchant network might help a Gujarati trader obtain credit, a Japanese resident find an interpreter, or a Malay shipmaster arrange harbor protection. Yet their possibilities were altered by the legal powers of the host ruler and the policies of distant states. Japan's restrictions on departure affected Japanese trade in ways different from the commercial and dynastic disruptions affecting Malay and Indian Ocean routes. A port city therefore contained several migration histories at once; their chronology cannot be reduced to a general narrative of ever-increasing cosmopolitanism.

Division IV · Section 91.13

Migrant Soldiers, Mercenaries, and Military Settlements

Armed service was another channel of mobility. Courts hired foreign soldiers: Ayutthaya employed Japanese and other foreign troops and Portuguese gunners; Cambodian kings used Malay, Chinese, and other forces; Bugis and Makassarese fighters served in maritime disputes; the VOC used European, Asian, and enslaved soldiers; and Chinese military refugees entered Nguyễn service.

Service could bring status, land, political influence, and commercial opportunity, but also danger. Foreign troops could be suspected of disloyalty, as in the 1686 Makassarese revolt in Ayutthaya. Military settlements and garrison colonies, including those in Nguyễn territory, combined defense with agriculture. Historians distinguish recruited soldiers (who bargained for pay or rank), captives (compelled to fight), conscripts (obliged through service systems), and military slaves (owned by masters), since their freedom and rewards differed greatly.

The military labor market was inseparable from regional diplomacy. Rulers recruited outsiders for skills in navigation, firearms, cavalry, fortification, and the command of armed followers. Foreign soldiers could be useful precisely because they were not embedded in older local rivalries, but that same separation also made them potentially suspect. A group entrusted with a strongpoint might become a decisive political ally during a succession dispute. Its protection could disappear when the king died or a court faction turned against it. The category of mercenary thus covers different relationships: individually salaried soldiers, armed retainers of migrant leaders, and communities with negotiated military obligations.

Ayutthaya's foreign soldiers provide a revealing case. Japanese residents participated in court service in the early seventeenth century, while the later movement of Makassarese and other South Sulawesi fighters brought different armed networks into the kingdom. The Makassarese rising of 1686 during Narai's reign shows that relations could deteriorate dramatically. Contemporary European accounts describe conflict and the use of armed force against Makassarese groups; these accounts also reflect the interests and suspicions of their authors. The event belongs in its 1686 political setting and should not be collapsed into the different court crisis of 1688.

Military movement also reached beyond royal capitals. The 1679 Chinese commanders admitted by the Nguyễn illustrate the overlap between refuge, armed organization, and settlement. Men who retained leadership structures could become both strategic assets and intermediaries with newly settled communities. In mainland kingdoms, recruited or captured people might be assigned to particular service villages, while locally raised households provided food and transport for garrisons. The boundary between soldier and settler was therefore often permeable: a military community could support a frontier through farming, trade, patrols, and service to officials.

Military status should not be equated with freedom. Some fighters negotiated pay and rank; others were conscripted, transferred as captives, or compelled to follow a patron. Leaders might obtain titles while ordinary soldiers bore the dangers of campaigns and uncertain residence. This section therefore compares the terms of recruitment and the relationship to a host court, not simply the nationality of those carrying weapons. The broader historical consequence is that mobility supplied rulers with military capacity while also introducing new political dependencies and the risk of conflict within increasingly diverse court societies.

Division IV · Section 91.14

Agricultural Settlement, Frontier Expansion, and Displaced Communities

Settlement in new agricultural territories reshaped demography. Along the Nguyễn frontier, settlers, soldiers, and refugees cleared land, formed villages, and created markets; around Batavia, agricultural expansion supported the city with rice and sugar, using migrant and enslaved labor; on the mainland, rulers resettled households to rebuild villages or secure frontiers.

Government incentives, land clearance, military-agricultural settlement, irrigation, and market development all mattered. But expansion of cultivated land did not necessarily mean movement into empty territory. Existing communities, including Cham, Khmer, and upland groups, had rights and patterns of access to land and water that were disrupted. Chapter 88 examines the Cham and Khmer history in detail; this section emphasizes the comparative point that frontier settlement involved displacement as well as opportunity.

Agricultural migration developed through several mechanisms that must be kept separate. A ruler could grant a military leader land to populate and defend, officials could register households moving from crowded districts, and families might independently relocate in search of better conditions. In other cases soldiers forcibly removed populations from one territory to cultivate another. Settlement involved more than clearing a field: migrants needed access to water, food during the first growing season, local exchange, protection from violence, and recognized claims over land. When those conditions were absent, formal grants did not necessarily result in permanent villages.

The Nguyễn frontier offers a concrete comparison. As discussed in Chapter 88, Vietnamese and Chinese settlers moved into territories already shaped by Cham and Khmer communities. Nguyễn administrative appointments, military garrisons, and new markets enabled settlement in selected areas, but claims of sovereignty remained uneven. The experience of a Vietnamese household receiving official recognition differed from that of a Khmer family whose land or access to waterways became contested. The central issue was not a uniform national march southward but a combination of administrative projects, commercial opportunity, military power, and local negotiations or resistance.

Around colonial ports, agricultural expansion similarly depended on labor and coercion. Batavia's hinterland supplied food and, increasingly, sugar and other commercial crops, drawing on people of different origins and statuses. Company authorities, private landholders, merchants, and workers could have conflicting interests in the pace and character of development. A plantation worker transported as property cannot be described as a voluntary pioneer merely because new cultivated land resulted from the movement. Comparing a land grant to a merchant family with the assignment of an enslaved worker makes clear how economic expansion redistributed both resources and power.

Landscapes changed through these movements. Forest clearance, canal construction, irrigation, and the establishment of markets altered drainage, crop patterns, and the use of common resources. Environmental consequences could harm older residents even where no formal expulsion was recorded, since access to woodland, fishing, or water might be restricted by new claims. Indigenous knowledge was often indispensable to newcomers, yet incorporation into commercial agriculture could marginalize its holders. The chapter should therefore track whose rights to land and water were recognized and whose practices remained poorly visible in the official records.

Division IV · Section 91.15

Buddhist Monks, Islamic Scholars, Christian Missionaries, and Religious Mobility

Source context: Ward (2009); South African Government biography.

Religious travel connected distant communities. Buddhist monks moved along ordination lineages and pilgrimage routes, and manuscripts circulated among monasteries across the Theravada mainland. Muslim scholars and traders linked Southeast Asian ports to wider Islamic networks. Catholic missionaries and local catechists traveled through Philippine, Vietnamese, and other communities, building parish and mission networks.

Religious institutions helped migrants maintain or rebuild identity. A mosque or temple served worship and also served as a meeting place, a court of arbitration, and a source of leadership. Yet religious movement was not always voluntary. The VOC exiled political and religious figures, and Ward's work shows its forced-migration network reaching the Cape. Shaykh Yusuf is a leading example. These coerced exiles must be distinguished from voluntary pilgrims and missionaries, who chose to travel and generally kept their liberty.

Buddhist mobility was sustained by institutions as well as individual faith. Monks traveled for ordination, study, teaching, manuscript exchange, and pilgrimage; monastic centers could connect communities separated by royal boundaries. In mainland societies where rulers patronized Buddhism, an itinerant monk might be welcomed as a teacher and carry ritual authority even without commercial wealth. But monks depended on support from households and patrons, and political crises could disrupt routes or redirect religious communities. Religious travel thus had material foundations: boats, safe lodgings, donor networks, copying labor, and permissions from rulers.

Islamic scholarly networks connected ports and courts through teaching, pilgrimage, family ties, and commercial patronage. Religious specialists traveled alongside merchants but did not necessarily pursue identical goals. In some cases the line between political and religious mobility was especially difficult to draw. Shaykh Yusuf, associated with South Sulawesi and the court of Banten, was imprisoned after the struggle against the VOC and sent to Ceylon in 1684. The company subsequently decided to remove him farther from its Asian political networks, and he arrived at the Cape in 1694. His itinerary shows how forced exile could attempt to sever connections while inadvertently contributing to new religious communities at a distant destination.

Christian missions generated another kind of mobility. Portuguese and other Catholic missionaries visited royal courts and trading settlements, relying on translators, local catechists, patronage, and established routes. Religious conversion could change family and community affiliations, but it did not automatically sever them. Missionary letters often record their authors' hopes more readily than the complex motives of converts. A locally trained catechist's movement between communities might have greater practical importance than the journey of a better-known European missionary. The source record, however, tends to emphasize the latter and needs to be read against that imbalance.

Coercion could enter any of these networks. Religious minorities might flee violence; rulers or companies might deport scholars they considered politically dangerous; missionaries could encounter restrictions or expulsion. A pilgrim selecting a route, an exile under guard, and a catechist traveling with community support therefore occupied profoundly different positions. The comparative question is how religious institutions enabled attachment across distance while political authorities attempted to direct or restrict movement. The example of Shaykh Yusuf is particularly useful because his removal links Southeast Asian conflict with the Cape without making colonial exile a voluntary religious mission.

Division IV · Section 91.16

Women, Children, Marriage, Households, and the Human Consequences of Mobility

Comparative research: Andaya (2006).

Barbara Watson Andaya's work shows that women were central to early modern Southeast Asian economies and households. Women were traders and market operators in many ports, managed household finances, and served as intermediaries between local communities and foreign merchants. Marriage connected visiting merchants with local families, producing mixed households and new social identities. Inheritance, language, and religion passed through these families in varied ways.

Women were also captives, laborers, and the targets of coercion. Enslavement and captivity separated families and exposed women to sexual coercion; children's legal status depended on local law, the status of parents, and sometimes the owner's recognition. The sources seldom allow reconstruction of individual experience, so historians must be careful not to invent personal stories.

Women's work was crucial to the practical organization of mobile households. In market towns and ports, women traded food, cloth, prepared goods, and other products, sometimes sustaining families during seasonal absences of male relatives. Marriage to a local partner could give an arriving merchant access to languages, suppliers, and accepted forms of residence; the benefit of such connections was not necessarily shared equally within the marriage. Household strategies depended on differences in law, custom, religion, class, and inherited property. Barbara Watson Andaya's comparative work warns against treating a generalized image of Southeast Asian women's commercial autonomy as proof that all women enjoyed equivalent power.

Children experienced mobility through the decisions and misfortunes of adults. A child traveling in a merchant family could learn languages and acquire commercial connections; a child transferred as a captive might lose contact with parents and familiar institutions. In societies recognizing inherited forms of slavery, the child's legal status could depend on the status of one or both parents and on locally specific rules. Families created under coercive conditions could still establish meaningful relationships, but the possibility of family life did not make the coercion less severe. The reconstruction of children's experiences must distinguish known legal categories from stories that the sources do not preserve.

Marriage and domestic residence were also political. Court women could strengthen dynastic alliances, and migrant men might acquire standing through relatives in a host polity. At the same time, elite households could contain enslaved concubines, servants, dependents, and children with unequal recognition. The sources often give the identities of high-ranking spouses but not those of enslaved workers. This asymmetry is itself evidence of what officials considered worth recording. A chapter that describes only merchant patriarchs and military leaders will misunderstand the household relations on which ports and courts depended.

The everyday-life feature in this section should be read alongside an explicit methodological warning. It is possible to compare a free market trader, the spouse of a long-distance merchant, a war captive, and an enslaved household worker by asking who controlled earnings, residence, family separation, and the fate of children. It is not possible to assign invented speech or feelings to anonymous historical people. The purpose of comparative social history is to make unequal power visible while acknowledging the limits of the evidence, not to fill archival silence with reassuring or sensational fiction.

Everyday Life and Social History

Women, Children, and Households in Mobile Societies

A trading port's household might include a merchant, a local wife who ran a shop or managed credit, children raised in two languages, servants, and bonded or enslaved dependents who cooked, carried, and tended. Seasonal winds meant husbands might leave for months, so women often managed business and kin networks in their absence. In royal courts, captured and dependent women might serve as attendants, entertainers, or consorts. In plantation settings, enslaved families faced separation through sale.

Children's prospects varied. In some societies a child of a free father and enslaved mother inherited freedom or a path to it; in others status followed the mother. Education, religious upbringing, and marriage alliances determined whether a child entered a merchant family, a servant household, or a dependent class. The sources describing these lives are mostly written by officials, missionaries, and travelers, so students should ask what these observers noticed and what they missed.

Division V · Section 91.17

Port Cities, Foreign Quarters, and the Government of Migrant Communities

Port authorities governed diverse populations through designated quarters, appointed community leaders, religious institutions, taxation, and legal arrangements. In Batavia, the VOC appointed Chinese kapitan, beginning with Souw Beng Kong, and classified residents by origin and status. In Ayutthaya, foreign communities lived in recognized settlements under headmen with royal oversight. Manila's Parian housed Chinese merchants under Spanish supervision. Melaka under the Portuguese and then the Dutch had its own arrangements, and Hội An had distinct Chinese and Japanese quarters.

Migrants could have privileges unavailable to local subjects, such as trading rights or tax advantages, while facing restrictions, forced labor, or expulsion. The insecurity of status was dramatic in the 1740 violence against Chinese residents of Batavia, in which large numbers were killed; estimates of the death toll vary widely. The event followed tensions over sugar-industry decline, unemployment, and official fears, and it should not be read as an inevitable outcome of Chinese settlement. Rulers and companies shifted policy as interests changed.

Governing foreigners posed a recurring dilemma for courts and companies. Merchants supplied revenue, credit, shipping expertise, weapons, and access to distant markets. Governments also worried about their collective organization, rival loyalties, and ability to leave when policies became unfavorable. Different jurisdictions therefore recognized community representatives, assigned residence areas, regulated commerce, or demanded special taxes. Such arrangements could offer predictability to some migrants while exposing them to collective punishment. An official's assertion that a foreign quarter was well ordered cannot be assumed to reflect the experience of everyone living within it.

Under the VOC, Chinese kapitan could serve as intermediaries between company government and Chinese residents. They might help administer registration, taxation, disputes, and community affairs, but their recognized authority depended on the colonial state. Similar-sounding offices elsewhere operated under different rules. In Asian royal capitals, communities could be organized around trading patrons, religious establishments, or military leaders. Comparing these forms of leadership reveals that the ability to represent migrants was both a source of local influence and a means by which rulers extended control. Recognition could privilege particular merchants while leaving poorer residents without an effective voice.

Violence exposed the fragility of these arrangements. Spanish authorities at Manila relied on Chinese trade but presided over massacres in 1603 and 1639; the VOC's Batavia experienced anti-Chinese violence in 1740 amid mounting tension and coercive measures. Each episode requires its own local explanation. None should be presented as an inevitable expression of ethnic relations or as evidence that all migrant groups were treated alike. Rather, these crises illustrate how quickly a commercially important population could be recast as suspect under conditions of political fear, administrative conflict, and armed mobilization.

Migrant communities also responded strategically. Established families sought patrons, negotiated exemptions, supported religious institutions, cultivated marriage ties, and sometimes moved commerce to another port. For poorer residents, the ability to depart might be limited by debt, legal restrictions, family needs, or lack of transport. The chapter's comparison should therefore separate state policies from the responses available to differently positioned migrants. The same institutional system that protected a prominent broker might fail the servants, dependents, and laborers who contributed to the port's economy.

Division V · Section 91.18

Slavery, Labor, Production, and the Economic Transformation of Southeast Asia

Enslaved, bonded, corvée, and household workers contributed to production in different ways. Nutmeg on Banda, sugar around Batavia, agricultural settlements, workshops, transport, military construction, and household economies all depended on labor drawn from a range of statuses. Plantation slavery, in which large numbers worked in commodity production for a market, differed from domestic slavery within households, from temporary compulsory service, and from other dependencies.

Rulers and enterprises profited from labor while the costs of coercion fell disproportionately on dependent populations. Plantation development also transformed environments: forest clearance, changed land use, and monoculture altered landscapes, as Miske's work on Banda ecology suggests. The economic story of the period therefore includes growth for some and hardship for others, a distinction that economic totals alone cannot show.

The connection between labor coercion and commercial production varied across industries. In Banda, nutmeg production under VOC monopoly rested upon enslaved workers and the company's distribution of plantation plots. Around Batavia, agricultural and craft production used mixtures of enslaved, wage-earning, contracted, and dependent workers whose status cannot be inferred simply from the crop they produced. In mainland royal centers, workers could be mobilized through service obligations rather than purchased in a slave market. These distinctions matter because the institutions controlling people shaped recruitment costs, everyday discipline, family residence, mobility, and the distribution of profit.

Coercion was often linked to the transfer of specialized skills. Harvesting nutmeg required knowledge of ripeness and tree care, while weaving, metalwork, construction, and navigation required experience that could not be created by orders alone. Plantation owners and kings therefore depended on workers whose expertise might provide some limited leverage. In Banda, Miske's evidence shows continuity with preconquest growing practices and the importance of surviving Bandanese knowledge even after catastrophic violence. The imposed production regime could attempt to control workers without fully controlling the ecological and technical conditions of cultivation.

Commercial expansion also depended on unpaid or undercompensated reproductive labor: feeding households, maintaining shelters, caring for children, preparing goods for market, and sustaining workers through the agricultural cycle. These activities were often performed by women and dependents and are poorly counted in records focused on cargo, customs, or plantation output. A port's prosperity cannot be measured adequately by the volume of exports without considering the households supporting the labor force. Women's market work also complicates any division of economic history into male merchants and anonymous domestic life. Household labor was part of the commercial system itself.

The environmental consequences were inseparable from labor practices. Expansion of sugar, rice, or tree crops could alter forests, water use, and settlement patterns, sometimes reducing older communities' access to customary resources. Plantation profitability was affected by forced labor, but also by transport bottlenecks, poor information, storms, disease, prices, and the power of monopolists. This section should resist explaining output through coercion alone: forced labor was a major means of organizing production, not a guarantee of technical efficiency. The broader comparison asks how people and environments were reorganized for the benefit of rulers and companies, and at whose expense.

Division V · Section 91.19

Resistance, Manumission, Cultural Adaptation, and New Communities

Migrants and unfree people responded in many ways: flight, negotiation, self-purchase where documented, manumission, petitions, collective resistance, and changes in legal status. The options differed. Wealthy merchants could bargain and relocate; soldiers could shift allegiance; household dependents negotiated within families; plantation laborers had few options except flight or revolt; and enslaved people often faced severe punishment.

Migration contributed to linguistic change, mixed communities, religious adaptation, and new forms of belonging. Creole languages, hybrid cuisines, and new family forms emerged. But these developments cannot be treated as compensation for suffering associated with forced movement.

Resistance is most visible where institutions tried to suppress it. In official records, the person escaping an owner appears as a fugitive, and a person petitioning against an obligation may appear as a troublemaker. Those labels reveal the authority of record-makers but not the entire meaning of the action. Flight, concealment, legal complaint, refusal to meet demands, and negotiation with a different patron could all challenge a claim over labor. The available choices differed dramatically: a merchant might threaten to relocate business, while an enslaved plantation worker faced severe punishment for an attempted escape. Both could move, but their capacity to defend that movement was unequal.

The Banda plantation evidence supplies an unusually concrete example. Miske identifies repeated escapes by enslaved workers and suggests that the forested working environment and the need for skilled harvesting reduced owners' ability to supervise every action. Such observations must not be romanticized. Relative autonomy in a particular task coexisted with ownership, violence, and restricted movement. Nevertheless, they reveal that enslaved people did not simply execute administrative instructions. Their knowledge, choices, and resistance altered labor arrangements and could impose costs on planters and the company. The historian can recover aspects of that agency even when the workers' words are absent.

Manumission and redemption introduced other possibilities, but they were dependent on legal rules, resources, and recognition by authorities. Release might enable a former dependent to enter trade, establish a household, or join a community with more secure standing. In other cases former owners retained influence or social prejudice continued. Children of migrants and formerly enslaved people grew up in new linguistic and religious environments, creating identities that official ethnic labels could not capture. The formation of mixed communities should be described as a historical development, not as compensation for the violence that had contributed to it.

Political and religious exile produced similar tensions between coercion and adaptation. The VOC sent Shaykh Yusuf across imperial stations to constrain his political and spiritual influence, but his presence at the Cape became important to later Muslim community history. This outcome was not necessarily the company's objective, and it should not erase his experience of forced removal. The comparative lesson is that displaced people could shape receiving societies without acquiring equal control over the circumstances of arrival. Agency can be real while freedom remains sharply constrained.

Division V · Section 91.20

Mobility and Unfreedom: The Historical Significance of Early Modern Population Movement

Kingdoms and commercial companies depended on people moving between settlements, ports, military zones, and agricultural frontiers. Rulers could encourage migration, recruit outsiders, deport opponents, seize captives, or restrict dependent communities. The relationship between mobility and state power was therefore double-edged: movement expanded networks and revenue, but also gave rulers and companies greater ability to mobilize and control labor.

Migration simultaneously strengthened political institutions, expanded commercial networks, transformed communities, and exposed large numbers to violence, displacement, and unequal power. The records limit confident generalization, and imposing one universal definition of slavery on every society risks distortion. This synthesis prepares Chapter 92, which treats the broader commercial world to which this demographic and labor history contributed.

The chapter's cases demonstrate several different relationships between mobility and power. War captives moved because armies exercised coercion; merchants crossed regions through networks of credit, kin, and legal protection; soldiers migrated through recruitment or allegiance; enslaved people were transported as labor; monks and religious scholars followed both voluntary itineraries and paths imposed by exile. Describing them all as mobile does not explain their histories unless the institutions determining movement are identified. The central comparative axis is therefore who controlled departure, destination, residence, labor, family relations, and the possibility of return.

Regional governments often valued people as a political resource. Agricultural production, armies, port markets, and religious institutions depended upon households, skills, and relationships that land alone could not provide. Rulers who captured or attracted people could expand the reach of their courts; those who lost populations might lose the capacity to maintain irrigation, collect revenue, or wage war. Such calculations explain the coexistence of immigration incentives and forced relocation. They also demonstrate why migration history belongs within the history of state formation rather than being treated as a separate social topic.

The commercial comparison is equally instructive. Wajorese trading communities used customary law and leadership to facilitate circulation of goods and credit; the VOC coordinated commercial empire through monopoly, shipping, forced labor, and punishment. Both operated through networks that crossed political boundaries, but their structures and claims over individuals were different. The prosperity of a port could depend on free commercial exchange alongside enslaved domestic work and compulsory labor. It is therefore inaccurate to describe early modern commerce in general either as a straightforward expansion of freedom or as uniformly dependent on identical forms of bondage.

The sources also limit the conclusions that historians can draw. Merchant law preserves transactions more clearly than the lives of poorer sailors; royal chronicles privilege victories and dynastic status; company records make enslaved populations legible as administrative quantities while silencing many individual experiences. Archaeology, family records, and critical source comparison can recover part of what those records conceal. The appropriate conclusion is a qualified account of unequal choices, not a synthetic population total or a single explanatory law for every society. A full history must preserve regional variation alongside genuine connections.

By about 1750, mobility had altered the populations and institutions of many ports and kingdoms, while migration continued to operate through older family, religious, and commercial channels. Some people achieved new political rank, built prosperous networks, or established durable households; others endured seizure, enslavement, deportation, and loss of land. Their histories intersected but did not cancel one another. Chapter 92 can now examine the regional commercial economy with these human foundations visible: the circulation of commodities depended on multiple, unequal ways of moving the people who produced, transported, and exchanged them.

Historical Debate

Did Commercial Expansion Increase Freedom or Dependence?

The question: Did the growth of trade between about 1500 and 1750 expand opportunity for most people, or did it increase demand for dependent and coerced labor?

Interpretation A, commercial expansion increased dependence: Some scholars argue that rising demand for labor in trade and production, together with warfare and the capacity of states and companies to seize people, increased slavery and bondage in this period.
Interpretation B, expansion offered varied opportunity: Others stress that trade created paths for merchants, women traders, migrants, and some dependents to improve their positions, and that many forms of dependency predate the period and varied by society.

Evidence: Both views draw on law codes, trade records, company registers, and travelers' accounts, as well as comparative studies such as the Reid and Brewster volume and Andaya's study of women and slavery.

Points of disagreement: Disagreement concerns scale (how much slavery increased), chronology (when), causation (commerce versus warfare), and the meaning of dependency across societies. Evidence is uneven and numbers are rarely reliable.

Why it matters: The answer shapes whether early modern commerce is seen as liberating, as exploitative, or as both for different groups.

Chapter Summary

Early modern Southeast Asia was a region in motion. Maritime routes through the Strait of Malacca, the Java Sea, the Makassar Strait, and the South China Sea, and mainland river and overland corridors, carried merchants, soldiers, settlers, religious travelers, refugees, and captives. Political boundaries rarely coincided with the networks along which people moved.

The chapter's central distinction was between movement and freedom. Voluntary migration, induced movement, war captivity, chattel slavery, debt bondage, corvée, military service, and forced relocation were different institutions, though they overlapped in practice. Defining them carefully allowed comparison without erasing legal and human differences.

Rulers competed for people as much as for land. Warfare produced captives who were resettled as farmers, soldiers, artisans, and servants, and mainland states organized labor through service systems such as Siamese phrai obligations and Burmese ahmudan groups. Evasion and flight shaped the limits of state power.

Debt bondage, hereditary slavery, and patronage structured households and economies. Redemption and manumission existed, but legal paths did not guarantee practical freedom, and enslavement was neither voluntary nor benign merely because it was legally regulated.

The VOC integrated forced migration into commercial empire. The 1621 conquest of Banda displaced the Bandanese, and the perken system placed plantation lots in the hands of perkeniers using enslaved labor. Batavia depended on migrant and enslaved populations, and Company networks carried convicts and exiles as far as the Cape. Recent archival research suggests plantation development was more uneven than older narratives implied.

The Makassar War of 1666–1669 dispersed Makassarese, Bugis, and Wajorese people, who formed commercial, military, and political networks from Borneo to the Malay Peninsula. Wajorese organization shows how customary law and cooperation sustained a diaspora, and Bugis influence in Johor-Riau after 1722 shows how migration could become political power.

Chinese, Japanese, Indian, Malay, and other merchant communities settled in ports under varying systems of quarters, appointed leaders, taxation, and protection. Chinese communities gained opportunity but faced insecurity, as at Manila in 1603 and 1639 and Batavia in 1740. Japanese communities declined after the 1630s restrictions.

Soldiers, settlers, and religious travelers extended the picture beyond trade. Foreign troops could gain status or suffer suspicion; frontier settlement disrupted existing communities; and religious networks supported identity while forced exile showed coercion in religious life.

Women, children, and households were central. Women traded, managed households, and mediated between communities, while enslavement and captivity exposed them to separation and coercion. Children's status varied by law and custom.

The chapter concludes that mobility and unfreedom were interconnected. Migration strengthened states and commerce and created new communities, but it also exposed many people to violence and dispossession. Chapter 92 builds on these findings in its synthesis of the early modern commercial world.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows that the political and commercial histories of earlier chapters rested on people who moved, or were moved. Kingdoms, companies, and ports were built from households, soldiers, merchants, and laborers, and the competition for them shaped warfare, law, and trade.

It also shows that mobility was unequal. Those who chose to move, such as merchants with kin networks, differed from those compelled to move, and the chapter has tried to hold both in view without confusing them. Legal categories, gender, age, and wealth determined what movement meant.

Later developments built on these foundations: Bugis and Chinese communities remained significant in port economies, colonial states expanded systems of classification and labor control, and many present-day communities trace their histories to these migrations. The chapter's themes recur in the colonial plural societies of Chapter 121 and the economic transformations of Chapter 122.

Chapter 92 now synthesizes the early modern commercial world, in which the movement of people was as important as the movement of goods.
