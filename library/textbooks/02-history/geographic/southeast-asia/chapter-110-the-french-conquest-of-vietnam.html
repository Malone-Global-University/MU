Chapter Opening

Vietnamese sovereignty was dismantled progressively, through military interventions, annexations, unequal treaties, and diplomacy with Qing China, not extinguished in a single battle or treaty.

This chapter examines how the Nguyễn monarchy lost territorial and diplomatic independence through a succession of coercive interventions between 1858 and the 1880s and how resistance continued into 1896. It distinguishes the 1862–1867 annexation of southern Cochinchina from the later French protectorates over Annam and Tonkin. The processes involved decisions by French ministers and officers, Vietnamese emperors and mandarins, Qing diplomats, local military leaders, missionaries, and communities whose experiences cannot be read directly from the treaty signatures. Its central question is how naval force, law, and administration combined to make foreign power effective—and why that power nevertheless remained contested.

French military victories did not produce a single act of conquest. Initial attacks failed at Đà Nẵng, French possession in the south grew in stages, and northern adventures produced both reverses and concessions. The diplomatic language of independence and protection increasingly restricted the court’s choices. By examining documents beside conflicts in villages and at imperial courts, the chapter differentiates the legal transfer of authority from its practical enforcement.

Source evidence is uneven. Official French records preserve the priorities of a conquering power; Nguyễn chronicles and Vietnamese accounts reveal other commitments but are also shaped by court politics and later memory. The chapter uses clearly identified treaty texts, scholarly reconstructions, and cautious analysis of resistance without representing paraphrases as literal quotations. Detailed colonial economic administration belongs to Chapter 112, whereas Cambodia, Laos, and the wider Indochinese Union receive their main treatment in Chapter 111.

Historical Context

The Nguyễn dynasty, established under Gia Long (Chapter 99), ruled a unified Vietnam from Huế. By the mid-nineteenth century it faced rebellion, fiscal strain, and European naval power. Detailed colonial economy and administration are reserved for Chapters 111 and 112.

The Nguyễn dynasty arose from the political settlement created by Gia Long at the beginning of the nineteenth century, described in Chapter 99. By Tự Đức’s reign the court at Huế supervised provinces with different social histories, agricultural systems, and connections to regional trade. The dynasty possessed a highly developed political language and official apparatus but could not command the steam-powered naval forces available to European expeditions. Fiscal pressure, regional uprisings, and competing defense priorities constrained choices without making failure inevitable.

Catholic communities had existed for generations before the conquest. Their relationships with other villagers and with Nguyễn rulers varied, and French claims to protect Christians must be distinguished from the full range of French imperial aims. Qing-Vietnamese tributary relations likewise need to be separated from direct Chinese administration: Beijing’s diplomatic position mattered to French negotiations, but Nguyễn officials governed Vietnam. The end point of this chapter, 1896, denotes the suppression of a major phase of Cần Vương resistance rather than the end of all opposition.

Section 110.1

The Nguyễn Dynasty and the Crisis of Sovereignty

When Tự Đức ascended the Nguyễn throne in 1847, he inherited a state that was extensive, literate, and administratively ambitious, but also difficult to govern from Huế. Provincial governors, magistrates, military commanders, tax collectors, and clerks transmitted commands through a hierarchy modeled in part on Chinese precedents and revised by Nguyễn practice. Examination success offered a route into government for educated families; the ruler's authority rested on ancestral rites, Confucian moral language, and the ability to allocate appointments and resources. These arrangements provided real governing capacity. They did not, however, give the court equal knowledge of every frontier or secure automatic compliance from distant villages. The Mekong Delta, central highlands, and northern uplands posed different administrative and logistical problems, while regional interests could diverge sharply from priorities at the capital.

Land taxes, population assessments, labor obligations, and the movement of grain sustained government operations. Their effectiveness varied with harvests, population movement, corruption, and the readiness of local elites to cooperate. In the 1840s and 1850s, floods, local rebellions, fiscal strain, and demands on soldiers crowded the court's agenda. Public resources had to be divided among canals and dikes, military garrisons, examinations, court ceremonial, and relief measures. A central administration could be impressive on paper and yet move slowly when a sudden crisis required coordinated men, boats, food, and money across several provinces. Describing the dynasty as helpless or simply declining would obscure its continued strengths; describing it as stable would obscure the pressures under which its decisions were made.

The Nguyễn army combined provincial formations, fortified positions, garrisons, irregular auxiliaries, and weapons acquired or manufactured at different dates. Fortification and small arms had undergone reform during Gia Long's reign, but the court did not maintain an ocean-going steam fleet capable of challenging a European expeditionary squadron. Technological contrast did not determine every engagement: coastal defenses, local knowledge, monsoon conditions, and Vietnamese tactical choices could frustrate attackers. The decisive difference was strategic. French steamships could move units and supplies along the coast, select points of attack, and withdraw from unfavorable positions more readily than Nguyễn commanders could concentrate comparable naval power. The gulf mattered most when battle was linked to diplomatic pressure on a central court responsible for territory hundreds of kilometers apart.

Vietnam's relationship with Qing China further complicates the word sovereignty. The Nguyễn emperors participated in a diplomatic order in which missions, recognition, calendars, and investiture acknowledged the Qing emperor's superior ritual standing. Vietnamese officials simultaneously exercised their own authority over taxation, law, warfare, and appointments. Neither a Qing embassy nor an investiture certificate meant that Beijing administered Vietnamese provinces. European jurists tended to translate such relationships into terms of sovereignty and suzerainty, but those categories imposed a sharper division than much older regional practice required. In the 1870s and 1880s, competing interpretations of this relationship became politically consequential as French negotiators claimed that China should have no role in Vietnam's foreign relations.

The court confronted painful strategic choices. Concessions to a foreign power might preserve the dynasty temporarily while alienating officials and people committed to resistance. A decision to resist might produce local solidarity but endanger civilians and expose a coastal province to bombardment. Some officials argued for improving weapons, diplomacy, or practical administration; others prioritized the established moral and political order. These were not arguments between a uniformly progressive faction and a uniformly backward one. They arose from different estimates of military capacity, political loyalty, fiscal constraints, and what a legitimate emperor owed his subjects. The crisis of sovereignty was therefore both external and internal: an imperial government had to decide how much practical authority it could surrender while still claiming to rule.

Historical Vocabulary

Historical Vocabulary: Sovereignty and Tributary Relations

Tributary relations were ritual and diplomatic exchanges in which a ruler acknowledged the Chinese emperor's precedence, typically through missions, investiture, and gifts. Sovereignty in nineteenth-century European law meant independent control of territory and foreign policy. The two concepts did not map neatly onto each other, and France exploited the difference.

Misunderstanding: Tributary status did not mean Chinese rule over Vietnam. Terms: Nguyễn dynasty, mandarin, imperial court.

Historical setting. Under Nguyễn rule a tributary mission to the Qing court did not give Chinese officials routine administrative jurisdiction over Vietnamese provinces. France later treated recognition by another state as a way of determining who could negotiate Vietnam’s external relations. The issue was not simply terminological: changes in diplomatic recognition altered the court’s range of strategic partners.

Analytical caution. Modern international law provides one useful vocabulary for sovereignty but must not be projected without qualification onto older East Asian tributary practices.

Section 110.2

Catholic Missions, Religious Controversies, and French Imperial Ambitions

Christianity in Vietnam had a history well before French soldiers landed. Missionaries and Vietnamese catechists built congregations over generations, translating religious instruction, cultivating local patrons, and creating family and village networks. Communities differed by region and were often embedded in the same agricultural, commercial, and kinship worlds as their non-Christian neighbors. Some clergy relied on foreign institutions, yet the daily endurance of Catholic worship depended on Vietnamese believers, teachers, and lay organizers. To treat Catholics simply as a foreign presence would ignore that history; to treat them as a single political constituency in favor of conquest would misrepresent their varied judgments and vulnerabilities.

Successive Nguyễn governments restricted missionary activities and at times executed missionaries and Vietnamese converts. Their concerns included religious doctrine, but also the possibility that independent loyalties and foreign connections might challenge the administrative order. Religious conflicts interacted with denunciations by neighbors, local power struggles, and government suspicion after disturbances. Officials differed over enforcement. In some districts communities concealed their religious affiliation or negotiated with magistrates; elsewhere arrests and punishments were severe. Later French accounts made persecution central to justifying intervention, while Vietnamese official records often interpreted missionaries as agents of disorder. Historians must read both kinds of statements critically and distinguish evidence of actual suffering from the political use made of it.

French action developed through several partially overlapping institutions. Missionary societies and Catholic newspapers pressed for protection. Naval officers thought intervention could defend national prestige and create strategic advantages. Commercial advocates imagined access to Chinese markets and Vietnamese ports, sometimes without demonstrating that particular routes would be profitable. Napoleon III's Second Empire sought overseas status while balancing European commitments, military expenditures, and domestic constituencies. A French diplomatic mission under Charles de Montigny in 1856–1857 illustrates that the imperial government considered negotiation as well as force. There was no single French cabinet memorandum in which all subsequent annexations were laid out in advance; policy changed as reports, opportunities, and military events changed.

Spanish involvement also prevents a purely bilateral story. Spain ruled the Philippines and maintained its own missionary interests in Asia. Spanish forces, including soldiers recruited in the Philippines, participated in the expedition of 1858. Their presence reflected Catholic protection and imperial cooperation, not an arrangement in which Vietnamese territory would necessarily be divided between Spain and France. The differences between the two European partners became visible in later treaty terms and indemnity provisions. The campaign should therefore be seen as a coalition whose participants did not possess identical long-term objectives.

The Vietnamese court faced a genuine dilemma when reports of killings and arrests became international diplomatic instruments. It could refuse foreign demands and risk gunboat coercion, negotiate under conditions of unequal naval strength, or announce concessions whose local enforcement it could not guarantee. These options were constrained but not nonexistent. Vietnamese Catholics likewise faced difficult decisions. A French landing could bring protection from particular edicts while also intensifying suspicion, exposing Christian villages to retaliation, and placing believers between occupying soldiers and neighbors who opposed the invasion. No religious category reliably predicts each person's political allegiance.

The causal lesson is not that missionary persecution automatically produced a colony. It supplied an important legitimating argument for people who already had several reasons to act and for others who regarded intervention as a moral duty. Once troops arrived, military success, the interest of officers who controlled occupied ports, and calculations made in Paris acquired their own momentum. Religious controversy was one strand in a larger process whose outcomes were shaped by choices after the first landing, not simply by the complaints that preceded it.

Section 110.3

The Franco-Spanish Intervention at Đà Nẵng, 1858–1859

The expedition against Đà Nẵng in September 1858 followed from the strategic geography of the central Vietnamese coast. The port, often called Tourane in French sources, offered access to a broad bay and lay relatively near the imperial center at Huế. French planners hoped that a conspicuous naval demonstration, supported by Spanish troops from the Philippines, would compel the Nguyễn court to negotiate. Steam power and naval artillery permitted the expedition to attack coastal works quickly. The landing nevertheless did not bring the expected political collapse. An imperial government could lose positions near a harbor without losing the obedience of provincial officials or immediately surrendering its capital.

Vietnamese commanders used fortifications and containment rather than relying solely on a decisive open battle. Forces restricted the invaders' ability to move inland, while local roads, water sources, and the climate complicated expeditionary logistics. Monsoon conditions, illness, and difficulties obtaining food and labor eroded the occupiers' effectiveness. Cholera and dysentery in such operations could disable soldiers more reliably than enemy fire; one must still avoid repeating casualty totals without specifying their sources and what counts as a death attributable to disease. The expedition's difficulties reveal the limits of military asymmetry: a fleet could dominate sea approaches but not automatically supply or administer a hostile hinterland.

For the Nguyễn court, a defensive success was not uncomplicated. Maintaining troops around the landing area cost money and demanded supplies from nearby communities, and French naval mobility meant that a new attack could appear elsewhere. An official in Huế had to weigh whether to concentrate forces near the capital or preserve garrisons in the south and north. The imperial state operated across regions with distinct economies and patterns of settlement. Every decision to draw men from one province could leave another exposed to revolt or weaken its tax collection. French commanders exploited the uncertainty produced by this scale even when their first strategic objective failed.

The decision to redirect operations toward Saigon changed the nature of the confrontation. Saigon was a fortified administrative and commercial center connected to the Mekong Delta's rivers and agricultural surplus. It was farther from the Nguyễn court and presented opportunities for naval access through connected waterways. Taking it would give French forces a foothold supported by rice supplies and regional trade. The choice did not require the French to occupy all of Vietnam; it offered a more defensible position from which to bargain. A shift that looked like a solution to military difficulties at Đà Nẵng therefore opened the possibility of long-term territorial rule in the south.

The campaign also reshaped local conditions before any treaty. Troops required guides, provisions, boats, and places to store ammunition. Coastal people encountered blockade, requisition, and disrupted fishing and commerce. Missionary communities, merchants, port workers, Vietnamese soldiers, and village officials encountered the expedition from different positions. Treating the whole region as a passive stage for European operations would erase the people whose cooperation or resistance affected what the invading soldiers could accomplish. Local geographical knowledge mattered even though the fleets that used it were foreign.

A map of the campaign should therefore be read as a map of decisions. The distance between Đà Nẵng and Huế helps explain the original ambition; the route south to the Saigon River explains the later opportunity; the interior connections and river channels explain both the promise and the difficulties of holding territory. The result was not simply a failed attack followed by a successful one. It was a change from pressure on the monarch to the occupation of a region whose inhabitants would experience several decades of political and social transformation.

Section 110.4

The Conquest of Saigon and the Treaty of 1862

The occupation of Saigon in February 1859 gave French and Spanish forces a foothold but did not immediately settle control of the southern provinces. Units occupied important positions while Nguyễn commanders continued to organize resistance in the surrounding countryside. French operations were also affected by the wider war in China, which absorbed ships and troops. These circumstances made the early years of occupation unstable. A military flag over a fort or port was not the same as a functioning government that could assess fields, enforce contracts, collect customs, and secure routes beyond the garrison perimeter. The distinction between military seizure and durable authority becomes central to the history of Cochinchina.

Once French reinforcements became available, the balance shifted. The assault on the Vietnamese defensive lines at Kỳ Hòa in February 1861 was an important turning point, allowing the expedition to expand beyond its narrow initial position. Further campaigns placed pressure on provincial centers and waterways. Nguyễn commanders faced difficulties concentrating forces against mobile attacks and protecting the civilian resources on which defense depended. France's commanders increasingly regarded their occupied territory as an asset to be organized and retained, while the government at Huế had other costly crises to face, including unrest in the north. These pressures made negotiations possible without implying that the Nguyễn court accepted the justice of French claims.

The treaty signed at Saigon on 5 June 1862 was concluded by France and Spain on one side and the Nguyễn government on the other. Its Article 3 ceded Gia Định, Biên Hòa, Định Tường, and Côn Đảo to France in sovereignty. The islands, ports, and provinces named in such documents were political spaces populated by people who did not participate in drafting the instrument. The treaty also protected Christian worship and expanded rights of commerce. Article 4 constrained future Nguyễn territorial cessions to other foreign powers by requiring French consent, showing that the agreement weakened diplomatic independence even outside the ceded provinces. It was more than a settlement of the battlefield.

Article 8 imposed an indemnity of four million dollars payable over ten years, in annual payments of four hundred thousand; the treaty specified a conversion into taels for the Vietnamese government's payments and credited an earlier payment against the sum. The unit was the treaty dollar, commonly described in accounts as the piastre, not a modern U.S. dollar and not simply a four-million-franc levy. Such monetary precision matters because incorrect conversions can badly distort the apparent fiscal burden. The text also recognized a role for Spain, whose forces had participated in the war, even though the sovereign territorial acquisitions went to France.

Treaty obligations must be distinguished from implementation. Some positions occupied during campaigning were not transferred in the same way as the three named provinces, and the treaty made further arrangements concerning military movement and a French position at Vĩnh Long. The precise terms of clauses concerning commerce, amnesty, criminal fugitives, and ratification mattered to officials trying to determine where French soldiers could operate and where Nguyễn magistrates retained a role. The exchange of ratifications in 1863 added another step to the legal sequence. A date on a diplomatic text should never be used as proof that political life changed everywhere on the same day.

For inhabitants of the ceded provinces, the treaty raised questions that the signatories did not answer. Who would collect taxes in a village where an older official had fled? Would land titles or communal obligations be recognized by new authorities? What became of soldiers and villagers accused of helping resistance groups? Families linked to court service or missionary communities might interpret the new order in sharply different ways. The treaty's significance lies both in its legal clauses and in the gap between those clauses and the experience of occupying, defending, and living on the transferred land. That gap continued to generate conflict after 1862.

Section 110.5

The Treaty Settlement and Attempts to Recover Southern Vietnam, 1862–1865

The June 1862 settlement did not end bargaining about southern Vietnam. Officials in Huế continued to regard the loss of three provinces as a severe compromise, and some hoped that diplomacy might recover territory without another ruinous campaign. Phan Thanh Giản, a senior scholar-official who had participated in the treaty negotiations, led an embassy to Europe in 1863. His mission shows that the Nguyễn government did not merely retreat into an isolated traditional worldview. It sought to understand the policies of European governments and to use their diplomatic divisions. The embassy confronted a problem, however: the French empire's public promises and its colonial officers' calculations were not always aligned.

In France, positions differed over whether the new possessions were worth their costs. The Ministry of Marine and Colonies, naval commanders, commercial advocates, missionaries, and senior political officials assessed the territory according to different criteria. Some saw a fertile export region and a base for wider influence, while others worried about garrison expenses, disease, and a commitment that could divert resources from European priorities. Disagreement did not necessarily make a return of the provinces easy. A withdrawal might be seen as a loss of prestige, and a promise made in Paris could be resisted by men who had established administrative and commercial interests in Saigon.

The draft agreement associated with Gabriel Aubaret in 1864 offered a possible alternative: France considered returning the annexed provinces under arrangements that would preserve extensive French diplomatic, commercial, and strategic privileges. The proposed settlement was never ratified. Its failure illustrates contingency rather than a missed opportunity that can be reconstructed with certainty. We do not know what would have happened if the proposal had been executed. Officials in Vietnam might have disagreed over implementing its provisions, and French forces might still have sought deeper control later. Yet the very existence of the proposal disproves the idea that full annexation was simply a predetermined plan followed mechanically from the landing of 1858.

Local conditions reduced the space for compromise. French officers reported continued attacks and uncertainty in districts nominally settled by treaty. Vietnamese commanders and rural leaders sometimes refused to disband, while officials appointed by the court were caught between the requirement to honor diplomatic obligations and the expectation that they defend communities and royal authority. French negotiators could point to disorder as evidence that stronger control was needed; Vietnamese critics could argue that the presence of foreign soldiers created the disorder. The labels attached to resistance were therefore part of the struggle over legitimacy, not descriptions that historians should accept without examination.

There was also an important international connection. French influence in Cambodia was expanding during the same period, altering the geopolitical significance of the Mekong Delta and the remaining Nguyễn provinces to its west. French administrators viewed the river network not only as fields and villages but as the approach to Cambodia and potentially to markets inland. The pressures on the Nguyễn government's position were compounded by developments along borders that had never been sealed lines in the modern sense. This story belongs chiefly to southern Vietnam here; the wider reorganization of Cambodia forms the subject of the next chapter in the series.

The lesson of the negotiations is that imperial expansion could proceed through failed compromises as well as successful battles. The Nguyễn court tried envoys, proposals, and political reassurance. French policymakers considered costs and alternatives. Colonial officers and local networks pushed for outcomes that were not always identical to metropolitan policy. When the possibility of withdrawal faded, France's position in the south was strengthened, but the process had involved real choice at several points. A convincing historical explanation must account for those choices instead of explaining the result only from the fact that it ultimately occurred.

Section 110.6

The Annexation of the Western Provinces in 1867

The three western provinces—Vĩnh Long, An Giang, and Hà Tiên—remained under nominal Nguyễn administration after the 1862 treaty. Yet their position was increasingly precarious. They bordered French-controlled districts and formed part of a larger Mekong region in which traders, cultivators, armed groups, and religious networks crossed lines that diplomats tried to define. Officials at Huế could issue orders, but orders could not guarantee that garrisons were adequately supplied or that provincial authorities would receive assistance if French forces attacked. Residents of the western provinces experienced this uncertainty through trade routes, taxation, rumors of troop movement, and disputes about the authority of neighboring governments.

French commanders regarded the partial settlement as unstable. From their perspective, control of the western districts would provide defensible boundaries and reduce the need to negotiate with Nguyễn officials across a shifting frontier. Strategic arguments were reinforced by commercial hopes and by the concerns of officers who had already developed careers administering Cochinchina. The language of security obscured an expansionary choice: it defined the presence of an independent neighboring administration as a danger that could be removed through occupation. The fact that an imperial administration could describe its next conquest as a means of stabilizing an earlier one is a recurring pattern in nineteenth-century colonial history.

In June 1867, forces under Admiral Pierre-Paul de La Grandière took control of the western provinces. Phan Thanh Giản, then responsible for the region, judged that armed resistance would lead to destruction without a reasonable prospect of victory. Vĩnh Long was handed over, and the remaining provincial administration passed under French control. His conduct has been judged differently by later writers, some emphasizing the surrender of territory and others the moral burden of a man facing overwhelming coercion. He subsequently took his own life. Simplistic categories of cowardice or heroism fail to account for the strategic circumstances, the expectations of Confucian official morality, and the human cost he believed resistance might impose on the population.

The province names and dates are not mere bookkeeping. Annexation meant that civil and fiscal claims could be made by a new government; it also altered the status of subjects who had previously been governed through Vietnamese institutions. Land, canals, local policing, village assemblies, court records, and market duties became sites of negotiation and resistance. Some officials left rather than serve the new rulers, while others continued local responsibilities or entered the French administration. Some merchants benefited from new arrangements; others lost established protections. The presence of indigenous intermediaries in colonial government should not be mistaken for widespread consent, just as nonparticipation in an uprising does not prove that inhabitants welcomed foreign rule.

Resistance did not cease with the disappearance of organized Nguyễn provincial defense. Rural militias, local religious figures, and other opponents of the occupation remained active in different places. French reporting sometimes grouped them together as brigands, although their motives and relationships to the population varied. An attack on a patrol might be initiated by followers of a political leader, an armed group protecting local revenues, or individuals caught up in a broader wartime economy. Historians need corroboration before assigning every act of violence to a single political cause. The continuing campaigns also placed heavy burdens on villagers who could be suspected of supplying one side or the other.

By the end of 1867 France held all six provinces traditionally grouped as southern Cochinchina. The outcome expanded an administrative jurisdiction, but did not create an instantly peaceful colony. It linked the earlier treaty of 1862 to a more extensive form of direct territorial government and widened the gulf between the Nguyễn court's claimed moral responsibility and its ability to protect subjects in the south. That gulf became a central source of debate among Vietnamese officials and later historians assessing the collapse of sovereignty.

Section 110.7

Southern Resistance: Trương Định, Nguyễn Trung Trực, and Local Communities

Trương Định's career demonstrates why southern resistance cannot be explained simply as the action of an army that ignored an otherwise settled treaty. He had organized local military colonists and supporters in the southern provinces, drawing on relations among settlers, village elites, cultivators, and fighting men. His authority was not identical to that of a regular provincial governor. When the 1862 treaty required the court to cease organized hostility, the problem became whether loyalty to the emperor meant obeying an order to withdraw or continuing to defend the land and people from occupation. Trương Định's refusal to abandon resistance exposed that contradiction and helped turn local wartime leadership into a political challenge to the settlement.

Colonial reports described his movement as rebellion, but from the standpoint of supporters it could be understood as defense against invasion and the betrayal of obligations that rulers owed their subjects. The labels were not neutral. An occupying government had an interest in presenting armed opponents as criminals after a peace treaty had been signed; resistance leaders could claim a higher loyalty to the dynasty, community, or moral order than obedience to a particular command. These arguments should be reconstructed from available correspondence and later accounts with attention to the circumstances of their preservation. Much of what is remembered today was shaped by subsequent commemoration as well as by contemporary action.

Nguyễn Trung Trực is another important figure, associated with the destruction of the French vessel Espérance in December 1861 and with continuing resistance later in the decade. His capture and execution in 1868 became part of a durable memory of southern opposition. The distinction between an attack on military transport, an assault on an isolated post, and a conventional battle matters because each required different resources and exposed different communities to reprisals. Small mobile bands could exploit familiarity with waterways and villages, yet they could not easily replace provincial tax systems, arsenals, or the supply of a large army. This imbalance shaped both resistance tactics and the French countermeasures designed to separate fighters from their supporters.

Village life was inseparable from warfare. Boats carried rice and people along canals; markets moved goods and information; family relationships connected men serving in different armed groups. French forces needed interpreters, guides, tax officials, and suppliers, and these activities could expose collaborators to retaliation. Resistance leaders similarly depended on rice, shelter, and intelligence from inhabitants who were not always able to refuse. A village might support resistance out of conviction, comply under duress, or attempt to remain beyond the reach of both sides. Accounts that divide the population into loyalists and collaborators overlook the pressures on people trying to preserve livelihoods and households.

Religious and social diversity also mattered. Vietnamese Catholics did not all respond alike, and wartime retaliation could turn confessional identity into a dangerous marker even when individual congregations had no part in military decision-making. Khmer communities and other people living in the broader southern region had their own relations to court authority and expanding French control. A resistance narrative focused entirely on famous Vietnamese men risks erasing these experiences. Women supplied labor, maintained households, moved information, and endured displacement; their actions are much less fully documented than those of male commanders and officials. The asymmetry of the archive should be disclosed rather than allowed to determine what counts as history.

The southern movements failed to reverse annexation, but failure of that immediate objective does not make their political significance imaginary. They revealed limits in the ability of treaties to command obedience and forced both French officials and Vietnamese rulers to confront local claims about legitimate authority. Their memory later fed national narratives, yet the original participants did not necessarily share the categories of twentieth-century nationalism. The historian's task is to recover their circumstances and choices before interpreting their subsequent symbolic uses.

Section 110.8

Everyday Life during the Conquest of Cochinchina

A household in the southern provinces during the 1860s experienced conquest less as a continuous series of famous battles than as unstable expectations about what would happen tomorrow. Rice cultivation depended on seasonal water, coordinated labor, canal maintenance, seed reserves, and access to animals and tools. Farmers might have to decide whether to plant a field when soldiers could requisition a harvest or when evacuation might be necessary before the crop ripened. A market boat needed reliable passage along waterways that could be blockaded or inspected. Families also had to ask which authority would recognize a debt, settle an inheritance dispute, or protect a customary arrangement when a village official disappeared.

The preconquest world was not free of coercion. Nguyễn officials demanded taxes and labor; local notables exercised influence that could be unequal; military campaigns could impose burdens. But conquest introduced uncertainty about which established authorities remained effective. A district official might continue serving the court in name while a French garrison controlled the nearby port, or a French administrator might appoint a local intermediary whose legitimacy was contested. Provincial archives record regulations and revenue more readily than the deliberations of families. To reconstruct daily life, historians combine those administrative records with petitions, missionary writings, Vietnamese accounts, and evidence about land use and settlement patterns.

Movement mattered. Some people fled areas of fighting for villages controlled by the Nguyễn court or for districts where kin could offer shelter. Others returned when the danger seemed lower or remained because crops, elderly relatives, or debts tied them to the locality. Displacement altered labor supplies and family support networks. People whose homes survived could still suffer if the movement of boats, salt, cloth, or rice was interrupted. Markets linked households beyond the immediate battlefield, making an event in a fortified town capable of affecting a village many kilometers away. The economic impact of occupation therefore cannot be measured solely from the area officially annexed.

Religious life was affected in contradictory ways. Colonial authorities claimed to protect Christian worship after the treaty, but association with the occupying power could place particular Catholic families at risk from retaliation. Buddhist temples, village cults, ancestor rituals, and communal ceremonies continued, although war could interrupt donations, processions, or the maintenance of buildings. Confucian learning remained important to educated families even when its route into Nguyễn state service was narrowed in French-held territory. New forms of schooling and administration emerged gradually. It would be anachronistic to imagine that the full institutional apparatus of later French colonial society arrived with the first landing.

Relations between women and men were shaped by work, kinship, and the disruptions of conflict. Women farmed, traded, maintained households, cared for children and elders, and sometimes supported displaced relatives or fighters. Their economic responsibilities could increase when men were conscripted, absent, killed, or in hiding. Yet the available official sources more often describe heads of households or male political leaders. A source analysis that infers every family experience from these records would overstate what can be known. Household narratives should identify which details are grounded in contemporary evidence and which are cautious reconstructions of ordinary social practices.

The central lesson of everyday-life history is that occupation transformed the problem of ordinary governance. Who collected a tax, who maintained an embankment, who registered a marriage, and who could travel without interference were not minor questions beside the treaties. They determined whether a claim to sovereignty became real in the daily lives of inhabitants. They also help explain why some people resisted openly, others entered new administrative relationships, and many adapted in ways that leave little direct documentary trace.

Division 3 Tonkin, the Red River, and the Protectorate Treaties
Section 110.9

Tonkin, the Red River, and the Struggle for Access to China

The decision to focus on Tonkin emerged from a larger regional geography. The Red River rises in the uplands of what is now Yunnan, travels through northern Vietnam, and enters a densely cultivated delta before reaching the Gulf of Tonkin. French merchants and naval officers imagined that boats moving upriver from the coast could unlock commerce with southwestern China. In their calculations, the river offered an alternative to costly overland routes and an opportunity to turn Vietnam into a corridor serving markets far beyond its borders. These hopes were persuasive in political argument because they promised that intervention could pay for itself; their economic realism was more uncertain. River conditions, border controls, transport expenses, and competition among existing commercial networks limited what could be achieved.

Tonkin was neither empty nor commercially isolated when French interest grew. Hanoi was an established political and cultural center. The delta sustained an extensive rice economy, craft production, local markets, and religious and scholarly institutions. Chinese merchants and borderland communities connected northern Vietnam with Yunnan and Guangxi through trade conducted under overlapping customs and political authorities. The northern uplands were inhabited by varied peoples whose relations with Vietnamese and Qing officials did not always conform to claims made from either capital. Armed bands displaced by turmoil in southern China had also crossed into the region. Their presence affected security but did not make every frontier community a source of lawlessness.

The Nguyễn administration struggled with the northern frontier partly because it had several obligations to balance. Provincial officials needed to protect villages, collect revenue, and preserve commercial routes while dealing with armed groups capable of negotiating, fighting, and changing patrons. Qing officials might tolerate or oppose particular groups according to frontier circumstances. Vietnamese commanders sometimes used such forces to check a more immediate threat. A colonial description of an armed group as a pirate band therefore tells us how the observer wanted to define authority as much as it tells us about the group's activities. Historians should examine recruitment, command, taxation, and political alliances before adopting the label.

French commercial expectations intersected with strategic anxieties. A northern position might complement the existing colony of Cochinchina, prevent rival powers from securing influence near China's southern border, and allow French diplomats to reshape Vietnamese foreign relations. Yet French officials differed over the costs of occupying a densely populated delta and a mountainous frontier. European merchants wanted access but did not necessarily agree that military annexation was the cheapest way to obtain it. The gap between trade advocacy and military policy is important: a government can justify an intervention in commercial language even when immediate operations cost more than trade receipts could plausibly replace.

Geography also complicated French action. A force that seized Hanoi might still be vulnerable along roads and waterways connecting it to the sea. The Red River's branches, seasonal flows, and surrounding settlements required constant attention. Fortified towns could be captured without securing all routes to them, while defenders could use knowledge of the terrain to isolate smaller expeditionary units. The northern delta differed from southern Cochinchina not only in its distance from Saigon but also in the density of existing Nguyễn institutions and proximity to Qing forces. Those differences shaped the much longer and more diplomatically contested road to conquest.

The Red River should thus be understood as an imagined commercial artery, a real network of local livelihoods, and a strategic corridor with uncertain boundaries. Each meaning drew different actors into conflict. A French merchant seeking to ship supplies upriver, a Hanoi magistrate responsible for security, a borderland trader dependent on familiar rules, and a Qing commander concerned with the frontier did not see the same waterway. Their differing interests help explain why what began as a commercial dispute in the early 1870s developed into a crisis over the sovereignty of northern Vietnam.

Section 110.10

The Garnier Expedition of 1873 and the Treaty of Saigon of 1874

The Tonkin crisis of 1873 arose from the collision of merchant initiative and public authority. Jean Dupuis sought to transport goods, including weapons and other supplies, along the Red River toward Yunnan. His operations led to disputes with Nguyễn officials, who regarded the movement of armed commerce and the conduct of outsiders as questions of security and jurisdiction. French authorities in Saigon could present the dispute as a demand to protect trade or resolve local disorder. Neither formulation made the Nguyễn government's objections meaningless. The court still claimed to regulate commerce and armed entry into territory it governed, even if its ability to enforce that claim was uneven.

Francis Garnier was sent north with a limited official mandate but acted far beyond a narrowly diplomatic role. In November 1873 he seized the citadel of Hanoi and expanded military operations into other northern positions. Small European-led detachments were able to exploit the vulnerabilities of fixed defenses, yet their rapid successes exceeded their capacity to establish a stable administration. The fall of a citadel was politically dramatic, but French forces remained dependent on waterways and exposed to counterattack. Garnier was killed in December 1873 in fighting involving the Black Flag force under Liu Yongfu. The episode exposed the risk of an expedition whose ambitions had outgrown the reinforcement and legitimacy available to it.

The Nguyễn court and French representatives subsequently negotiated to prevent the confrontation from becoming an open-ended war. French troops withdrew from important northern positions, but the settlement was not a simple restoration of the status quo. The 15 March 1874 Treaty of Saigon confirmed French possession of Cochinchina and expanded the terms under which French commerce and officials could operate in northern Vietnam. The diplomatic language recognized Vietnam's independence from foreign suzerainty and claimed protection of that independence by France. This formulation undermined Qing diplomatic pretensions while increasing France's influence over the court that it purported to recognize as independent.

A treaty that pronounces independence can simultaneously diminish practical autonomy. French control of southern territory was already established; new commercial privileges and diplomatic arrangements gave France further means to intervene. The distinction becomes clearer if students separate four questions: who possessed sovereignty over the provinces already ceded, who controlled foreign relations, who could police the Red River, and who would enforce the treaty when interpretations differed. The treaty did not establish the definitive protectorate of the 1880s. It did, however, alter the legal and political terrain on which later interventions occurred.

The agreement also illustrates the limits of diplomatic texts as historical sources. A treaty records what authorized negotiators were willing or compelled to sign, not the private convictions of a ruler, the wishes of provincial officials, or the judgment of farmers along the river. Its provisions could be read differently in Paris, Saigon, Huế, Hanoi, and Beijing. Qing authorities had reason to reject France's effort to reclassify their relationship with Vietnam. Vietnamese officials had reason to question whether French promises of protection were compatible with repeated demands for special rights. These tensions persisted because the treaty had tried to settle several distinct conflicts with clauses that could not themselves create consensus.

There was also a lesson in personal initiative. Garnier's decision to seize Hanoi made official French policy more complicated, not more coherent. The subsequent retreat demonstrated the government's reluctance to sustain every action of a distant officer, but the treaty converted the crisis into further recognized privileges. A short-lived military adventure therefore contributed to a long-term redistribution of authority. That pattern would recur during the Rivière expedition: officers could provoke crises, governments could disavow or revise their actions, and treaties could subsequently consolidate advantages generated under extreme pressure.

Section 110.11

Henri Rivière, the Black Flag Army, and the Renewed Tonkin Crisis, 1882–1883

The relative diplomatic calm after 1874 was unstable because the underlying disputes in Tonkin remained. French merchants and consuls complained about obstacles to trade and insecurity on the Red River. The Nguyễn government sought to preserve provincial authority and limit foreign demands without triggering a war it feared it could not win. Chinese merchants and armed groups continued to cross the northern border, while the Qing government viewed French encroachment as a matter affecting the security and prestige of its southern frontier. These interests intersected in ways that a commercial clause in a treaty could not resolve.

Henri Rivière arrived with military forces and, like Garnier before him, advanced from a contested administrative mandate to direct coercion. In April 1882 he seized the Hanoi citadel. The operation demonstrated that a well-armed expeditionary detachment could overcome fixed defenses, but it did not eliminate the Nguyễn government's claim to rule or the ability of nearby forces to contest French movement. Local commanders sought reinforcement, and Nguyễn authorities appealed to Qing officials and to forces including Liu Yongfu's Black Flag Army. Such alliances should not be imagined as proof of a single Vietnamese-Chinese political identity. They were tactical responses by actors who feared the growth of French power for different reasons.

Liu Yongfu's forces had emerged from the turmoil of nineteenth-century southern China and developed military and commercial interests in the frontier region. They could engage in revenue extraction, fighting, and negotiation with state authorities. To French officers the Black Flags were often described as pirates, a label that delegitimized their political role and justified operations against them. To Nguyễn officials, they could be useful allies against an immediate invading force, although their independence also posed problems. A full account must therefore analyze their activities without accepting either a romantic image of national defenders or the blanket criminalization of colonial reports.

Rivière was killed near Hanoi in May 1883 in an encounter involving Black Flag forces. His death became a rallying point in French political debate and strengthened pressure for a larger commitment. Yet explaining French escalation solely as revenge would miss the strategic and commercial ambitions already at work. The existence of French forces in Hanoi, the prestige of officers, the prospects of access to China, and the expectations of colonial officials all pushed toward stronger intervention. Events acquired political meaning through newspapers and parliamentary debates in France, which did not necessarily reflect the views of the population whose territory was being contested.

The Nguyễn court faced a simultaneous crisis of military defense and legitimacy. Requesting Qing assistance could protect the northern frontier, but invited greater involvement by another power whose relationship with Vietnam was itself disputed. Negotiating with France might avert immediate bombardment but risk severe limits on sovereignty. The court did not have unlimited choices, yet different officials advanced different strategies. In the summer of 1883 these disagreements became more acute as Tự Đức died and the imperial succession entered a period of rapid uncertainty. The vulnerability of a court dealing with succession could be exploited by an adversary whose ships threatened its capital.

The Rivière expedition thus links the economic geography of the Red River to the diplomatic destruction of Nguyễn independence. A specific dispute over trade and security became an episode in which European officers occupied Vietnamese positions, local and Qing-backed forces resisted, and the death of one commander helped mobilize broader French intervention. The subsequent treaty settlement was neither an unavoidable response to piracy nor a simple consequence of French technological superiority. It was made through successive political decisions, each narrowing the court's room for maneuver.

Section 110.12

The Treaties of Huế, 1883 and 1884

The death of Emperor Tự Đức in July 1883 intensified a struggle within the Nguyễn court at precisely the moment when French forces were pressing their advantage. Succession and regency were matters of high politics, but they also affected the court's ability to issue consistent orders and establish an authoritative negotiating position. Factions disagreed about accommodation and resistance, while officials tried to preserve royal legitimacy under the threat of direct assault. Military action at the approaches to Huế created an immediate coercive context for diplomatic negotiation. The resulting treaty should therefore be read as an instrument signed under pressure, not a voluntary decision to redesign Vietnamese sovereignty.

The Harmand agreement of 25 August 1883 accepted a French protectorate on terms that transferred extensive powers. Article I in a contemporary printed translation placed Vietnam's foreign relations, including those involving China, under French control. Other provisions assigned ports, customs, roads, communications, and important police or military responsibilities to the French sphere. Although Nguyễn officials continued in office, the text made them subject to French oversight in critical matters. The position of the resident at Huế had symbolic as well as practical significance: it inserted a representative of foreign power into proximity with the monarch, whose authority had previously been expressed through court-appointed institutions.

The text made a severe territorial rearrangement. Bình Thuận was to be attached to French Cochinchina, while the north-central provinces Thanh Hóa, Nghệ An, and Hà Tĩnh were placed outside the reduced core of Annam under the proposed administrative division. This fragmentation went beyond abstract questions about foreign relations. It threatened to separate populations and officials long linked through the Nguyễn administrative system. The treaty also provided for French intervention in Tonkin and sustained the military framework within which political decisions would be enforced. In presenting detailed clauses, historians should distinguish the printed treaty's proposed arrangements from changes actually implemented during the unsettled months that followed.

The replacement treaty negotiated by Jules Patenôtre on 6 June 1884 revised the earlier agreement without restoring full independence. Bình Thuận and the three north-central provinces were returned to Annam's administrative jurisdiction, making the territorial settlement less punitive and more workable. This was a real difference, not merely a softer tone in diplomatic language. Yet France retained decisive control over foreign relations and preserved mechanisms for supervising the Nguyễn government. The court's ceremonial survival could coexist with the loss of independent diplomatic standing. A ruler could still receive officials and conduct rituals while other people determined which foreign agreements could be concluded.

The two treaties reveal that a colonial protectorate was neither an unchanging formula nor necessarily a uniform mode of rule. In central Vietnam, French authorities had reasons to retain local institutions through which administration could continue; in Tonkin, military security and frontier policy encouraged a stronger presence of French officers. The distinction between legal texts, official appointments, and actual control was often blurred by contemporaries seeking to proclaim a settled outcome. A treaty's legal language should be placed beside the extent of troop deployment, the decisions of provincial authorities, and the continued existence of armed opposition.

For source analysis, the strongest comparison asks which clauses identified the monarch's surviving role, which transferred control of international relations, and which treated provinces as units to be reassigned by negotiation. The 1883 translation in the United States diplomatic series is itself a mediated text: it was transmitted from a printed version in a Saigon newspaper and requires care when compared with French or Vietnamese originals. The 1884 agreement should similarly be tied to an identifiable edition. These documentary limits do not eliminate the central conclusion: by mid-1884 French power could dictate the terms under which Nguyễn government was permitted to continue.

Section 110.13

Qing China, the Sino-French War, and the Struggle for Tonkin, 1884–1885

The confrontation in Tonkin became a conflict between France and Qing China because both governments regarded northern Vietnam as strategically consequential. For the Qing court, the frontier was connected to the security of Guangxi and Yunnan, the stability of southern provinces recently affected by rebellion, and the prestige of established relationships with neighboring rulers. For France, accepting Qing military activity in Tonkin threatened the credibility of its newly proclaimed protectorate. Vietnamese rulers and provincial authorities were central to the dispute's origin but did not control the diplomatic framework through which the larger powers increasingly sought to settle it. This asymmetry is one of the war's defining historical features.

Before open war, French and Qing forces operated alongside Vietnamese and Black Flag formations in an unstable landscape of alliances. The French occupied Sơn Tây in late 1883 and Bắc Ninh in early 1884, demonstrating the effectiveness of combined expeditionary operations against important positions. Yet those victories did not resolve control over every route or command unquestioned obedience from the frontier. French units encountered enemies of differing military organization and allegiance, including Qing regulars, local defenders, and irregular forces. It is essential to distinguish their roles instead of presenting the conflict as a straightforward two-army campaign in which the population of Vietnam played no part.

The preliminary Tianjin convention of 11 May 1884 sought to avert a wider war. China agreed to withdraw forces from Tonkin and respect agreements concluded between France and the Huế court, while France promised to respect the southern Chinese border and abandoned an immediate indemnity demand. However, disagreements over implementation and timing led to the Bắc Lệ confrontation in June. French political authorities regarded the incident as a breach of the understanding, and escalation followed. Military and diplomatic records from each side reveal different interpretations of responsibility; historians should not reduce the transition to war to an uncomplicated claim that one actor deliberately violated an unambiguous order.

Fighting then widened beyond the Tonkin frontier. French naval forces attacked Chinese maritime positions and brought war to places whose inhabitants had little direct connection to the Nguyễn court. Land campaigning in northern Vietnam included advances and reversals around the approaches to Lạng Sơn. The political crisis surrounding the French retreat from Lạng Sơn in March 1885 contributed to the fall of the Ferry government in Paris, an example of events in Southeast Asia reshaping metropolitan politics. Yet the battlefield did not deliver one simple result. France could suffer serious reverses without relinquishing its protectorate ambitions, and Qing commanders could fight effectively without persuading their government to undertake an unlimited war.

The French expedition relied on ships, artillery, trained troops, and the ability to project force along coastal routes. Qing forces drew on provincial armies, commanders with varying resources, and logistical networks oriented toward the Chinese frontier. Both faced problems of supply and coordination. Vietnamese communities had to manage garrisons, requisition, displacement, and interrupted trade. Those experiences do not appear adequately in diplomatic maps showing arrows and occupied towns. The same village could be described by foreign soldiers as a strategic objective and by inhabitants as farmland, burial grounds, and the center of family obligations.

A historically precise account avoids turning the outcome into proof that one civilization was superior to another. France obtained an advantageous diplomatic settlement, but the path involved contested operations, failed understandings, changes of government, and decisions made far from the Vietnamese people most directly affected. The military record helps explain why Qing leaders accepted a settlement. It does not establish that a Vietnamese political community consented to French rule or that resistance within Vietnam would end when France and China agreed to peace.

The Treaty of Tianjin of 1885 and the International Settlement

Two agreements must be distinguished in the diplomacy of the Sino-French conflict. The preliminary convention at Tianjin of 11 May 1884 was a proposed basis for a broader settlement; the definitive treaty concluded on 9 June 1885 followed a period of warfare. The sequence matters because each document belongs to a different political context. In the first, negotiators attempted to prevent the crisis from broadening. In the second, they established terms for relations after conflict and sought to delimit responsibilities along the frontier. Treating them as a single unchanging treaty would erase both the military escalation and the choices that followed it.

The 1885 treaty bound China to respect the agreements France had made or would make with the court at Huế, while France undertook obligations concerning order in border provinces and the security of the frontier. Its Article II is particularly important for understanding the claimed legitimacy of French rule: the Qing government agreed not to undermine the protectorate structure France was building. The treaty also addressed boundary commissions, cross-border commerce, travel documents, and the status of people moving between Chinese and Vietnamese territory. These clauses show how diplomatic recognition was tied to a practical attempt to govern movement and trade. The document did not simply exchange grand declarations of friendship.

The treaty did not make Vietnam a signatory to the Franco-Chinese settlement. Vietnamese officials and communities had therefore to live with arrangements negotiated by governments asserting different claims over their territory. A Qing decision to respect French treaties should not be translated into the claim that China transferred a province it had been administering as its own. Qing-Vietnamese relations had involved ritual precedence and periodic political intervention, but the Nguyễn state had governed the country. Similarly, a French declaration that Qing interference was settled did not itself establish effective control over every Vietnamese village, district, or upland frontier.

French diplomacy pursued several connected aims. Its government wanted secure acknowledgment of its position in Tonkin, a stable boundary with China, and trading arrangements that could make its costly intervention appear commercially worthwhile. Qing negotiators sought an end to conflict, protection of their southern territories, and preservation of diplomatic dignity. The treaty's language of reciprocal respect concealed an unequal distribution of consequences. For France and China, it established a basis for peaceful relations; for Vietnam, it narrowed the field in which its rulers could seek alternative support against French demands.

Reading the text also exposes differences between political aspiration and enforcement. A boundary commission could be appointed by treaty, but marking a border across mountainous and settled terrain required later surveys and negotiations. A promise to restrain armed bands did not make those groups vanish. Commercial rights could be granted without ensuring that tariffs, roads, or river conditions made trade profitable. These implementation problems were especially acute where administrative power depended on local intermediaries and where groups moved across boundaries that had not previously functioned as rigid barriers.

In historical synthesis, the Tianjin settlement should be placed alongside the treaties of Huế. The latter concerned the arrangements by which France claimed to supervise Nguyễn government; the former sought to remove Qing diplomatic and military objections. Together they altered the external setting of Vietnamese political life. They did not erase village loyalties or make foreign occupation legitimate in the eyes of all inhabitants. Events in Huế in July 1885, just weeks after the final Tianjin treaty, demonstrated that an international settlement and a stable domestic political order were entirely different achievements.

The Huế Crisis, Hàm Nghi, and the Cần Vương Proclamation

The July 1885 crisis at Huế emerged from a court divided over the terms of French protection and over which officials could speak legitimately for the dynasty. The emperor's position had been weakened by rapid succession changes after Tự Đức's death, and French officials had become direct participants in determining the political conditions under which monarchs and regents could operate. Tôn Thất Thuyết and his associates favored resistance to what they saw as intolerable foreign domination. Other senior figures argued that the survival of the monarchy required accommodation. These disagreements had immediate military implications because court factions controlled soldiers, arsenals, and access to the young emperor.

In early July, forces associated with Tôn Thất Thuyết attacked French positions at Huế. The assault failed, and French troops counterattacked. The fighting endangered the capital and led to the flight of Tôn Thất Thuyết with Emperor Hàm Nghi toward mountainous areas. The movement of the emperor transformed the conflict from a dispute among officials into a challenge over the bearer of legitimate royal authority. A palace could be occupied, and another monarch could be supported by French officials, but a ruler outside their control could still be invoked as the focus of loyalty by scholar-officials and communities resisting the protectorate.

The Cần Vương appeal, commonly translated as Aid the King, drew on Confucian obligations to the monarch and on the idea that legitimate government required opposition to foreign interference. It was not identical to a twentieth-century proclamation of national independence in the name of a sovereign people. The restoration of rightful dynastic authority was central to its political language. Yet this difference should not be used to deny that participants were opposing colonial domination. Historical actors can resist foreign conquest through ideas that do not exactly match later nationalist doctrines. The movement's vocabulary reveals the resources of legitimacy available to leaders in the 1880s.

The appeal reached officials, village elites, local militias, and communities in differing ways. Some scholar-officials believed their obligations required action; others continued to serve courts or provincial administrations under French supervision. Religious divisions became acute as insurgents sometimes suspected Catholic communities of association with French power and carried out attacks. These attacks were real episodes of violence against civilians, not merely unfortunate misunderstandings. At the same time, Catholic Vietnamese cannot be treated collectively as agents of the French. Their positions varied by locality and circumstance, and many confronted pressures from both colonial authorities and insurgents.

French authorities also sought legitimacy through the continuing Nguyễn monarchy. The selection and maintenance of a ruler who would operate under the protectorate provided the appearance of continuity with an established political order. The strategy rested on a real institution whose ceremonies and personnel still mattered to Vietnamese society, while transforming its diplomatic and military independence. Historians must therefore distinguish the institution of monarchy from claims made by rival individuals to embody it. Following the emperor's flight, both colonial officials and Cần Vương supporters could claim that they were defending orderly government, but they meant fundamentally different distributions of political authority.

The crisis explains why the diplomatic triumphs of 1884 and 1885 did not end the conquest. Control of ports and treaty signatures had not eliminated the political capacity of local leaders to organize fighters, claim moral authority, or mobilize supplies. The Cần Vương movement that followed was geographically uneven and militarily fragmented, yet it showed that the question of legitimate rule was still open in much of the countryside. Understanding it requires attention to court politics, regional networks, religious tensions, and the experiences of villagers as well as to campaigns and proclamations.

The Cần Vương Resistance, 1885–1896

The Cần Vương resistance took shape through regional networks rather than through a single command capable of directing all fighters from a central headquarters. The appeal to aid Hàm Nghi supplied a shared moral language, but groups operated in different ecological and political settings. Delta villages, upland districts, fortified settlements, and forested hills offered different resources for defense. Scholar-officials drew on personal reputation, examination networks, family connections, and relations with local notables. These links could mobilize men and food, but they did not solve the difficulties of transporting weapons, coordinating campaigns, or sustaining resistance against an opponent with larger logistical resources.

The Bãi Sậy movement in the northern delta exploited the cover provided by wetlands, villages, and a complicated waterway system. Its fighters relied on the intimate geography of a settled agricultural landscape rather than on permanent control of major cities. The Ba Đình struggle in Thanh Hóa involved fortified positions and concentrated defense, producing a different set of opportunities and vulnerabilities. The Hương Khê movement associated with Phan Đình Phùng operated across parts of Hà Tĩnh and Nghệ An, where forested and mountainous terrain helped sustain a prolonged insurgency. A chapter that collapses these campaigns into one continuous march misses the strategic differences that made resistance both persistent and fragmented.

Hàm Nghi's capture in 1888 and exile to Algeria did not immediately end the movement. The cause had already acquired a social life beyond the physical presence of the emperor. Leaders could continue claiming fidelity to legitimate rule and opposition to foreign power, even while the colonial administration promoted a monarch willing to function under French supervision. The persistence of campaigns after the emperor's removal demonstrates that political legitimacy cannot be reduced to possession of a palace or the body of a ruler. It was expressed through allegiance, memory, correspondence, regional organization, and the refusal to accept colonial authority.

French counterinsurgency relied on patrols, fortified positions, intelligence, recruitment of local auxiliaries, and pressure on communities suspected of helping fighters. Military reports often describe operations in the vocabulary of pacification, but that administrative term can conceal substantial coercion. Arrests, destruction, forced movement, and requisition affected civilians, while insurgent actions also imposed costs on inhabitants asked to provide supplies or loyalty. It is important to be precise about attribution: not every rural hardship can be assigned to one commander or one side without evidence. Still, a campaign against armed movements that depended on villages necessarily became a struggle over civilian cooperation and survival.

Phan Đình Phùng died in late 1895, and the remaining Hương Khê resistance was suppressed in 1896. Those dates mark a major phase of military consolidation, not an abrupt disappearance of all opposition to France. Other forms of local conflict, refusal, and reform-minded political argument continued. Some future leaders would conclude that the moral resources and military organization of the Cần Vương era were inadequate for the new colonial situation, but the later political transformations belong to subsequent chapters. The present narrative must end with the situation known to participants in the 1890s, not with the future projected backward as their intention.

Comparing southern resistance in the 1860s with Cần Vương highlights both continuity and change. Both drew on dynastic loyalties and local defense, but after 1885 the monarch himself became an especially prominent symbol for a network of movements fighting an established protectorate. The French state had gained stronger diplomatic recognition and a broader military presence; the Vietnamese opposition had become geographically extensive but could not consistently coordinate across regions. The failures of these campaigns help explain the consolidation of French control. Their endurance explains why conquest must be studied as a contested social process rather than the legal implementation of treaties alone.

Section 110.17

Colony, Protectorate, and the Fragmentation of Vietnamese Government

By the later 1880s, the geographical names Cochinchina, Annam, and Tonkin referred not only to regions of Vietnam but to different arrangements of French imperial power. Cochinchina had been ceded and annexed as a colony, with French officials claiming direct sovereign authority over its administration. Annam remained centered on Huế and the Nguyễn court but functioned under a protectorate that deprived the monarch of independent foreign relations and subjected important decisions to French supervision. Tonkin was likewise labeled a protectorate, yet the proximity of the Chinese frontier, a history of military campaigns, and continuing rural conflict encouraged a particularly strong French administrative and security presence. A single expression such as French Vietnam therefore hides meaningful differences in legal standing and daily government.

The words colony and protectorate must be interpreted in their historical setting. In legal arguments presented by French authorities, a protectorate implied a surviving indigenous government, a ruler whose legitimacy might continue to organize internal affairs, and an external power assuming responsibility for international relations. In practice, the power that controlled troops, customs, taxation, and appointments could influence many aspects of internal life. A colonial government could preserve local titles and rituals while changing their political substance. Conversely, the continuation of village institutions did not prove that all ordinary administration had been entirely remade. Formal legal status and effective authority need to be studied together rather than treated as synonyms.

The distinction mattered to Vietnamese officials. In Cochinchina, careers linked to the imperial examinations and provincial Nguyễn administration were disrupted by direct annexation. New forms of French administrative service and translation provided alternatives for some educated families. In Annam, officials could remain within a court-centered hierarchy while confronting French residents, military demands, and restrictions on policy. In Tonkin, provincial mandarins often operated in settings where French forces, administrative officers, and local armed groups all competed to define authority. Whether a magistrate retained a title was less important than whether he could enforce judgments, collect taxes, and refuse an order from a foreign supervisor.

The organization of colonial security reflected this unevenness. French officers depended on European troops and units recruited locally or from other parts of the empire. They also needed informants, guides, interpreters, and officials capable of negotiating with villages. A military map with districts colored according to French jurisdiction should not be mistaken for an exact measure of consent or reliable policing. Some valleys and towns could be controlled more effectively than neighboring uplands; some officials reached accommodations with local leaders, while others relied on repeated punitive operations. The administrative frontier often moved more slowly than the diplomatic frontier created by treaty.

The fragmentation carried symbolic consequences. The Nguyễn court had previously presented itself as the central institution of a kingdom spanning a long north-south territory. Under the new structure, territory was administered through different legal categories established by France, despite continuing social, commercial, and cultural connections among regions. The legal position of a resident of Saigon differed from that of a subject in Huế or a villager in the Red River Delta. These distinctions shaped access to courts, taxation, schooling, and later political organizing, although the detailed development of those institutions belongs to the chapters on colonial economy and society.

In 1887 French authorities established the Indochinese Union, which brought Cochinchina and the protectorates within a larger framework together with Cambodia. The fact of federation helps explain the consequence of conquest, but this chapter should not prematurely narrate the union's entire history, later incorporation of Laos, or twentieth-century economic reforms. The immediate political point is that French administrators sought to coordinate territories acquired under different legal instruments without erasing all their institutional differences. Imperial centralization could coexist with jurisdictional diversity.

The surviving Nguyễn monarchy made this settlement especially complicated. The presence of a ruler in Huế could give administrative acts an appearance of continuity with older authority, and Vietnamese subjects might continue to express loyalty to the court. But the French government could veto or direct decisions of international and strategic importance. The resulting division between ceremonial authority and practical power was not always stable, and officials on each side disputed where a religious, customary, or domestic question ended and a matter of imperial security began. Such ambiguities were tools of colonial governance as much as legal defects to be corrected.

A proper map of late-nineteenth-century Vietnam should therefore show three kinds of information: the treaty-defined jurisdictions, the continuing political centers of Vietnamese administration, and the uneven geographical reach of French enforcement. The conquest had broken the independent foreign policy of the Nguyễn monarchy, but it had not instantly replaced every social institution with an imported one. This combination of rupture and continuity is essential to understanding why foreign rule could persist while remaining contested.

Section 110.18

Vietnamese Responses: Resistance, Accommodation, and Political Reorientation

Vietnamese responses to conquest varied because the people being governed occupied different positions within the political and social order. An imperial official responsible for defending a fortress confronted choices unlike those of a farmer trying to save a harvest, a merchant dependent on access to a port, or a Catholic family caught between hostile armed groups. Some individuals resisted openly; others maintained local institutions, accepted employment, negotiated with colonial officers, or tried to keep their families beyond military conflict. None of these choices can be understood fully without considering the available alternatives and the consequences of refusal. The categories resistance and collaboration are useful questions for historians, but they are often inadequate as complete descriptions of lived decisions.

The scholar-official class experienced a particularly visible crisis. The Nguyễn state had linked examination learning, moral authority, and public office in a system that gave educated men reasons to identify with the dynasty. French occupation disrupted the relationship between learning and legitimate government. In some provinces, officials relinquished their posts rather than serve an administration they regarded as foreign; others remained to limit damage or preserve order. Some joined armed movements that invoked the emperor's authority. Disagreement among educated elites did not demonstrate the disappearance of Confucian values. It showed that principles such as loyalty, public responsibility, and the welfare of subjects could lead to conflicting judgments under coercive conditions.

Local notables and village representatives were indispensable to both the old administration and the new. They understood landholding, community resources, labor obligations, family relationships, and customary procedures. French officials needed this knowledge to move beyond the garrison into routine administration. A village chief might transmit taxes and regulations while privately opposing the regime or judging that compliance protected local inhabitants from reprisals. Another might welcome the removal of a rival official or seek advancement through the new bureaucracy. The evidence for such choices is often indirect, preserved in petitions, disputes, or reports drafted by colonial authorities. Their descriptions should not be accepted as transparent statements of political loyalty.

Economic activity created additional forms of adaptation. Merchants had to decide whether to use new ports and transport opportunities while confronting customs rules and political insecurity. Artisans and boat owners supplied armies or administrations, sometimes voluntarily and sometimes under compulsion. Communities connected to the growing written use of quốc ngữ or to new schools could seek opportunities without endorsing colonial domination as a political principle. To portray any adoption of a French-sponsored institution as proof of ideological consent would mistake practical behavior for a full declaration of allegiance.

Religious affiliations intersected with these choices in complex ways. Vietnamese Catholics had faced persecution under some Nguyễn policies, and French authorities presented themselves as protectors. Yet Catholics could remain loyal to local communities and to Vietnamese political traditions, just as non-Catholics could cooperate with colonial officers. Anti-Christian violence committed by some resistance groups was not justified by the fact of French intervention; it also cannot be attributed indiscriminately to all opponents of colonialism. Temples, lineage shrines, Catholic congregations, and scholarly associations provided social networks through which information and supplies could circulate, but each community developed its own responses to danger and change.

The political significance of women and households is especially easy to miss. War and the absence of officials or soldiers altered expectations concerning labor, caregiving, property, mobility, and family survival. Women helped maintain agricultural and market activity, sustained relatives in hiding or displacement, and sometimes carried information. Colonial court records might preserve their names when disputes over property or criminal charges occurred, but military narratives generally foreground male commanders. A comprehensive chapter should explain this imbalance rather than infer that women had no political or historical agency. The difference between an archive's visibility and historical importance is itself a lesson in method.

It would be equally misleading to imagine that all resistance shared a coherent long-term program of national liberation identical to those of the twentieth century. Many fighters understood their obligations through loyalty to the emperor, inherited moral categories, or the defense of a locality. Other people came to think differently about dynastic government, reform, and the relationship between political sovereignty and social change. The organized movements that later drew on new publications, educational institutions, and international revolutionary models must be addressed in their own chronological setting rather than inserted into the motivations of people in the 1860s and 1880s.

The political result by the mid-1890s was a colony and two protectorates in which French officials claimed superior authority, but Vietnamese society continued to make decisions, maintain institutions, remember conflicts, and challenge imposed power. Some responses were dramatic and documented as battles; many others were quiet and difficult to recover. Historical analysis must hold both in view. Without the record of negotiation and adaptation, colonial rule appears much more mechanically effective than it was. Without the record of coercion and repression, adaptation can appear to be unqualified acceptance.

Section 110.19

The Chronology of Conquest, 1858–1896

The chronology of conquest is most instructive when events are classified by what they achieved. In September 1858 French and Spanish forces attacked Đà Nẵng, initiating an expedition that did not obtain the desired political concessions. February 1859 marked the seizure of Saigon, not the complete annexation of southern Vietnam. The French victory at Kỳ Hòa in February 1861 enlarged their ability to operate, but legal sovereignty over named provinces was asserted through the Treaty of Saigon of June 1862. These distinctions teach students that a military event, a diplomatic document, and the routine administration of territory are different kinds of change.

The 1862 treaty also shows why legal chronology cannot be reduced to a sequence of signatures. Its ratifications were exchanged in 1863; Nguyễn diplomats then sought to regain the territory, and the unratified Aubaret proposal in 1864 exposed unresolved French policy. The occupation of three more southern provinces in June 1867 changed the territorial facts. Taken together, these dates show an expansion from coastal intervention to a six-province colony through a series of choices and confrontations. They also reveal how an apparently completed settlement could become the starting point of a new round of conquest.

The northern sequence followed a different pattern. Garnier's seizure of Hanoi in November 1873 was followed by his death in December and a negotiated withdrawal, yet the March 1874 Treaty of Saigon granted France greater diplomatic and commercial standing. Rivière's occupation of Hanoi in April 1882 and death in May 1883 again transformed an officer's aggressive action into a broader political crisis. The August 1883 Harmand Treaty was concluded after military pressure near Huế, while the Patenôtre revision of June 1884 modified territorial provisions without restoring independence. This chronology cannot be explained solely as a succession of victorious French battles; temporary setbacks and negotiations were repeatedly converted into lasting advantages.

The international sequence was also layered. The preliminary Franco-Chinese convention at Tianjin in May 1884 did not produce lasting peace, and the ensuing conflict included the Bắc Lệ crisis, operations around the northern frontier, and French attacks on Chinese maritime positions. The definitive treaty of June 1885 followed the war and required China to respect France's agreements with the Huế court. One month later, however, fighting in the Vietnamese capital and the flight of Hàm Nghi initiated a new and geographically dispersed phase of resistance. Peace between France and China and political submission within Vietnam therefore belong to different timelines.

Resistance after 1885 did not conform to a single date of defeat. Regional movements in the delta and highlands rose and declined at different times. Hàm Nghi was captured in 1888, but the movement continued under other leaders. Phan Đình Phùng died in late 1895 and Hương Khê resistance was defeated in 1896. These events make a reasonable concluding boundary for the chapter, provided the text does not imply that all local conflict ended that year. The end date describes the closure of a major phase of anti-French military resistance, not a definitive endpoint of political opposition.

Students should construct a four-column chronology rather than a single undifferentiated line. A military column would include Đà Nẵng, Saigon, Kỳ Hòa, Hanoi, Thuận An, northern frontier battles, Huế, and the regional resistance campaigns. A diplomatic column would record the treaties of 1862, 1874, 1883, 1884, and 1885. A political column would track court succession and disputes about legitimate authority. A social column would note civilian displacement, religious conflict, and the gradual reorganization of local administration. Where exact quantitative evidence is absent, the column should identify a process and its date range rather than fabricate a precise number.

This method exposes chronological gaps in official narratives. A French military account may announce the pacification of a district when the next year's administrative reports still describe uncertainty. A treaty may describe a border before surveyors can mark it. A Vietnamese court proclamation may assert loyalty far beyond the places where its supporters command troops. These are not trivial inconsistencies: they show how different institutions represented authority. Placing the sources side by side allows students to distinguish an aspiration, a legal claim, an operational achievement, and a later retrospective judgment.

The overall pattern from 1858 to 1896 was incremental conquest followed by contested consolidation. France gained territorial possessions in stages, removed important diplomatic alternatives, and built institutions capable of governing in the name of the empire. Vietnamese rulers and communities repeatedly made decisions that shaped how quickly this process advanced and where resistance continued. The chronology is thus a framework for causal explanation, not a substitute for it.

Historical Synthesis: Why Nguyễn Sovereignty Collapsed

Why did the Nguyễn monarchy lose independent sovereignty? The most immediate answer concerns the uneven military and diplomatic capabilities of the opposing governments. France could deploy steamships and naval artillery across a long coast, concentrate expeditionary forces at selected points, and reinforce them when European and Asian commitments permitted. The Nguyễn court commanded soldiers, fortifications, and an established bureaucracy but could not reliably prevent attacks on distant ports or match the mobility of the French navy. Yet a technological explanation alone is insufficient. French operations failed to achieve their initial objective at Đà Nẵng, individual expeditions were overextended, and metropolitan policymakers sometimes considered alternatives to permanent annexation. Military superiority created opportunities; political decisions determined how they were used.

The dynasty's internal difficulties mattered without making it uniquely incapable or morally responsible for its own conquest. Taxation, provincial defense, public works, regional revolts, and court succession imposed competing demands on limited administrative capacity. Officials could disagree about resistance and negotiation while still acting from sincere commitments to government and community. The court could not abandon northern security to defend the south without consequences, nor could it defend every locality simultaneously against a mobile expeditionary enemy. When treaties were signed under threat, some inhabitants refused to follow withdrawal orders, weakening the ability of the central government to present a unified position even as continued resistance made French military control costly.

French policymaking was itself divided and improvised. Missionaries and their supporters mobilized claims about religious protection; merchants envisioned markets accessible through Vietnamese rivers; naval commanders pressed strategic opportunities; metropolitan ministers assessed expense and diplomatic risk. None of these interests was identical to the others. Once officers had occupied land, however, they could argue that withdrawal would endanger prestige or security. One acquisition could thus produce arguments for a second. The 1864 negotiations over a possible return of southern territory make this process particularly clear: the result was not determined in every detail from the moment the first soldiers landed.

Diplomacy widened the consequences of conquest. The 1874 treaty purported to recognize Vietnamese independence from foreign suzerainty while strengthening French influence. The agreements of Huế in 1883 and 1884 subordinated Nguyễn foreign relations to France while preserving portions of royal government. The Franco-Chinese settlement of 1885 removed Qing opposition to those arrangements. These documents should not be treated as equivalent. One reorganized rights in already annexed territory; another claimed to protect a state while limiting its decisions; another settled a conflict between two foreign governments over a third political community. Together they narrowed the Nguyễn court's freedom of action, but each addressed a different dimension of authority.

Local agency operated throughout the process. Vietnamese commanders defended fortifications and waterways; diplomats pursued alternative settlements; village leaders supported or resisted new administrations; religious communities navigated persecution and danger; scholars appealed to the emperor's authority. French policies were also shaped by the responses they encountered. Resistance increased occupation costs, required garrisons and intelligence systems, and undermined claims that the country had been peacefully reorganized. Cooperation and negotiation made other forms of administration possible. A serious account therefore neither reduces Vietnamese people to victims of irresistible foreign power nor assumes that local action could easily overcome the material imbalance.

The consequences were geographically differentiated. Cochinchina became a colony under direct French sovereignty; Annam remained centered on a constrained Nguyễn court; Tonkin became a protectorate with stronger French involvement in administration and security. These arrangements were united by empire but were not legally identical. They altered the meaning of a Vietnamese official's position, the channels of foreign diplomacy, and the possibilities for local political initiative. Some social institutions endured, yet endurance did not imply independence. A village could retain its communal rituals while losing the capacity to appeal to a government that controlled its own external relations.

The Cần Vương movement shows why a legal end to sovereignty was not the same as an accepted political settlement. Supporters of Hàm Nghi and regional commanders used dynastic loyalty to organize resistance after France secured its principal treaties. Their networks could persist without a unified command, but coordination problems and colonial counterinsurgency limited their ability to reverse the new order. The defeat of Hương Khê by 1896 marked a major stage of consolidation. It did not dissolve Vietnamese language, scholarship, family life, religious institutions, or political memory, nor did it remove the conditions from which future opposition could arise.

Historians may assign different weights to military power, political contingency, missionary intervention, market expectations, and court divisions. The strongest interpretation does not force these factors into exclusive explanations. Military power explains why threats carried such weight; domestic politics explains why responses differed; commerce and religion help explain why particular French groups advocated action; diplomacy explains how initial conquests acquired broader international recognition; local society explains why administration remained contested. Each mechanism operated at a different moment and scale. The chapter's final judgment is therefore that French conquest was a prolonged dismantling of Nguyễn sovereignty, not a single defeat followed by passive acceptance. By the mid-1890s the imperial political framework was established, but the history of Vietnamese responses to that framework remained unfinished.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Overwhelming Power or Contingent Decisions?

Power: Technology and naval reach decided the outcome.
Contingency: Divisions and decisions shaped timing and form.

Student task: Which explanation better accounts for 1864 and 1885?

The structural interpretation. A modern fleet, fiscal and technological disparities, and imperial diplomacy gave France leverage that the Nguyễn state struggled to match. This explains why threats of attack could force negotiations and why occupied positions were difficult to recover.

The contingent interpretation. The failed attack at Đà Nẵng, the 1864 proposed territorial restoration, the Garnier and Rivière crises, and political disputes in Paris and Huế demonstrate genuine decision points. The scale and form of French rule did not proceed from a fixed plan implemented without interruption.

How to judge. These explanations address different causal levels and need not be treated as mutually exclusive. A good answer should distinguish why France possessed superior leverage from why a particular decision produced a specific territorial or diplomatic outcome.

Chapter Summary

The French conquest of Vietnam was not a single battle or a continuous offensive. Between 1858 and 1896 it moved from naval intervention through territorial annexations and protectorate treaties to a prolonged struggle over the legitimacy of French rule. Military force enabled demands; diplomats expressed those demands in documents; local officials and communities determined how far the imposed arrangements could operate.

The Nguyễn dynasty under Tự Đức governed through a court-centered bureaucracy whose strengths in provincial administration did not include parity with the French navy. Fiscal strains, regional rebellions, competing defense priorities, and disagreement among officials limited its response. Those difficulties did not erase its political institutions or predetermine every defeat.

Missionary controversies were a major argument used to justify French intervention, but they cannot explain the entire colonial project. Naval ambition, commerce, international rivalry, and political decisions within Napoleon III’s government also shaped policy. Vietnamese Catholic communities had a history and interests of their own and should not be treated as politically uniform.

The first attack at Đà Nẵng in 1858 failed to compel rapid submission. The expedition shifted south, seized Saigon in 1859, and expanded its position after Kỳ Hòa in 1861. The June 1862 treaty ceded three provinces and Côn Đảo, imposed a four-million-treaty-dollar indemnity over ten years, and gave France influence beyond its new territorial holdings.

Nguyễn diplomats pursued alternatives during 1863–1864, but the possible restoration of territory was not implemented. French forces occupied the remaining three western provinces in 1867. Cochinchina became a directly administered colony, although the annexations did not end local resistance or settle every question about legitimate authority.

Southern resistance included Trương Định and Nguyễn Trung Trực, whose movements reflected local defense and dynastic commitments in circumstances that did not map neatly onto later categories of nationalism. Households experienced warfare through requisitions, mobility restrictions, trade disruption, and uncertainty concerning taxes, courts, and livelihoods.

French ambitions moved toward the Red River and access to Yunnan. Garnier’s seizure of Hanoi in 1873 and Rivière’s renewed intervention in 1882 illustrated how individual officers could precipitate crises. The 1874 treaty expanded French influence without establishing the later protectorates; the deaths of Garnier and Rivière did not reverse the long-term diplomatic trend.

The Harmand Treaty of August 1883 established a severe protectorate framework after pressure at Huế. The Patenôtre Treaty of June 1884 restored four provinces to Annam’s administrative jurisdiction but retained decisive French control of foreign relations. Cochinchina, Annam, and Tonkin developed different legal and practical relationships to colonial power.

Qing China contested French claims in northern Vietnam. The preliminary convention of May 1884 failed to prevent war; the June 1885 Treaty of Tianjin established terms for Franco-Chinese relations and required respect for France’s treaties with the court at Huế. Vietnamese representatives did not sign that settlement, which did not guarantee acceptance within Vietnam.

The Huế crisis of July 1885 and the Cần Vương appeal initiated a diffuse movement invoking loyalty to Hàm Nghi. Bãi Sậy, Ba Đình, and Hương Khê used different regional strategies. The emperor was captured in 1888, but important movements continued until the defeat of Hương Khê in 1896. The consolidation of French institutions did not end Vietnamese political memory, society, or later opposition.

Chapter Reflection

A treaty can declare peace while inaugurating years of conflict over who may collect taxes, appoint officials, or speak for a political community. The conquest of Vietnam illustrates the difference between a diplomatic claim to authority and its uneven exercise among people who were not represented at the negotiating table.

The next chapters examine how the imperial state extended its institutions beyond Vietnam and how colonial society and economy changed under the resulting arrangements. Those later developments should be approached with an awareness that conquest was neither uncontested nor administratively uniform, and that Vietnamese political and social life did not disappear when independent diplomacy was lost.
