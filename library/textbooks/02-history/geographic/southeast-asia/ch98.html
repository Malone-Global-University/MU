India’s Economic Liberalization and the Coalition Era

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On July 24, 1991, the finance minister of a government that did not command a majority in Parliament presented a budget to the Lok Sabha. During the preceding weeks the rupee had been adjusted downward twice, and emergency gold transactions had helped secure foreign currency. On the same day as the budget, the government placed its new industrial-policy statement before Parliament. Usable foreign-exchange reserves had fallen to a level officials measured in weeks of imports. What Manmohan Singh presented was therefore at once an emergency response and a statement about the future of the Indian economy.

This chapter studies what followed, over roughly the next thirteen years, in two linked domains. The first is economic. The Indian state moved away from detailed licensing of private industry, tight import controls, and extensive barriers to foreign capital, toward a more open and competitive economy. The second is political. The Indian National Congress, which had dominated national politics for most of the period since independence, lost that dominance; regional parties acquired growing leverage in Parliament; and governments came to be formed through minority arrangements and coalitions. The chapter's central interpretive question connects the two: how did India sustain a substantial change in economic policy while undergoing fragmentation and reorganization of its political system?

The chapter resists two common simplifications. The first is that 1991 marked an instantaneous transition from socialism to capitalism. India's economy was already a mixed economy; some reforms preceded 1991; and the changes after the crisis were gradual, uneven, and partial, leaving the state a major owner, employer, and regulator. The second is that reform and its effects can be summarized by a single figure such as the rate of growth. Growth rose, but the benefits and costs were distributed unequally across social classes, sectors, and regions, and the evidence on poverty and employment is contested.

The chapter also distinguishes concepts that are often merged. Liberalization, disinvestment, and privatization are different processes; a balance-of-payments crisis is not the same as a budget deficit; a minority government is not a coalition; and stabilization is not structural reform. Each distinction is defined where it first matters and each helps explain what actually happened.

The evidence is varied. It includes official statements such as the budget speech and the Statement on Industrial Policy of July 1991, the Economic Survey, reports of expert committees, Reserve Bank and national-accounts statistics, statutes, parliamentary records, and the programs of coalition governments, together with the work of economists, political scientists, and historians who disagree about causation and distribution. Statistical series changed their base years and survey designs during the period, so the chapter uses rounded and approximate figures and states their limits.

This chapter opens Part XVIII, Liberalization and Contemporary South Asia, 1991–2026, in Volume VI. It follows Chapter 97, which examined nuclear politics, regional cooperation, labor migration, and the international pressures that preceded 1991. Its political story is complemented by Chapter 99, which treats caste mobilization after the Mandal Commission, the Ayodhya movement, and the rise of the Bharatiya Janata Party, and by Chapter 100, which follows India through the twenty-first century. This chapter focuses on economic restructuring and the coalition politics that sustained it, and leaves the ideological and social history of the party system largely to Chapter 99.

Historical Context

Chapter 89 described the development strategy of the first decades after independence: planned industrialization, a large public sector, licensing of private capacity, protection of domestic industry, and food self-sufficiency pursued through the Green Revolution (Chapter 94). Chapter 93 and Chapter 94 traced the political changes of the 1970s and 1980s, in which Indira Gandhi centralized power, the Emergency tested democratic institutions, and new regional and caste-based political forces appeared. Chapter 97 examined the international setting of the late Cold War, including labor migration to the Gulf and the remittances on which many households and the national economy had come to depend.

The economic inheritance in 1991 was mixed. India had a large and diversified industrial base, a substantial scientific and technical workforce, and an agriculture that fed a growing population in normal years. Growth had accelerated in the 1980s. But the economy remained highly regulated, with limited export orientation, and the central and state governments ran large deficits. Per capita incomes were low, and a large share of the population remained poor and employed in agriculture and the informal sector.

Politically, the Congress system that had dominated national politics was in decline, and the general elections of 1989 and 1991 produced no single-party majority. The coalition and minority governments that followed operated under the anti-defection law, a more assertive Election Commission, and new judicial limits on the dismissal of state governments. The Mandal and Ayodhya movements (Chapter 99) were transforming the electorate even as the economic reforms were being launched.

Internationally, the collapse of the Soviet bloc, the Gulf War, and the apparent success of export-oriented East Asian economies altered assumptions about development strategy. Multilateral institutions, notably the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank, promoted stabilization and structural adjustment. These conditions shaped both the pressure for reform and the arguments about it, as the Historical Debate in Section 98.4 shows.

Division I — Economic Crisis and the Rao–Singh Reform Government

Sections 98.1–98.4 · The collapse of economic confidence and the creation of a new policy direction

Section 98.1

The Road to the 1991 Economic Crisis

India's economic emergency of 1991 did not arrive without warning, and it did not arise from a single cause. It was the product of vulnerabilities built up across the 1980s, intensified by a cluster of external shocks in 1990 and early 1991, and then made acute by a loss of confidence among foreign lenders and depositors. Understanding the difference between these layers is the first task of this chapter. A student who sees only the shocks will miss the structural weaknesses that made India so exposed to them; a student who sees only the structural weaknesses will miss why the crisis broke when and how it did. The economic system that entered this crisis had been shaped by the development strategy examined in Chapter 89: an economy of five-year plans, a large public sector, extensive licensing of private industrial capacity, high tariffs and import controls, and a pervasive concern with self-reliance. That system had delivered a diversified industrial base, a substantial scientific and technical workforce, and, after the Green Revolution examined in Chapter 94, food security in ordinary years. It had also delivered slow growth for much of the period, a protected and often high-cost industrial sector, and limited export competitiveness.

The decade of the 1980s complicates any simple picture of stagnation followed by sudden reform. Growth accelerated during the 1980s, averaging somewhat above five percent a year by the standard national-accounts series, well above the pace of the 1960s and 1970s. Economists disagree about why. Dani Rodrik and Arvind Subramanian argued that the acceleration began around 1980 and reflected a shift in official attitudes toward private business and away from hostility to it, rather than a dismantling of controls. Others, notably Arvind Panagariya, have emphasized that the 1980s expansion rested on unsustainable fiscal and external borrowing and that the policy changes after 1991 were needed to sustain growth. The two positions are not entirely incompatible. Both agree that India's economy in 1991 was not static, and that the post-1991 reforms built on, as well as broke from, what had gone before.

The reforms of the Rajiv Gandhi government, which held office from 1984 to 1989, belong to this background. That government raised the asset thresholds at which firms became subject to the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act, broadened the capacity that firms could install without fresh licences, introduced a modified value-added tax on manufacturing inputs (MODVAT) in 1986, eased the import of capital goods and components, and adopted a computer and electronics policy that encouraged the software and electronics industries. These measures were partial, selective, and often reversed in practice. They favored the private corporate sector without creating a competitive open market, and they were accompanied by growing public spending. They also made little change to the government's budgetary position, which is where the roots of the later crisis lay.

The fiscal story is straightforward in outline. Through the 1980s the central government's expenditure rose faster than its revenue. Subsidies on food, fertilizer, and fuel expanded; interest payments on accumulated debt grew; defense spending and the salaries and pensions of public employees absorbed an increasing share of the budget; and tax revenue did not keep pace. The central government's gross fiscal deficit stood at roughly eight percent of gross domestic product at the end of the decade, with the deficit for the central and state governments combined considerably higher. Much of this deficit was financed by borrowing from domestic banks, which the government could do cheaply because banks were required to hold a large proportion of their deposits in government securities, and by borrowing abroad. The domestic and external accounts were therefore connected: a government that spends more than it collects, in an economy that also imports more than it exports, will find the shortfall appearing in both places.

The external position deteriorated accordingly. India's current-account deficit, the gap between its earnings from exports, services, and remittances and its payments for imports and other obligations, widened from about one percent of gross domestic product in the early 1980s to roughly three percent by 1990–91. The country financed this gap increasingly through commercial borrowing from foreign banks, deposits placed by non-resident Indians in Indian banks at favorable interest rates, and short-term trade credits, instead of through longer-term concessional loans or direct investment. External debt rose from the low tens of billions of dollars at the start of the decade to somewhere in the range of seventy to eighty billion dollars by 1991, depending on the definition used. More important than the total was its composition. A growing share consisted of short-term and commercial obligations that creditors could decline to renew, and of deposits that holders could withdraw. Debt of this kind is the kind that turns a manageable deficit into a payments crisis once lenders lose confidence.

That loss of confidence was triggered by events that, individually, were not under India's control. In August 1990 Iraq invaded Kuwait, and the price of crude oil, which India imported in large quantities, roughly doubled within a few months. The war disrupted the Gulf economies in which several million Indian workers were employed, interrupted employment and remittance flows on which many households depended, and forced the evacuation of tens of thousands of Indian citizens from Kuwait and Iraq; the migration system behind these flows is described in Chapter 97. Exports to the region fell. At the same time, the unraveling of the Soviet and Eastern European trading systems weakened rupee-settlement trade and the protected markets that had absorbed substantial Indian exports; these relationships eroded unevenly rather than disappearing overnight. Political instability compounded these pressures. A National Front minority government under V. P. Singh fell in November 1990 after its confrontation over reservations and Hindu nationalist mobilization, treated in Chapter 99, and was replaced by a short-lived minority government under Chandra Shekhar that survived only on the outside support of Congress. International rating agencies and commercial lenders lowered their assessment of India, and non-resident deposits began to leave.

Historians therefore distinguish long-term structural weaknesses (large fiscal deficits, a growing current-account deficit, a debt profile tilted toward short maturities, and an economy insulated from competition) from short-term triggers (the oil price rise, the Gulf crisis, the loss of Soviet trade, and political uncertainty). The long-term weaknesses explain why India was vulnerable; the shocks explain the timing and severity of the emergency. Reformers in 1991 used the crisis to argue that the weaknesses themselves had to be addressed, while critics argued that a temporary shock was being used to justify a permanent change of direction. That argument, and the evidence bearing on it, runs through the rest of this chapter.

Section 98.2

The Balance-of-Payments Emergency of 1990–1991

A balance-of-payments crisis is not the same thing as a budget deficit, although the two are often confused. A government's budget deficit measures the shortfall between its revenue and its expenditure, and it is financed by borrowing, mostly in rupees. A balance-of-payments crisis arises when a country cannot obtain enough foreign currency to pay for its imports and to service its external obligations. The central bank's holdings of foreign-exchange reserves are the buffer between the two, and a crisis occurs when that buffer approaches exhaustion and foreign creditors will not lend more. A large budget deficit can contribute to such a crisis, as it did in India, but a country can in principle run a payments crisis with a small deficit or avoid one with a large deficit. The distinction matters because the remedies differ: closing a budget gap requires fiscal adjustment, while closing a payments gap requires some combination of export growth, import compression, foreign borrowing, and changes in the exchange rate.

Historical Vocabulary
Balance of Payments

The balance of payments is the record of a country's economic transactions with the rest of the world over a period. Its current account records trade in goods and services, income, and transfers such as migrant remittances; its capital account records investment and borrowing. A balance-of-payments crisis occurs when a country cannot finance its current-account deficit from capital inflows or reserves, and is forced to compress imports, borrow on emergency terms, or adjust its exchange rate.

In 1991 the term mattered because it separated India's emergency from its budget deficit. The fiscal deficit was a cause of the external difficulty, but the immediate danger was the exhaustion of foreign currency, not an inability to borrow rupees. Everyday speech often uses “deficit” for both; historians should not.

The evidence for the crisis lies in the movement of reserves. India's reserves of foreign currency fell steeply during 1990 and early 1991, and by the beginning of 1991 the usable foreign-currency portion of reserves had declined to a level that officials described as equivalent to only a few weeks of imports. Figures differ depending on whether gold and special drawing rights held at the International Monetary Fund are included and on the date chosen, which is why this chapter distinguishes carefully between total reserves and usable foreign-currency assets. The Quantitative Study below sets out rounded values. What the figures show consistently is that by mid-1991 the margin of safety had nearly vanished.

The government's response proceeded in stages. In January 1991 the Chandra Shekhar government drew on an IMF facility for emergency foreign exchange, while the Reserve Bank of India, under Governor S. Venkitaramanan, tightened import financing to conserve scarce currency. Import compression preserved reserves but constrained production by restricting access to imported components. The gold operations require careful distinction. In May 1991, the government leased approximately 20 tonnes of gold from its holdings to the State Bank of India, which sold it abroad with an option to repurchase it after six months; this was a sale-and-repurchase arrangement, not the same transaction as the later RBI collateral pledge. In July, after P. V. Narasimha Rao took office, the RBI pledged 46.91 tonnes of gold to the Bank of England and the Bank of Japan, obtaining approximately US$405 million in loans. These separate operations became powerful symbols of the shortage of foreign currency. The Ministry of Finance, first under Yashwant Sinha and then Manmohan Singh, and the RBI managed successive stages of the crisis. The episode is documented in the Economic Survey 1991–92, whose account should be preferred to retrospective claims that all of the gold was simply sold off.

For the separate May and July gold arrangements, see the Economic Survey 1991–92, “The Payments Crisis”; for the 46.91 tonnes/US$405 million collateral transaction, see the Government of India parliamentary answer.

Primary Source Window

Economic Survey 1991–92 on the Payments Emergency

Evidence type: Official annual economic review · Document: Economic Survey 1991–92, Ministry of Finance, Government of India (Economic Division), published after the 1991–92 fiscal year, not with the July 1991 budget · Access: Union Budget archive, Ministry of Finance

Context: The Economic Survey is published annually by the Ministry of Finance as an official assessment of the economy. The 1991–92 edition reviewed the year of the crisis and the first stabilization measures, and presents the government's subsequent retrospective account.

Paraphrase of contents (not a quotation): The Survey describes the payments emergency as the result of accumulated fiscal and external imbalances compounded by the Gulf crisis; reports the fall in reserves, the loss of confidence among lenders and depositors, and the emergency import compression; and presents the exchange-rate adjustments, new industrial and trade policies, and fiscal consolidation as a coordinated response.

Analysis: The Survey is an invaluable summary of the official view and of the statistical series available to policymakers. It is also a retrospective, self-justifying account by the government that carried out the response; it offers little on the social costs of import compression or on dissenting views, and later revisions of statistical series mean its numbers should be checked against later RBI and national-accounts data.

Several institutions played distinct roles. The Reserve Bank of India managed the reserves, the exchange rate, and the import-control measures, and its leadership took part in designing the adjustment program. The Ministry of Finance, under Yashwant Sinha in the Chandra Shekhar government and then Manmohan Singh under Rao, negotiated with the IMF and World Bank and prepared the budget. The International Monetary Fund provided short-term balance-of-payments support and, in October 1991, approved a stand-by arrangement of roughly two billion dollars with conditions on fiscal adjustment and structural policy; the World Bank followed with structural adjustment lending. The caretaker position of the Chandra Shekhar government, which presided over the worst of the emergency but lacked a parliamentary mandate for far-reaching change, limited what it could do. The elections of May and June 1991 and the formation of a new government created a political opening that its predecessor had lacked.

The decisive early step was a pair of adjustments to the exchange rate on July 1 and July 3, 1991, through which the Reserve Bank lowered the rupee's value against the major currencies by approximately nine percent and then approximately eleven percent, a cumulative reduction usually put at roughly eighteen to twenty percent depending on how it is measured. Alongside the adjustment, the government withdrew cash compensatory subsidies on exports, which had been designed to offset the disadvantages exporters faced in a high-cost, protected economy. The logic was that a cheaper rupee would make Indian exports more competitive abroad and imports more expensive at home, narrowing the current-account deficit by shifting demand toward domestic production. The cost was that everything imported, from oil and fertilizer to capital goods and components, became more expensive in rupees, adding to inflation and to the cost of production. The measure was therefore both an economic correction and a political risk, and the manner in which it was carried out, in two stages within a few days and shortly after a new government had taken office, shaped how later economic decisions were presented and defended.

Emergency measures were not the same as structural reform. Import compression, gold pledging, and devaluation addressed the immediate shortage of foreign exchange. They did not alter the system of controls that made Indian industry uncompetitive, the fiscal structure that had produced persistent deficits, or the regulatory arrangements governing banking and capital markets. The government's decision to treat the emergency as the occasion for deeper change, instead of restoring the previous system once the crisis eased, is the subject of the next two sections.

Section 98.3

P. V. Narasimha Rao and the Political Decision to Reform

The general election of 1991 took place in the shadow of political violence. Rajiv Gandhi was assassinated on May 21, 1991, at an election rally at Sriperumbudur in Tamil Nadu, during the campaign, an event whose connection to the Sri Lankan conflict is noted in Chapter 96 and Chapter 103. The remaining phases of the election were postponed and held in June. Congress won 244 seats, well short of a majority in the Lok Sabha, but far ahead of the Bharatiya Janata Party, which won about 120, and the Janata Dal, which won about 59. Congress could therefore form a government, though it would have to rely on the support of other parties and independents, or on the abstention of opponents, in order to pass legislation.

The choice of leader was not obvious. Sonia Gandhi declined to take an active political role, and the Congress parliamentary party, after consultations among senior leaders, chose P. V. Narasimha Rao, a Telugu-speaking politician from Andhra Pradesh in his seventies, who had served as chief minister of his state and as foreign minister, home minister, and minister of human resource development at the center. Rao was a scholar of languages and literature, widely read, and politically cautious. He had been regarded as a figure on the edge of active politics and had been planning to retire. He became prime minister on June 21, 1991, heading what was effectively a minority government.

Rao's most consequential appointment was that of Manmohan Singh as finance minister. Singh was an economist, not a professional politician. Born in what is now Pakistan, educated at Cambridge and Oxford, he had served as chief economic adviser to the government, governor of the Reserve Bank of India, secretary-general of the South Commission, head of the Planning Commission, and chairman of the University Grants Commission. He was not a member of Parliament, entering the Rajya Sabha from Assam only in October 1991. The appointment brought into the cabinet a person with deep experience of the Indian economy and of international economic institutions, and it signaled to domestic and foreign audiences that serious economic management was intended. Rao also appointed P. Chidambaram as minister of state for commerce, and retained the industry portfolio himself, which gave him personal authority over the licensing system that the new industrial policy was about to dismantle. Montek Singh Ahluwalia, whose work in the commerce and finance ministries during 1990 and 1991 contributed to policy drafting, played an important official role.

The relationship between Rao and Singh is essential to understanding how reform happened. Rao exercised political leadership: he decided that the crisis should be treated as an occasion for structural change, he managed the party's internal resistance, he handled relations with the president, parliamentary opponents, and state leaders, and he decided what could and could not be done in the circumstances of a minority government. Singh exercised policy and technical leadership: he designed the budget and the macroeconomic program, negotiated with international institutions, and explained the reforms to the public and to Parliament. Neither could have accomplished what happened without the other, and attempts to credit the reforms to one alone distort the historical record. Political scientists such as Rob Jenkins have also emphasized that reform in this period proceeded largely through a deliberately low-key and incremental style, through a sequence of executive notifications and administrative decisions rather than a single sweeping legislative package, which reduced opportunities for organized opposition to mobilize.

The government had to present its program in terms that were acceptable to a party whose identity was bound up with the Nehruvian development legacy. It did this in two ways. First, it described liberalization as a response to a genuine emergency, with the implication that the changes were necessary rather than ideological. Second, it presented the changes as a continuation of Rajiv Gandhi's efforts to modernize the economy and, in some statements, as consistent with the underlying aims of planned development, which were growth, self-reliance, and social justice, even if the instruments changed. Congress left-wingers and the party's traditional constituencies remained uneasy. Trade unions, the Left parties, and the Hindu nationalist movement's economic nationalists in the Swadeshi Jagran Manch, which supported the Bharatiya Janata Party, all opposed key aspects of the program, each for different reasons, and the government had to calculate how far each of these opponents could damage it.

The government also framed reform as a longer-term strategy and not merely an emergency response. Rao and Singh argued that the economy could not return to its earlier trajectory, and that India's participation in a changing global economy, in which East Asian economies had demonstrated the benefits of export-oriented growth, required a more open and competitive framework. This framing was important because it justified a continuing sequence of changes after the immediate crisis had eased, and because it gave later governments a shared vocabulary of reform even when they differed over its pace and content.

Section 98.4

Manmohan Singh, the 1991 Budget, and Economic Stabilization

Manmohan Singh presented the budget for 1991–92 to the Lok Sabha on July 24, 1991, after the rupee adjustments of July 1 and 3; the new Statement on Industrial Policy was announced on July 24, the same day as the budget. Budget speeches in India are formal statements of fiscal and economic policy, and this one was unusual in the explicitness of its argument. Singh described the economic situation as a crisis of unprecedented gravity, summarized the fiscal and external imbalances, and invoked a remark attributed to Victor Hugo about the force of an idea whose time has come, a line that became closely associated with the reforms in public memory. The speech should be read as a landmark policy document, not as an isolated piece of oratory, because it presented a coordinated program in which fiscal adjustment, trade policy, industrial policy, and financial reform were linked.

Stabilization and structural reform are different undertakings, and this budget aimed at both. Stabilization sought to restore macroeconomic balance quickly: to reduce the fiscal deficit, to contain inflation, and to stabilize the external payments position. The government set a target of reducing the central government's fiscal deficit from about eight and a half percent of gross domestic product in 1990–91 to roughly six and a half percent in 1991–92. The budget sought savings by reducing certain subsidies, including those on fertilizer and sugar, increasing the prices of some commodities, restraining non-plan expenditure, and increasing revenue. Opposition from within Congress, from farmers' organizations, and from the Left forced the government to modify some of these proposals; the increase in fertilizer prices, for example, was partially rolled back for small and marginal farmers. The government also reduced peak import tariffs, which had reached extraordinary levels in 1990, from above 300 percent to around 150 percent, and withdrew export subsidies, in line with the exchange-rate adjustment.

Structural reform, by contrast, sought to change the underlying capacity and efficiency of the economy: to reduce regulatory barriers to entry and expansion, to open trade and investment, and to modernize the financial sector. These changes would take years to have measurable effect, and some, such as the opening of the telecommunications sector, would take more than a decade. The budget and the accompanying industrial policy statement set the direction, but did not complete the process. The distinction between the two types of measure explains why the economy's initial performance in 1991–92 was poor, with growth falling sharply to somewhere near one percent and industrial output contracting, even as the foundations for later recovery were laid. Stabilization imposed immediate costs, while structural reforms promised benefits that would arrive later.

The immediate external effects of the adjustment were positive. The Reserve Bank's records show that reserves began to recover from the second half of 1991, assisted by the IMF stand-by program, World Bank loans, bilateral assistance, the inflow of non-resident deposits after the exchange-rate adjustment restored confidence, and improved export performance. Inflation, which had risen to near thirteen or fourteen percent in 1991–92 as a consequence of the rupee's depreciation and the removal of subsidies, fell back in later years. The government also established a National Renewal Fund in 1992 to provide retraining and severance support for workers affected by industrial restructuring, an acknowledgment, at least in principle, that adjustment costs would be unevenly distributed.

Critics raised objections at the time and have continued to do so. Some argued that the program reflected pressure from the IMF and the World Bank and compromised national economic sovereignty. Others accepted the need for stabilization but argued that it was carried out through cuts in public expenditure that affected the poor, including spending on social services, while sparing wealthier groups. Still others questioned the sequencing, arguing that rapid import liberalization before the financial sector and infrastructure had been reformed might harm domestic producers. Defenders responded that without adjustment the alternative was a deeper payments default, with far greater costs for the poor, and that the government had taken care to maintain core social and rural programs.

Historical Debate

Compelled or Chosen? Why Did India Liberalize in 1991?

The question: Were the reforms of 1991 imposed on India by the crisis and by the conditions of the IMF and the World Bank, or were they a domestic choice prepared in advance?

Interpretation A: The reforms were a response to external compulsion. The crisis left India little room, creditors demanded adjustment, and structural-adjustment lending came with conditions. On this view, the speed of change in July 1991 reflects the pressure of the emergency.
Interpretation B: The reforms were substantially a domestic choice. Policy ideas had been developed within the government in the 1980s and in 1990, the Rajiv Gandhi reforms had begun a shift, many Indian economists and officials favored change, and the government retained control of the sequence. The IMF program supported measures already being prepared.

Evidence: Both rely on documentary evidence (IMF and World Bank agreements, the budget and industrial-policy statements, internal government papers prepared in 1990–91) and on the recollections of participants, which are themselves interested accounts. The timing of the exchange-rate and industrial-policy measures, taken before the IMF stand-by was approved in October 1991, is often cited in support of B; the severity of the reserves crisis supports A.

Why it matters: The question affects how the reforms are remembered politically. If they were imposed, they can be presented as a loss of sovereignty; if chosen, as an act of national policy. It also affects whether later governments are seen as continuing a domestic strategy or implementing foreign preferences.

Section 98.5

Industrial Liberalization and the End of the License Raj

Before 1991, Indian private industry operated within a framework of controls established principally by the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act of 1951, the Industrial Policy Resolutions of 1948 and 1956, the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act of 1969, and the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act of 1973. Under this framework a private firm that wished to start a new industrial enterprise, expand capacity significantly, diversify its product line, or import substantial machinery usually needed government licenses, often from several ministries. Sectors considered strategic were reserved for the public sector, and a long list of products was reserved for small-scale producers. Large business houses faced additional restrictions under the MRTP Act. The system had been created for legitimate public purposes: to direct investment toward priority sectors and backward regions, to prevent concentration of economic power, to protect small producers, and to allocate scarce foreign exchange. In practice it also generated delays, rent-seeking, and a bias toward incumbents, since a firm that had obtained a license had effectively obtained protection against competitors who might not receive one. The term License Raj, or the license-permit-quota system, a phrase associated with criticism from C. Rajagopalachari and the Swatantra Party in the 1950s and 1960s, became shorthand for this world.

It is important to distinguish administrative control that served no sustained public purpose from regulation that protects the environment, public safety, workers, or consumers. Reformers in 1991 sought to abolish the former, not the latter. The pre-1991 system conflated the two, since licensing for capacity expansion was administered together with environmental or safety scrutiny, and the removal of licensing therefore required the creation of different forms of regulation for the functions that remained.

The Statement on Industrial Policy tabled in Parliament on July 24, 1991, the same day as the budget, was the principal instrument of change. It abolished industrial licensing for all industries except a short list, initially eighteen, retained for reasons of security and strategic concern, social reasons, hazardous chemicals, environmental considerations, and items of elitist consumption. It reduced the list of industries reserved for the public sector from seventeen to eight. It removed the requirement that firms covered by the MRTP Act obtain prior approval for expansion, shifting the Act's emphasis toward the control of monopolistic and restrictive practices. It permitted automatic approval for foreign equity participation up to fifty-one percent in a specified list of high-priority industries, and for technology agreements within specified limits. It ended the mandatory phased manufacturing programs that required firms to localize production over time, and it abolished the clause that allowed public financial institutions to convert their loans to firms into equity.

The expected benefits were an increase in competition, productivity, and investment. If firms could enter and expand without prior permission, and if imports and foreign investment disciplined domestic producers, resources would flow to more efficient uses. The effects, however, were felt unevenly. Established business houses with technological and financial capabilities, such as large groups with experience in petrochemicals, automobiles, and consumer goods, were able to expand quickly and in several cases pursued new projects on a scale that had been impossible under the licensing regime. Some firms that had thrived behind protection lobbied for a slower pace of change; a group of industrialists known as the Bombay Club, which formed in 1993, argued for a level playing field and for a gradual opening that would give domestic firms time to adjust. New entrants and foreign firms gained opportunities in consumer goods and services. Public-sector employees and their unions saw the reforms as a threat to the stability of their sector, though the immediate changes affected only the future allocation of investment.

Small-scale producers occupied a more ambiguous position. Reservation of products for the small-scale sector continued, with a gradual reduction in the list in later years, and the investment ceiling defining small-scale units was raised, but small firms faced competition from larger domestic firms and, increasingly, from imports. Many small producers also supplied large firms and benefited when their customers expanded. The net effect was complex and varied by industry and region.

Finally, the abolition of central licensing did not end the Indian state's interactions with business. Firms continued to require clearances from state governments for land, electricity, water, and environmental compliance, from municipal authorities for construction, and from tax and labor inspectors. Critics noted that reform at the national level exposed the persistence of what some called an inspector raj at lower levels. The experience of reform thus depended heavily on the quality of state governments and on the interaction between central and state policies, a theme that returns in Section 98.19.

Section 98.6

Public Enterprises, Disinvestment, and the Changing Role of the State

Historical Vocabulary
Liberalization, Disinvestment, and Privatization

Liberalization means reducing restrictions on economic activity: licensing, tariffs, exchange controls, limits on foreign equity. Disinvestment means the government sells some or all of its equity in an enterprise; if it keeps a majority stake and management control, the enterprise remains public. Privatization means that ownership or control actually passes to private hands.

The three processes can occur separately. India liberalized rapidly in 1991, disinvested cautiously from 1991–92, and privatized (in the sense of transferring control) only in a handful of cases after 1999. Treating the terms as synonyms obscures why most major public enterprises remained state-owned in 2004.

By 1991 the central public-sector enterprises were among the largest institutions in the Indian economy. Created under the Industrial Policy Resolution of 1956, which declared that the state should occupy the commanding heights of the economy, they dominated steel, coal, heavy engineering, petroleum, power equipment, telecommunications, banking, and insurance, and they employed a substantial share of the organized workforce. They offered stable employment, often with reservations for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and had served as instruments of regional development and industrial training. By the late 1980s, however, many were burdened by overstaffing, political interference in appointments and pricing, low returns on capital, and heavy losses. Chronically loss-making firms could not easily be closed, since the Sick Industrial Companies Act of 1985 and the Board for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction offered protection rather than exit, and labor law required government permission for retrenchment in larger establishments.

The reforms of the early 1990s approached public enterprises through several channels at once. The first was competition: by reducing the list of reserved sectors, the 1991 policy opened areas such as power equipment, telecommunications equipment, and eventually telecommunications services to private firms, so that public enterprises now faced rivals. The second was greater managerial autonomy, and in 1997 the United Front government introduced the Navratna category, which gave nine of the most profitable public enterprises enhanced powers over investment and joint ventures. The third was restructuring, including voluntary retirement schemes funded partly through the National Renewal Fund and the reference of sick enterprises to the Board for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction. The fourth was disinvestment, the sale of part of the government's equity.

Early disinvestment was modest and cautious. From 1991–92 the government sold small minority stakes in selected enterprises, usually bundled in packages and sold by auction to financial institutions and mutual funds, partly to raise revenue and partly to test the political response. A committee under C. Rangarajan in 1993 recommended that sales could eventually extend to forty-nine percent of equity without losing government control; a Disinvestment Commission established in 1996 under G. V. Ramakrishna advised on the sale of individual companies; and several enterprises, such as the overseas communications monopoly VSNL and the telephone utility MTNL, raised money through global depositary receipts in the late 1990s. The revenue raised was typically below the targets set in budgets, in part because share prices were weak and in part because political resistance slowed the process.

The distinction between disinvestment and privatization is central. Selling a minority stake does not transfer control, and for much of the 1990s the government retained a majority and management control of the enterprises it partly sold. Genuine privatization, which transfers control to private owners, began only under the National Democratic Alliance government after 1999, which created a separate Department of Disinvestment in 1999 and a Ministry of Disinvestment in 2001. Strategic sales included the transfer of management control in Modern Foods, the sale of a majority stake in the aluminum producer BALCO to Sterlite in 2001, which provoked a lengthy strike by its workers, and the sale of a significant stake in VSNL to the Tata group and of the petrochemical firm IPCL to Reliance in 2002. The government also reduced its stake in the automobile manufacturer Maruti Udyog, in which Suzuki had held a minority share since the 1980s. In 2003 the Supreme Court held that the sale of the large oil companies Hindustan Petroleum and Bharat Petroleum required parliamentary approval, effectively halting that program.

The arguments over this process were fiscal, economic, and political. Supporters contended that the government should not own businesses in competitive sectors, that sales could reduce the deficit and free funds for social spending, and that private owners would manage assets more efficiently. Opponents argued that public enterprises served social goals, including employment security and regional development, that assets were being sold cheaply, and that private monopolies might replace public ones. Trade unions across the political spectrum opposed sales, including the Bharatiya Mazdoor Sangh, the union affiliated with the Hindu nationalist movement and therefore with a party in government, a reminder that reform was contested within as well as between political formations.

Most large public enterprises in fact remained under government control in 2004. Banks, insurance companies for much of the period, major energy and mining companies, railways, and defense industries stayed public, and the state continued to be a major economic actor. The outcome of the 1990s was therefore a mixed economy with a different balance, in which the private sector had gained space and the state's role had shifted from direct production and detailed control toward regulation, finance, and social provision, but not a withdrawal of the state.

Section 98.7

Trade Reform, Exchange Rates, and Financial-Sector Modernization

Trade reform proceeded steadily after 1991 but through a series of administrative and budgetary decisions, not a single legislative act. Before 1991, imports were controlled through licenses and canalization, which restricted imports of many items to designated state agencies, and through very high tariffs. The trade policy announced on July 4, 1991, and subsequent Export-Import policies abolished import licensing for capital goods and intermediate inputs for most industries and introduced a replenishment system tied to exports. The Chelliah Committee on tax reform, which reported in 1991 and 1992, recommended reducing tariff dispersion and peak rates, and successive budgets lowered the peak tariff from about 150 percent in 1991–92 to approximately 50 percent by 1995, around 40 percent by 1997, and progressively lower levels in the early 2000s. Consumer goods and agricultural products were liberalized later than capital goods, and many quantitative restrictions on consumer imports remained until 2001, when India removed the remaining restrictions on several hundred product lines following a dispute settlement process at the World Trade Organization.

Exchange-rate policy followed a similar progression, which students should distinguish from the emergency measures of July 1991. The two-stage rupee devaluation of July 1991 was an emergency adjustment within a fixed-rate system. In March 1992 the government introduced the Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System, a dual arrangement under which exporters and others could sell a portion of their foreign-exchange earnings at a market-determined rate while the remainder was surrendered at an official rate. In March 1993 the two rates were unified and the rupee became market-determined within a managed float, with the Reserve Bank intervening to limit volatility. In August 1994 India accepted the obligations of Article VIII of the IMF's Articles of Agreement, making the rupee convertible for current-account transactions such as trade payments, though not for capital-account transactions, which remained regulated. This partial convertibility was important in explaining why, during the Asian financial crisis of 1997, India was affected much less than countries with open capital accounts and heavy short-term foreign borrowing.

The capital markets also changed. The Securities and Exchange Board of India, which had been set up in 1988 as a non-statutory body, was given statutory powers by an Act of Parliament in 1992, after the securities scandal associated with the stockbroker Harshad Mehta revealed the weakness of existing regulation and the diversion of bank funds into share speculation. The Controller of Capital Issues, which had approved the timing and pricing of share issues, was abolished in 1992. The National Stock Exchange was incorporated in 1992 and began trading in 1994, introducing screen-based trading that reduced the dominance of the older Bombay Stock Exchange, and the Depositories Act of 1996 established electronic holding of securities. Foreign institutional investors were permitted to invest in Indian equities from September 1992, opening a new channel of portfolio capital.

These changes show a general pattern. Liberalization did not mean the absence of regulation. It meant replacing discretionary permission systems with rules-based regulation administered by specialized bodies, including SEBI, a strengthened Reserve Bank, and later the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India, established in 1997, and the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority, established in 1999. Whether those institutions regulated effectively was a continuing question, but the shift toward arm's-length regulation was one of the lasting institutional legacies of the period.

Section 98.8

Foreign Investment and India's Integration into Global Markets

Foreign investment had been a sensitive issue in independent India. The Foreign Exchange Regulation Act of 1973 restricted foreign equity in most companies to forty percent and led several well-known multinationals, including IBM and Coca-Cola, to leave India in 1977–78. Technology imports, royalty payments, and the repatriation of profits were controlled closely, in keeping with a strategy of self-reliance and fears that foreign capital might reproduce the dependence of the colonial economy. The policy of 1991 reversed the presumption: foreign investment up to fifty-one percent equity in a specified list of thirty-four industries was approved automatically, and larger proposals were considered by a new Foreign Investment Promotion Board. The lists were widened in successive years and the permitted shares raised.

The distinction between foreign direct investment and portfolio investment is important. Foreign direct investment involves a lasting interest in an enterprise, usually with a controlling or substantial ownership stake, and brings capital, technology, management practices, and access to international markets. Portfolio investment consists of purchases of shares and bonds by investors who can sell them quickly, and it is more volatile. Both increased after 1991. Direct investment inflows, which had been below two hundred million dollars in 1990–91, rose to the range of two to three billion dollars annually by the late 1990s, still small relative to the Chinese experience. Portfolio inflows surged in 1993–94 after foreign institutional investors were admitted, and they fluctuated with global conditions.

The reception of foreign investors was mixed. Soft-drink companies returned, with Pepsi entering in 1989 through a joint venture and Coca-Cola reentering in 1993. Automobile manufacturers, including Suzuki's partnership with the government-owned Maruti, and later Hyundai, Honda, Ford, Daewoo, and others, established plants. Foreign banks, consulting firms, and consumer-goods companies expanded. The Enron-sponsored Dabhol power project in Maharashtra became a lasting controversy: its original contract, approved in 1993, was criticized for its terms and cancelled by the state government in 1995, then renegotiated, and the project eventually collapsed amid financial and political disputes after 2001. The case showed the difficulty of designing contracts and regulatory structures for large infrastructure investment, and it was used by both sides in debates over foreign capital.

Indian firms also became participants in global markets. From 1992 they were permitted to raise funds abroad through global depositary receipts, and several, including Reliance Industries, did so. By the end of the decade some Indian companies in pharmaceuticals, software, and engineering were acquiring or establishing overseas operations, a development that foreshadowed the later outward expansion of Indian capital.

India's participation in the multilateral trading system also changed. The Uruguay Round negotiations, concluded in 1994, led to the creation of the World Trade Organization on January 1, 1995, of which India was an original member. In 1993 and 1994, the proposed agreement, in the form of the draft associated with Arthur Dunkel, provoked large demonstrations by farmers' organizations, notably in Karnataka, and criticism from the Left and from economic nationalists, who feared the effects of agreements on agriculture, intellectual property, and services. The agreement on trade-related intellectual property rights required India to introduce product patents for pharmaceuticals, with a transition period that ended in 2005. These concerns reflected a general anxiety that international rules might limit India's policy autonomy.

Integration with global markets was not an unqualified benefit. Foreign capital brought technology and employment in some sectors, but it also generated concern about profit repatriation, competition for domestic firms, and vulnerability to sudden capital outflows. India's caution regarding capital-account liberalization, together with its large reserves after 1993–94, helped limit exposure to shocks, but it also meant that India's integration remained partial. By 2004 the ratio of trade to gross domestic product had roughly doubled from its level in 1990–91, yet India's share of world trade remained small.

Section 98.9

Information Technology and the Foundations of India's Software Industry

India's software industry is often presented as a spontaneous product of liberalization, but the historical record shows a longer foundation. The Indian Institutes of Technology, the first of which opened at Kharagpur in 1951, trained engineers in numbers and quality that were unusual for a poor country; the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research and the Indian Statistical Institute built early computing capacity; and public-sector institutions in telecommunications, defense, space, and electronics created a pool of technical expertise. The Department of Electronics, established in 1971, and public enterprises such as the Electronics Corporation of India supported domestic hardware production, with mixed results. In 1978, after the government's enforcement of the foreign-exchange law, IBM left India, and its departure encouraged domestic computing firms. Tata Consultancy Services, founded in 1968, and later firms such as Wipro, which moved into computers from vegetable oil in the 1980s, and Infosys, founded in Pune in 1981 by a group of professionals and relocated to Bengaluru, grew in this environment.

Two features of the earlier period proved important. The first was the export-processing model: the Santacruz Electronics Export Processing Zone in Bombay, established in 1973, allowed firms to import equipment duty-free for export production. The second was what became known as body shopping, in which Indian programmers were sent to client sites abroad, especially in the United States, to work under contract. This brought exposure to international clients and standards and earned foreign exchange, though it offered limited value added. Indian engineers who emigrated to the United States, many of whom worked in the technology industry and several of whom later founded companies in California, created a diaspora network that connected Indian firms to markets and ideas.

Government policy shifted in the 1980s. The computer policy of 1984 and the software export, development, and training policy of 1986 reduced duties on imported equipment, recognized software as an industry, and encouraged export-oriented firms. Texas Instruments established a design center in Bengaluru in 1985, a step that signaled the potential of the city as a site for multinational research and development. The National Association of Software and Service Companies, established in 1988, became the industry's principal representative body.

These facts support a central conclusion: the software sector grew from investments in education, institutions, infrastructure, and human capital that long preceded 1991. Liberalization mattered because it lowered the costs of importing equipment, allowed firms to expand and raise capital, permitted foreign investment and partnerships, and eased exchange controls on travel and business. The sector's growth was a joint product of earlier public investment and later policy change, and it would be misleading to credit either alone.

There is also a question of scale and employment. By the end of the 1990s software and related services employed a few hundred thousand people directly, which was small beside a workforce of roughly four hundred million. The sector's importance lay in its export earnings, its demonstration effects, its influence on urban regions, and the new class of salaried professionals it created, not in the number of jobs it provided.

Section 98.10

Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Telecommunications, and the Rise of Global Services

During the 1990s India moved from software exports based on sending programmers abroad to offshore delivery of services from Indian locations. Clients in the United States and Europe contracted Indian firms for programming, maintenance, testing, and engineering tasks, which were carried out in India and delivered over data links. The model depended on cost advantages, a large pool of English-speaking engineering graduates, and the time difference, which allowed work to continue around the clock. The late 1990s brought an especially large demand for remediation of the year-2000 date problem in older computer systems, which employed Indian firms on a large scale and introduced them to many new clients. After the technology bubble burst in 2000–01 growth slowed, but the industry's client base and capabilities had been established.

The industry was spatially concentrated but not in a single city. Bengaluru, long a center of public-sector aerospace, defense, and space research and of institutions such as the Indian Institute of Science, became the leading hub, and it attracted multinational research centers and domestic firms. Hyderabad rose rapidly in the late 1990s under the state government of Chandrababu Naidu, which promoted a planned technology district called HITEC City and attracted a Microsoft development center in 1998. Chennai drew on a large supply of engineering graduates from Tamil Nadu's colleges. Pune combined an automobile industry, educational institutions, and early software firms. Mumbai remained the financial center and the site of the earliest export zone. The National Capital Region, particularly Gurgaon and Noida, became a center for business-process services, in which companies such as General Electric established back-office operations serving customers abroad. The expansion of privately financed engineering colleges in Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, and Tamil Nadu supplied graduates, though quality varied widely and access depended heavily on family income.

Telecommunications reform provided the infrastructure. Before 1991 the Department of Telecommunications held a monopoly over services, with long waiting lists for telephone connections in cities and very low connection rates in villages. The National Telecom Policy of 1994 opened basic and cellular services to private operators, and the first mobile-phone call in India was made in Kolkata in July 1995. Early licensing was marred by high bids, litigation, and controversy, including allegations of corruption against a minister in 1996. The Telecom Regulatory Authority of India was created in 1997. The New Telecom Policy of 1999 replaced fixed license fees with revenue-sharing, rescuing operators that had bid unsustainably; in 2000 the telecommunications department's operations were corporatized as Bharat Sanchar Nigam Limited; and the monopoly of VSNL on international long-distance services ended in 2002. Public access to the Internet began through VSNL in August 1995. Meanwhile the spread of public call offices, with their distinctive yellow signs, which allowed anyone to make long-distance calls for a fee, made telephone access a daily experience for millions of people who had no telephone at home.

These developments should not be told only through celebrated entrepreneurs. Companies such as Infosys, Tata Consultancy Services, and Wipro became prominent, and their founders and chairmen, including N. R. Narayana Murthy, Azim Premji, and F. C. But the industry rested equally on tens of thousands of programmers, testers, and support workers, on state governments that provided land and power, on public-sector telecommunications engineers, and on the diaspora professionals who linked Indian and American firms. Women's participation in services, though limited compared with the overall workforce, expanded in call centers and software, creating new patterns of work and mobility for urban middle-class and aspirant families. Section 98.11

Manufacturing, Domestic Enterprise, and International Competition

Information technology received much of the attention, but the transformation of industry was broader. The removal of licensing, the cheaper imports of machinery and components, and the arrival of foreign competitors changed the strategies of established firms. The passenger-car industry offers the clearest example. For most of the 1980s the market was dominated by Maruti-Suzuki, supplemented by older models from Hindustan Motors and Premier. After 1991 foreign manufacturers entered, including Mercedes-Benz in partnership with Tata Engineering in 1994, Honda, Daewoo, Ford in partnership with Mahindra, and Hyundai, which established a plant near Chennai in the mid-1990s. In December 1998 Tata Motors launched the Indica, the first passenger car substantially designed in India. Competition raised quality and choice, lowered waiting times, and generated a large components industry, though it also bankrupted some weaker producers.

Consumer goods and electronics showed similar patterns. Foreign brands in televisions, refrigerators, and washing machines, such as LG and Samsung, entered the market in the mid-1990s and competed with Indian firms such as Videocon and Onida, which in turn tried to upgrade. Hindustan Lever, Nestle, and other multinational affiliates already operating in India extended product ranges, while new domestic brands appeared. Satellite television, which began reaching Indian homes from 1991 with Star TV and Zee TV, increased advertising and created a mass market for branded goods.

Pharmaceuticals followed a different path. Because the Patents Act of 1970 recognized process patents but not product patents for drugs, Indian firms such as Ranbaxy, Cipla, and Dr. Reddy's built the capacity to manufacture generic versions of medicines at low cost, and by the 1990s India was a significant exporter. The TRIPS agreement of 1995 required a change to product patents by 2005, giving the industry a decade to adapt through investment in research and expansion into regulated export markets. Business groups such as Reliance, Tata, Birla, and others also reorganized. Reliance built large petrochemical and refining capacity, including the Jamnagar refinery that began operating in 1999, and the Tata group's acquisition of the British tea company Tetley in 2000 was an early example of an Indian firm buying a well-known foreign brand.

The effects on employment must be separated from effects on productivity. Output and productivity per worker in organized manufacturing rose, partly because firms modernized and used more capital-intensive technology, and partly because less efficient plants shut. Yet employment in the organized sector did not grow commensurately, and in some years it fell. Most Indian workers remained in the unorganized or informal sector, which includes small workshops, household enterprises, contract labor, and casual work without legal protection or social security. Several features of the regulatory environment, including the Industrial Disputes Act's requirement that establishments above a certain size obtain government permission to retrench workers, the reservation of products for small units, and the difficulty of acquiring land, encouraged firms to stay small or to use contract labor. Scholars refer to this as the missing middle of Indian manufacturing and debate its causes. The contrast with labor-intensive export manufacturing in East Asia, which absorbed large numbers of workers from agriculture, was frequently drawn.

The experience of the 1990s therefore showed that industrial liberalization could raise efficiency and expand output while leaving the problem of mass employment unresolved. The garment industry, which employed many workers in clusters such as Tiruppur, and the diamond-cutting industry of Surat expanded, but not on a scale sufficient to transform the labor market.

Section 98.12

Agriculture, Rural Economies, and the Uneven Reach of Reform

In 1991 agriculture contributed close to thirty percent of gross domestic product and employed a little over sixty percent of the workforce, so that any assessment of reform had to consider rural India. Agriculture was shaped by monsoon variability, irrigation, public procurement of wheat and rice at support prices, subsidized fertilizer, electricity, and water, and a credit system dominated by cooperatives, commercial banks with priority-sector obligations, and moneylenders. The Green Revolution, examined in Chapter 94, had concentrated high productivity in Punjab, Haryana, and western Uttar Pradesh, leaving rainfed regions and the east far behind.

The 1991 reforms did not directly restructure agriculture. The major measures were in industry, trade, and finance, and their effects on farmers were indirect. The exchange-rate adjustment improved the profitability of agricultural exports; restrictions on the export of products such as rice were relaxed during the 1990s, and basmati and other exports grew. Industrial and trade liberalization altered the terms of trade between agriculture and industry, though economists disagree about the extent to which these shifted in favor of farmers. Phosphate and potash fertilizers were decontrolled in 1992, raising prices, although urea remained controlled. The WTO Agreement on Agriculture, in force from 1995, set rules on subsidies and market access, and India's remaining quantitative restrictions were removed in 2001.

Agricultural growth was weaker in the 1990s than in the 1980s, slowing to roughly three percent a year in the first half of the decade and lower afterward by many estimates. Several factors contributed: a fall in public investment in irrigation, rural infrastructure, and research as fiscal consolidation squeezed capital spending; the exhaustion of gains from the first phase of the Green Revolution in the leading regions; falling water tables in intensively irrigated areas; and slower growth of rural credit as banks became more cautious. Procurement prices rose sharply in the late 1990s, and government stocks of wheat and rice grew to more than sixty million tonnes at times in 2001–02, while many households continued to experience undernutrition, a paradox that illustrated the weakness of the distribution system and of purchasing power. The government introduced the Targeted Public Distribution System in 1997 to focus subsidized grain on poorer households and the Kisan Credit Card in 1998 to ease farm borrowing.

Farm distress became more visible in the late 1990s, especially among cotton and other commercial-crop growers in Andhra Pradesh, Maharashtra, and Karnataka, where reports of farmer suicides drew national attention. The causes were debated: indebtedness to private moneylenders, rising costs of seeds, fertilizer, and irrigation, crop failure and price falls, and the exposure of small producers to market risk after the withdrawal of some state support were all proposed, and researchers differ about the relative weights. What is clear is that reform was not designed with the vulnerable small farmer principally in mind, and that regional experience diverged sharply.

Sections 98.13–98.16 · Political fragmentation, changing electoral coalitions, and continuity in economic policy

Section 98.13

The Erosion of Congress Dominance and the Fragmentation of the Party System

For most of the first four decades after independence, Indian national politics was organized around the Indian National Congress, which won every general election until 1977 and, after the interruption of the Janata government (Chapter 93), returned to power in 1980 and 1984. The earlier chapters of this volume describe how this dominance rested on a broad coalition of upper-caste elites, Dalits, Muslims, and rural interests, on the legacy of the freedom struggle, and on a party organization that reached into the villages. By the 1980s that structure had weakened. Indira Gandhi's centralization of authority had substituted loyalty to the leader for organizational elections and state-level leadership, and the party's capacity to mediate local conflicts declined. New political groups, based on regional identity, caste, and linguistic or religious appeals, challenged Congress in the states, as Chapter 94 describes, and the Congress coalition frayed.

The general election of 1989 marked a decisive change. Congress, which had won an overwhelming 404 seats in 1984, fell to 197, and a National Front government of the Janata Dal and regional parties took office under V. P. Singh with the outside support of both the Bharatiya Janata Party and the Left, two forces that agreed on little else. The experience showed that a national government could be formed without Congress and that unlikely combinations of parties could sustain it, at least for a time. It also showed their fragility. The detailed development of caste-based mobilization following the Mandal Commission, of the Ayodhya movement, and of the rise of the Bharatiya Janata Party is reserved for Chapter 99. This section concerns only the structural change in the party system, because it determined the political conditions under which economic reform was carried out.

The new party system had several features. First, regional parties became durable national actors. Parties such as the Telugu Desam Party in Andhra Pradesh, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam and the All India Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam in Tamil Nadu, the Akali Dal in Punjab, the Asom Gana Parishad in Assam, and later the Samajwadi Party and the Bahujan Samaj Party in Uttar Pradesh, and the Rashtriya Janata Dal in Bihar, won state governments and then sent substantial delegations to the Lok Sabha. Second, Indian elections increasingly became a sum of state-level contests with different principal competitors in different states. Third, the electorate became more mobilized: turnout among poorer, lower-caste, and rural voters rose, which the political scientist Yogendra Yadav has called the second democratic upsurge. Fourth, the Election Commission, under the Chief Election Commissioner T. N. Seshan between 1990 and 1996, enforced the model code of conduct and the electoral law more strictly, changing the conduct of campaigns.

Several constitutional and legal developments also shaped the new system. The Supreme Court's judgment in S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) restricted the central government's use of Article 356 to dismiss state governments, making state-level governments more secure from arbitrary removal. The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments of 1992–93, adopted under the Rao government, gave constitutional status to panchayats and municipalities. The anti-defection law of 1985 penalized legislators who changed party allegiance, which made it more difficult to assemble majorities through individual defection, though party splits remained a way around it; the 91st Amendment of 2003 tightened these provisions and capped the size of ministries. These rules affected how coalitions were negotiated and how long they lasted.

It is essential to distinguish the loss of a parliamentary majority from the loss of political influence. Congress remained the largest party in 1991, continued to form governments, and retained a national organization, though it no longer commanded the electoral dominance of earlier decades. Its coalition was reduced and its competitors varied by state. Political dominance gave way to competitive multiparty politics rather than to the dominance of another party. What changed was the manner in which power was acquired and exercised: through negotiation, outside support, and bargaining among parties with different bases, instead of through the working majority of a single party.

Section 98.14

Narasimha Rao's Minority Government, 1991–1996

Governing without a majority was the defining political problem of the Rao government. In 1991 Congress held 244 seats and needed the support, or acquiescence, of a number of smaller parties and independents. Over the next years the government's numbers rose through by-elections, the accession of independents and small parties, and splits in opposition parties, so that by the mid-1990s it commanded a working majority, but its position was never secure. Parliamentary management became a skill in itself, and Rao, who was known for his patience and for a preference for waiting out difficulties, used it systematically: he delayed decisions, avoided confrontations that might have forced a vote, worked through committees, and negotiated with leaders of other parties. Rob Jenkins has argued that this style of reform by stealth, with its sequence of administrative measures that avoided drawing attention, was well suited to the constraints of minority rule.

The government's survival was tested in July 1993, when it faced a no-confidence motion that it defeated by a narrow margin of roughly fourteen votes. Subsequently allegations were made that members of the Jharkhand Mukti Morcha and others had been paid to vote against the motion. The case reached the Supreme Court, which in 1998 held, by a majority, that members of Parliament were protected by the constitutional privilege of parliamentary speech and voting from prosecution for bribes taken to vote in a particular way. That ruling was reconsidered and overruled by a seven-judge bench of the Supreme Court in March 2024, in a later case, a reminder of the way historical episodes can be reinterpreted by later institutions.

Reform proceeded under these conditions. Several key measures, including the industrial policy, the trade policy, exchange-rate changes, and the notification of foreign-investment rules, required only executive action, not legislation, which suited a government lacking a majority. Measures that required Parliament, such as the SEBI Act of 1992 and constitutional amendments on local government, were passed with the support of various parties. Reforms that required confronting powerful interests directly, such as changes to labor law, the closure of loss-making public enterprises, large-scale privatization, and reforms of agricultural subsidies, were mostly avoided. The government's political capital was finite and it spent it selectively.

The costs and controversies were considerable. The securities scandal of 1992 involving Harshad Mehta undermined public confidence in the financial system and raised questions about the oversight of banks, and the demolition of the Babri Masjid in Ayodhya in December 1992, followed by violence in Mumbai and other cities (examined in Chapter 99), shook the government and tested its authority. The government was also associated with controversies over telecommunications licensing, the hawala cases involving payments to politicians, and other allegations that damaged its reputation by 1996. Within Congress, senior leaders including N. D. Tiwari and Arjun Singh left the party in 1994–95 over disagreements with Rao, further weakening it. State elections in 1993–95 produced Congress losses in several states, and the party's dependence on regional support was exposed.

The Rao government was nonetheless a single-party government, with a single prime minister and cabinet drawn from one party, and it differs from the multiparty coalitions that followed. A minority government rules on the basis of issue-by-issue support or abstention by others; a coalition government is formed by a negotiated agreement among parties that share ministerial office and agree to common policies. This distinction, which Section 98.15 develops further, helps explain why the Rao government could be both strong in executive decision-making and fragile in parliamentary arithmetic. In the 1996 general election Congress was reduced to about 140 seats, the lowest in its history to that time, and Rao resigned as prime minister in May 1996.

Section 98.15

Coalition Experiments and the United Front Governments, 1996–1998

The 1996 election produced the first of a series of hung parliaments. The Bharatiya Janata Party won about 161 seats and Congress about 140, with the Janata Dal and the Left parties and a range of regional parties holding most of the remainder. President Shankar Dayal Sharma invited the leader of the largest party, Atal Bihari Vajpayee, to form a government, and Vajpayee was sworn in on May 16, 1996. Unable to find sufficient allies, he resigned on May 28 without facing a vote of confidence, after thirteen days in office.

Historical Vocabulary
Minority Government, Coalition Government, and Outside Support

A minority government is formed by a party (or group) that lacks a majority in the legislature and survives on issue-by-issue support or abstention. A coalition government is formed by a negotiated agreement among parties that share ministerial office and a program. Outside support means a party backs a government in confidence votes without joining the ministry, and so keeps the ability to withdraw support without leaving office.

These forms had different strengths. The Rao government (1991–96) was a single-party minority government; the United Front (1996–98) was a coalition dependent on Congress's outside support; the National Democratic Alliance (1998–2004) was a coalition built around a dominant partner, the BJP, with some allies in and some outside the ministry.

A broad group of non-BJP parties then formed the United Front, led by the Janata Dal and including the Telugu Desam Party, the DMK, the Tamil Maanila Congress, the Samajwadi Party, the Asom Gana Parishad, and the Communist Party of India among others, with the Communist Party of India (Marxist) supporting from outside. Congress, now under the leadership of Sitaram Kesri after Rao stepped down as party president, supported the government from outside without joining it. The CPI(M) declined an offer to lead the government through its veteran Jyoti Basu, a decision later described by Basu himself as a historic blunder, and H. D. Deve Gowda, until then chief minister of Karnataka, became prime minister on June 1, 1996. A Common Minimum Programme laid out the coalition's agenda.

Section 98.16

Vajpayee, the National Democratic Alliance, and Policy Continuity, 1998–2004

The 1998 election returned the Bharatiya Janata Party as the largest party with about 182 seats, and Vajpayee formed a government on March 19, 1998, as head of a coalition of the BJP and numerous allies, including the Samata Party, the Akali Dal, the Shiv Sena, and, with support from outside, the Telugu Desam Party. The All India Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, led by J. Jayalalithaa, joined the coalition, and its withdrawal in April 1999 caused the government to lose a confidence vote by a single vote. After a brief interval, the elections of September and October 1999 returned the National Democratic Alliance with a clear majority of around 300 seats, of which the BJP held 182, and Vajpayee's government remained in office until May 2004. This was the first coalition government at the center to complete a full term.

The NDA was more durable than its predecessors for several reasons. It had a clear leader in Vajpayee, who was acceptable to many parties, and a single dominant partner in the BJP, whereas the United Front had been a coalition of equals dependent on an outside supporter. It adopted a National Agenda for Governance in 1998, a common program that left aside the BJP's most divisive ideological commitments, including the construction of a temple at Ayodhya, the abrogation of Article 370, and a uniform civil code, which allies would not accept. A coordinating structure, with George Fernandes as convenor, managed disputes. Chapter 99 examines the BJP's ideology and rise; this section is concerned with the NDA as an instrument of governance and economic policy.

Economic policy displayed continuity with the preceding period, with some acceleration. In 1998, after the nuclear tests at Pokhran, treated in Chapter 105, the United States and other countries imposed sanctions that had limited economic effect and were largely lifted within a few years. The Asian financial crisis of 1997–98 had little direct effect on India, which was protected by capital controls and a modest dependence on short-term foreign borrowing. The government pursued disinvestment through the new department and ministry; opened insurance to private companies and to foreign equity of up to twenty-six percent through the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority Act of 1999, over the opposition of the Left; adopted the New Telecom Policy of 1999; passed the Electricity Act of 2003 to restructure the power sector; and enacted the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act of 2003, which set targets for reducing deficits. It launched the National Highways Development Project, including the Golden Quadrilateral connecting Delhi, Mumbai, Chennai, and Kolkata, and the Pradhan Mantri Gram Sadak Yojana for rural roads in 2000. New central government employees were brought under a contributory pension system from 2004. The Fifth Pay Commission's recommendations, implemented in 1997–98, raised public-sector wages and widened fiscal deficits in the early years of the period, a reminder that the fiscal consolidation of the early 1990s was not sustained.

The coalition also experienced tensions over economic policy. The Swadeshi Jagran Manch and the Bharatiya Mazdoor Sangh, organizations affiliated with the Hindu nationalist movement, opposed foreign investment in sectors such as insurance and retail and objected to the pace of disinvestment, while the Left opposed privatization and labor reform. Yashwant Sinha, finance minister from 1998 to 2002, and later Jaswant Singh, had to reconcile these pressures with the needs of allied parties and with the investor-friendly direction of policy. The government generally avoided labor-law reform and the sale of the largest public-sector firms, and some initiatives, such as the sale of Hindustan Petroleum and Bharat Petroleum, were blocked by the Supreme Court and by opposition. The continuity of reform under a party that had once been associated with economic nationalism indicates that it was supported by a wide range of political actors, including state governments of various parties that competed for investment.

Several patterns emerge. After a sharp slowdown in 1991–92, growth recovered and averaged approximately six to seven percent in the mid-1990s, reaching its peak in 1995–96 and 1996–97, driven by industrial expansion and investment, exports, and good monsoons. The Reserve Bank's retrospective assessments document this recovery. Growth slowed in 1997–98 with the Asian crisis, a poor monsoon, and weak industrial performance, recovered in the late 1990s, and then fluctuated in the early 2000s, with a drought in 2002–03 producing a low figure and a rebound in 2003–04. Over the whole period from 1992 to 2004, growth was on average higher than in the 1980s, but not dramatically so, and growth in the 1980s was itself significant.

The composition of output shifted. The share of agriculture in gross domestic product fell from about thirty percent to roughly twenty-two percent, that of industry remained about a quarter, and services rose from about forty percent to over fifty percent, driven by telecommunications, finance, trade, business services, and software. This pattern differs from the typical East Asian experience, in which industry and manufacturing expand first, and it has led economists to discuss whether India was moving to a services-led model. The employment structure changed much more slowly: the share of the workforce in agriculture fell from roughly sixty-four percent in 1993–94 to about sixty percent in 1999–2000 and under sixty percent by 2004–05. India thus had a structure in which most output came from non-agricultural sectors while most workers remained in agriculture. Gross domestic saving and investment rates rose, with the saving rate increasing to around thirty percent of GDP by 2003–04, and exports of goods and services grew substantially, as did foreign-exchange reserves, which by 2004 were about one hundred billion dollars or more.

Section 98.18

Poverty, Employment, and the Distribution of Economic Gains

Growth does not translate automatically into improvements in living standards, and the evidence on poverty and employment in the 1990s has been contested. The Planning Commission's official estimates, based on household consumption surveys conducted by the National Sample Survey Organisation, showed that the proportion of the population below the poverty line declined from about thirty-six percent in 1993–94 to about twenty-six percent in 1999–2000. These estimates, however, were affected by a change in the survey's design in the 55th round of 1999–2000, which altered the recall period for reporting expenditure on some items and made the results not strictly comparable with those of the preceding round. Angus Deaton and Jean Drèze, in an influential 2002 analysis, adjusted for this problem and concluded that poverty did decline in the 1990s but by less than the official figure suggested; Abhijit Sen and Himanshu reached more skeptical conclusions about the pace of decline. The debate illustrates the problem of comparing statistical series across survey changes.

Distinguishing among income, consumption, employment security, and access to public services is also essential. A household may have higher consumption yet face greater insecurity, and services such as education, health care, and sanitation depend on public provision as well as income. Public spending on health and education remained low in the 1990s, and social indicators in several states, particularly in the north and east, lagged behind. Literacy rose substantially between the 1991 and 2001 censuses, and child mortality fell, but progress was uneven among states and social groups.

Employment, in particular, grew much more slowly after 1993–94 than in the preceding decade. Estimates from the National Sample Survey show employment growth of about two percent a year between 1983 and 1993–94 and about one percent between 1993–94 and 1999–2000, even though output growth was higher in the later period. This pattern, described by some as jobless growth, reflected slower growth in agricultural employment, the capital-intensive character of some industrial growth, and the concentration of expansion in services requiring skilled labor. Real wages for agricultural workers appear to have stagnated in the later 1990s, and open unemployment rose.

The experience of different groups varied. Urban professionals with technical or managerial skills, particularly in information technology, finance, and consulting, saw rising incomes and new opportunities. Organized-sector industrial workers faced restructuring and a reduction in the relative size of their sector, though those who remained often had secure jobs. Informal-sector workers, who constituted the great majority, experienced changes that depended on local labor markets, with some gaining from construction and services demand and others facing greater insecurity. Farmers differed according to region, holding size, and crop. Women's participation in the labor force did not rise significantly overall, but some women found new employment in software, call centers, garments, and microfinance-supported enterprises. Dalit and Adivasi communities, with lower asset holdings and less access to education, were less well placed to take advantage of new opportunities, and the expansion of mining, dams, and industrial projects displaced many Adivasi communities, with compensation and resettlement often inadequate. Later scholarship using tax and survey data, including that associated with the World Inequality Database, suggests that income inequality, especially at the top of the distribution, increased during this period.

Historical Debate

Did Liberalization Reduce Poverty and Spread the Gains of Growth?

The question: Did the growth of the 1990s reduce poverty substantially and widely, or did uneven growth and weak employment create new inequality?

Interpretation A: Growth-led interpretation: faster growth after 1991 expanded incomes, raised consumption, and reduced the proportion of people below the poverty line, and further liberalization would accelerate the process. Advocates such as Jagdish Bhagwati and Arvind Panagariya argued that growth is the main route to poverty reduction and finances social programs.
Interpretation B: Distribution-focused interpretation: the gains were concentrated, employment growth slowed, social-sector provision lagged, regional and social inequalities widened, and some groups were displaced. Scholars such as Jean Drèze and Amartya Sen argued that growth had to be accompanied by public investment in health, education, and employment, and that India’s social indicators lagged behind those of countries with similar income.

Evidence: The evidence includes NSS consumption surveys (with the comparability problems described in Section 98.18), employment surveys, wage data, social indicators from censuses, and later work on income distribution. The two positions are not mutually exclusive: many scholars accept that poverty fell and that inequality and deprivation persisted.

Why it matters: The question shapes how later policy is understood, including the rights-based welfare legislation examined in Chapter 100, which responded to criticism that growth alone was inadequate.

The responsibility of the historian is to assess the evidence and the competing interpretations rather than to celebrate growth or condemn liberalization. Poverty fell, probably less quickly than the official estimates suggested, and certainly not equally across regions and groups. Employment did not expand in proportion to output. The reform era thus presents a mixed record: gains in overall income and in the opportunities available to some groups, together with persistent deprivation and growing inequality.

Section 98.19

Regional Inequality and the New Economic Geography of India

One of the most significant consequences of liberalization was the changing distribution of economic activity across India's regions. Under the earlier system, the central government influenced the location of industry through licensing, public-sector investment, and freight-equalization policies, which reduced the advantages of states close to resources and ports. After 1991, with licensing abolished and public investment less dominant, private firms chose locations on the basis of infrastructure, labor, market access, governance, and the incentives offered by state governments. Investment gravitated toward states with better infrastructure, skilled workforces, and administrative capacity, and the gaps in per-capita income between richer and poorer states widened in the 1990s, according to most studies.

Section 98.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

The preceding sections trace a connected sequence. A fiscal and external imbalance, built through the 1980s and aggravated by the oil shock, the Gulf crisis, the end of Soviet trade, and political instability, produced the balance-of-payments emergency of 1990–91. A new government with a minority in Parliament, led by Rao and with Singh as finance minister, treated the emergency as an occasion for structural change, combining stabilization with industrial, trade, and financial reform. Subsequent measures liberalized foreign investment and integrated the economy with global markets; the information technology and services sectors, built on earlier investments in education and institutions, expanded; and manufacturing, agriculture, and regions experienced the changes differently. Meanwhile the political system shifted from the dominance of one party to a competitive multiparty system in which regional parties held the balance, and governments were formed by minority arrangements and coalitions. The measured results included higher and more stable growth, rising reserves and exports, and declining but unequally distributed poverty, alongside weak employment growth, agrarian distress, and widening regional gaps.

The central question of the chapter was why market-oriented reform persisted despite unstable governments and political fragmentation. The evidence suggests several mutually reinforcing explanations. First, the crisis of 1991 created a shared perception that a return to the older system was not feasible, and the recovery that followed reinforced this belief. Second, reform proceeded incrementally and largely through executive action, with limited legislation, which lowered the visibility of change and reduced opportunities for opponents to organize; the most politically costly reforms, such as labor law and the closure of public enterprises, were largely postponed. Third, the reforms benefited concentrated, organized groups, including business, professionals, and exporters, who had a stake in continuity, while those who bore the costs were dispersed and had fewer channels of influence. Fourth, federalism and coalition politics gave regional parties and state governments a stake in reform, since chief ministers could attract investment and fiscal resources. Fifth, new regulatory institutions, such as SEBI, the Telecom Regulatory Authority, and a more autonomous Reserve Bank, and the commitments undertaken in the WTO created institutional and international constraints on reversal. Sixth, the parties that governed after 1996, from the United Front to the NDA, differed in their ideologies but converged on the main lines of economic policy, while differing about pace, social protection, and ownership.

Who experienced change most directly? Urban professionals, large firms, exporters, and workers in restructured sectors. Who experienced more continuity? Small farmers, informal workers, and residents of poorer states, for whom insecurity and limited services largely persisted. Why the pattern matters: It shows that a profound change in policy can coexist with continuity in everyday life for many people.

Yet the persistence of reform did not mean the absence of contest. The reform agenda was shaped, slowed, and modified by opposition from the Left, unions, farmers' movements, and the economic nationalists within the Hindu nationalist movement itself, and the defeat of the NDA in 2004 after its India Shining campaign showed that economic indicators did not directly translate into electoral support. Voters in India's democracy responded to caste, region, local governance, and the experience of their own households, as much as to national economic narratives. The tension between the policy consensus at the top and the diversity of electoral demands below was a defining feature of the era.

India thus entered the twenty-first century with a more internationally integrated economy, a larger private sector, new regulatory institutions, a growing services and technology sector, and a coalition-centered political order, but also with persistent social and regional inequalities and unresolved problems of employment, agriculture, infrastructure, and human development. The chapter's central proposition is that liberalization was not simply the achievement of one government in 1991. It became an enduring and contested policy transformation, carried forward by successive governments operating within a changing democratic system.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how India's balance-of-payments emergency of 1990–91 became the starting point for a transformation in economic governance and in the party system. It argued that the crisis arose from long-term structural weaknesses (large fiscal deficits, a growing current-account deficit, short-maturity external debt, and an economy insulated from competition) and was triggered by short-term shocks including the Gulf crisis and the end of Soviet trade.

The crisis was a shortage of foreign currency, which is different from a budget deficit. The government responded with emergency import compression, gold pledges, IMF assistance, and, in July 1991, a two-stage adjustment of the rupee. The new Rao government, a minority administration, treated the emergency as an occasion for structural change.

Rao and Singh each played an essential part: Rao provided political leadership and protection; Singh designed and explained the macroeconomic and budgetary program. The budget and Statement on Industrial Policy of July 24, 1991, combined stabilization with structural reform and were presented as both a response to crisis and a long-term strategy. Historians debate whether the reforms were compelled by external pressure or chosen domestically, and the best reading combines both.

Industrial licensing was largely abolished, the public-sector reserved list was reduced, the MRTP Act's restraints on expansion were removed, and foreign investment was welcomed. Trade, exchange-rate, and financial reforms followed through the early 1990s, supported by new regulatory institutions such as SEBI. Liberalization changed the form of regulation rather than ending it.

Liberalization, disinvestment, and privatization were distinct. Disinvestment began cautiously in 1991–92, and genuine privatization occurred after 1999 in a few cases. Most major public enterprises remained under state control in 2004, so India's economy remained mixed with a different balance.

The software and services sector grew from earlier investments in education, research institutions, and export infrastructure, accelerated by liberalization, telecommunications reform, and global demand. Manufacturing, agriculture, and regions experienced reform differently. Manufacturing output and productivity rose without commensurate employment, agriculture slowed and showed distress in some areas, and growth concentrated in states with better infrastructure and administration.

Politically, Congress dominance gave way to a competitive multiparty system, in which regional parties held the balance. The Rao government governed as a minority and used executive action and cautious management. After 1996 the United Front governments, depending on outside support, and the NDA coalition under Vajpayee continued the main lines of economic policy despite ideological differences and instability.

The results included higher and more stable growth than in the 1980s in some periods, rising reserves and exports, and a shift toward services. Poverty declined, though the extent is contested because of survey changes, and employment growth slowed, rural distress appeared, and regional and social inequalities widened.

Reform persisted because the crisis made reversal seem infeasible, because change was incremental and executive, because beneficiaries were organized, because regional parties and state governments gained from investment, because new institutions and international commitments constrained reversal, and because governments converged on the broad policy framework while differing on pace and social protection.

India entered the twenty-first century with an integrated, more competitive economy and a coalition-centered political order, but with persistent inequalities. The economic and political transformations continue in Chapter 99, on caste mobilization and the rise of the BJP, and in Chapter 100, on India after 2004.

Foreign currency, gold, and related assets held by the central bank.

A deliberate lowering of a currency's value under a fixed or managed regime.

Short-term measures to restore macroeconomic balance.

Changes to rules and institutions that alter long-term productive capacity.

Reduction of restrictions on economic activity.

Government sale of equity in a public enterprise.

Transfer of ownership or control from the state to private interests.

A status given from 1997 to selected profitable public enterprises, granting more autonomy.
Tariff
A tax on imports.

The record of investment and borrowing flows; India retained controls on it.

Investment giving a lasting stake and often control in a firm.

Purchase of shares or bonds that can be sold quickly.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because the transformation it describes reshaped the setting in which every later chapter of Volume VI unfolds. A more open, more competitive economy and a coalition-centered party system became the standing conditions of Indian public life, and the arguments of the 1990s about the role of the state, the distribution of growth, and the balance between markets and welfare continue.

The chapter also shows how historical change combines contingency and structure. A crisis that was triggered by external events exposed weaknesses built over a decade; leaders who held different kinds of authority used the crisis to change direction; and a changing political system, rather than preventing reform, shaped its form. The result was neither a revolution nor a mere adjustment, but a prolonged and contested transformation.

Connections with other regions of South Asia run through this story. The Gulf crisis linked Indian households to the Middle East; the technology and services economy linked Indian cities to the United States and Europe; and the opening of the economy altered India's relations with its neighbors, whose own economic and political trajectories are the subject of Chapters 101 through 104. Chapter 99 turns to the political changes that unfolded alongside economic reform, and Chapter 100 follows the consequences into the twenty-first century.

Within the six-volume sequence, the chapter opens the final Part of the work and begins the history of contemporary South Asia. It reminds students that transformations that appear sudden in retrospect have deep roots, and that their consequences are not shared equally.
