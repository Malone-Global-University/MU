Chapter Opening

Religion in colonial Southeast Asia was not a traditional remnant waiting to be displaced by modernity. Monks, scholars, clergy, and lay organizers built schools, presses, charities, and associations that helped create modern Southeast Asian societies.

When Chapter 124 closed, students had seen how cities, schools, newspapers, and voluntary associations created new publics. This chapter asks how religious communities took part in that transformation. Between roughly 1870 and 1940, Buddhists, Muslims, Christians, and followers of new religious movements reorganized how doctrine was taught, how authority was recognized, and how communities financed and governed themselves.

Colonial rule shaped these developments but did not fully explain them. In Upper Burma, the end of the monarchy in 1885–1886 removed the royal patron of the Buddhist monastic order. In the Netherlands Indies and British Malaya, Muslim scholars returning from Mecca and Cairo debated how to interpret scripture. In the Philippines, Filipino clergy challenged the control of Spanish religious orders. In southern Vietnam, new revelations produced the Cao Đài religion in 1926. These cases differed greatly, yet each involved religious authority being rebuilt under new institutional conditions.

A central caution governs the chapter. Religious reform and political nationalism overlapped but were not the same thing. A movement could found schools, publish journals, and run clinics without seeking independence. Some religious leaders cooperated with colonial governments; others resisted; many did both at different times. Detailed political histories belong to Chapters 126–132, and the later military histories of Cao Đài and Hòa Hảo to wartime and postwar chapters.

Evidence includes religious treatises, periodicals, school and charity records, colonial census reports, mission archives, and the writings of reformers. Each reflects an author's position, and census categories in particular forced overlapping religious practices into rigid classifications.

The religious institutions discussed here were material as well as intellectual. A school required teachers and premises; a journal required money, readers and distribution; a monastery required a community willing to support its work. These requirements help explain why merchants, farmers, women organizers, students and teachers are as important to the chapter as the best-known religious founders. Religious confidence alone did not guarantee institutional success, and the survival of a community depended in part on its capacity to manage resources.

The divisions proceed from the changing geography and authority of religion to the histories of Buddhist, Muslim, Christian and other religious communities. The final division considers the conditions under which religious resources acquired political significance. The aim is not to assign a fixed political character to a religion, but to explain how particular institutions and communities responded to the opportunities and constraints of the period.

Historical Context

By 1870 the region's religious life was already plural. Theravāda Buddhism predominated across much of Burma, Siam, Cambodia, and Laos; Mahāyāna Buddhism, Confucian practice, and Daoist and folk traditions shaped Vietnam and Chinese communities; Islam was widespread in Malaya, Sumatra, Java, and parts of Borneo, Sulawesi, Mindanao, and Sulu; Catholicism predominated in the lowland Philippines; and Hindu, Sikh, and indigenous traditions persisted in many places. Chapter 75 treated Christianity in the Spanish Philippines and Chapters 62–66 the spread of Islam.

Colonial states reorganized this inheritance. Courts that once patronized religious institutions were abolished or subordinated, colonial law classified communities by religion, and governments chose how far to regulate or ignore religious education. Steamship travel, printing, and postal networks, discussed in Chapter 124, simultaneously increased pilgrimage and the circulation of texts.

Changes in colonial jurisdiction followed different patterns. The British annexed Upper Burma but maintained protected monarchies in the Malay states; France governed Cochinchina directly while administering other parts of Indochina through varied protectorate arrangements. The American Philippines inherited longstanding Catholic institutions without perpetuating the legal establishment of the Spanish Church. Siam retained sovereignty and initiated its own program of monastic centralization. Religious authority therefore developed within different legal and political structures even where neighboring populations shared practices and institutions.

Economic expansion and migration changed the resources available to religious communities. New merchant and professional groups could finance schools and publications, while migrants relied on temples, mosques, churches and mutual assistance in unfamiliar settings. Some associations depended on the incomes of households whose livelihoods were vulnerable to fluctuations in commodity prices. The Great Depression later intensified many of these pressures, a development treated systematically in Chapter 133.

The period's evidence preserves competing definitions of religious renewal. A monastic treatise expresses an author's doctrinal position; a colonial regulation defines administrative jurisdiction; a journal shows what its editors chose to publish; a local resolution preserved in a court case records a particular claim. None is a complete account of community life. The four source windows and comparative tables are intended to develop the student's ability to connect such evidence with historical argument.

Division I — Religion, Colonialism, and the Transformation of Religious Authority · Section 125.1

The Religious Landscape of Colonial Southeast Asia

Religious institutions constituted a geography of authority that was neither identical to the map of empires nor reducible to the population of each colony. At one scale, a village monastery, neighborhood mosque, parish or temple provided a place for the transmission of knowledge and the observance of obligations. At another scale, scholars and clergy traveled to distant centers of learning and consulted texts that circulated internationally. Individuals were attached to particular households, villages and occupations as well as to these larger religious communities. The importance of the period from approximately 1870 to 1940 lies in the reorganization of these relationships, as older forms of authority encountered new states, new means of communication and changing systems of work and education.

Across the Irrawaddy valley, Buddhist monasteries and pagodas linked the religious life of royal centers with that of agricultural communities. Theravāda Buddhism also sustained institutions throughout Siam, Cambodia and the Lao territories, but the institutions of each society had developed within its own political history. Ordination lineages and the study of Pali texts connected monks across borders. The relationship between monasteries and monarchs, however, differed dramatically between an independent kingdom and a society incorporated into a European empire. Buddhist communities in the Shan states and other frontier areas also possessed traditions of learning and ritual not fully represented by the institutions of Mandalay or Rangoon. A regional description must therefore account for distinctions within Buddhism as well as among religions.

In Vietnam, the institutions of Mahāyāna Buddhism existed alongside ancestor veneration, the ethical teachings associated with Confucianism, Daoist ritual, village cults, and Catholic congregations. A family might maintain an ancestral altar and also participate in Buddhist festivals, without understanding these actions as contradictory religious affiliations. In the southern delta, migrants and settlers developed distinctive religious networks amid changing landholding and commercial relations. Khmer Buddhist communities within Cochinchina connected the religious landscape of the delta to Cambodia. The common association between a territory and one prevailing religious tradition consequently conceals the overlapping practices that structured daily life.

The Malay Peninsula, Sumatra and Java formed important parts of the Muslim world. Muslim scholars, merchants, rulers, teachers and pilgrims maintained connections across the Straits of Malacca and beyond. Aceh and the Minangkabau highlands had their own histories of religious scholarship and political controversy. In Java, mosque-centered learning, pesantren and court traditions existed in a richly varied cultural landscape. Muslim communities also inhabited coastal Borneo, Sulawesi and eastern islands, the southern Philippines and towns scattered throughout the mainland. Neither a common religious identity nor the use of related languages produced a single network of political command.

Christianity was particularly prominent in the lowland Philippines, where centuries of Spanish rule had created an extensive parish system. In other areas, conversion histories followed different paths. Portuguese Timor, the Moluccas, northern Sulawesi, the Batak highlands and Christian communities in Burma and Vietnam illustrate the uneven relationship between missionary activity and political authority. The spread of Christianity was sometimes closely connected to colonial expansion, but neither all Christians nor all missionary organizations served the same political interest. Christian institutions established by Asian clergy and congregations often developed agendas that differed from those of foreign administrators.

Migration further complicated colonial religious landscapes. A Hindu temple in a plantation district, a Sikh gurdwara in a port, or a Chinese community temple might serve a population far from its place of origin. These institutions organized mutual assistance and preserved cultural connections under unfamiliar conditions. Bali retained a distinctive Hindu religious life, while indigenous communities in upland and island environments maintained complex relationships with ancestors, spirits and sacred territories. The historical term “animist,” commonly used by census officers, grouped together extraordinarily varied practices. It is useful when examining administrative documents, but not as a sufficient explanation of the beliefs of the people classified under it. As Chapter 121 established for colonial population categories, classification was itself an exercise of institutional power. Religious identity could be expressed through a census answer, a recorded affiliation, a practice, or a relationship of obligation; these forms of evidence should not be confused.

Division I — Religion, Colonialism, and the Transformation of Religious Authority · Section 125.2

Colonial Rule and the Transformation of Religious Authority

Colonial rule altered religion principally by redistributing the powers previously exercised by kings, scholars, religious courts and local patrons. The conquest of Upper Burma in 1885 and incorporation in 1886 broke the relationship between the Konbaung throne and the Buddhist order. The imperial government did not acquire, or wish to exercise, all the religious responsibilities of the former king. In Cambodia, by contrast, the monarchy continued under French protection; the Nguyễn court survived in Annam, though its political power contracted. The sultans of the Malay states continued to occupy significant positions in Muslim religious life. These arrangements differed fundamentally from the complete removal of a ruling house.

In precolonial Buddhist societies, rulers had supported monasteries through grants, construction and ceremonial patronage, and could attempt to regulate disputes over discipline and ordination. Such intervention did not mean every monastery obediently followed a royal order. Monastic authority was also derived from learning, seniority and recognized discipline. Under colonial rule, gifts from local patrons continued even where the court's support ended. Wealthy merchants, urban professionals and village households financed the buildings and services that continued to give Buddhism institutional presence. A change in patronage might therefore weaken a central office while strengthening other forms of organization.

In the Malay states, British residents advised rulers on administration under arrangements that commonly reserved matters of Islam and Malay custom. The terms varied by territory, and the Straits Settlements were a different jurisdiction. These exceptions did not isolate religion from colonial economic or legal change. Land registration, government revenues, migration and the growth of towns affected the property and personnel available to religious bodies. A sultan's religious jurisdiction also became a defined area of authority within an increasingly specialized state, rather than an unrestricted continuation of earlier sovereignty. Religious offices and councils could expand bureaucratically even as the ruler's authority over other matters contracted.

In the Netherlands Indies, Dutch officials and scholars debated the relationship between Islamic jurisprudence and local customary law, known as adat. Administration could recognize religious courts for specified matters while restricting their jurisdiction. The distinction had consequences for marriage, inheritance and the control of property. It was not simply a description of two naturally separate systems. Local legal practices already involved overlapping religious and customary obligations. The colonial effort to assign jurisdiction could harden boundaries and produce incentives for competing interpreters of the law to defend the authority of their own institutions. Snouck Hurgronje's distinction between Islamic worship and politically threatening mobilization likewise illustrates the effort to govern religious life selectively rather than prohibit it wholesale.

A contrasting legal change took place in the Philippines after the collapse of Spanish sovereignty. The Catholic Church had enjoyed a privileged institutional position in the Spanish regime, while the American administration proclaimed religious liberty and separated public authority from the established church. Disputes over parish property and large friar estates did not disappear with the introduction of new constitutional principles. Courts and land transactions became mechanisms through which religious institutions negotiated the new political order. The case examined in Section 125.14 demonstrates that the right to change one's church affiliation was not equivalent to the right to transfer a building or its furnishings.

Siam's reforms demonstrate that religious bureaucratization was not peculiar to European empire. The Chakri monarchy reorganized the Buddhist order as part of an effort to consolidate territorial administration and authority. In independent Siam, as in the colonies, however, requiring the recognition of a central hierarchy was easier than transforming the practices of every local religious community. The survival of regional languages and manuscript traditions shows the limits of the administrative project. Across Southeast Asia, the most productive comparison is not whether governments supported or opposed religion in general. It is which powers they transferred, recognized or withheld, and how religious actors mobilized their existing resources to respond. A statute or decree reveals intended arrangements, whereas disputes, institutional accounts and local records show how the rules operated in practice.

Multiple Perspectives I

Competing claims to religious authority

Religious leadersMonks, priests and jurists claimed competence derived from ordination, scholarship or office. A government might recognize that competence while limiting its jurisdiction. Religious leaders could therefore seek protection from the state in one controversy and autonomy from it in another.
AdministratorsOfficials valued the provision of education and order, but feared institutions capable of mobilizing people beyond administrative control. Their reports often divided legitimate religious practice from political activity, a distinction that could serve the interests of the government.
Households and donorsFamilies had practical interests in schooling, marriage, burial, worship and the use of communal property. They could support a reforming teacher without accepting every demand made by the reform movement. Their choices determined whether institutions could sustain their work.

The evidence for these perspectives is unequal. A regulation preserves an official decision; a petition preserves a claim presented to authorities; accounts of donations may show actual support. None alone establishes that a community reached consensus.

  2. What evidence would demonstrate that a religious official actually exercised a legal power described in a statute?
  3. How could a local community benefit from colonial recognition while resisting administrative supervision?

Religious Reform, Modernity, and Moral Regeneration

Reform movements commonly began from the conviction that religious life could be improved. The diagnosis might be insufficient study, a decline in discipline, the absence of effective schools, or the influence of practices regarded as incompatible with authoritative teachings. These claims were themselves historical interventions. Reformers defined certain problems as religious problems and proposed solutions that reorganized the lives of believers. What they classified as corruption, other members might understand as inherited custom, an established interpretation or a valuable source of communal belonging. Historians must distinguish the reformer's description of society from an independent judgment about the society being described.

One recurrent change was the emphasis on systematic education. Monks and scholars had long transmitted complex bodies of knowledge through personal instruction, memorization and study of manuscripts. Print and graded schooling offered new ways of distributing such knowledge. A teacher could prepare a common lesson for several schools; an association could require examinations or organize a series of classes; a publisher could reach people who could not reside permanently with an expert. These arrangements did not automatically make education more accessible. Fees, literacy, language, travel and the availability of teachers continued to determine who could participate. They nonetheless altered the possible scale and structure of religious instruction.

Some reformers took science and technical learning seriously as matters of religious and collective welfare. A school that taught arithmetic, geography and hygiene alongside the scriptures challenged the assumption that pupils must choose between modern occupational qualifications and religious education. In a colonial context this was particularly important because government and mission schools could provide qualifications that traditional religious institutions did not always offer. Founding an alternative school made it possible to compete for pupils without surrendering educational initiative. Yet it also required money, the recruitment of teachers and a workable curriculum. Reform was a problem of administration as much as a dispute about doctrine.

Modernity was not a single destination. Islamic modernists often argued that renewed engagement with scripture and reason could meet contemporary circumstances; traditionalist scholars defended the disciplined use of established jurisprudence and transmitting lineages. Both might use print, congresses and charitable organizations. The modernization of a means of teaching did not necessarily entail a change in the content of what was taught. Buddhist movements to disseminate classical doctrine to householders could accomplish something similarly new through a commitment to preserving the tradition. Christian demands for an indigenous clergy could involve the defense of continuity in worship alongside a radical demand for ecclesiastical self-government.

The different temporal horizons of reform deserve attention. A reformer might see present conditions as a decline from an authoritative past, as an opportunity to restore a proper way of life, or as evidence that the world was entering a new religious era. A movement expecting far-reaching spiritual transformation was not necessarily organized around the same practical objectives as a committee seeking additional funds for a school. Both could draw crowds and inspire sacrifice, but their expectations of change and institutional arrangements might be very different. In Vietnam, the emergence of new religions illustrates how claims of revelation and charismatic authority could coexist with rapidly developing formal organizations.

The increased visibility of moral reform also reflected the new publics examined in Chapter 124. Periodicals enabled people to challenge religious teachers in print, while associations gave committees power to collect funds and adopt resolutions. A public controversy could involve people who never met its original participants. Religious authority became a matter of debate in new venues, not simply a declining inheritance of traditional leadership. Women who had long financed worship and taught children could use some of these venues to establish organizations and claim formal responsibilities. This chapter treats modernization in that institutional sense, without assuming that every development diminished religious conviction. The central historical question is how religious values shaped the uses of new institutions, and how the institutions in turn reshaped the exercise of religious authority.

Historical Vocabulary I

Reform, revivalism, modernism and orthodoxy

Reform describes efforts to change religious practice or institutions. Revivalism emphasizes renewed vitality, often through study, worship and moral conduct. Modernism identifies a historically specific effort to engage contemporary knowledge and institutions; it does not mean that a movement abandons authoritative religious sources. Orthodoxy describes a claim to correct teaching, which must be understood in relation to the standards and authorities recognized by particular communities.

These categories overlap. A monk who disseminates a classical text through printing may be a revivalist and an institutional innovator simultaneously. A scholar who defends a legal school may establish an association equipped with elected officers and a modern publication. Pilgrimage, Religious Printing, and Transnational Networks

Pilgrimage, religious printing, and transnational networks became increasingly interdependent during the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Religious travel was not new: the pilgrimage to Mecca and the movement of Buddhist monks between centers of study had older histories. Steam navigation, international postal services and commercial publishing changed the opportunities for travel and communication. The advantages were unevenly distributed, since pilgrimages could consume a household's savings and overseas study required sponsors or employment. Ports and shipping firms became intermediaries in religious life, while governments attempted to regulate movement for reasons of disease control, political security and taxation.

For Southeast Asian Muslims, the pilgrimage to Mecca could be an act of worship and an encounter with a much larger intellectual world. Some pilgrims remained in the Hijaz to pursue study, forming relationships with teachers and fellow students. A community of Southeast Asian origin, commonly called Jawi in the Arabic-speaking centers of learning, connected people who might come from very different islands or political jurisdictions. Returning pilgrims and scholars were not merely carriers of foreign opinions. Their authority depended on the way their learning was recognized in their communities at home, and they participated in local disputes with their own objectives. Cairo's publishing and educational institutions became increasingly important to some reformists, without displacing Mecca's significance.

Buddhist circulation followed a different institutional logic. Ordination lineages, pilgrimage to sites associated with the Buddha, and the study and exchange of manuscripts connected Ceylon, Burma, Siam and other societies. An international movement to restore Buddhist access to sacred sites in India, associated with Anagarika Dharmapala and the Maha Bodhi Society founded in 1891, attracted attention from Asian and other Buddhists. It illustrates how a geographically dispersed religious community could organize around an identifiable sacred place. At the same time, not every monk had the resources or wish to travel. Locally maintained monasteries remained the ordinary sites of instruction, ritual and merit-making.

For Cambodian and Lao monks, journeys to Siam could serve the acquisition of scholarly competence and religious prestige. The French effort to encourage separate centers of Buddhist scholarship within Indochina demonstrates that religious routes could be interpreted as politically significant by imperial administrators. A border negotiated by governments might separate people who shared language, ritual and educational interests. The establishment of new schools and libraries was therefore a way to redirect students and books, although not to eliminate older connections completely. Section 125.8 compares the contrasting objectives of the colonial officials and the monks who participated in these institutions.

Christian networks were sustained by different arrangements of authority. Catholic bishops, religious orders and Rome were connected through appointments, correspondence and requests for support; Protestant mission societies mobilized congregations and funds in Europe and North America. Missionaries traveled with training, supplies and publications, but local interpreters, teachers and converts made religious communication possible. Chinese temple associations and reformist societies also maintained links to southern China through merchant networks, remittances and publications. These systems shared a reliance on intermediaries, even though they did not follow a common ecclesiastical or administrative model.

Printed materials could travel independently of their authors. A journal published in Cairo might be read and adapted in Singapore, then distributed to towns in the Netherlands Indies and the Malay states. The translation of an educational article could matter more to its subsequent readers than the original writer's broader political views. Postal networks, agents and booksellers linked centers of production with scattered readers. Authorities faced the difficulty of distinguishing religious literature from political propaganda, particularly when both drew on similar languages of reform. But the existence of a correspondence network is not proof of coordinated action, just as a mapped connection between two ports is not evidence that every traveler followed a particular itinerary. Historical interpretation requires a distinction between the infrastructure of movement, a demonstrable journey or publication link, and the actual consequences of reception.

Burmese Buddhism after the Fall of the Monarchy

The fall of the Konbaung monarchy transformed the institutional framework of Burmese Buddhism. When British forces occupied Mandalay in November 1885 and King Thibaw was sent into exile, the Buddhist order lost a royal patron whose functions included support for monasteries and intervention in disciplinary disputes. The conquest followed earlier annexations of coastal and Lower Burma territories; therefore, the experience of imperial rule was not new to all Burmese Buddhists. In the lower country, merchants, donors and monks had already been adapting to rapid commercial change. The incorporation of Upper Burma placed the last royal center within the colonial administration and sharpened apprehension about the future of the Buddhist dispensation.

The office of thathanabaing, the senior monastic patriarch, did not vanish on the departure of the king. Its ability to exercise authority under a non-Buddhist colonial state, however, became uncertain. When the incumbent died in 1895, there was a prolonged dispute over recognition of a successor. British recognition was eventually accorded to a new patriarch in the early twentieth century but did not recreate the old monarch's relationship to the order. The government was reluctant to undertake the role of enforcing monastic discipline as a Buddhist king had done. This history demonstrates the important distinction between the persistence of an office and the weakening of its effective powers.

Alicia Turner argues that Buddhist lay and monastic organizers were concerned with the survival and vitality of the sāsana, the dispensation of the Buddha. Confronted with anxiety about decline, they devoted attention to the preservation of texts, observance of moral precepts, the quality of education, and religious giving. Such activity was not a disguised expression of nationalism in every case. An association might seek to improve the moral life of its members without advocating a change in government. Even when its language later became associated with national identity, its earlier motivations require independent examination.

Economic changes offered new opportunities for patronage while undermining older social arrangements. Commercial agriculture and the growth of towns gave some merchants and professionals disposable incomes that could be dedicated to religious purposes. Collections, publications and new schools could be organized beyond the immediate circle of a royal patron. Rural households nevertheless continued to sustain monks through alms, maintenance of monasteries and participation in ceremonies. The contrast was not simply between a traditional rural Buddhism and a modern urban one; monks, pupils, donors and texts circulated between the two settings. Educational initiatives originating in towns could rely on relationships established in village monasteries.

Institutions such as the Young Men's Buddhist Association, founded in Rangoon in 1906, provide evidence of this changing organizational environment. Its form drew partly on examples of modern voluntary associations, but its objectives were shaped by Buddhist concerns. Religious instruction and the respect accorded to Buddhist practices could become matters of public discussion. The controversial wearing of shoes by visitors on pagoda precincts became an issue of religious dignity and colonial inequality in the 1910s. Association leaders and monks could make a matter of etiquette into a public demand; the resulting tensions were neither inevitable nor uniform across the Buddhist community. The detailed political development of the association belongs to Chapter 129.

Women's contributions to the maintenance of the sāsana were substantial even when they were not represented proportionately in institutions of formal monastic authority. Food, money, religious instruction in households and the organization of donations enabled religious communities to function. The existence of women renunciants, including thilashin, also complicates any description of Buddhist religious authority as an exclusively male social world, while their position was not identical to that of fully ordained monks. The historical record favors named religious leaders and literate male association officers, so donations and household practice are particularly valuable evidence for assessing the breadth of the reform movement. Burmese Buddhism continued to draw authority from older institutions while developing new ones, and neither continuity nor innovation alone adequately describes the period.

Buddhist Modernism, Meditation, and Lay Religious Participation

Ledi Sayadaw was born in 1846 and died in 1923, his career spanning the destruction of the Burmese monarchy and the emergence of new Buddhist institutions under colonial rule. His scholarly training gave him expertise in Pali literature, monastic discipline and Abhidhamma, the systematic analysis of phenomena developed in the Buddhist textual tradition. After periods of study and teaching he became associated with a monastery near Monywa, and his widespread reputation was furthered by the circulation of his writings. His significance lies not in inventing meditation, which had long formed part of Buddhist practice, but in reshaping the relationship among scholastic knowledge, lay instruction and practice at a moment of rapid social change.

Erik Braun's study of Ledi's career places the development of modern insight meditation within a long-established religious intellectual tradition. The textual analysis of experience was not an unnecessary preliminary to practice. For Ledi, understanding categories such as conditioned phenomena and impermanence could help provide the knowledge necessary for insight. He sought to communicate difficult doctrinal material in forms accessible to more readers, including people who did not enter monasteries for advanced study. The growth of print publishing and literacy created possibilities for distributing such instruction across distances, although oral teaching and direct relationships between monks and disciples remained indispensable.

The distinction between calm and insight was particularly important to the new presentation of meditation. Calm practices aim at cultivating concentration and tranquility; insight concerns the discernment of the characteristics of conditioned experience. The distinction should not imply that the two are always independent or that all Buddhist schools assign them identical roles. In the Manual of Insight, Ledi describes the possibility of insight practice in ordinary circumstances when knowledge is mature. A householder's continued obligations do not necessarily prevent progress in religious understanding. The manual also includes substantial doctrinal explanations; it would be misleading to interpret it simply as a set of modern relaxation techniques.

The extension of instruction to householders carried institutional implications. Traditionally, monks depended upon lay supporters, whose religious activities included almsgiving, keeping precepts and accumulating merit. Lay meditation and scholarship had precedents. In the colonial period, however, some forms of study and practice could be organized on a wider scale, and lay students could acquire considerable religious competence without relinquishing household life. A student who learned from a monk might later teach other laypeople. Saya Thetgyi, who became an important lay meditation teacher, is associated with Ledi's network. His career illustrates the possibility of religious authority derived through recognized competence rather than exclusively through monastic office, without abolishing the authority of the sangha.

The intended readership of a religious manual must also be considered. The translated Manual of Insight circulated in English through Buddhist publication organizations. Its translator's preface addresses the availability of religious instruction to Western Buddhists as well as the intellectual demands of the teaching. English editions published after Ledi's death are evidence of the subsequent international transmission of his work, not proof that an English-reading nineteenth-century audience was already receiving the same text. The surviving edition is itself a layered historical artifact, containing the author's argument, the translator's choices and the publication history of the later institution.

The most significant consequence was not an abrupt substitution of individual meditation for communal religious life. Lay instruction could support the preservation of Buddhism, which was understood as a collective responsibility. The dissemination of doctrinal knowledge was therefore connected to the broader changes in funding, association and public education described in Section 125.5. Different Buddhist teachers and organizations developed distinct meditation methods, and later international movements should not all be treated as direct replicas of Ledi's program. Nevertheless, his career illustrates how a reform grounded in the preservation of an authoritative tradition could make possible new kinds of participation. The case also cautions against a narrow interpretation of modernization in which religious study becomes progressively less important. Here, intensified study was itself a means by which believers responded to modern circumstances.

Buddhism, Monarchy, and Religious Reform in Siam

Siamese Buddhism developed under conditions different from those in neighboring colonial territories. The monarchy retained sovereignty and continued to support Buddhist institutions while implementing large-scale administrative reforms. The relationship between ruler and religious order was therefore not severed, as in Upper Burma. Instead, it was reorganized as the Chakri kings sought to integrate regional political authorities into a centralized state. Religious and territorial administration became connected, but the nature of their relationship was negotiated by monks, royal patrons and local communities over a long period.

The reforming prince who later became King Mongkut spent many years in the monkhood before his accession in 1851. His concern for stricter discipline and the study of authoritative texts contributed to the development of the Dhammayut order. The order emphasized carefully regulated ordination and observance of the monastic rules. It remained smaller than the Mahanikai, the larger community of monks representing diverse established traditions. The distinction did not mean that the latter was inherently hostile to scholarship or reform. Both operated within the broader Buddhist world, and membership in an order was not a direct indication of an individual's political beliefs. Royal support gave Dhammayut institutions an influence beyond their numerical strength.

Under King Chulalongkorn, administrative reforms brought provincial jurisdictions into a more direct relationship with Bangkok. The Sangha Act of 1902 organized monastic administration through a hierarchy that corresponded in important respects to the emerging territorial bureaucracy. Senior religious authorities gained recognized responsibilities, and provincial ecclesiastical administration was brought into closer relation with the center. Educational reform reinforced this project. Prince Wachirayan Warorot, a leading monk and later supreme patriarch, was particularly important in the development of monastic studies and the dissemination of instruction. New textbooks and examinations provided common expectations of religious learning, alongside the continued study of Pali.

Centralization had advantages for monks seeking recognition of their training and for administrators seeking consistent lines of authority. A common curriculum could facilitate communication between distant institutions and make qualifications comprehensible beyond a particular monastery. Yet the reform also privileged particular languages, texts and institutional styles. Northern Thai and northeastern communities had their own forms of manuscript education, ordination and local connections. A student might learn religious material in a local script and language that was not the preferred medium of the central system. The extension of Bangkok's authority could accordingly represent a loss of autonomy for regional religious leaders.

The career of the northern monk Khruba Siwichai illustrates the tensions involved. His prestige among local supporters and his role in religious construction projects gave him authority that was not wholly dependent on the administrative hierarchy. Disputes concerning ordination and recognition brought him into conflict with officials of the centralized monastic order. The fact that local donors supported him does not make him a simple opponent of every aspect of the Siamese state. Rather, his case demonstrates the unevenness of a process in which religious charisma, inherited local relationships and statutory authority could coexist uneasily. The state's ability to formulate rules did not automatically ensure universal acceptance.

Siam's constitutional revolution in 1932 altered the political environment but is not the central subject of this section. The significance of Buddhist reform before that watershed is that the monarchy used religious education and administration to help construct a more centralized territorial order. The process simultaneously gave monks and lay donors new opportunities to pursue religious goals and generated disputes over the scope of official authority. Some aspects of this reorganization resembled changes in colonial societies, including standardization, written regulations and the creation of formal hierarchies. Their political foundations were different. Comparative study should therefore avoid explaining every modern religious bureaucracy as a product of European colonial domination. The Siamese state exercised its own initiative while responding to imperial pressures, regional political conditions and arguments concerning religious discipline.

Buddhist Revival in Cambodia, Laos, and Vietnam

Buddhist reform in French Indochina took place across societies with distinct languages, literary traditions and institutional arrangements. Cambodia and Laos shared important aspects of Theravāda Buddhism with Siam, but their political relationships to neighboring powers and to France differed. Vietnam's Buddhist revival was rooted largely in Mahāyāna traditions and in religious communities shaped by a different literary and ritual inheritance. The Indochinese federation created an administrative framework within which officials sought to manage religious life. It did not transform the different Buddhist communities into a single order or give them identical opportunities for renewal.

In Cambodia, monasteries remained significant sites of literacy, moral instruction, ceremonial life and the preservation of manuscripts. Anne Ruth Hansen has examined how modern Cambodian Buddhist ethics developed through debates over correct conduct and religious understanding. Monks and other reformers employed older literary forms while also producing texts for the changing circumstances of colonial rule. The growth of urban society and access to printing created opportunities to communicate ethical instruction beyond an individual monastery. The reform of teaching was not simply a French initiative: Cambodian religious scholars pursued their own objectives and were concerned about the quality and accessibility of Buddhist learning.

French administrators nevertheless had a political interest in supporting local scholarship. Cambodian monks often traveled to Siam for training. A policy intended to strengthen separate Cambodian institutions could reduce dependence on Bangkok, while enhancing the image of France as a protector of local culture. Søren Ivarsson traces the development of this project through a Pali school established in Phnom Penh in 1914 and the Royal Library established in 1925. The Buddhist Institute was organized in 1930. Suzanne Karpelès was important to the institutional work of collecting manuscripts and supporting publication, while Cambodian scholars including Chuon Nath and Huot Tat contributed their religious expertise. The institution's activities should not be identified exclusively with the political objectives of the colonial administration.

In Laos, the monasteries and networks of teachers along the Mekong and across the border with Siam continued to matter. French officials sought to encourage a distinctive Lao cultural identity within Indochina. Reorganization of the clergy in 1928 preceded the opening of a Buddhist Institute section in Vientiane in 1931 and another in Luang Prabang in 1933. Prince Phetsarath and scholar Maha Sila Viravong were associated with the Vientiane institution. The collecting of Lao manuscripts, preparation of texts and promotion of local Pali studies could meet the religious and literary needs of Lao scholars while serving French geopolitical interests. This overlap does not demonstrate that Lao participation was simply an endorsement of colonial authority. Lao intellectuals could appropriate resources made available by the administration for projects whose meanings were their own.

In Vietnam, revivalists confronted problems of monastic education, lay instruction and competition for authority in a religiously plural society. Associations founded in Cochinchina, Annam and Tonkin during the 1930s supported publications, classes and the renewal of temple life. The Association for the Study of Buddhism in Cochinchina, established in 1931, published Từ Bi Âm; an Annam association founded in 1932 supported the journal Viên Âm. In the north, a Buddhist association formed in 1934 and developed the periodical Đuốc Tuệ. These associations reflected local constituencies rather than the uniform application of a single regional policy. The use of quốc ngữ publications enabled the dissemination of Buddhist arguments to new groups of educated readers. Lay participation and the cooperation of monks with professionals helped support the organizations.

The comparison reveals a range of relationships between reform and political authority. French-sponsored institutions could support goals that were genuinely important to Cambodian and Lao scholars while attempting to direct their external connections. Vietnamese associations drew on the growing vernacular public sphere to make claims about Buddhist learning and social renewal. In none of these cases should official patronage be equated with complete official control, nor should Buddhist revitalization be presumed to have had national independence as its immediate objective. The reformers' writings, institutional regulations and actual publications provide more reliable evidence of their priorities than a retrospective label of nationalism.

Division III — Islamic Reform, Education, and Muslim Organization · Section 125.9

Southeast Asian Islam and the Global Reformist Movement

The Islamic reform movements of Southeast Asia belonged to a wider history of Muslim intellectual exchange, but they cannot be explained adequately as a diffusion of ideas from a single Middle Eastern center. Muslim communities in the archipelago, peninsula and mainland had long supported scholars, religious schools, mosques, pilgrimage and the transmission of legal learning. The expansion of steam navigation and print made some older networks denser, while colonial conquest and economic transformation created problems that demanded new responses. Reformers in different places shared certain questions about education, authority and the proper practice of Islam, but they did not necessarily agree on the answers. Their capacity to act depended on their relationship to local rulers, merchants, students and established scholarly institutions.

Mecca was both a pilgrimage destination and a center of advanced religious learning. Southeast Asian scholars who resided there could study jurisprudence, theology, Arabic and other subjects in circles attended by students from many regions. The term Jawi was used in this environment for a broad Southeast Asian constituency and did not denote a single modern nation or ethnic group. Connections formed there continued through letters, books, teaching and the circulation of students. Important teachers transmitted the established scholarly traditions of Islam as well as debates over renewal. A traveler who later supported a reformist school and another who defended a traditional pesantren might have drawn on overlapping networks of religious learning.

Egypt became an important source of intellectual materials, particularly for Muslims interested in the relationship between religious knowledge and modern education. Muhammad Abduh, who died in 1905, and Rashid Rida, editor of the Cairo journal Al-Manār from 1898, were among the most influential figures in these discussions. Their work cannot be compressed into a single political doctrine: their writings encompassed interpretation, education, religious authority and the conditions of Muslim societies. Reformist readers in Southeast Asia selected and translated particular texts. An argument about the education of children, for example, could be adapted to the needs of a Malay readership without importing every controversy in which the original writer was involved. The journal discussed in the next section provides direct evidence of such a translation relationship.

The debate over ijtihād and taqlīd concerned who could interpret authoritative texts and by what methods. Advocates of renewed reasoning criticized what they saw as the uncritical repetition of inherited opinions. Defenders of established schools of jurisprudence regarded transmitted learning as a disciplined method of interpretation, rather than an abandonment of thought. Most Southeast Asian Muslim scholarly communities were historically associated with the Shafi‘i school of law, though practice and intellectual commitments varied. Differences over the interpretation of a legal question could become disputes about the authority of a teacher, a religious office or a local institution. The labels modernist and traditionalist are useful shorthand only when historians specify the issue at stake.

Pan-Islamic solidarity was another dimension of the international religious world. The idea that Muslims formed a community extending across political frontiers could sustain appeals to fellow believers and concern about events in distant lands. This was not equivalent to membership in a centrally directed political organization. Ottoman political authority, the First World War, the abolition of the caliphate in 1924 and changes of power in Arabia altered the circumstances in which such appeals were made. Different Muslims interpreted these developments differently, and governments often exaggerated the coherence of international solidarity when assessing security risks. A colonial intelligence report must therefore be read as evidence of official perceptions as well as of the activities it describes.

Commercial and social networks made intellectual exchange practicable. Muslim merchants could finance travel and publishing, while Jawi and Roman-script Malay periodicals reached readers beyond a single locality. Hadhrami families connected parts of the archipelago to southern Arabia through migration, marriage, commerce and scholarship. These communities were themselves internally differentiated; disputes over descent, education and authority could generate competing associations. Al-Irshad, established in the Indies in 1914, is an example of reform and institution-building within this environment. Commercial wealth did not dictate a particular religious program, but it furnished resources with which organizers could experiment. The schools and associations that developed in the Indies and Malaya were thus products of local initiatives operating within global religious and commercial networks. Their history demonstrates that the circulation of ideas becomes socially consequential when recipients possess the resources, authority and incentives necessary to turn those ideas into durable practices.

Historical Vocabulary II

Jurisprudence and transnational solidarity

Ijtihād refers to disciplined independent juristic reasoning; taqlīd to following established juristic authority. The terms describe complex methods of interpretation, not simply freedom of thought and blind imitation. A madhhab is an established school of Islamic jurisprudence. The Shafi‘i school had substantial influence in Southeast Asia. The ummah is the community of Muslims, understood as transcending political borders. Pan-Islamism describes diverse ideas and projects of Muslim solidarity across political boundaries.

These concepts belong to different analytical levels. Ijtihād and taqlīd concern the authority of interpretation. The ummah expresses a religious community. Pan-Islamism concerns particular projects of solidarity and can acquire political significance. A Muslim who acknowledged the ummah did not necessarily support a political campaign associated with a particular ruler or organization. Distinguishing the terms prevents colonial suspicions about transnational Islam from being mistaken for evidence of an actual coordinated movement.

Kaum Muda, Kaum Tua, and Reform in the Malay World

The terms Kaum Muda and Kaum Tua became prominent in debates about Islamic reform in the Malay-speaking world. The former, meaning the younger or new group, was associated with educational and religious renewal, while the latter referred broadly to defenders of established religious authorities and practices. These labels did not literally divide populations by age. Nor did they define permanent political parties. They emerged through controversy in which particular teachers, journalists and religious officials sought to establish the legitimacy of their own interpretations. The debate developed differently in Singapore, the Malay states and the Minangkabau regions of Sumatra because these societies were governed differently and possessed distinct histories of scholarship.

Singapore was a particularly important location for reformist publishing. As a port outside the jurisdiction of a Malay sultan, it provided a commercial and intellectual environment in which publishers could serve readers in multiple territories. The journal Al-Imam, issued between 1906 and 1908, was associated with figures including Tahir Jalaluddin and Syed Sheikh Al-Hadi. Tahir had developed scholarly connections beyond the region and brought expertise in religious subjects to the reformist enterprise. Al-Hadi subsequently became notable for educational and literary initiatives in the Malay world. Their activities demonstrate the overlap between the intellectual networks examined in Chapter 124 and the religious networks of the present chapter.

The first issue of Al-Imam, dated 23 July 1906, contained an article titled Pelajaran dealing with the methods of educating children. Scholarship identifies the article as a translation of two pieces published in Cairo's Al-Manār during April 1899. This documentary relationship is particularly instructive because it permits historians to identify an actual exchange rather than assume that any resemblance between Egyptian and Malay reform proves direct borrowing. The choice to publish an educational text in the inaugural issue also demonstrates the importance of schooling to the journal's program. Translation and distribution were activities through which editors transformed a foreign-language discussion into a locally accessible intervention. The nature of the surviving evidence, including secondary identification of the original articles, is explained in the primary-source window below.

The Malay states presented a different institutional environment. Sultans and appointed religious officials retained recognized jurisdiction over Islam and Malay custom. Reformers who questioned established teaching could therefore challenge the authority of offices integrated into state administration. Disputes concerned such matters as the justification of customary practices, the authority of inherited opinions, and the appropriate content of religious education. Established scholars were not necessarily opposed to new schools or publications; a person could adopt graded instruction while rejecting particular reformist theological conclusions. The conflict is best understood through particular controversies and locations instead of treating all official religious authorities as a single conservative bloc.

In West Sumatra, a tradition of religious education in the surau formed an important basis for renewal. Scholars including Abdullah Ahmad and Abdul Karim Amrullah, often known as Haji Rasul, participated in debates about education and authoritative practice. The journal Al-Munir, published in Padang from 1911 to 1915, extended reformist discussion in the region. Initiatives associated with the Adabiah school and the development of Sumatra Thawalib illustrate the gradual reorganization of teaching through classrooms, curricula and associations. Minangkabau social arrangements, including matrilineal inheritance and the role of customary authorities, made disputes over the relationship between Islam and adat particularly significant. Reformers confronted historical problems rooted in local society, not merely the intellectual currents of Egypt or Mecca.

Educational reform also affected women's opportunities. In Padang Panjang, Rahmah el Yunusiyah founded Diniyah Puteri in 1923, a school specifically for girls and young women. The institution demonstrated that women could be educated in religious subjects and trained for roles beyond those conventionally recognized by male educational authorities. Its existence complicates the tendency to treat Islamic reform history solely as disputes among male scholars. Schools required administrators, teachers, premises, financial support and the trust of families. In this respect they were practical responses to questions raised in journals, but their organizational conditions were not identical to those of publishing.

Reformist journalism did not speak for every Muslim, and access to periodicals depended on literacy, price, language and the reach of distributors. The short publication runs of Al-Imam and Al-Munir nevertheless should not be taken as evidence that their influence ended when the titles ceased appearing. Ideas and trained personnel could move into other publications and educational institutions. The comparative significance of the two journals is not that they established an uncontested doctrine, but that they helped create a public in which the legitimacy of religious teaching itself could be debated across colonial jurisdictions.

Muhammadiyah and the Institutionalization of Islamic Modernism

Muhammadiyah was founded in Yogyakarta in November 1912 under the leadership of Ahmad Dahlan, a Javanese Muslim scholar associated with the Kauman mosque community. Its development illustrates a form of religious reform in which the establishment and administration of durable institutions was central. Dahlan's concern for religious knowledge and practice was inseparable from questions about education, social welfare and the responsibilities of believers. The organization developed in a city where the court, mosque officials, traders, government schooling and other modern associations existed in close proximity. Its founders were responding to the social conditions of Java as well as to international Islamic scholarship.

The educational program addressed an institutional dilemma faced by Muslim families. Colonial schools could provide general subjects and occupational credentials, but they did not necessarily provide the religious instruction that parents desired. Older religious schools offered valued forms of learning but might not prepare students in the same ways for new occupations and institutions. Muhammadiyah schools sought to bring religious and general subjects into a single educational structure. Classroom teaching, timetables, examinations and specialized syllabuses could be adopted without surrendering the central place of Islam. This synthesis did not arise automatically from a theological position. It required an organization capable of recruiting teachers, maintaining facilities, raising money and supervising its institutions.

Institutional growth depended on supporters whose activities lay beyond the formal leadership. Merchants and families provided donations; teachers supplied professional expertise; local committees identified educational needs; parents entrusted children to new schools. Recognition by the Dutch administration imposed certain legal conditions and did not make the institution an extension of the colonial school system. Expansion beyond its original locality proceeded through the formation of branches and the recruitment of members in other regions. A branch could adapt a general program to local circumstances, although the central organization and its meetings helped maintain a common identity. Muhammadiyah's administrative structure was thus a historical achievement requiring repeated investment and negotiation.

The organization's approach to welfare was equally important. The provision of care for the poor, medical services and support for orphans translated religious obligations into sustained social services. Early Muhammadiyah welfare programs developed through the body known as Penolong Kesengsaraan Oemoem, or PKO, which became associated with institutional care. The significance of this initiative is not that charity was new to the Muslim world. Religious giving and care had much older histories. Rather, the organization sought to coordinate resources through permanent facilities, regular administration and personnel with recognized responsibilities. A clinic or orphanage created a relationship with beneficiaries that extended beyond the individual occasion of a gift. Its continued operation required accounting, supervision and a reliable supply of funds.

Women played a central role in the development of these institutions. Aisyiyah emerged in 1917 from earlier women's religious study and organization. Siti Walidah, also called Nyai Ahmad Dahlan, was a leading mentor and organizer; the first formally appointed chair was Siti Bariyah, who served from 1917 to 1920. The distinction is important because institutional memory can simplify collective leadership by focusing on the spouse of a prominent founder. Aisyiyah's own historical account describes Bariyah's education, command of Malay and Dutch, and experience explaining religious material. Her work also included an interpretation of Muhammadiyah's organizational aims published in September 1923. Women were therefore participants in the intellectual definition of the movement as well as in the provision of services.

The establishment of the periodical Suara Aisyiyah in 1926 created another channel of communication among branches and readers. The organization also supported education, religious instruction and welfare, giving women an institutional setting in which to develop leadership. Participation in wider women's associational networks by the late 1920s illustrates how religiously grounded social reform could connect with other agendas without abandoning its own identity. It would be misleading to infer from these developments that all women acquired equal opportunities or that all constraints within households and institutions disappeared. The achievement lay in the construction of new, sustained positions of responsibility, often grounded in religious obligations and family support.

Muhammadiyah should not be treated as a political party in disguise. Individual members participated in wider social and political movements, and the organization's reputation could become a significant resource for collective action. Its characteristic institutional program, however, involved education, religious renewal and welfare. By the interwar period, repeated experience in administering such services had produced forms of expertise and trust that could survive changes in leadership. This explains why its importance cannot be measured solely by the political statements of its founder or by membership figures. The organization helped create a durable Muslim public whose activities affected daily life regardless of whether national independence was an immediate aim.

Nahdlatul Ulama, Traditionalist Scholarship, and Religious Pluralism within Islam

Nahdlatul Ulama, established in Surabaya in January 1926, emerged from networks of Islamic scholars with strong connections to the pesantren of Java and other parts of the Netherlands Indies. The prominent scholar Hasyim Asy'ari, associated with the Tebuireng pesantren near Jombang, became a central religious authority within the new organization. Wahab Hasbullah and other scholars were important organizers whose activities preceded its formal establishment. Its founders were concerned about the legitimacy of established scholarly practices, the future of traditional religious institutions and the representation of their interests in a changing international Islamic environment. NU's emergence was therefore a collective institutional response to several pressures rather than the initiative of one scholar alone.

The pesantren constituted a long-established form of Islamic education. A recognized teacher, commonly called a kiai in Java, guided pupils in the study of texts, practice and discipline, while boarding students or santri could acquire religious knowledge over an extended period. The authority of a kiai depended on scholarship, teaching relationships and reputation, and in many places also on the support of a wider community. Different pesantren varied in scale, curriculum and approach. A graduate might establish a new school or return to a village to teach, extending the influence of his scholarly lineage. Such networks provided the social foundation from which a formal organization could draw leadership and constituencies. It is misleading to regard the absence of a centralized modern association before 1926 as evidence that these scholars had previously been unorganized.

The doctrinal arguments concerned the use of established schools of jurisprudence and the religious validity of practices criticized by some modernists. NU's founders defended the importance of following recognized scholarly traditions, including the four Sunni schools of law, with the Shafi‘i school especially influential in the region. They also defended many practices linked with the veneration of religious teachers, visits to graves and forms of communal religious observance. Such matters involved complex arguments about authority, precedent and the appropriate interpretation of texts. They did not divide Muslims into people who used reason and people who refused to think. A jurist trained in a school of law could reason within that tradition, while reformists also depended on learned interpreters and institutions.

Developments in Arabia gave the debates a new urgency. The conquest of the Hijaz by forces of Ibn Saud in the mid-1920s followed the dissolution of the Ottoman caliphate and raised questions about access to pilgrimage sites, religious practices and the representation of Muslims from outside Arabia. Traditionalist scholars were concerned that the new authorities might restrict practices and institutions they considered legitimate. The Hijaz Committee associated with Wahab Hasbullah sought representation of their concerns. The establishment of NU in 1926 enabled scholars to articulate a collective position and maintain communication beyond individual pesantren. The Arabian context does not mean that disputes in Java were simply copies of conflicts elsewhere; it provided an international issue that sharpened the need for organization at home.

NU adopted modern organizational arrangements, including congresses, written resolutions and a formal leadership structure. Its scholarly bodies provided mechanisms for the examination of religious questions, and teachers could cooperate across local institutions. These arrangements allowed a network of established authorities to act collectively without erasing the autonomy or character of its constituent schools. Its educational work did not require it to adopt the identical curriculum or methods of Muhammadiyah. Over time, both groups adapted to changing demand for general schooling as well as religious learning. Their institutional competition could stimulate expansion while also dividing constituencies and making local controversies difficult to resolve.

It is useful to compare their different relationships to religious leadership. Muhammadiyah placed considerable emphasis on an association organized around a program of reform, educational provision and welfare. NU drew especially on the authority of scholarly networks and used the association to coordinate their work. Both forms depended on donations, local participation, teaching and formal arrangements for making decisions. Neither was wholly urban or wholly rural, and the boundaries between their supporters and other Muslim organizations were not immutable. The terms modernist and traditionalist help identify intellectual and organizational tendencies but do not describe every individual, school or regional branch.

The political importance of NU likewise must be located in the period rather than read backward from its later roles in independent Indonesia. Scholars could defend religious interests, cooperate with other Muslim organizations, or resist specific colonial regulations without operating as a unified nationalist party. By the late 1930s, efforts at cooperation among Islamic organizations demonstrated that doctrinal disagreement did not prevent agreement on selected collective objectives. The formation of the federation MIAI in 1937 illustrates this possibility. The detailed electoral, wartime and postindependence histories of Muslim organizations lie beyond the chapter's assigned period. Here their significance is that an authoritative tradition could be defended through new administrative forms and that religious pluralism within Islam helped shape the institutional landscape of the modern Indies.

Christianity, Missions, Education, and Colonial Society

Christianity in colonial Southeast Asia was neither a single institutional system nor a religion exclusively introduced during the period of European high imperialism. Catholic communities had existed for centuries in the Philippines, parts of Vietnam and Portuguese Timor; Protestant communities in parts of eastern Indonesia likewise had older historical roots. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, missions expanded or reorganized their educational, medical and evangelical work. The relationship between missionary organizations and state authority depended on the colony, Christian denomination and particular circumstances of expansion. An adequate account must place the initiatives of local Christians at the center of this history rather than treating them as passive recipients of foreign religious teaching.

The mission school was an institution with several purposes. Its founders might hope to attract converts, train clergy, provide literacy or educate teachers and local leaders. Families could seek schooling for reasons of employment, social advancement or interest in Christian teaching. Attendance at a mission school was not in itself proof of conversion. Different institutions required different forms of religious participation and offered different kinds of qualifications. Girls' schools created opportunities for education where other facilities were limited, although some curricula emphasized domestic roles and restricted access to advanced professional training. The graduates of these schools could use the knowledge acquired there for purposes not envisaged by the founders, including criticism of racial inequality and the development of indigenous professional associations.

In Burma, the American Baptist mission, whose activities dated to the early nineteenth century, developed substantial work among Karen communities and other populations. The translation of scriptures and the development of written materials in local languages helped create institutions of literacy and teaching. Karen evangelists and teachers were indispensable to the extension of these networks; they understood local languages, communities and circumstances in ways foreign missionaries could not. The resulting religious affiliations were uneven within and among communities. Some saw the mission school as a pathway to education and a source of social resources, others maintained established religious practices, and still others combined elements of earlier beliefs with Christian commitments. Christian affiliation should not be treated as synonymous with a particular ethnicity.

In the Netherlands Indies, the growth of Christian institutions in the Batak highlands of Sumatra illustrates another pattern. The Rhenish Mission, associated with Ludwig Ingwer Nommensen and local evangelists, established centers of teaching and congregational life from the nineteenth century. The emergence of a Batak Protestant church with its own organization in the early twentieth century provided structures for the development of indigenous leadership. In Minahasa and parts of Maluku, Christian schools and churches operated in societies with longer histories of engagement with European power. Educational attainment and employment in colonial institutions sometimes became connected to mission schooling, but these relationships varied widely by locality and cannot be generalized to all converts.

Vietnamese Catholic communities had developed long before France's military conquest, and their experience included periods of persecution as well as negotiation with indigenous rulers. French colonial rule altered their relations with state authorities, but it did not make Vietnamese Catholic interests identical to those of French officials. Local clergy, parishioners and religious associations possessed their own commitments. In Portuguese Timor, Catholic missions operated in a territory where colonial administration had limited resources and authority beyond selected centers. Mission institutions could offer schooling and create links with administrative centers without transforming the religious lives of all Timorese communities. Indigenous authorities and practices remained significant in many areas. These cases reveal the danger of interpreting the geographical extent of colonial sovereignty as a measure of religious conversion.

Mission institutions could benefit from access to land, official recognition or the movement of colonial transport and personnel; governments might value their schools and medical services as substitutes for costly public provision. Yet missionaries could criticize official conduct, and colonial authorities sometimes regarded denominational competition or religious activism as sources of disorder. Local Christians might contest the foreign leadership of their own churches as well as criticize the state. Thus Christianity's relationship to empire was contingent, not intrinsic. The most useful evidence includes school regulations, church correspondence, the work of locally trained clergy and records of congregational life. Mission reports, often designed to raise money from overseas supporters, need to be read critically: their categories of progress and conversion may obscure the degree to which local communities redirected institutional resources toward their own goals.

The Philippine Independent Church and Indigenous Christian Movements

The Philippine Independent Church, or Iglesia Filipina Independiente, emerged from a prolonged struggle over ecclesiastical authority in the Spanish Philippines and the political upheavals surrounding the Revolution and the subsequent establishment of American rule. The issue of Filipino clergy's access to parish appointments had been particularly important under Spanish rule. Religious orders enjoyed extensive control over parishes, and Filipino secular priests repeatedly challenged the restrictions placed upon them. In this context secular clergy means priests who are not members of a religious order, not people who reject religion. The dispute concerned the distribution of authority within the Catholic Church as well as the broader racial and political inequalities of the colonial regime.

The execution of the priests Mariano Gómez, José Burgos and Jacinto Zamora in 1872 gave the issue lasting symbolic importance. Their deaths were associated in subsequent political memory with colonial injustice and the exclusion of Filipinos from positions of authority. The detailed history of the Propaganda Movement and Revolution belongs to Chapters 116 and 117; here the essential point is that the transition from arguments for greater equality within an existing church to the establishment of a separate church was neither automatic nor universally accepted. Filipino Catholics could desire changes in clerical leadership while continuing to recognize the authority of Rome.

Gregorio Aglipay was a Filipino priest who became involved in the revolutionary struggle and later emerged as the first supreme bishop of the independent church. Isabelo de los Reyes, a writer and labor organizer, played an important role in proclaiming the new church in 1902. The proclamation on August 3 occurred in the context of meetings associated with the Unión Obrera Democrática. Aglipay's subsequent acceptance of leadership helped connect the new institution to the clergy and to networks established during the Revolution. The movement brought together people with different intellectual and theological commitments. The development of its doctrines and ecclesiastical relationships continued after the founding; the beliefs of its members in 1902 should not be inferred from official positions adopted many decades later.

The establishment of a separate church raised a practical question: who controlled the buildings, furnishings and other resources that had sustained parish life? A local congregation or priest who transferred allegiance to the new institution might regard the parish church as the property of the local community. Roman Catholic authorities could assert that the same property belonged to the established ecclesiastical institution. These claims involved different conceptions of ownership and religious authority. The new American legal order provided a framework for resolving the dispute, but its recognition of religious freedom did not necessarily support the transfer of existing ecclesiastical property to a newly founded institution.

In the case Barlin v. Ramirez, decided by the Philippine Supreme Court in 1906, the surviving record includes a November 1902 letter by the parish priest of Lagonoy, Vicente Ramirez, along with a local resolution. The resolution gave voice to grievances about the recognition of Filipino clergy and presented separation from Roman authority as conditional upon those grievances. It also expressly maintained continuity in religious belief and sacramental practice. This is strong evidence that at least some local supporters initially understood ecclesiastical independence as a dispute over leadership rather than a complete repudiation of Catholic religious teaching. The court ruled in favor of the Roman Catholic ecclesiastical claimant's right to possession of the property in dispute. The judgment demonstrates the distinction between freedom to join a religious organization and the legal ownership of the building in which its members had worshipped.

The independent church nonetheless developed its own congregations, leadership structures and religious identity. The development of other Filipino Christian institutions, including Protestant congregations established during the American period and the Iglesia ni Cristo founded in 1914 under Felix Manalo, reflected a changing religious environment in which multiple organizations could seek members. Their theological foundations and political histories were different, and they should not be treated as equivalent forms of ecclesiastical nationalism. The significance of the independent church for the chapter's central argument is the way a religious community's demands for control of its own institutions could become linked to national dignity and political transformation. It also illustrates a limit to that process: changes in sovereignty did not automatically redistribute the material resources required to sustain religious autonomy.

Cao Đài, Hòa Hảo, and Religious Innovation in Southern Vietnam

The religious movements Cao Đài and Hòa Hảo arose in southern Vietnam under conditions shaped by colonial rule, migration, commercial agriculture and a varied religious landscape. Both developed extensive constituencies, but the circumstances of their origins, their doctrines and their forms of organization were different. It is important to distinguish the formative religious history of each movement from the military and political importance it acquired during the Second World War and after 1945. Colonial officials' later perceptions of these organizations as political forces are not sufficient evidence that such roles were their original or exclusive purpose.

Cao Đài emerged from networks of spirit-mediumship in Cochinchina during the 1920s. The practice of communicating with spiritual beings through mediums was familiar in several religious traditions operating in the region, and it also attracted people acquainted with forms of European spiritualism. Ngô Văn Chiêu, an official in the colonial administration, is conventionally associated with early revelations and the symbolism of the Divine Eye. Other organizers, including Lê Văn Trung and Phạm Công Tắc, were important in the development of the public religious institution and its leadership. They did not all follow an identical personal religious trajectory; the development of a formally organized church involved negotiations among people who brought different experiences and understandings to the project.

The formal steps taken in October and November 1926 must be distinguished. On October 7 the founders presented a declaration to the colonial authorities concerning the establishment of the religion. The Universal Manifesto, published October 15, articulated the religious message for a wider audience. In November, an inauguration at Gò Kén near Tây Ninh marked the public institutional emergence of the movement. These events are related but are not alternative dates for the same document. The movement developed a hierarchy, rules of religious conduct, sites of worship and ceremonies. Its subsequent center at Tây Ninh became an important focus of institutional development, although different Cao Đài communities were not always united under the same leadership.

The religion's theology understood several established religious traditions as related within a larger scheme of divine revelation. Its name and terminology expressed the founders' understanding of a new era of salvation and religious teaching. Elements of Buddhist, Confucian, Daoist and Christian traditions were incorporated into an organized religious system rather than merely assembled as unrelated symbols. The founders also employed forms of administrative organization familiar to literate people in colonial Cochinchina. The relationship between the spiritual authority conveyed through revelation and the officials responsible for the ordinary administration of a church was a central institutional problem. Janet Hoskins and other scholars have emphasized the value of examining these religious claims in their own terms, rather than reducing the movement to political nationalism or labeling it an arbitrary mixture of beliefs.

Hòa Hảo emerged in 1939 through the teachings of Huỳnh Phú Sổ in the western Mekong delta. Its history drew on local Buddhist-inspired traditions of religious renewal, including the legacy of the nineteenth-century Bửu Sơn Kỳ Hương movement. The teachings emphasized moral conduct, devotional practice and the possibility of religious life within households rather than dependence on elaborate temples and ritual institutions. The new teacher's reputation, including accounts of healing and the circulation of his religious verses, contributed to the movement's expansion. Its organization and devotional style differed from the formal hierarchy and extensive ritual structure developed by Cao Đài. The different kinds of authority exercised by a charismatic teacher and a formally organized church are important subjects of historical comparison.

The delta's social conditions helped shape opportunities for both movements. Settlement, commercialization, inequalities in landholding and changing relationships between rural communities and towns affected the resources and grievances of local populations. Religious teachers could offer moral explanations of suffering and forms of belonging to people experiencing insecurity. Huệ-Tâm Hồ Tài's research on millenarian traditions and peasant politics illustrates the importance of examining religious expectations in relation to specific historical circumstances. Poverty alone, however, cannot explain why adherents embraced one particular teaching rather than another. The meanings assigned to suffering, salvation, community and moral obligation were part of the historical process. By 1940 the emergence of these movements had already demonstrated the capacity of southern Vietnamese communities to establish new religious institutions. Their subsequent political alliances, armed organizations and conflicts belong to later chapters.

Chinese Religious Associations, Indigenous Traditions, and Community Life

Religious associations in Southeast Asia also developed among communities whose beliefs and institutions are poorly represented by a simple comparison of the region's largest religious traditions. Chinese migrants and their descendants maintained temples, burial associations, clan organizations and forms of charitable activity that frequently involved overlapping religious, social and occupational relationships. A temple could provide a place of worship while also serving as a focal point for the management of property, the organization of festivals and the recognition of community leaders. Its ritual activities cannot necessarily be assigned exclusively to Buddhism, Daoism or Confucianism. The practical religious life of an individual might encompass ancestor veneration and devotion to particular deities alongside participation in associations devoted to education or welfare.

The commercial and demographic expansion described in Chapters 121 and 122 created new conditions for such institutions. Chinese communities in Singapore, Penang, Bangkok, Saigon–Cholon and other cities included people linked to different places of origin and language groups. Associations raised funds and provided assistance with funerals, burial grounds and other matters that could be especially important for migrants living far from their relatives. Wealthy patrons might acquire prestige through donations, while poorer members benefited from collective provision. The balance between philanthropy and the authority of association leaders varied. A community institution could sustain solidarity while also reinforcing distinctions based on wealth, gender, language or descent. Such organizations were not necessarily representative of all people whom colonial officials classified as Chinese.

The spread of educational reform and new political ideas from China also affected religious and associational life. The Tiong Hoa Hwee Koan, founded in Batavia in 1900, promoted Chinese education and reform associated with Confucian moral teaching. Its schools and publications participated in a wider debate about the cultural future of Chinese communities under colonial rule. The term Confucian revival can refer to different kinds of projects, including ethical instruction, educational organization and claims concerning the recognition of a religious identity. It should not imply that all Chinese community schools were religious institutions. The development of such associations demonstrates that educational, moral and ethnic projects could overlap without being identical.

Hindu and Sikh communities in colonial Burma and Malaya developed institutions linked in part to migration from South Asia. Temples and gurdwaras were sites for worship, instruction and the maintenance of community ties under conditions of plantation work, urban employment and geographical separation. A temple supported by laborers could have financial and administrative arrangements very different from those of an institution endowed by wealthy merchants. Religious affiliation was only one aspect of social identity: caste, language, region of origin and occupational circumstances could also shape who participated in particular institutions. Sikh gurdwaras could offer congregational worship and communal meals, providing a social environment for people outside the villages from which they had migrated.

Bali presents a different Hindu history, rooted in local religious and political institutions rather than primarily in recent labor migration. Dutch conquest and consolidation altered the relationships among courts, priests and village communities. Balinese religious life remained organized through temple obligations, festivals and the transmission of ritual knowledge. New debates concerning education, religious identity and social hierarchy occurred under colonial conditions, but twentieth-century processes of religious standardization should not be projected backward as if completed by the 1930s. The locally maintained institution and the later nationally recognized religious category are different objects of historical inquiry.

The indigenous spiritual traditions of upland mainland communities and island societies similarly demand historically specific treatment. Sacred land, relations with ancestors and ritual obligations could be fundamental to community authority. Missionary contact, schooling and conversion sometimes altered these relationships; in other cases communities continued ritual practices even as officials categorized them under a new religious label. Some people adopted Christian or Muslim identities while retaining aspects of local social practice, but the patterns differed widely. Historians should neither describe such processes as the inevitable disappearance of supposedly primitive beliefs nor romanticize every prior practice as unchanged. The categories found in colonial censuses and mission reports may reveal the aims of the people collecting information more directly than the meanings attributed to rituals by those who performed them. Festivals, burial customs, donation records and local histories help recover a wider range of agency than elite religious reform texts alone.

Everyday Life and Social History

Religious life beyond formal leadership

Consider a temple festival or funeral as a social institution. Preparing an event requires labor, supplies, a place, religious knowledge and some allocation of costs. A donor's contribution may express devotion, honor obligations to relatives or enhance standing in a community. A burial association can preserve records such as burial registers and accounts that document collective provision more directly than public proclamations.

A different example is the work involved in maintaining a village monastery or school. Preparing offerings, teaching children, maintaining premises and raising funds are activities often absent from histories centered on institutional founders. The historian must not invent personal testimony to illustrate these experiences. Instead, surviving accounts, photographs, school records, correspondence and the evidence of everyday material life can show aspects of participation. Women, lower-income contributors and migrants may be difficult to identify in the administrative archive. A lack of named individuals is not evidence that their labor was unimportant.

Religious Schools, Charities, and the Infrastructure of Collective Organization

Religious organizations developed capacities that were important to collective action even when their immediate objectives were not political. The material basis of such capacity consisted of premises, funds, trained personnel, records, channels of communication and relationships of trust. A school could serve hundreds of families over successive years; a periodical could maintain contact with distant readers; a religious association could use regular meetings to decide how to allocate its resources. These arrangements mattered in societies where many people had little direct access to the formal political institutions of the colonial state. The authority of religious leaders could provide a basis for organizing services outside the governmental bureaucracy, although such institutions were not free from regulation or internal inequalities.

The relationship between teaching and collective organization is especially important. A school operated by a religious association required an administrative structure that could function when its founder was absent. It needed revenue, trained teachers, a curriculum, premises and mechanisms for supervision. Over time, the establishment of multiple schools required coordination across communities. Graduates could enter occupations in which literacy, accounting and correspondence were useful. Some returned to become teachers or administrators; others participated in professional or political organizations. These consequences did not follow automatically from religious instruction. They depended on access to advanced education, the opportunities available to graduates and their decisions about how to use their skills. The educational institutions of Chapter 124 and those of this chapter should therefore be examined as overlapping parts of the same historical transformation.

Religious giving supplied a crucial set of motivations and resources. Buddhist merit-making, Islamic charitable obligations and Christian giving all supported religious institutions, although they were grounded in different theological traditions. Donations might finance the construction of a building, food for students, a publication, the support of clergy or services to people in need. An endowed property could provide income across generations, whereas annual subscriptions made an association dependent on the continued willingness of its members to contribute. Neither form was necessarily more effective in every circumstance. Funds collected for designated religious purposes also carried obligations concerning their use. The ability to mobilize donations for a religious school did not mean that an organization's leaders were free to spend those resources on an unrelated political project.

Muhammadiyah's schools and welfare programs illustrate the importance of sustained administration. Aisyiyah provides particularly concrete evidence of the development of leadership among women, because it established structures through which women could supervise educational work, communicate with other branches and participate in conferences. The activities of women in Buddhist communities reveal a different relationship between formal authority and practical contribution. Donations, religious instruction within households and participation in community fundraising supported monasteries without requiring that all donors acquire positions in the monastic hierarchy. A comparison between the two cases must therefore distinguish named leadership in a formal association from less visible forms of authority exercised through networks of families and local patrons.

The institutions of Chinese migrant communities and Christian congregations furnish further examples. A burial association needed a system for collecting dues, keeping records and assisting families; a mission-trained local teacher could connect a school to a wider cultural or linguistic community. In each case, repeated cooperation created expectations about mutual responsibilities. These expectations could help organizers obtain support for new projects. Yet relationships of trust could be limited to particular groups, and a strong network within one community did not guarantee effective cooperation with its neighbors. Religious institutions could make civil society denser while also reinforcing the boundaries separating different associations.

Such networks became politically significant under identifiable conditions. An ordinance restricting independent schools, an official action perceived as insulting to religion, or a dispute over the allocation of resources could turn members' attention toward governmental authority. Existing communication channels reduced the cost of organizing a petition or public meeting. Trained officials and respected teachers could articulate grievances in a shared language. In some cases, the same organization continued its ordinary educational or welfare work while publicly addressing a specific political issue. The extent of mobilization depended on whether leaders and members agreed that the issue fell within their mission, whether other associations joined the effort, and how the state responded. This institutional explanation avoids assuming that religious commitment invariably produced nationalism. It instead identifies the mechanisms through which existing religious activity could acquire a political dimension.

Continuity and Change II

From patronage to sustained association

An endowment, household gift, rotating subscription and annual membership fee are distinct mechanisms for sustaining a religious institution. Each distributes responsibilities differently. An endowment can generate long-term revenue but depends on the protection and management of property. Regular subscriptions give members a continuing stake but may be vulnerable to economic crisis. Donations of labor or food can sustain local institutions without appearing as cash revenue.

The development of formal associations did not replace all older arrangements. Modern schools and welfare programs could depend on religious giving and personal loyalties established long before a constitution or list of officers was adopted. Aisyiyah's leadership and publications reveal how formal organization could make previously less visible participation public. In other cases the existing donor network remained the principal form of involvement. Sacred Authority, Political Protest, and Anticolonial Resistance

Sacred authority became politically significant when decisions made by officials or other powerful actors affected religious institutions, moral obligations or the status of a community. For people who understood their well-being in terms of religious commitments, the defense of a pagoda, mosque, school or parish could be a matter of collective dignity rather than a narrowly private concern. Colonial governments often attempted to separate legitimate worship from prohibited political activity. Such a distinction was difficult to maintain because religious life involved property, education, access to public space and the organization of people. Each of these could bring religious authorities into conflict with a government even when their initial demands did not concern national sovereignty.

The campaign against the wearing of shoes on Buddhist pagoda grounds in colonial Burma illustrates this progression. At issue was the respect expected from visitors to a sacred place. Europeans' unwillingness to observe customary restrictions acquired a wider significance in a society where political and racial inequality were part of everyday life. Buddhist associations could formulate a particular grievance, circulate arguments, and make demands of officials or temple trustees. The religious significance of the campaign was not merely a pretext for politics. Because sacred space mattered to participants, the demand for respect helped expose the unequal terms on which different groups occupied public life. The campaign's broader political consequences are examined in Chapter 129.

Religious moral authority could likewise shape rural resistance. The Saya San rebellion of 1930–1932 in Burma involved grievances related to economic hardship and colonial authority, and its historical interpretation has often emphasized the use of royal and Buddhist symbols. Historians have challenged simple accounts that present its participants as members of a uniform religious or millenarian movement. Maitrii Aung-Thwin's study of the rebellion draws particular attention to the categories constructed in colonial legal proceedings and subsequent historical narratives. A trial record can show the state's attempt to assign motives and establish criminal responsibility without necessarily revealing an agreed program shared by the participants. The relevance of the example here is methodological: evidence of sacred symbols does not by itself establish either the cause or the organizational structure of a rebellion.

In the Netherlands Indies, the government's 1932 Wild Schools Ordinance became a point of conflict with independent educational movements. Religious organizations as well as secular educational associations were concerned about the implications of official restrictions for their ability to govern schools. The controversy demonstrated the possibility of cooperation among groups whose educational objectives were otherwise different. The relevant common interest was institutional autonomy rather than identical religious doctrine. When access to teaching became a public question, existing associations could mobilize their leaders and supporters. This is an example of how a religious institution's ordinary operations could create the material interests around which more overtly political action formed.

The Philippine case presents another relationship between religion and resistance. The demand for Filipino ecclesiastical leadership developed alongside a wider struggle against colonial inequality, yet the establishment of an independent church did not transfer political sovereignty. Its members faced the problem of securing congregations and property under American rule. Their experience shows how a religious project could challenge one kind of authority while appealing to a court established under another. The choice to pursue legal action or negotiate administrative recognition was not necessarily inconsistent with a commitment to collective dignity. Resistance was not limited to rebellion, and accommodation did not imply complete agreement with the governing system.

Millenarian expectations also deserve analytical care. A charismatic leader's promise of a transformed moral or spiritual order could give meaning to experiences of insecurity and create a strong community of followers. Such expectations could acquire political significance, but the promised transformation was not necessarily equivalent to a program of constitutional or territorial independence. Cao Đài and Hòa Hảo illustrate the importance of considering theological and devotional goals in their own right. Government surveillance of religious movements demonstrates that officials perceived possibilities for mobilization; it does not prove that every adherent shared those intentions. The historian must ask what leaders taught, what participants actually did, what institutions they established, and how officials interpreted the activity. These distinct bodies of evidence permit a more reliable account of the circumstances under which religious action became anticolonial resistance.

Historical Vocabulary III

Religion and anticolonial action

Religious nationalism connects religious identity or moral tradition to claims concerning a political community. Millenarianism describes expectations of far-reaching transformation, often religiously understood. Anticolonial resistance refers to action challenging colonial domination or particular exercises of colonial power. These terms can overlap, but they do not identify the same historical phenomenon.

A petition seeking respect for a pagoda is evidence of a particular grievance. A religious leader's teaching concerning collective renewal is evidence of a moral or spiritual claim. A movement's constitution, records of meetings and actions can establish its stated objectives and organizational methods. Each must be examined independently. Colonial officials sometimes described forms of religious resistance using language that implied irrationality, danger or coordination. Those descriptions should be tested against evidence from participants, not repeated as neutral categories.

Religious Nationalism, Secular Politics, and Intercommunal Tensions

The emergence of mass political organizations created questions about the boundaries of the communities they claimed to represent. Religious communities often extended beyond the territory of a colony, while emerging nationalist movements increasingly claimed to speak on behalf of people within a particular territory or imagined homeland. The two kinds of community could reinforce one another, but they were not identical. A person might recognize solidarity with fellow believers abroad while supporting a territorial political movement that included citizens of different religions. Another might believe that a national organization ought to give special recognition to the predominant religious tradition. These alternatives reflected disputes over political membership, institutional power and moral obligation rather than a fixed division between religion and politics.

In the Netherlands Indies, the growth of Muslim associations and the emergence of organizations based on Indonesian nationality brought these issues into public discussion. The history of Sarekat Islam demonstrates the potential of Muslim identity and commercial networks to sustain broad association. It also shows that membership did not ensure agreement on economic or political strategy. The intellectual and organizational conflicts among nationalists, socialists, communists and Muslim reformers are treated in Chapter 126. For the purposes of the present chapter, the essential distinction is between a religiously constituted public and the political coalition formed in response to a specific issue. The establishment of the MIAI federation in 1937, bringing together organizations with differing religious approaches, shows how cooperation could arise without complete doctrinal agreement. Such federations were themselves products of negotiation and cannot be understood as natural consequences of common faith.

In Burma, the close historical relationship between Buddhist kingship and the religious life of many Burmans gave efforts to defend Buddhism a powerful political language. Yet colonial Burma included Muslims, Hindus, Christians and many communities whose languages, political histories and religious practices differed. An account that makes Buddhist identity equivalent to the population of Burma excludes important participants in the country's political and economic life. The urban violence of the 1930s, including the anti-Indian disturbances of 1930 and the anti-Muslim violence of 1938, illustrates the dangers of converting social and economic grievances into collective accusations against religious or ethnic groups. These disturbances arose within a colonial society shaped by migration, labor competition, unequal power and inflammatory public discourse. They should not be explained as an inevitable conflict between religions, nor should all Buddhists or all members of any other religious community be assigned responsibility for the actions of participants.

In Malaya, the historical connection between Islam, Malay rulers and Malay identity helped shape political organization, while Chinese, Indian and other communities developed associations with diverse priorities. The legal and political arrangements of the Malay states differed from those of the Straits Settlements. Religious jurisdiction, language, education and migration all affected the boundaries of possible political coalitions. Some Muslim reformists participated in discussions of Malay advancement, but their religious networks extended across the political boundaries of British Malaya. The challenge of developing a territorial political community that encompassed multiple populations is examined more fully in Chapter 130.

The Philippines and Siam provide different comparisons. A predominantly Catholic population in the Philippines did not prevent disputes concerning the church's authority, the separation of religious institutions and political government, or the existence of other Christian and Muslim communities. Siam's monarchy and Buddhist order had historically close relationships, but the movement toward constitutional government in 1932 created new questions about the foundations of political authority. The terms secular politics and religious politics should not be applied as mutually exclusive descriptions of these societies. A political institution could be constitutionally separate from a church while its leaders and members drew on religious moral commitments. Conversely, a religious organization might refrain from endorsing any political party while seeking changes in public policy affecting its members.

Intercommunal relations were not confined to conflict. Schools, medical institutions, trade, intellectual exchange and common grievances could produce cooperation among members of different religions. The degree of cooperation depended on access to institutions and the willingness of organizers to recognize common interests. Colonial administrative categories could sharpen divisions by distributing opportunities or authority through separate communal institutions, but governments did not create every difference or control every outcome. Religious communities possessed their own histories and internal debates. The challenge for historians is to trace the processes through which these boundaries were strengthened, crossed or redefined. Nationalism was one important context in which those choices acquired greater political consequences, but the historical record does not support treating any single model of religious nationalism as the inevitable destiny of the region.

The choice need not be exclusive. Religious Reform and the Making of Modern Southeast Asia

The preceding sections allow a comparative interpretation of religious reform as a major dimension of modern Southeast Asian history. The principal development was not a uniform shift from traditional to modern religion. It was a reorganization of the relationship among religious knowledge, institutional authority, social participation and political power. The forms of that reorganization differed according to the historical character of each religious tradition, the opportunities available for education and communication, the economic resources of communities and the arrangements of colonial or independent government. The differences are as important as the shared patterns because they explain why religious organization could lead to very different political outcomes.

One major axis of comparison is the relationship between religious authority and the state. In Burma, the dissolution of the monarchy weakened a central framework of Buddhist patronage, encouraging the development of new forms of lay support and collective organization. In Siam, the monarchy itself pursued closer integration of the monastic order into a centralized administrative structure. In Cambodia and Laos, French-sponsored religious institutions supported the advancement of local scholarship while attempting to weaken intellectual dependence on Siam. These histories involved overlapping techniques of education and administration, but the motives and distribution of political authority differed. They show why a shared religious tradition cannot be used to predict a common model of religious modernization.

A second axis concerns the organization of knowledge and social services. Islamic reformers used periodicals, translations and schools to bring debates about authoritative teaching into new institutional environments. Muhammadiyah and Nahdlatul Ulama developed different approaches to religious knowledge and leadership, while both created modern organizations capable of coordinating activity across local communities. Ledi Sayadaw's writings provide a Buddhist example in which extended access to doctrinal understanding could sustain rather than displace an authoritative religious tradition. Christian mission schools and indigenous churches demonstrate the importance of language, literacy and local leadership in the construction of religious communities. In all these cases, the transmission of texts involved people, funds and institutions. An explanation that focuses on ideas alone misses the material circumstances under which ideas acquired durable social consequences.

A third axis is the distribution of participation. The development of religious associations gave some people new opportunities to become teachers, administrators, donors and public speakers. Aisyiyah offers clear evidence of women's formal leadership, while religious donations and everyday instruction provide evidence of forms of participation that were less visible in organizational records. Migrants could maintain relationships of trust through temples, mosques, churches and mutual-aid societies. Rural communities could respond to the teachings of religious innovators without the same access to urban schools and journals as educated professionals. These different forms of participation make it impossible to describe religious modernity as the experience of a single social class. They also remind historians that administrative records and published texts may capture only one part of a community's life.

The political consequences must be analyzed through the interaction between institutions and particular events. The capacity to assemble people, circulate a petition, raise funds or sustain a campaign made associations relevant to political life. Whether these capacities were used for national independence, religious defense, education, community welfare or other purposes depended on the decisions and circumstances of the participants. A colonial restriction on schools could prompt cooperation among organizations with different doctrines; an act perceived as an insult to a sacred space could turn religious dignity into a public demand. Conversely, the provision of government support for a religious library might create an area of cooperation even between people who disagreed about colonial sovereignty. Institutional capacity was a condition of certain forms of political action, not a sufficient cause of them.

Chapters 123, 124 and 125 thus establish three complementary dimensions of the interwar transformation. The war destabilized established arrangements and altered expectations; the growth of cities, schools, print and associations changed the means of communication and organization; religious networks gave many communities moral authority, obligations and institutional resources through which to pursue collective goals. Other developments, including rural distress, economic crisis and political repression, would shape the circumstances in which these resources were used. No single dimension alone explains the emergence of national political movements. The following chapters examine the specific organizations, conflicts and political choices through which the possibilities described here were realized or frustrated. Religious institutions continued to matter beyond independence, but their diverse political futures were not predetermined by the reformers of the colonial period. The historical task is to explain the connection between religious conviction and institutional action without reducing either to the other.

Chapter Summary

Religious reform in Southeast Asia between approximately 1870 and 1940 involved the active construction of institutions through which people taught, studied, worshipped and organized collective life. The region's religious geography was plural, and colonial census categories only imperfectly captured the overlapping traditions found within local communities. Neither religious affiliation nor the territory of an empire established a single political community.

European conquest altered the institutions supporting religious authority in different ways. Burma lost its monarchy, whereas Cambodia and the Malay states retained rulers with varied religious responsibilities. In independent Siam, kings and monks participated in the centralization of religious administration. The contrast demonstrates that modern bureaucratic forms could emerge both within and outside direct colonial rule.

Burmese Buddhists organized to preserve the sāsana, developing forms of lay association in a changing patronage environment. Ledi Sayadaw's career demonstrates the importance of doctrinal scholarship and vernacular instruction in the growth of lay practice. Such developments were rooted in Buddhist tradition rather than a simple rejection of religious authority.

The French-sponsored Buddhist Institute supported scholarly work in Cambodia and Laos while attempting to redirect religious connections away from Siam. Vietnamese revival associations established publications and new educational opportunities within a different Buddhist setting. Women religious writers and teachers contributed to these movements, though their participation was not equally reflected in all archives.

Islamic reform depended on older traditions of scholarship as well as new routes of publication and education. Al-Imam's translated educational article reveals a traceable connection between Cairo and Singapore. In West Sumatra and Java, scholars and organizers established educational programs suited to local circumstances.

Muhammadiyah and Nahdlatul Ulama embodied distinct approaches to authority and teaching while both establishing modern forms of association. Aisyiyah's leadership and publishing show that women's participation extended to the intellectual and institutional definition of religious programs. Religious welfare and education were enduring goals in their own right.

Christian missions, local congregations and indigenous clergy interacted with imperial authority in different ways. The Philippine Independent Church sought ecclesiastical autonomy, yet its members confronted legal disputes over the material resources of parish life. The Lagonoy case demonstrates that religious allegiance, political sovereignty and ownership were separate questions.

Cao Đài and Hòa Hảo developed distinctive religious institutions and devotional programs in southern Vietnam. Their later political significance should not obscure their founding religious concerns. Chinese associations, Hindu and Sikh institutions, Balinese religious communities and indigenous spiritual traditions likewise demonstrate that religious life included multiple kinds of collective organization.

The capacity to provide education, raise funds, publish material, hold meetings and maintain services made religious associations important to public life. Political action could emerge when a government decision affected religious dignity or institutional autonomy, but religious solidarity could also sustain welfare, scholarship and worship without a nationalist objective.

The chapter completes the analysis of imperial disruption, new publics and religious organization begun in Chapters 123 and 124. Subsequent chapters will explain the specific movements and political choices that transformed these conditions into different national histories. No uniform religious path to independence followed from the period's institutional developments.

Key Terms

Sāsana
The Buddhist dispensation: the teachings, practices and institutions through which Buddhism is preserved and transmitted. In colonial Burma its preservation became a central objective of lay and monastic associations.
Sangha
The ordained Buddhist community. The term may be used more broadly in some contexts, but here it chiefly denotes monks and monastic institutions, not the entire Buddhist population.
Thathanabaing
Senior Burmese monastic patriarch. The office survived the end of the monarchy with disputed recognition and diminished powers of arbitration.
Theravāda
Buddhist tradition with a Pali canonical inheritance, prominent in Burma, Siam, Cambodia and Laos; its institutions differed across those societies.
Mahāyāna
A broad family of Buddhist traditions, prominent in Vietnam and Chinese religious environments; it encompasses varied teachings and practices rather than a single institution.
Dhammayut
Siamese monastic order associated with Mongkut's reform of discipline and learning; it received substantial royal patronage.
Mahanikai
The larger Siamese monastic order, encompassing diverse established traditions; membership did not imply opposition to reform.
Abhidhamma
Systematic Buddhist analysis of phenomena, particularly important in the scholastic work and lay instruction associated with Ledi Sayadaw.
Vipassanā
Insight into the characteristics of conditioned experience; a longstanding Buddhist practice whose modes of transmission changed in the colonial period.
Samatha
Cultivation of tranquility or calm, often discussed alongside insight practice; distinctions and sequencing differ among teachers.
Pali
Language of the Theravāda canon and major traditions of monastic scholarship; Pali studies linked institutions across regional borders.
Merit-making
Religiously significant actions, including giving and support for the monastic community; an important basis of Buddhist patronage.
Thilashin
Burmese women religious renunciants; their status should not be conflated with fully ordained Buddhist nuns or ordinary laywomen.
Religious patronage
Material, legal or ceremonial support of religious institutions by rulers, donors and other patrons; it can take the form of land, revenue or contributions.
Religious reform
Efforts to reorganize religious practice, learning or institutions according to particular understandings of authority and appropriate conduct.
Revivalism
Efforts to renew religious vitality and participation, often through the reinterpretation or renewed transmission of established teaching.
Religious modernism
Historically specific engagement with contemporary knowledge and institutional techniques from within a religious tradition; not a synonym for secularization.
Orthodoxy
A claim to correct belief or practice according to recognized religious authority; its content can be contested by rival interpreters.
Hajj
Pilgrimage to Mecca performed according to Islamic requirements; historically also an occasion for travel, study and communication.
Jawi
A designation used for Southeast Asian Muslims in wider Islamic scholarly networks; also the name of an Arabic-derived script used for Malay.
Ijtihād
Disciplined independent juristic reasoning within Islamic legal scholarship; its permissible scope and qualified practitioners were debated.
Taqlīd
Following recognized juristic authority or precedent within Islamic jurisprudence; not simply an absence of reasoning.
Madhhab
An established school of Islamic jurisprudence, such as the Shafi‘i school historically influential in maritime Southeast Asia.
Ummah
The Muslim community understood as extending beyond local and territorial boundaries; it does not itself prescribe a particular political organization.
Pan-Islamism
Projects and ideas emphasizing Muslim solidarity across political boundaries; participants differed in aims and political commitments.
Kaum Muda
Malay-world label for reformist or younger-group intellectual currents; not a literal age category or single political party.
Kaum Tua
Broad label for defenders of established religious authority in Malay-world debates; their institutional practices were not uniformly conservative.
Adat
Customary norms and legal practices in the Malay-Indonesian world; colonial attempts to classify them in relation to Islamic law often obscured their overlap.
Pesantren
Islamic educational institution, especially associated with Java, often involving residential study with a scholar and a network of graduates.
Surau
Place of prayer and religious learning, especially important in Minangkabau society; some surau developed into centers of reform and schooling.
Kiai
Javanese title for a respected Islamic teacher or scholar, whose authority rested in part on learning and recognized community relationships.
Santri
Student or participant in Islamic learning; often used for students associated with pesantren, with additional meanings in social analysis.
Waqf
A religious or charitable endowment under Islamic legal arrangements; property and income are dedicated to designated purposes.
Zakat
Obligatory Islamic almsgiving according to applicable religious rules; distinguish from voluntary charitable gifts.
Ecclesiastical autonomy
The capacity of a church or religious body to govern its affairs and leadership, distinct from the political sovereignty of a state.
Secular clergy
In Catholic usage, priests not belonging to a religious order; the term does not imply a rejection of religion.
Denomination
A distinct organized religious body or tradition, particularly used in discussions of Christian churches; boundaries vary historically.
Spirit-mediumship
Religious practices in which communication with spiritual beings is mediated through particular persons or techniques; significant in early Cao Đài circles.
Revelation
Knowledge or instruction understood by adherents as divinely communicated; historians distinguish the participants' claims from independently verifiable institutional events.
Millenarianism
Expectation of far-reaching transformation, often religiously framed; it does not automatically entail a coordinated uprising or nationalist program.
Religious nationalism
Political identification that links the nation or political community to a religious tradition or identity; its forms and boundaries vary.
Voluntary association
Organization whose participants join and cooperate around designated objectives, commonly governed through officers, rules, membership or subscriptions.
Religious pluralism
Coexistence of multiple religious traditions and institutions within a society; not necessarily evidence of equal legal status or harmonious relations.
Anticolonial resistance
Actions challenging colonial domination or particular exercises of colonial authority; includes petitions, protest and other forms, not only rebellion.
Jurisdiction
Recognized legal or administrative authority over a particular subject, territory or category of cases; important in religious property and governance disputes.

Chapter Reflection

Religious reform shows why historians must distinguish the meanings actors assigned to their institutions from the purposes later observers attribute to them. An association organized to preserve a tradition might eventually play a role in a national movement, but the later role does not establish its original objective. The evidence must be read in chronological order, attentive to what participants could know and what they chose to do.

The comparative method reveals differences that a single national narrative might obscure. Burmese and Siamese Buddhist institutions developed under different political arrangements. Indonesian Muslim associations could differ in theological orientation while employing related administrative techniques. Filipino churches and Vietnamese religious movements encountered distinct problems of law, leadership and community. Comparing these institutions illuminates both religious agency and the conditions under which it was exercised.

Historical sources illuminate particular dimensions of this process. The Lagonoy resolution preserves a claim about authority and continuity; the translation of an educational article demonstrates an intellectual connection; the census provides administrative categories and population proportions. These are different kinds of evidence. A rigorous explanation draws on them without treating any single source as a complete picture of religious life.

The following chapters turn to nationalist movements, parties and constitutional changes. As students encounter these histories, they should remember that political mobilization was sustained by people who also pursued education, religious commitments, family obligations and livelihoods. The religious institutions developed in the interwar period created possibilities, not predetermined outcomes.
