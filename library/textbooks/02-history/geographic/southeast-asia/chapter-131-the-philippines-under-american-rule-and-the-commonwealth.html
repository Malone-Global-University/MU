The Philippines under American Rule and the Commonwealth

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

The Commonwealth transformed political authority without ending U.S. sovereignty or resolving conflicts over land, economic dependence, elite leadership, and citizenship.

On November 15, 1935, a crowd in Manila witnessed Manuel L. Quezon take the oath as first president of the Philippine Commonwealth. The ceremony seemed to confirm decades of constitutional advocacy. The Philippines now possessed an elected executive, a written constitution, and a timetable for independence. Yet the United States still held sovereignty, controlled important international affairs, and conditioned the transition through legislation enacted in Washington. The inauguration expressed both an extraordinary political achievement and the unfinished character of national self-government. A careful history must explain the difference between public sovereignty claimed, domestic authority exercised, and independence legally scheduled.

This chapter follows Filipino politics from the opening of the Philippine Assembly in 1907 to the eve of Japanese invasion in December 1941. It examines how Nacionalista politicians negotiated American colonial institutions; how export industries developed under preferential U.S. trade; and how workers, tenants, religious movements, women reformers, and political radicals contested the meaning of independence. The Commonwealth was not simply a waiting room for the future republic. Its leaders made consequential decisions about constitutional powers, language, public administration, land policy, electoral rights, and defense while substantial areas of authority remained outside their control.

Electoral nationalism was genuine, but it operated within striking social inequalities. Provincial landholding families and professionals dominated many constituencies. They could advocate Filipino nationhood while controlling farms, credit, government appointments, and party nominations. Economic modernization could enrich mill owners and exporters without providing secure livelihoods for tenants and laborers. Political modernization and social hierarchy were therefore not opposing eras in a neat sequence; they developed together. Understanding how they did so is essential to evaluating both the achievements and limitations of the Commonwealth.

The chapter also recovers political programs that did not originate in the major parties. Agrarian religious associations, the Colorum and Tayug movements, the Sakdal movement of Benigno Ramos, union organizers, Socialists, and Communists challenged different aspects of colonial and elite power. Their methods and ideas differed sharply, and their supporters were not a single undifferentiated mass. The opposition they represented forces an important question: was independence principally the right of a Filipino government to exercise sovereignty, or must it also change wages, land tenure, and the everyday relations of power?

Women campaigners illuminate another dimension of the struggle. Filipino women had organized for civic and political rights long before the 1937 suffrage plebiscite. Their successful campaign widened formal citizenship, although literacy, wealth, and gendered expectations still shaped participation. Muslim communities in Mindanao and Sulu, and indigenous peoples in the Cordillera and other regions, contested the assumption that the constitutional nation represented everyone on identical terms. A history attentive only to officials in Manila and Washington misses these debates about inclusion, law, land, and identity.

The text draws on the Jones Act, the 1934 independence legislation, the original 1935 Constitution, Commonwealth statutes, political journalism, government reports, and later historical scholarship. Documents are read for their intended audiences and limitations, not treated as transparent descriptions of society. Chapter 119 covers the earlier construction of American colonial rule; Chapter 133 examines the Depression comparatively; and Chapter 137 covers the Japanese invasion and occupation. This chapter ends at the threshold of war, when Filipino self-government was significant but neither fully sovereign nor socially settled.

Historical Context

American sovereignty followed the Philippine Revolution against Spain and a prolonged Philippine-American War. The war had divided regions and families and made the new colonial government dependent initially on military force. By the early twentieth century, U.S. officials promoted education, a civil service, and municipal elections while retaining ultimate authority. Former revolutionaries, lawyers, merchants, and provincial notables adapted differently to these arrangements. Chapters 116–119 examine the revolution, war, and early colonial structures; Chapter 131 studies how later generations attempted to turn those institutions into instruments of political independence.

The archipelago differed sharply by geography and social structure. Luzon contained Manila and major rice and sugar regions; the Visayas included established ports and agricultural economies; Mindanao and Sulu contained diverse Muslim and indigenous communities as well as expanding settler populations. The islands were connected by shipping, commerce, schooling, and government offices but did not form a socially uniform territory. These variations shaped electoral access, land relations, and the legitimacy of state policies presented as national. Even a national constitution could not eliminate the inherited differences among regions.

Preferential trade with the United States encouraged sugar, coconut oil, abaca, and other export sectors. Landowners and merchants frequently gained, while tenants and laborers faced unstable prices, unequal contracts, and debt. American manufacturers and consumers also became central to Philippine economic life. These relationships gave many business interests practical reasons to maintain market access even while advocating political independence. Later congressional negotiations treated tariff quotas, immigration restrictions, and military reservations as central parts of the independence settlement.

Constitutional nationalism gained force under the Philippine Assembly after 1907 and the Jones Act after 1916. The elected leadership built enduring organizations, but the United States retained sovereignty and key executive powers. In the 1930s the Depression, American commercial politics, and intra-Filipino competition resulted in an independence law with a ten-year transitional Commonwealth. The new government faced demands that had not been resolved by diplomatic agreement: rural land security, union rights, political participation for women, and inclusion of communities skeptical of Manila-centered nationalism.

The approaching Pacific War placed the Commonwealth under growing strategic pressure. Defense planning began under Commonwealth Act No. 1 in 1935, but the army and government remained dependent on American military capacity. By the end of 1941, national institutions were more developed than those of 1907, yet the Philippines had not attained the international legal authority promised for 1946. The distinction between constitutional achievement and unresolved social and strategic problems provides the organizing theme of this chapter.

Division 1 · Section 131.1

American Sovereignty and the Emergence of Filipino Political Institutions

After the Philippine-American War, the Organic Act of 1902 created a civil government in which the appointed Philippine Commission, dominated by Americans, held legislative power. Filipinos first gained office through municipal and provincial elections under a narrow franchise based on property, literacy, and prior office. Representation was real but supervised: sovereignty, the governor-general's veto, and final authority rested with Washington.

The passage from military conquest to civil administration occurred before the law that later defined its framework. The Second Philippine Commission began exercising legislative powers in 1900, the Spooner Amendment of March 1901 authorized a civilian structure, and William Howard Taft assumed office as civil governor on July 4, 1901. The Philippine Organic Act, enacted July 1, 1902, then codified the emerging arrangement and contemplated a representative assembly after a census and the fulfillment of security conditions. These distinctions matter: the United States did not first establish civilian authority through a statute in 1902, and civil government itself was neither a transfer of sovereignty to Filipinos nor the termination of armed resistance in every region.

The institutions carried two conflicting political claims. Americans described civil rule as tutelage, a temporary education in self-government designed to prepare an allegedly unready people for greater responsibilities. Filipino critics could point to an earlier revolutionary congress and republic as evidence that national institutions were not simply a gift of conquest. American officers and civilian officials still treated the islands as a United States possession, and the President and Congress determined the limits within which elections could proceed. A Filipino municipal official might oversee roads, sanitation, or schooling while lacking any voice in tariff policy, migration rules, foreign affairs, or the appointment of the highest colonial officers. That division between local administration and ultimate power recurred throughout the Commonwealth era.

Early elections also excluded many inhabitants. The electorate was restricted by rules involving sex, age, literacy, property, taxation, or prior service; women were not enfranchised nationally until the late 1930s. The qualifications selected a small portion of the population and disproportionately benefited property owners, officeholders, and men who had access to formal education. Municipal political figures learned to build support through relatives, neighborhoods, debt relationships, and the distribution of practical favors. Such political activity was not necessarily fraudulent: schools, wells, roads, or protection against a hostile landlord could be tangible gains. But it gave existing local notables advantages that national independence advocates could not easily overcome.

Geography reinforced those inequalities. The archipelago's numerous islands required a mixture of coastal shipping, railways, roads, telegraph communications, and local intermediaries. Manila was the seat of colonial government and a publishing center; provincial capitals connected regional leaders to the insular bureaucracy. Access to office depended on knowing legal procedure and on being able to finance travel to Manila, campaign gatherings, and correspondence. Communities in the Cordillera, parts of Mindanao, and the Sulu archipelago encountered special administrative arrangements and missionary or military institutions instead of the same electoral framework found in many Christian lowland provinces. A national assembly could therefore be recognized as Filipino and still leave substantial populations inadequately represented.

The Philippine Commission combined legislative and administrative functions and initially contained an American majority, though Filipinos such as Benito Legarda, Trinidad Pardo de Tavera, and José de Luzuriaga joined it. Appointed bodies could make sweeping decisions about finance and public resources before the planned assembly existed. The Organic Act preserved a dual structure: the appointed Commission would serve as an upper legislative chamber while an elected Assembly would form a lower chamber. It also included civil-liberty guarantees patterned on American constitutional language but applied within a territory where the United States retained superior authority. Local use of elections and constitutional language thus coexisted with the unequal relationship between colony and empire.

The politics of this transition must not be reduced to two groups, collaborators and resisters. Some former revolutionaries entered municipal office, reasoning that public institutions could be used to defend Filipino interests and ultimately negotiate independence. Others refused colonial office or criticized accommodation. Local farmers could support a respected nationalist politician while resisting his family's demands for rent; workers could favor independence while opposing the employers who led political parties. In the early twentieth century, the meaning of independence remained a question about who would rule, how decisions would be made, and what social order the new nation would inherit. This section establishes the arena in which those questions were fought, not a foreordained route to sovereignty.

Division 1 · Section 131.2

The Philippine Assembly, the Nacionalista Party, and Electoral Nationalism

The Assembly elected in July 1907 opened that October, with Sergio Osmeña as Speaker and Manuel Quezon a leading member. The Nacionalista Party, founded in 1907, campaigned for independence and defeated parties favoring eventual statehood or gradual change. Its success rested on alliances among provincial notables, which turned revolutionary nationalism into electoral and legislative strategy.

The first nationwide election for the Philippine Assembly took place in July 1907, and the body convened in October. Campaigns turned broad anticolonial aspiration into votes, platforms, and speeches: participants asked which group could most effectively secure independence while working within the American constitutional structure. The Nacionalista Party emerged as a formidable coalition, although local interests and factional alignments remained important. Its success showed that a party could demand a sovereign state while using elections created by the colonial government. There was no contradiction in parliamentary practice itself; the question was whether electoral recognition would eventually constrain the government that authorized the elections.

Sergio Osmeña of Cebu became speaker and developed an impressive command of parliamentary organization. Manuel L. Quezon of Tayabas, an articulate advocate with substantial experience in provincial politics, gained a national profile and later took a leading role in Washington. Their partnership was productive but was never insulated from competition. The Assembly voted budgets and debated local legislation, yet it could not dismiss the American governor-general or independently alter policies reserved to Washington. Conflicts with the appointed Philippine Commission made the practical bounds of self-government visible. Filipino legislators could mobilize public opinion, withhold cooperation, and force procedural arguments, but they operated within a hierarchy of power.

Opposition parties did not all offer the same alternatives. Some initially argued for more gradual political preparation, continued American association, or particular forms of autonomy; others differed from Nacionalistas chiefly over personalities and provincial alliances. Party labels could be elastic, because patronage, election laws, and local leadership networks frequently mattered as much as philosophical commitments. A candidate who promised early independence might also seek increased funding for an irrigation project, a judicial appointment for an ally, or protection for a local commercial interest. Political analysts must therefore distinguish a party manifesto from the bargaining required to maintain an actual legislative coalition.

Campaigning reshaped the public sphere. Newspapers printed speeches, electoral results, and accusations against officials. Lawyers and schoolteachers translated disputes into terms of rights, representation, and the people's will. Politicians traveled to provincial towns and returned to Manila claiming to speak for whole districts. Campaigns could widen political participation indirectly even where suffrage remained narrow, because nonvoting residents attended meetings, read or heard reports, and pressed candidates about grievances. Yet those excluded by literacy and property qualifications, including many women and peasants, generally had to seek influence through kinship, petitions, associations, or street demonstrations rather than the ballot.

The Assembly's creation also changed nationalist leadership. The revolutionary struggle against Spain and subsequent war against the United States had elevated military figures, local commanders, and reformist intellectuals. Electoral politics elevated people able to manage committees, speak to legislative audiences, build alliances, and negotiate the demands of the colonial bureaucracy. Several political leaders had participated in earlier revolutionary activity, so the shift was neither an abrupt generational break nor the abandonment of the independence goal. It was a change in institutional methods, in the audience whose approval mattered, and in the kinds of compromise that could be defended publicly as patriotic.

In Washington, resident commissioners offered another path of influence. Elected by the colonial legislature but without the same voting power as representatives of U.S. states, they could make arguments before congressional committees and build relationships with legislators. The political meaning of an independence mission was therefore double: it reassured Filipino voters that their representatives were pursuing sovereignty and exposed those same leaders to American congressional concerns about tariffs, military facilities, and business interests. The development of an elected Assembly made nationalism more durable and legible to international observers. It also consolidated particular families and political groups as brokers of the independence project, a tendency that critics would challenge in the 1930s.

By the early 1910s the question was no longer merely whether Filipinos desired independence. It was who could credibly negotiate its conditions, whether the colonial power accepted a timetable, and which elected institutions could claim to represent the population. Those questions carried forward into the Jones Act of 1916 and eventually the constitutional crises of the 1930s. The Assembly's importance lay in its creation of a sustained nationalist institution under foreign sovereignty, not in any immediate transfer of ultimate authority.

Continuity and Change I — From Revolutionary to Electoral Nationalism

Continuity: Many leaders continued to demand national sovereignty and to commemorate the anti-Spanish revolution; several entered institutions under American administration without abandoning the independence objective. Public writing and legal argument remained central ways of asserting a national political identity.

Change: After 1907 campaigns, legislative committee work, party alliances, and congressional missions increasingly displaced armed struggle as the nationally recognized route to independence. That shift favored leaders able to fund elections, master legal language, and negotiate patronage. The franchise remained restricted, so changing methods did not necessarily widen equal influence.

The Jones Act of 1916 and the Promise of Philippine Independence

The Philippine Autonomy Act of August 29, 1916, replaced the Commission with an elected Senate, alongside an elected House of Representatives. Its preamble declared the intention of the United States to withdraw sovereignty once a stable government existed. That was a conditional statement of purpose, not a date; the governor-general, U.S. presidential veto, and congressional authority remained.

The Philippine Autonomy Act of August 29, 1916, generally known as the Jones Act, emerged from the interaction between an American Democratic administration inclined to promise eventual independence and Filipino politicians pressing to convert assurances into institutional power. Its preamble identified American withdrawal of sovereignty as a stated objective once a stable government could be established. No date or mechanism for declaring that condition satisfied was specified. For Filipino nationalists, the language provided an authoritative statement to cite in future campaigns; for American officials, its conditional form left essential decisions under U.S. control. The same sentence could thus support optimism in Manila and caution in Washington.

Institutionally the law replaced the appointed Philippine Commission's legislative role with an elected Senate, alongside an elected House of Representatives. This was an important extension of Filipino legislative authority, even though the franchise remained restricted and provisions for representation of special areas differed from ordinary districts. Quezon emerged as a prominent Senate leader, while Osmeña continued to command the lower house. Their positions gave the major nationalist politicians practical experience with lawmaking, budgets, executive scrutiny, and national political competition. The new system enabled legislators to claim greater democratic legitimacy than an appointed commission had possessed.

American authority remained unmistakable. A governor-general appointed by the U.S. president led the executive branch; certain legislative measures required approval at the American level; and the United States remained responsible for diplomatic relations, the international status of the islands, and the final terms of independence. The law did not make the Philippine Senate a sovereign body, nor did its preamble create a right to secede on a date chosen by elected Filipino representatives. It offered a framework for incremental Filipino participation while retaining the imperial relationship at its center. To students of constitutional history, the difference between a declaration of purpose and a legally enforceable entitlement is fundamental.

The Jones Act also complicated the vocabulary of political debate. Filipinos could describe their country as increasingly autonomous, yet still demand independence; Americans could assert that they were preparing the islands for self-rule, while retaining authority to decide when preparation was sufficient. The condition of a stable government appeared neutral, but it raised political questions. Who defined stability? Did it require a particular party to be in office, a compliant budget, control of agrarian disorder, or acceptance of American defense arrangements? Critics later argued that a vague condition invited permanent delay, especially when Washington's economic and strategic interests changed.

Filipino legislators used the new institutions to make national politics more visible. Senate elections increased provincial political competition, and standing committees created specialist audiences for disputes over education, land, agriculture, commerce, and public works. Officials trained in the colonial civil service became increasingly experienced administrators. But an elected legislature did not dissolve unequal power inside Philippine society. Landowners and professional groups continued to dominate access to campaigning and political finance. Thus constitutional democratization and social oligarchy could advance together, a combination that made the commonwealth period so politically contested.

The international environment helped make the act intelligible. American debates over imperialism and self-determination intensified around the First World War; Filipino speakers compared their claims to those of other colonized peoples. Yet war also strengthened arguments that the United States should retain strategic positions in Asia. The Jones Act must therefore be read within imperial and trans-Pacific politics, not as an isolated benevolent reform. American legislators could celebrate liberal language while preserving naval and commercial options, and Filipino leaders could accept a partial reform while immediately seeking more.

In subsequent independence missions, the Jones Act's preamble became a political resource. Advocates could hold Washington to its professed purpose and point to the growing experience of Filipino legislators and civil servants as proof that government was stable. The eventual legislative agreement of 1934 would replace the conditional, undated promise with a transitional timetable. That later development should not be read backward as though it were guaranteed in 1916. At the time of enactment, the crucial result was a more representative colonial legislature within an unchanged relationship of sovereignty, containing both opportunity and a source of future conflict.

Elite Power, Provincial Political Families, and the Limits of Representative Government

Landowning and professional families dominated nominations, patronage, and office, within a restricted electorate. Many of these politicians sincerely opposed colonial rule while also benefiting from American-era institutions. The Quezon–Osmeña rivalry, and later the Roxas factions, show that nationalism and oligarchic competition developed together rather than as opposites.

The phrase 'elite politics' describes the concentration of influence among landed families, lawyers, commercial figures, and officeholders, but it should not be used to dismiss all elected leaders as insincere. Many had helped defend independence through wartime resistance or legal organizing, and their opposition to American sovereignty was often enduring. At the same time, they operated through institutions in which wealth, English or Spanish education, and family connections made political success easier. A politician who owned agricultural land could press for national autonomy without supporting the reforms demanded by tenants on his estate. Historical analysis must keep these positions in view simultaneously rather than treating patriotic language as proof of social egalitarianism.

Provincial political families had resources unavailable to most constituents: funds for campaigns, access to newspapers and lawyers, control of jobs, relationships with municipal officials, and the capacity to recommend candidates for appointments. Elections required local organizers who could identify qualified voters and negotiate support. Public works projects, school appointments, and road construction became tangible signs of a legislator's effectiveness. Patronage, the distribution of favors or benefits to sustain political loyalty, had a strong institutional basis in a society where a government position or irrigation grant could substantially change household prospects. Not every patronage exchange was illegal, but the system made independent candidacies expensive and reinforced inherited political influence.

The electorate's limitations multiplied these advantages. Women were largely excluded until 1937, and literacy and property or tax-related qualifications disadvantaged many rural people earlier in the period. In provinces where tenancy was common, the landlord might also be a candidate, employer, creditor, or local intermediary with government officers. Tenants could still bargain, petition, resist, or support alternative parties, but their choices were not exercised on equal terms. The distinction between formal representative government and the social distribution of power is essential. A constitution can create elections without eliminating the local relationships that determine who can compete.

The partnership and rivalry of Osmeña and Quezon illustrates both institutional development and elite competition. Osmeña built a disciplined parliamentary organization as speaker, and Quezon developed the capacity to negotiate directly with congressional leaders in the United States. Their disagreements concerned office, personal authority, party tactics, and sometimes the most effective strategy for securing independence. Manuel Roxas's emergence within the younger leadership complicated these alignments. Disputes among Nacionalistas could decide who headed an independence mission and who would claim credit for its success. Those contests were genuinely political, but they also directed public attention toward the status and honor of leading figures.

Elite leadership was not uniformly metropolitan. Provinces differed in crops, landholding, commercial development, linguistic traditions, and the prestige of local families. A sugar-growing province in Negros might be especially concerned about access to American markets; a rice-growing district of Central Luzon might confront tenancy disputes; Cebu politicians had regional networks distinct from Manila's. Political families sometimes represented those economic interests effectively. The challenge was that effective representation for an export miller or large landlord could diverge sharply from the interests of agricultural laborers or debt-burdened tenants in the same district.

The colonial executive also shaped elite power. Governors-general negotiated with elected leaders and could use appointments or budget disputes to pressure them, while politicians employed public opinion to defend their autonomy. The result was not an unchanging ruling class but a constantly negotiated relationship among American administration, national politicians, and provincial notables. Filipino political agency was real within that system, but constrained and unequal. Readers should resist two equally simplistic histories: one describing Filipino leaders as mere collaborators and another depicting all progress toward independence as a triumph achieved without internal social conflict.

These contradictions became especially visible during the 1930s. Electoral elites negotiated an independence timetable, but movements such as Sakdal condemned the delay and accused established politicians of preserving a landlord-dominated social order. Socialist and communist organizers demanded protections more extensive than those proposed by the major parties. The persistence of local political families after the introduction of national constitutional institutions shows why sovereignty alone did not settle the question of whose interests a future republic would serve. Elite political control is therefore part of the chapter's explanation, not a detachable criticism introduced after celebrating legislative milestones.

Free Trade, American Markets, and the Political Economy of Dependence

The Payne–Aldrich Act (1909) opened preferential trade with quotas on some goods; the Underwood–Simmons Act (1913) removed the main limits. Sugar, coconut products, abaca, and tobacco expanded for the U.S. market. Production growth was real, but gains concentrated among exporters, millers, and landowners, and dependence made independence an economic as well as political question.

The Philippine economy was transformed through access to United States markets, but the connection was neither instantaneous nor evenly beneficial. The Payne–Aldrich Tariff Act of 1909 granted preferential entry for Philippine products while retaining quantitative limitations on some major commodities. The Underwood–Simmons Act of 1913 removed important restrictions and enlarged the scope of duty-free exchange. As growers and exporters adjusted, sugar, copra and coconut oil, abaca, and tobacco became increasingly tied to American prices and purchasing decisions. The changes made the colonial relationship an economic structure as well as a political hierarchy. An eventual transfer of the flag would not by itself determine whether that commercial dependence continued.

Sugar provides an especially visible example. Plantations and mills in Negros and Pampanga benefited from investment in central milling technology and the opportunity to sell in the American market. Sugar output rose dramatically during the interwar period, though acreage, yields, and weather varied; published series should be read carefully because figures for raw sugar, refined sugar, and crop years are not interchangeable. Central mills required substantial capital and concentrated bargaining power. Landowners who could borrow money or secure mill contracts were better placed than small producers and tenants to participate in growth. The prosperity visible in export records therefore cannot be translated directly into higher living standards for every person laboring in the fields.

Other export sectors created different patterns. Abaca production involved numerous regions of the central and southern islands and exposed producers to fluctuations in shipping and industrial demand. Coconut agriculture supported smallholders as well as larger commercial enterprises, but the processing and export trade created intermediaries who could capture a disproportionate share of returns. Tobacco, grown under varied property arrangements, also remained sensitive to policy and world prices. Manila's port, banks, shipping connections, and foreign firms played decisive roles in moving agricultural products outward and manufactured commodities inward. A colonial trade balance can thus conceal divergent local relationships among farm households, creditors, mill owners, and merchant houses.

Economic dependence also influenced independence negotiations. Certain American producers, including sugar and dairy interests, feared competition from Philippine imports. Some trade unions and immigration restrictionists viewed Filipino labor migration and imports as threats to wages and jobs. For these groups, the argument for independence could serve protectionist objectives: ending colonial status might allow the United States to impose tariffs, quotas, and immigration restrictions that were politically difficult while the Philippines remained under American sovereignty. Filipino negotiators therefore faced an unusual alliance. An American supporter of Philippine independence could favor its political freedom while demanding commercial terms that disadvantaged Philippine producers and workers.

The Great Depression intensified these disagreements. Falling global demand and trade uncertainty made export industries vulnerable even where access to the American market offered some protection. The Jones–Costigan sugar legislation of 1934 and the Philippine independence settlement illustrated the increasing willingness of Congress to manage quantities and future trade relations. Planters and mill owners worried about the security of the market they had come to rely on. Agricultural workers, however, often had little voice in determining the negotiating position of those industries. The result was a struggle over future independence whose technical language of quotas and tariffs obscured distributional conflicts within Philippine society.

Free trade also influenced consumption and political culture. Imports of American flour, machinery, textile goods, and manufactured products altered urban commerce, while American financial and educational connections fostered aspirations toward professional and consumer lifestyles. Not all imported goods displaced local production, and different classes participated in consumption differently. The larger issue was that colonial infrastructure, government fiscal policy, and commercial incentives favored an exchange oriented toward one external market. Economic diversification required deliberate policy and investment, which the elected Philippine legislature did not wholly control. Market access could be an engine of growth and a source of strategic dependence at the same time.

The historical question is not whether free trade was simply good or bad. It enabled substantial production, financed infrastructure, and expanded some industries while creating uneven power within value chains and making future economic autonomy difficult to define. A landowner, a mill worker, a coconut smallholder, and a Filipino delegate to Washington could all evaluate the arrangement differently. Constitutional independence would force the question of which interests should pay for ending preferential treatment. That dilemma links the economics of this division to the congressional bargaining explored in Division III.

Landownership, Tenancy, and Rural Social Inequality

Hacienda agriculture, debt, and sharecropping shaped sugar districts in Pampanga and Negros and rice districts of Central Luzon. Land registration often favored those with capital and legal knowledge. Smallholding persisted in other regions, so rural inequality varied sharply by crop, locale, and distance from Manila. Colonial land reforms achieved limited redistribution.

Land was at the heart of rural politics because a farm was simultaneously a workplace, a household's means of subsistence, a source of credit collateral, and a foundation of local political authority. Colonial land-registration systems promised clearer title and more reliable transactions, but the legal costs and requirements could advantage people who already possessed capital or documentary proof of ownership. In some places older communal or customary claims were not readily converted into the forms recognized by courts and surveyors. Landlords could use access to lawyers and officials to secure title, while cultivators who had worked the land for many years might have little documentary protection against eviction or higher rent.

The term hacienda describes a large agricultural estate but cannot stand for all Philippine agriculture. Sugar production in Pampanga and Negros depended on specific relationships among estate owners, central mills, tenants, wage laborers, and creditors. Rice-growing districts in Nueva Ecija, Bulacan, and parts of Pampanga often used share-tenancy arrangements under which the harvest was divided between landholder and cultivator, but details varied with contracts, advances, and access to work animals. Smallholders predominated in other regions, and some families shifted between tenancy, ownership, and wage labor over their lives. Without regional distinctions, a national narrative of one uniform peasantry would be misleading.

Debt helped reproduce unequal relations. A tenant might borrow rice before harvest, obtain seed or draft animals on credit, or owe money for family ceremonies and medical costs. Repayment could depend on the season's yields and the market price received for crops. A failed harvest or illness could turn an apparently manageable advance into a longer dependency on a landlord or merchant. Share agreements did not always reveal the real burden because disputes over weighing, deductions, transport, and land improvement could reduce the tenant's net share. The landlord's ability to influence local politics made adjudication uneven: complaints were costly to sustain where the opposing party controlled employment and credit.

Colonial land policies produced mixed results. American administrators purchased friar lands and promoted public-land settlement, but these initiatives did not amount to comprehensive redistribution of privately held estates. Homesteading could encourage settlement in frontier areas and raise difficult questions about the rights of indigenous inhabitants. The acquisition of legal title sometimes improved security for cultivators, yet it also facilitated the transformation of land into a commodity available to creditors and investors. Every program had a geography: the same legal rule could affect a Central Luzon rice tenant, a Negros sugar worker, and a Mindanao settler in sharply different ways.

Rural grievances were expressed through everyday bargaining before they became movements. Tenant groups petitioned for fairer shares, argued over rents or harvest accounting, organized rent withholding or refused particular demands. Some appealed to priests, local lawyers, or provincial officials; others joined devotional associations in which hope for moral and social renewal intertwined with criticism of established authority. Newspapers provided another route for publicizing conflict. Such actions were not automatically nationalist or communist, and individuals could shift from petition to electoral opposition or rebellion as circumstances changed. The critical analytical task is to explain how particular organizations converted grievances into durable collective action.

Agrarian legislation during the Commonwealth acknowledged the problem but faced serious obstacles. The Rice Share Tenancy Act of 1933, Act No. 4054, and its 1936 amendment under Commonwealth Act No. 178 addressed aspects of landlord-tenant relationships, yet scope, enforcement, and local power limited their reach. Legislators included landowners whose families were affected by reform proposals. Administrative agencies lacked the capacity to investigate every dispute, and tenants could fear retaliation for invoking legal protections. The distance between a reform law in Manila and an enforceable right in a distant village is one of the major themes of Commonwealth social history.

Agrarian inequality helps explain why national independence inspired both hope and skepticism. A wealthy sugar producer might demand continued market access and freedom from American sovereignty; a tenant might care primarily whether the next harvest would leave enough rice to feed children. Both could favor an independent Philippines without sharing a program for its political economy. Later Sakdal, Socialist, and Communist movements grew in precisely this field of unequal expectations. Understanding land tenure also prevents the mistake of treating their supporters as followers of abstract ideological labels detached from work, household survival, and local authority.

Public Education, Political Journalism, and the Emergence of New Social Groups

Chapter 119 treats school building; this section stresses political consequences. English-language education, universities, a larger civil service, and a vernacular and English press created teachers, lawyers, clerks, and students who expected citizenship and careers. When opportunities lagged behind expectations, educated groups became both supporters and critics of elite politics.

The American school system expanded access to formal education and introduced English as a central medium of public instruction, but the political significance of schooling depended on what students did with new opportunities. Young Filipinos acquired literacy that enabled them to read legal debates, follow independence missions, enter the colonial civil service, and publish political criticism. Teachers could serve as local authorities in towns where other national institutions were distant. Schooling did not guarantee an occupation or eliminate class inequality, however, and the widening gap between educated aspirations and available positions became a source of political discontent. Educational policy should therefore be treated as a process that created new constituencies as well as new skills.

The University of the Philippines, founded in 1908, and other colleges helped produce lawyers, doctors, scientists, teachers, and government employees. Manila drew students from multiple provinces, placing regional communities in contact. English offered a language shared across some linguistic divisions, though it also created educational distinctions between those who mastered it and those who did not. Spanish continued to matter among older elites and in some legal and literary settings, while local Philippine languages remained central to everyday conversation, political persuasion, and religious life. The result was a multilingual public, not a seamless replacement of earlier cultural identities by American English.

The press provided the bridge between education and politics. National newspapers reported legislative arguments, congressional independence bills, rural disturbances, and the public careers of Quezon and Osmeña. Provincial papers and local-language publications translated those debates into regional contexts. Editors could support the Nacionalista Party, criticize colonial officials, or question whether the leadership in Manila represented peasants and workers. A newspaper's reach depended on circulation, literacy, and the communal practice of reading aloud. Its editorial voice is evidence of a particular political public, not proof that all residents of its circulation area agreed with it.

For women, educational opportunity opened new occupational and organizational pathways even before national enfranchisement. Female teachers, nurses, writers, and civic activists supported schools and campaigns for social welfare and voting rights. Access remained unequal by region and wealth, and some reformers framed women's advancement through expectations about motherhood and social service rather than identical roles in every profession. Women's associations nonetheless developed methods of petition, fundraising, journalism, and public persuasion that challenged the assumptions behind exclusion from electoral politics. Their later suffrage victory cannot be understood without these earlier institutional foundations.

The civil service expanded another kind of national experience. Clerks, inspectors, teachers, postal employees, and technical officials worked within standardized administrative structures, creating opportunities for stable salaries and social mobility. Employment rules and examinations could weaken certain forms of personal patronage while also privileging graduates from particular schools. Political leaders understood that control over appointments and budgets gave them power within a growing state. Workers outside salaried service observed these inequalities sharply. Urban clerical positions could appear secure beside the uncertainty of port employment or the vulnerability of agricultural tenancy.

Students also became critics of state policy. Debating associations, literary societies, newspapers, and public commemorations encouraged arguments about colonial authority and national culture. Some students embraced constitutional independence; others explored socialism, labor organization, or radical nationalist approaches. A college education did not produce a uniform political worldview, but it supplied contacts and organizational skills that could sustain movements beyond a single town. Educational expansion made both the governing elite and its challengers more capable of developing programs and addressing national audiences.

The continuing limitation was exclusion. Many rural children left school for work, schools differed widely in staffing and facilities, and geographic distance could make advanced education impossible. English literacy helped some Filipinos communicate with American officials but could also distance national politicians from constituents who used other languages. A graduate's ambition for civil-service employment was not the same as a farm household's demand for secure land. The political impact of schooling is best understood through these contrasts: it broadened public debate, produced organized reformers, and strengthened administrative capacity while reproducing social disparities that later opposition movements sought to change.

Everyday Life and Social History — School, Work, and Citizenship

A young woman training as a teacher, a male clerk preparing for civil-service examinations, and a rural child leaving school to help with harvest labor would experience educational expansion very differently. The expanding school system fostered literacy, professional careers, and political journalism, but it required household resources and often involved travel or a change of language.

Consider a small-town teacher who read independence debates in English, translated them for neighbors in a local language, and organized a civic meeting. Such an individual might strengthen the national public sphere without holding office. This is an illustrative reconstruction of social possibilities, not a claim about a documented person. Students should seek diaries, local newspapers, school reports, and memoirs to recover individual experiences.

Labor Organizations, Urban Politics, and the Boundaries of Colonial Reform

Port, transport, tobacco, and factory workers organized unions and strikes in Manila and other cities, facing policing and employers' resistance. Elected politicians offered some social legislation, but organized labor often pursued separate strategies, including socialist and later communist ones, showing the limits of elite-led reform.

Organized labor in the early twentieth-century Philippines developed within a colonial economy that offered new concentrations of workers but retained powerful employers and a state willing to police public order. Porters, printers, tramway employees, dockworkers, tobacco workers, and employees in small manufacturing and transport enterprises could assemble more readily than scattered rural tenants. Their wages depended on local markets, employers' commercial fortunes, and sometimes colonial government contracts. Where workers shared workplaces or occupational identities, mutual-aid societies and unions could transform individual complaints into collective demands for pay, shorter hours, safer conditions, or protection against arbitrary dismissal.

Early labor organizing cannot be described as a direct copy of American trade unionism. Philippine workers drew on mutual-aid customs, artisan associations, nationalist thought, and the organizational experience of newspapers and political clubs. Isabelo de los Reyes helped found the Unión Obrera Democrática in 1902, an early milestone, and later organizations differed over whether bargaining, strikes, political campaigning, or socialist ideas offered the best route to improvement. Some associations were short-lived or closely associated with particular leaders; others created enduring connections among workplaces. Nationalist language and class-based claims overlapped without always pointing toward the same policy.

Colonial authorities operated between competing objectives. They promoted orderly labor relations and sometimes studied industrial conditions, but police and courts could intervene against assemblies considered threatening. Employers argued that strikes disrupted transport, export trade, and confidence in commercial stability. Workers countered that there was little effective route to fair wages without organized pressure. The distinction between permission to form an association and the enforceable capacity to bargain was crucial. Legal recognition alone did not give workers control over hiring, safety standards, or the distribution of profits.

In Manila, journalists and lawyers brought labor questions into public debate. Strikes were reported as economic conflicts, disturbances of order, or expressions of nationalist grievance depending on the publication and the political sympathies of its editors. The same labor protest might involve workers seeking subsistence wages, organizers advocating wider social transformation, politicians seeking votes, and authorities worried about public security. A good source analysis must identify which voice is speaking and avoid treating a police report or union manifesto as a transparent description of every participant's motives. Women's industrial labor, including work in tobacco and service occupations, adds another dimension frequently obscured by male-centered political biographies.

Divisions within labor politics grew during the 1920s and 1930s. Crisanto Evangelista became a prominent figure in trade union and communist organization; the Partido Komunista ng Pilipinas was established in 1930. Socialist organizing in Pampanga under Pedro Abad Santos developed partly from agricultural struggles, showing that urban industrial politics and rural tenancy conflicts could be connected. Yet a union official in a Manila printing shop did not necessarily share the immediate priorities of a Pampanga share tenant or a Negros plantation laborer. The term working class covered varied workplaces, legal relations, and regional experiences.

Elected political elites responded unevenly. Some legislators championed mediation or protective measures, while others feared radical organization or depended on the support of landowners and industrial employers. Reform proposals required implementing agencies, funds, inspectors, and willingness to confront powerful local interests. Public declarations of social justice could consequently outrun administrative reality. The later Commonwealth built agencies and enacted legislation intended to address parts of the problem, but the contradiction between legal promise and everyday bargaining power persisted.

Labor history matters to the independence narrative because it expands the definition of politics. The question for workers was not only which government would possess sovereign powers after American departure; it was whether an independent state would recognize collective bargaining, enforce contracts fairly, improve public health and housing, and protect people whose only major asset was labor. Urban working people could celebrate national freedom while challenging leaders who expected wage questions to be postponed until after independence. Their organizational experience formed one of the foundations for the Socialist, Communist, and other popular political alternatives examined later in the chapter.

Historical Vocabulary I — Patronage, Caciquism, Tenancy, Labor Organization

Patronage is the use of appointments, favors, services, or material support to sustain political relationships. Caciquism describes the domination of local politics by influential bosses or families, a contested analytic term requiring concrete evidence rather than automatic application to every provincial leader.

Tenancy is the cultivation of another person’s land under rent or harvest-sharing arrangements; its economic burdens vary by crop and agreement. Labor organization refers to workers’ collective activity to negotiate wages, conditions, and employment security. These categories describe different power relations: a tenant could be both dependent on a landlord and engaged in political bargaining, while a union member might face wage-based rather than land-based conflict.

Independence Missions, Washington Politics, and the Struggle over Sovereignty

Filipino missions and Resident Commissioners lobbied Congress, while American sugar, dairy, and labor interests, and anti-Filipino immigration sentiment, increasingly supported ending free access. Arguments for independence therefore combined Filipino nationalism with American economic self-interest, a fact that shaped the terms eventually offered.

Philippine independence diplomacy operated at a distance. Manila's elected leaders could pass resolutions demanding sovereignty, but the legal power to change the islands' international status remained in Washington. Independence missions therefore became recurring political events. Delegates traveled across the Pacific, presented petitions to congressional committees, addressed civic and business audiences, and sought meetings with legislators whose immediate priorities often lay outside Asia. Each mission had a domestic audience as well. Politicians needed to demonstrate to Filipino voters that negotiation was producing progress, while rivals judged whether the leaders had made unnecessary compromises over trade or military rights.

The 1919 independence mission, led by Manuel Quezon and other prominent Filipinos, argued that the islands had developed institutions capable of self-government. Its logic drew strength from the Jones Act: if the United States had declared an intention to depart once a stable government existed, Filipino officials could point to functioning elections, legislatures, schools, courts, and civil administration. American responses nonetheless depended on changing administrations and congressional alignments. A friendly hearing was not equivalent to passage of an independence statute. The mismatch between a nationalist claim grounded in constitutional achievement and the fragmented structure of American lawmaking helps explain the long diplomatic interval before 1934.

Resident commissioners were crucial intermediaries. They served in the U.S. House of Representatives without the voting rights possessed by ordinary members, but could speak, lobby, and cultivate legislative allies. Their influence depended on committee relations, personal credibility, and the capacity to answer technical questions about tariffs, taxation, and defense. Osmeña, Quezon, and Manuel Roxas became associated with competing negotiating strategies. Congressional advocacy was also a domestic contest over who could best represent the Filipino nation. Political rivals accused one another of accepting weak terms or misrepresenting the strength of U.S. support; those accusations would become especially consequential when the first independence act was rejected.

American interests were divided. Some Democrats and anti-imperialists argued against permanent colonial possession. Naval planners and some strategic thinkers worried that abandoning the islands could create uncertainty in the western Pacific. American sugar and dairy producers criticized competition from Philippine imports, while restrictionist groups wanted firmer controls on Filipino immigration. Employers and merchants already invested in Philippine agriculture had reason to fear the loss of preferential treatment. The independence question therefore united people who did not share a moral or economic agenda. One supporter might see withdrawal as recognition of Filipino nationhood; another might welcome it because a new tariff barrier would protect American producers.

Filipino economic interests were similarly varied. Exporters wanted reliable access to the U.S. market; urban intellectuals sought political dignity and national institutions; labor activists hoped independence would enable stronger protections against employers; tenants were skeptical of any settlement that preserved existing land relations. Nationalist delegates tended to come from political and professional elites, and their bargaining priorities reflected that social position. Yet they operated under intense domestic scrutiny. An agreement that secured a date for independence but threatened sugar exports, or permitted American military reservations, could be celebrated as a breakthrough and condemned as a surrender of essential national interests.

The Depression intensified the political pressure to settle the issue. Falling prices, unemployment, and American demands for trade restrictions made the old colonial arrangement more difficult to defend. U.S. congressional committees considered measures that linked political freedom to quotas and changes in immigration status. Proposals no longer revolved solely around the philosophical question of whether the islands should eventually be independent. They also determined the division of future commercial risks. The irony was substantial: Philippine nationalism helped secure an independence timetable, but American economic protectionism helped make such a timetable politically possible.

The process was therefore not a straightforward diplomatic march from promise to fulfillment. It was bargaining between governments of unequal legal power, filtered through domestic rivalries, commodity markets, and changing ideas about imperial responsibility. The key analytical question for the next section is why an independence bill could pass the U.S. Congress, satisfy some negotiators, and still fail to win acceptance from the Philippine Legislature. The answer requires examining not only the language of sovereignty but the concrete terms concerning bases, trade, and political authority.

The sequence contains continuity, repeated negotiation, and rupture. Congress had legal authority to set conditions, but Filipino politicians had political incentives to accept or reject a proposed settlement. The date of each milestone is less significant on its own than the contrast between an undated conditional promise and a timetable reached through bargaining.

The Hare–Hawes–Cutting Act and the Tydings–McDuffie Settlement

Congress passed the Hare–Hawes–Cutting Act over President Hoover's veto in January 1933. The Philippine Legislature rejected it amid the Quezon–Osmeña–Roxas conflict and objections to its trade and military provisions. The Tydings–McDuffie Act of March 24, 1934, kept the ten-year Commonwealth framework, with independence set for July 4, 1946.

The Hare–Hawes–Cutting Act emerged from negotiations by Sergio Osmeña and Manuel Roxas and received congressional approval over President Herbert Hoover's veto in January 1933. It offered a defined path to independence after a ten-year Commonwealth period. This was a major change from the Jones Act's conditional promise, but the act also prescribed economic transition and American strategic reservations. Those conditions mattered because the future republic might receive formal sovereignty while retaining foreign military facilities and facing deteriorating access to the American market. The Philippine Legislature's decision whether to accept the law was therefore both constitutional and political.

Manuel Quezon opposed the measure and helped organize its rejection. His criticisms involved sovereignty, trade, and reservations, though rivalries with Osmeña and Roxas also influenced the struggle. The argument cannot be reduced simply to personal ambition, because the terms contained genuine controversies; neither can it be understood without competition for political leadership. An independence law that Osmeña and Roxas had negotiated could strengthen them at Quezon's expense. Rejecting it offered Quezon the opportunity to establish that he, rather than his rivals, would determine the acceptable terms of nationhood. The Philippine Legislature rejected Hare–Hawes–Cutting in October 1933.

The Tydings–McDuffie Act, enacted March 24, 1934, established a revised path. It provided for a constitutional convention, American approval of the drafted constitution, elections, a Commonwealth government, and recognition of independence after a ten-year period, on July 4, 1946. Importantly, the law did not simply announce independence and leave everything else to Filipino preference. It imposed conditions affecting foreign affairs, public debt, currency, immigration, and tariff relations. It also treated questions concerning American naval facilities differently from the rejected act. The settlement must be read carefully alongside Hare–Hawes–Cutting instead of described as the same instrument under a new name.

One difference concerned military reservations. Hare–Hawes–Cutting contained broad provisions for retaining American military and naval reservations. The later act addressed these matters in a revised fashion, including the prospect of negotiations over naval reservations rather than an unqualified permanent military presence in the same terms. American and Filipino sources sometimes emphasized different parts of the compromise. Congressional supporters could call it a reasonable adjustment; Filipino politicians could present the changes as the defense of national dignity. Historians should avoid suggesting that all base questions were settled permanently by the wording of the 1934 law.

Trade remained central. The transition was accompanied by restrictions on major commodities and plans to alter preferential relations after independence. Sugar producers, coconut exporters, and political leaders worried that the country's economic structure had become dependent on a market it could not control. The Philippine Independence Act thus bound together a promised constitutional transfer and economic changes likely to be uneven in effect. Exporters with large established businesses had different interests from tenants and wage laborers. Moreover, the same American legislators who supported withdrawal could insist on commercial rules favorable to U.S. producers. Independence was not simply the removal of an imperial administrator; it was the renegotiation of economic relationships built under empire.

The Philippine Legislature accepted the Tydings–McDuffie framework, and elections for a constitutional convention followed. Voters and political parties had to consider what a transitional government could accomplish. The law gave Filipino leaders substantial responsibility for constructing the institutions of the Commonwealth while retaining American supervision over their final constitutional form. The arrangement could foster genuine democratic practice, yet it also enabled the United States to approve or disapprove central elements of the political settlement. An independent constitution drafted under the supervision of a sovereign external power captures the ambiguity of the coming decade.

Historians disagree over how much of the 1933–1934 sequence was driven by principled objections and how much by Quezon's rivalry with Osmeña and Roxas. The evidence supports neither a purely heroic nor a purely cynical account. The compromises had real legal consequences, but the politicians who interpreted those consequences were also competing for the authority to lead the new Commonwealth. In public debate, the issue became whether the national interest was best served by rapid acceptance or by insisting upon revisions. For ordinary Filipinos, the remaining question was less abstract: would a ten-year delay and a new constitutional framework improve daily life, or merely transfer more responsibility to the same families and interests?

Peasant Grievances, Religious Movements, and Agrarian Resistance

Debt, tenancy, local politics, and religious expectation drove movements such as the 1924 Colorum uprising and the 1931 Tayug rebellion. These were not one ideology: millenarian belief, land disputes, and anti-elite anger could converge without being identical, and none can be reduced to communism.

The growth of electoral and constitutional nationalism did not absorb every form of rural political action. In the 1920s and early 1930s groups in several provinces challenged landlords, officials, and local authority through petitions, devotional meetings, collective refusals, and sometimes armed rebellion. Their aims cannot be inferred solely from slogans. A person might attend a religious gathering seeking healing or moral renewal, then join a protest over rent or taxation; another might invoke a promised new kingdom to express rejection of a local social order. The categories religious, agrarian, and nationalist describe overlapping dimensions of experience rather than neatly separated political parties.

The Colorum movements are a case in point. The term was applied by officials and observers to different associations with religious and millenarian features. Some cultivated expectations of miraculous protection or an approaching transformation of authority. In 1924 a series of uprisings revealed the danger of interpreting such movements only as curious beliefs detached from their social environment. Communities faced debt, perceived exploitation, unequal legal protection, and disputes over land or local officers. Religious promises could give courage to participants who otherwise had limited means of confronting armed police. But scholars must examine actual organizers, routes of mobilization, and local conditions before asserting that economic grievances caused every incident.

The 1931 Tayug rebellion in Pangasinan, associated with Pedro Calosa, further illustrates the difficulties. Calosa's following emerged among people dissatisfied with local power and attracted by a program that mixed opposition to established authority with symbols of social renewal. The event can be read in terms of peasant grievance, religious belief, political leadership, and the colonial policing of rural associations. None of these perspectives is sufficient by itself. A court record might prioritize criminal acts and official suspects, while an activist narrative might emphasize poverty and injustice. The historian's task is to compare them without assuming either is a complete account of participant motivation.

Land relations shaped the context. In rice-growing districts, harvest-sharing arrangements, labor obligations, and debts could leave families with few reserves. Tax collection and the conduct of constabulary officers or local politicians might be experienced as closely connected to landlord power. Changes in market prices could make tensions worse, particularly during the Depression. Yet many people experiencing hardship did not rebel, and different villages responded differently. Organization, personal trust, religious symbols, and expectations of success help explain why collective action emerged in certain places at particular times rather than everywhere at once.

The government's response exposed the limits of constitutional representation. Colonial and local authorities frequently interpreted rural uprisings as threats to order requiring police or constabulary intervention. Suppression could restore control while leaving economic complaints unresolved. Trials generated records that historians can use, but judicial documents were produced within the state's effort to assign responsibility and punishment. They are more reliable for identifying an official charge than for reconstructing every participant's worldview. Newspaper coverage also varied by political affiliation, urban readership, and access to the rural districts concerned. A complete history should place these sources in dialogue with scholarship on tenancy and local religious associations.

Popular protest also complicates a familiar nationalism narrative. The Nacionalista Party articulated a powerful claim to independence, but participants in rural movements could view the party as representing elites too closely connected to landlords and colonial institutions. They did not necessarily oppose independence; they disputed whether the government promised for the future would change the circumstances under which they lived. Rural activists could support immediate sovereignty while demanding land, debt relief, or administrative reform. Their political vision might be locally specific rather than fully described by an ideology developed in Manila or Washington.

These movements formed a context for the Sakdal challenge in 1935, but Sakdalism should not be treated as their automatic successor. The organizations, leaders, publications, and circumstances differed. What they reveal collectively is an enduring question about the foundations of citizenship. Was a Filipino's claim to political freedom satisfied by an elected national legislature and a scheduled date for independence, or did freedom also require limits on landlord power and meaningful protections in the village? The unresolved character of that question explains why agrarian protest belonged to the political history of independence rather than to a separate realm of rural disorder.

Benigno Ramos, the Sakdal Movement, and the Uprising of May 1935

Benigno Ramos's newspaper Sakdal built a following by demanding immediate independence and attacking elite rule, taxes, and landlordism. Sakdal candidates won office in 1934. On May 2–3, 1935, uprisings occurred in Laguna, Cavite, Bulacan, and nearby areas and were quickly suppressed. Participants' goals should not be conflated with Ramos's later choices, nor participation with party membership.

Benigno Ramos turned the word Sakdal, suggesting an accusation or appeal for redress, into the identity of a newspaper and political movement. His readers encountered criticism of American rule, the slow independence timetable, and the Filipino politicians who had made negotiated constitutional transition their principal achievement. The movement drew support among people who believed that sovereignty promised for a distant date would allow established landlords, creditors, and political dynasties to retain power in the meantime. Sakdal journalism articulated grievances that existing parties did not always address. Its appeal cannot be measured simply by the circulation of a paper, but neither can it be dismissed because much of its constituency was outside elite political networks.

The Sakdal Party contested elections and attracted supporters in towns and rural districts around Manila. Electoral victories and political organizing indicated that the movement was more than a spontaneous rebellion. The platform included demands for immediate independence and criticism of taxes and elite domination, though participants understood the program through their own circumstances. Some wanted relief from land or debt burdens, some resented municipal officials, and others were primarily animated by national dignity. Ramos's rhetoric and organizing methods helped connect these diverse expectations. Yet a party newspaper is an interested source: it describes what leaders wished followers to believe, not a complete record of individual motives.

The independence settlement of 1934 sharpened the conflict. Nacionalista leaders could claim a major diplomatic success: a constitution would be drafted and sovereignty transferred at the end of a ten-year transition. Sakdalists argued that ten additional years under American authority were unacceptable and questioned whether the chosen constitutional representatives would protect the poor. The movement therefore challenged the timetable and the social character of the coming Commonwealth. Its critique made a distinction that became central to Philippine politics: a government could be nationally led and still reproduce the economic hierarchy of the colonial period.

During the night of May 2–3, 1935, armed and partially armed groups associated with Sakdalism rose in towns principally around the Manila hinterland, including districts in Laguna, Cavite, and Bulacan. The government and constabulary suppressed the uprisings quickly, and many participants were killed or arrested. Published figures vary with sources and their definition of participants; historians should not equate party membership, sympathizers, and those who actually took part in a particular confrontation. Ramos himself was outside the country during the outbreak, a fact that complicates attempts to assign a single operational intention to everyone in the movement. The event occurred shortly before the May 14 constitutional plebiscite.

Officials portrayed the uprising as a threat to public peace; supporters interpreted it as resistance to an inadequate independence settlement and oppressive local conditions. Both accounts require scrutiny. Police reports were produced to justify coercion and prosecution. Sakdal claims could exaggerate support, simplify social divisions, or promise outcomes without a workable strategy for achieving them. Historians such as David Sturtevant and Motoe Terami-Wada have treated the movement in its wider ideological, electoral, and regional context, while more recent studies of radicalism reconsider its political arguments rather than reducing it to disorder. The surviving *Sakdal* newspaper provides a valuable counterweight to government descriptions when read critically.

The aftermath revealed both the power and limits of popular opposition. The uprising failed to displace colonial or elite authority and did not prevent the ratification of the Commonwealth Constitution. Yet its defeat did not erase the grievances it expressed. Agrarian inequality, weak safeguards for tenants, rural taxation, and distrust of politicians persisted. The Commonwealth government's subsequent emphasis on social justice must be interpreted partly in relation to such challenges. Reform could be a principled response to injustice and a strategy of managing discontent; those purposes were not mutually exclusive.

A serious account must also distinguish the movement of 1935 from Ramos's later political decisions. Some leaders' subsequent wartime alignments have shaped retrospective memory, but they do not establish that every person seeking immediate independence in 1935 endorsed later choices. Peasants and local supporters made decisions under the conditions known to them at the time. Sakdalism illustrates the central contradiction of the chapter: constitutional negotiations produced an independence date, while popular nationalism demanded to know who would benefit from the nation established under that agreement.

The Constitutional Convention and the Constitution of 1935

The 1934 convention, presided over by Claro M. Recto, drafted a constitution with a unicameral National Assembly, a six-year presidency, and a bill of rights. President Roosevelt approved it, and voters ratified it on May 14, 1935. Transitional provisions preserved U.S. authority over foreign affairs, defense, and trade.

The Constitutional Convention elected in 1934 was the place where the nationalist promise of self-government acquired an institutional design. Delegates came from different regions and political constituencies, although established elites remained influential. Claro M. Recto, chosen to preside over the convention, helped guide debates about executive and legislative powers, judicial independence, and the protection of liberties. The delegates did not work in a vacuum. The Tydings–McDuffie Act required a republican form of government, protection of individual rights, and American review before the document could take effect. The convention therefore combined genuine Filipino constitutional choice with constraints imposed by the sovereign colonial power.

The delegates faced several institutional options. Should the legislature consist of one chamber or two? How strong should the president be in relation to the assembly? What safeguards could prevent the political dominance of a single leader? How should courts defend constitutional rights in a society marked by concentrated wealth and unequal access to legal representation? The original document established a unicameral National Assembly and a president serving one six-year term without immediate reelection. It also provided for a Supreme Court, a bill of rights, and constitutional procedures for amendment. These choices reflected competing fears: legislative fragmentation on one side and excessive executive authority on the other.

The convention also confronted the problem of democratic participation. Suffrage rules built on earlier electoral qualifications, with women initially excluded unless a subsequent plebiscite met a specified threshold. Delegates could describe the constitution as an expression of national self-government while preserving restrictions affecting a large part of the adult population. Debates over representation were shaped by differences between Christian lowland political centers and other regions, including the administrative treatment of Muslim and indigenous communities. The document set national institutions in place, but it did not make social, linguistic, or political inequalities vanish by declaring a common citizenship.

One must also distinguish the constitution's original 1935 text from later amended or consolidated versions available in modern legal collections. The original contained a National Assembly rather than the later Senate and House, and a six-year presidency without reelection rather than the modified four-year term. Students using a legal website should check whether a printed provision reflects the amendments of 1940 or later constitutional changes. This is not a technical pedantry: confusing the versions erases the political struggle over executive tenure and legislative structure that became important before the war.

After the convention approved its draft in February 1935, President Franklin Roosevelt certified it in March. The Filipino electorate ratified the constitution in a plebiscite on May 14, 1935. These steps established a complicated chain of authority: Filipino delegates drafted; the U.S. president approved; qualified voters in the Philippines ratified. The process contained a real element of popular consent but also testified to the continuing hierarchy of sovereignty. American approval was necessary because the Commonwealth would be a government under U.S. authority until the scheduled date of independence.

The bill of rights offered important principles concerning speech, religion, due process, and property. Yet principles were filtered through the unequal conditions of daily life. A wealthy landlord could defend property in court with professional counsel, while a tenant might lack the resources to contest eviction. Newspapers could criticize officials, but the government might still employ security laws against movements characterized as subversive. Constitutional history thus requires two questions: what did the text formally establish, and how did people with different social resources experience its protections? The Sakdal uprising immediately preceding ratification illustrated the urgent nature of the second question.

The constitution gave national leaders a durable governmental framework with which to pursue independence. It also furnished new grounds for political opposition and constitutional dispute. The transition under American sovereignty meant that the Supreme Court, National Assembly, president, and executive agencies would develop capacities before full independence. But the question of whether constitutional self-government would become broadly democratic was left to political struggles after 1935. The next section considers the election and inauguration through which this institutional design became a functioning government.

The Commonwealth Elections and the Inauguration of Quezon and Osmeña

In September 1935 Manuel Quezon won the presidency and Sergio Osmeña the vice presidency, defeating Emilio Aguinaldo and Gregorio Aglipay among others. The Commonwealth was inaugurated on November 15, 1935. It symbolized self-government, yet a U.S. High Commissioner and Washington's reserved powers showed that sovereignty had not transferred.

The September 1935 election converted the constitutional transition into a contest for the leadership of the Commonwealth. Manuel Quezon stood as the leading Nacionalista candidate for president, with Sergio Osmeña as his running mate for vice president. Emilio Aguinaldo, associated with the revolutionary government at the turn of the century, and Gregorio Aglipay, the prominent religious reformer, also competed. The range of candidates carried historical symbolism. Revolutionary authority, religious-national leadership, and parliamentary organization all claimed a place in the political future, though the established party apparatus gave Quezon a powerful electoral advantage. The election was a real political event within the restrictions of the prevailing franchise, not a mere ceremonial appointment.

Quezon won decisively, while Osmeña became vice president. The results demonstrated Nacionalista organizational strength and the ability of its leaders to connect provincial political networks into a national campaign. They did not establish that every Filipino endorsed the program or that excluded social groups had been represented adequately. Many women could not yet vote, and the economic barriers facing rural and working-class people had not disappeared. Election returns can show who prevailed among eligible voters; they cannot, without further evidence, measure the attitudes of every adult resident of the archipelago.

On November 15, 1935, the Commonwealth government was inaugurated in Manila. The ceremony displayed new offices, constitutional symbols, and a Filipino head of state-like government. Quezon's presidency embodied decades of negotiation and was presented as evidence that Filipinos could manage national affairs. Yet the presence of an American high commissioner and the continuing role of the United States demonstrated the limits of the transition. Commonwealth agencies could undertake important domestic policies, but foreign affairs, ultimate sovereignty, and certain defense and economic matters remained dependent on Washington. Inauguration should therefore not be confused with the proclamation of an independent republic.

The new executive sought to make administration more systematic and visible. Cabinet officials, budget officers, provincial governments, and the National Assembly had to coordinate responsibilities under the new constitution. The transfer from colonial legislative arrangements to Commonwealth institutions involved continuity: many personnel had served within the American-era civil service, and much legislation remained in force. Public expectations nevertheless rose. The government now operated under a Filipino president whose promises could be judged directly by citizens and opposition movements rather than attributed to an American governor-general alone.

The appointment and authority of the American high commissioner illustrated a constitutional ambiguity. The office represented U.S. sovereignty during the transition and participated in matters governed by the independence legislation. Quezon could negotiate with American officials and present himself internationally as the leader of a nation moving toward independence, but he could not exercise every power of a president of a fully sovereign state. The Commonwealth's status can be described as autonomy within an imperial transition, though the practical scope of autonomy changed as political relationships developed. Students should distinguish the symbolism of the presidential inauguration from the legal status of the islands.

Political opposition remained significant despite the margin of victory. Aguinaldo and Aglipay appealed to traditions and constituencies not wholly contained within the Nacionalista establishment. Sakdal activists, recently suppressed, judged the electoral transition differently still. Newspapers debated leadership and the meaning of the new constitution, while farmers and workers asked whether policy would address their grievances. The government faced demands for social reform almost immediately. Its response could strengthen the claim that constitutional nationalism served the people or confirm the criticism that an elite had inherited more power without changing the economic order.

The 1935 election also made the eventual independent presidency a tangible political prize. Quezon and his associates began acting within a framework expected to lead to sovereignty in 1946, while rivals calculated their future positions. The institutional advantages of incumbency—access to public communication, appointments, budgets, and national administration—became more concentrated than under the former legislature. This success and danger would influence the constitutional amendments of 1940. The Commonwealth inauguration thus marks both a constitutional milestone and the beginning of new domestic struggles over how democratic leadership should be constrained.

Historical Vocabulary II — Commonwealth, Autonomy, Constitutionalism, Sovereignty

Commonwealth: The Philippine transitional government inaugurated in November 1935, possessing substantial domestic governing powers but not international sovereignty. Autonomy: The capacity to decide certain affairs within limits set by a superior government. Constitutionalism: The principle that governing institutions and public power are organized and restricted by constitutional rules.

Sovereignty: Ultimate legal authority over a territory’s external and internal status. In the Philippines, the United States continued to possess sovereignty until July 1946, despite Filipino elections, a president, and many domestic policies. Terms are used analytically; a formal title or patriotic ceremonial language cannot substitute for examining actual powers.

Agrarian Reform, Organized Labor, and Socialist and Communist Opposition

Quezon's social-justice program included tenancy and labor measures, but enforcement was weak. Pedro Abad Santos organized rural Socialists in Pampanga, and the Socialist and Communist parties merged in 1938. Reform commitments and implementation diverged, leaving tenants and workers dissatisfied.

Quezon's Commonwealth government repeatedly invoked social justice because the political order established in 1935 inherited profound inequalities. Landlords continued to exercise influence over tenants and local governments, and urban employers could resist organized labor. A president committed to social reform faced the challenge of converting public rhetoric into regulations that provincial authorities would enforce. A statute might establish standards for contracts, wages, or tenancy while leaving underfunded inspectors and local courts responsible for application. In places where officials were related to landowners, the costs of invoking the law could fall heavily on the very people it was intended to protect.

Agrarian legislation illustrated these limits. The Rice Share Tenancy Act of 1933, passed before the Commonwealth, sought to regulate contracts involving rice tenants. Commonwealth Act No. 178 of November 13, 1936, amended parts of that framework. The measures were meaningful in recognizing tenant grievances as questions for public law, but broad reform remained difficult because land redistribution and enforcement confronted powerful interests. Farmers might know that a tenancy statute existed yet still depend on a landlord for seed, credit, work animals, and the ability to remain on the farm. Formal contractual protections did not erase unequal bargaining power.

Pedro Abad Santos emerged as a significant figure in organizing socialists in Pampanga. His Socialist Party drew support from tenants and rural laborers who demanded changes in land relations and political representation, not merely patriotic declarations about independence. The Communist Party of the Philippines, established in 1930 with Crisanto Evangelista among its leading figures, developed different organizational networks, with an important base in labor politics. Government prosecution and restrictions shaped its activity. The Socialist and Communist parties merged in 1938, marking a new alignment of rural and urban radical politics, though their local constituencies and traditions remained distinct.

Workers' organizations called attention to wages, job security, social insurance, and employer authority. Some labor leaders cooperated with official mediation; others challenged employers more directly through collective action. The Commonwealth could enact protective laws and expand agencies, but administrative solutions required resources, inspection, and willingness to confront business interests. A government associated with elite nationalism might simultaneously welcome labor legislation and fear political organizations it considered revolutionary. Such combinations of reform and coercion are historically ordinary but analytically important: they show that state policy cannot be characterized adequately as entirely progressive or wholly repressive.

Agrarian opposition was not politically uniform. Some farmers remained loyal to Nacionalista politicians who could provide roads, schools, or legal assistance. Other villagers supported Socialist or Communist organizers because local landlords blocked reform. Still others remembered Sakdalism or participated in religious associations. Electoral behavior, petitions, cooperative societies, and strikes were all possible forms of agency. Historians should avoid treating membership in an organization as proof that every participant understood the same theory of class struggle. For many people, the central issue was practical security: the right to cultivate land, negotiate a harvest share, or avoid retaliation for collective complaint.

The Commonwealth's attempts at land settlement also raised difficult questions about territory and national belonging. Opening agricultural land in Mindanao could be presented as a means of relieving pressure in densely cultivated provinces, but such policies affected indigenous communities with existing claims to land and local authority. Settlement was not automatically a neutral solution to agrarian inequality; it could redistribute tensions geographically and deepen conflicts in places governed through special arrangements. Section 131.18 examines that problem in more detail. In the meantime, it demonstrates why a domestic reform must be evaluated beyond the immediate constituency that requested it.

Social justice remained a test of Commonwealth legitimacy. The government could point to new policy instruments and to constitutional procedures for addressing grievances, while opposition movements highlighted the distance between promise and enforcement. These developments continued a longer history of inequality but also changed the arena in which demands were made. A Filipino president and National Assembly were now publicly responsible for the outcome. Political independence was approaching, yet the social meaning of freedom remained contested. That unresolved tension linked everyday agrarian and labor struggles to the larger constitutional history of the nation.

Continuities: Agricultural tenancy, local patronage, uneven enforcement of legal safeguards, and the power of export industries continued after inauguration of the Commonwealth. Peasant and worker organizers still challenged established elites and demanded economic protections.

Changes: Filipino executive leaders now carried greater direct responsibility for reforms, and the Commonwealth enacted or amended tenancy measures, expanded administration, and negotiated with organized labor. Socialist and Communist networks developed new forms of coalition, including the 1938 merger, while opposition sought ways to use and challenge Commonwealth institutions. The transition altered the public arena in which grievances were addressed without resolving every grievance.

Women, Suffrage, and the Expansion of Filipino Political Citizenship

Women's organizations campaigned for decades. The Constitution allowed suffrage if 300,000 women voted yes in a plebiscite; Commonwealth Act No. 34 arranged it. On April 30, 1937, the vote was 447,725 to 44,307. Formal rights did not remove educational, economic, and institutional barriers.

The women's suffrage victory of 1937 resulted from decades of organization rather than an automatic decision by constitutional delegates. Early reformers such as Concepción Félix and Pura Villanueva Kalaw created associations that promoted civic education, social welfare, and women's participation in public affairs. Teachers, writers, professional women, and local clubs developed practical skills through fundraising, public lectures, petitions, and newspaper campaigns. These efforts challenged the assumption that citizenship should be exercised politically only by men while retaining varied views about marriage, work, religion, and family life. Women in the movement did not share a single class position or a complete agreement on the future of Philippine society.

Early campaigns confronted legal barriers and social arguments against enfranchisement. Critics questioned whether women possessed sufficient political education or whether voting would disrupt established family responsibilities. Suffragists responded by pointing to women's public service, education, economic contributions, and capacity for independent judgment. Their arguments sometimes appealed to maternal responsibilities as reasons women would improve politics, and sometimes asserted equal citizenship more directly. Both forms of advocacy mattered. It would be misleading to treat the movement as a simple imitation of American feminism or as a project detached from Philippine organizations and local reform traditions.

The constitutional settlement created a distinctive test. Article V of the original 1935 Constitution allowed women's suffrage if, in a plebiscite held within the prescribed period, at least 300,000 qualified women voted affirmatively. This was not a simple majority threshold alone. Women had to organize sufficient affirmative participation under existing eligibility rules, which required literacy and residence qualifications. Commonwealth Act No. 34, approved September 30, 1936, set out the procedures for the vote, registration, and ballots. The fact that women had to vote on whether their sex should receive the franchise was itself a striking expression of a political system in transition.

Campaigners responded with intense organization. Women's clubs, professional associations, and civic leaders distributed information, gave public speeches, and encouraged qualified women to register. The National Federation of Women's Clubs and other organizations, with support from figures such as Aurora Aragon Quezon, helped publicize the importance of an affirmative vote. Local organizers had to reach people who had never participated in national elections, explain registration procedures, and counter opposition in communities with varying levels of schooling. The campaign was therefore also an exercise in building the practical machinery of democratic participation.

On April 30, 1937, the plebiscite produced 447,725 affirmative votes and 44,307 negative votes, comfortably surpassing the constitutional condition. The achievement changed the formal definition of the electorate and demonstrated the power of associations operating outside the old male-dominated party structure. It did not mean that all adult women could immediately vote under identical conditions, because age, literacy, residence, and other legal qualifications continued to matter. Nor did it guarantee equal access to political nominations, campaign finance, or government office. Formal enfranchisement was an essential gain with social barriers still to overcome.

The story also illuminates a broader historiographical problem. Narratives centered on the male independence negotiators may give the impression that political rights expanded automatically as constitutional self-government developed. In reality, organized women fought for a legal change whose result was not predetermined. Their organizations created alliances, pressed political leaders, and mobilized a constituency that had previously lacked the ballot. The suffrage campaign is thus part of the history of Filipino self-government, not a detachable social achievement appended to a political biography of Quezon.

The significance of 1937 reaches into the chapter's central theme. The Commonwealth established a new political community under continuing U.S. sovereignty, and women challenged the boundaries of that community from within. Their victory shows the possibilities opened by constitutional reform and the continuing work required to make its promises meaningful. A republic's independence can be a matter of international recognition; democratic citizenship requires a continuing struggle over who may participate, what rights they possess, and whether those rights are effective in ordinary life.

Commonwealth Nation-Building, Social Policy, and the National Language

The Commonwealth expanded administration, education, and social programs. The Institute of National Language recommended Tagalog, and Executive Order No. 134 (December 13, 1937) adopted it as the basis of a national language, a unifying measure that raised questions for other language communities.

The Commonwealth sought to make Filipino self-government visible through administrative reform, economic planning, welfare initiatives, education, and symbols of national identity. These efforts mattered because independence was not solely a legal transfer scheduled for 1946. A government expected to assume sovereignty needed institutions able to coordinate an archipelago with varied economies, languages, and political loyalties. Quezon's administration inherited much of the colonial civil service and legal system, but sought to present public policy as the work of a national government answerable to Filipinos. The new symbolism could build loyalty, yet it also encouraged citizens to judge the government by results instead of by its promise of future independence.

Public administration involved practical problems that constitutional rhetoric could not solve. Provincial officials managed schools, public health, roads, irrigation, and local budgets, often with uneven staffing and funds. Rural communities differed in their ability to reach clinics or secondary schools, and storms and crop failures could overwhelm local resources. Central policy might seek uniform standards, while local needs demanded adaptation. The existence of offices in Manila was not proof of effective governance everywhere. A substantial textbook must examine how programs were financed, implemented, and contested, rather than assuming that declaring social justice produced equal conditions across the islands.

Language became a central concern. The country contained major speech communities using Tagalog, Cebuano, Ilocano, Hiligaynon, Bikol, Waray, Kapampangan, Pangasinan, and many other languages. English had expanded through schools and government, while Spanish remained influential among segments of the educated elite. Constitutional leaders sought a common national language as an expression of unity and cultural independence. Yet selecting one language as the foundation of a national standard risked privileging a particular region and literary tradition. Arguments for a common language could invoke administrative efficiency, national identity, and resistance to colonial cultural dependence, while critics raised questions of regional equality.

Commonwealth Act No. 184 established the Institute of National Language in 1936. After considering alternatives, the Institute recommended Tagalog as the basis for development of a national language, and Executive Order No. 134 formally proclaimed that choice in December 1937. A documentary caution is necessary: some legal indexes assign December 13 to the executive order, whereas its reproduced closing attestation names December 30. For this reason the chapter identifies the event as December 1937 rather than falsely treating one indexing convention as uncontroversial. The act of selection did not mean that English ceased immediately to be used for administration or that all regional languages disappeared from education.

Language policy illustrates the difficulty of constructing common citizenship through national institutions. A Tagalog-speaking teacher might welcome the prospect of a familiar linguistic base for a national literature. A Cebuano or Ilocano speaker could support independence while worrying about the relative status of a regional language. Muslim and indigenous communities might face still different issues when the state promoted schooling and administration in languages not used at home. These reactions cannot be attributed uniformly to whole populations, but they reveal why symbolic nation-building is a political process rather than a neutral technical decision.

Social programs and national identity were intertwined. Policies concerning schools, hygiene, social services, and public employment conveyed the idea that the Commonwealth would improve the lives of citizens; public ceremonies and the press represented the new state as a Filipino undertaking. Programs addressed genuine needs while also strengthening the legitimacy of Quezon's administration. Local political families sometimes mediated access to benefits, and opposition organizers criticized the distance between official projects and village conditions. Nation-building could widen political membership and reinforce particular forms of executive and bureaucratic authority simultaneously.

This record should not be evaluated by measuring how closely prewar policy matched the later independent republic. The people making decisions in the late 1930s faced uncertain trade relations, regional loyalties, political opposition, and increasing anxiety about Japan. They had to construct public institutions before they controlled the country's foreign relations or strategic defense. The Commonwealth's achievement was to make state-building a domestic political responsibility; its limitation was that a language decree, a school system, or a development plan could not by itself resolve the underlying distribution of power among regions, classes, and communities.

Division 5 · Section 131.18

Muslim Mindanao, Indigenous Communities, and the Boundaries of Filipino Nationhood

Moro and Cordillera communities faced settlement policies, land-registration systems, and new administration. Many leaders negotiated, petitioned, or resisted rather than passively accepting integration; their priorities included land, customary authority, and religion, and they did not all respond alike.

The phrase 'Filipino nation' did not have the same political meaning to all inhabitants of the archipelago. In Manila and many Christian lowland provinces, parties presented constitutional independence as the restoration of a common right to sovereignty. In Muslim communities of Mindanao and Sulu, some leaders worried that the transfer of authority to a predominantly Christian national government would weaken religious institutions, customary law, and local control over land. Many indigenous peoples of the Cordillera and the southern islands had experienced colonial rule through distinctive administrative categories and missionary or military arrangements. Their political histories cannot be written as passive reception of a uniform national project.

American rule had replaced earlier Spanish frontiers with new institutions. The Moro Province and later the Department of Mindanao and Sulu were designed in part to extend government authority over territories that had not been governed like the Christian lowlands. Political arrangements involved local leaders, religious officials, appointed administrators, and military or constabulary forces. Some communities negotiated for schools, roads, or more effective protection; others resisted taxation, interference with custom, or encroachment on land. Regional politics were complex, and neither 'Muslim' nor 'indigenous' described a single organization speaking for everyone.

The approach of Philippine independence intensified fears among certain Muslim leaders. In 1934 Maranao figures petitioned the American governor-general for protections concerning Islam and customary practices. In March 1935 another meeting at Dansalan in Lanao produced a petition associated with Maranao leaders who questioned incorporation into an independent Philippines under a Christian-majority government. The documents should be analyzed in their own context: some petitioners favored continuing an American relationship rather than joining the anticipated national state. This did not mean that all Muslim Filipinos rejected independence or shared the same institutional preferences. It did demonstrate that sovereignty was contested within the territory, not simply between Manila and Washington.

Land policy sharpened the problem. Legal surveys and public-land classifications sometimes failed to recognize customary or communal uses of territory as full property rights. Settlers from other Philippine regions sought farms, while colonial and Commonwealth officials presented development as a route to increasing agricultural production and relieving land scarcity. Such policies could place new households in direct competition with communities already occupying or using the land. Land could be described officially as 'public' or 'available' while local residents regarded it as inherited territory essential to farming, grazing, fishing, or ritual life. The language of empty land thus carried political consequences.

The Commonwealth institutionalized large-scale resettlement. Commonwealth Act No. 441 of June 3, 1939, created the National Land Settlement Administration, which supported schemes in regions including the Koronadal and Allah valleys of Cotabato. Administrators emphasized agricultural opportunity and security, including concerns about the country's strategic vulnerability. Scholars have shown that government-assisted settlement increased pressure on Muslim and indigenous landholding patterns. A social reform intended to give landless migrants new opportunities could, without adequate safeguards, deepen dispossession elsewhere. This example complicates any claim that a national redistribution policy was automatically fair or nationally inclusive.

The Cordillera presents different questions. Igorot and other highland communities had diverse customary institutions and encountered American schools, roads, public health campaigns, and officials who often categorized them as separate 'non-Christian tribes.' Some leaders used administrative channels to obtain resources or defend local authority; others challenged intrusion into land and customary life. Such policies could expand communication with national institutions while also reinforcing stereotypes that residents needed special tutelage. A nationally elected legislature's claim to represent the archipelago did not eliminate the need to investigate how different communities experienced its reach.

The central issue is not whether the Philippines ought to have had a common government, but how that government defined legitimate membership and responded to differing claims. Petitions from Muslim leaders, customary land arrangements, settlement conflicts, and language policy provide evidence of political agency that conventional Manila-centered narratives often miss. The Commonwealth could promise equal citizenship while pursuing programs that favored particular constituencies or assumptions about modernization. Its record should be interpreted through the perspectives of those whose consent was contested, not simply through national laws or the statements of the presidential administration.

Historical Debate II — Integration and the Claims of Muslim and Indigenous Communities

Integration argument: A shared constitutional state could provide common citizenship, schools, transport, and administrative services to regions previously ruled differently. Proponents saw settlement and uniform administration as necessary to nation-building.

Critical argument: Some Muslim leaders petitioned against automatic incorporation without guarantees for Islam and customary authority; indigenous communities faced land classification and settler schemes affecting existing claims. The 1935 Dansalan petition and 1939 settlement legislation are concrete evidence that the territorial reach of national policy was disputed.

Interpretive caution: Communities were internally diverse, and some residents pursued negotiated participation. A debate framed simply as modern nation against traditional resistance erases those choices.

Division 5 · Section 131.19

The Constitutional Amendments of 1940, Electoral Competition, and Executive Power

The 1940 amendments restored a bicameral legislature, set a four-year presidency with one reelection, and established the Commission on Elections. Quezon was reelected in November 1941, with Nacionalista dominance leaving limited effective opposition.

The original Commonwealth constitution established a single-chamber National Assembly and limited the president to one six-year term without immediate reelection. By 1940 that arrangement had become the subject of serious political reconsideration. Supporters of change argued that a bicameral legislature could provide additional scrutiny and that four-year presidential terms would make leaders more regularly accountable to voters. Critics questioned whether allowing reelection would consolidate executive power around Quezon and the Nacionalista establishment. The constitutional debate involved institutional design and the incumbent's political interests at the same time; neither aspect should be treated as irrelevant.

The National Assembly approved a package of constitutional amendments that voters ratified on June 18, 1940, with subsequent American approval under the transition framework. The changes restored an elected Senate alongside a House of Representatives, creating a bicameral Congress. They replaced the six-year nonrenewable presidential term with a four-year term permitting one reelection, subject to a limit of two consecutive terms. They also established the Commission on Elections to supervise electoral processes. These provisions altered the formal structure of the Commonwealth at a moment when the country was expected to prepare for sovereignty but remained constitutionally dependent on the United States.

Supporters of the Senate argued that a second chamber could represent national perspectives and check hurried or narrowly local legislation. Skeptics noted that upper chambers in a system dominated by established families might provide another arena for elite bargaining. The National Assembly had accumulated experience in making laws, but some critics saw its relative concentration of authority as a danger. Reform thus reflected a real constitutional issue. The result should be evaluated in terms of powers, elections, and legislative procedures, rather than interpreted solely as a partisan maneuver.

Presidential reelection attracted especially strong interest. Quezon had led the independence negotiations and possessed the prestige of the Commonwealth's founding presidency. Allowing him to seek another term could offer continuity during an increasingly dangerous international period. It could also reinforce the political advantages of incumbency through appointments, public spending, party leadership, and the ability to communicate national policy. The amendment was therefore defended through arguments about democratic choice and continuity, while exposing tension between an established leader's authority and the limits required for accountable government.

The new Commission on Elections was designed to strengthen supervision of electoral administration. Its creation acknowledged that confidence in elections required more than periodic voting; impartial procedures, registration, counting, and dispute resolution mattered. Yet an electoral commission did not automatically erase the unequal influence of money, landholding, and local political organizations. National laws and independent institutions could improve the formal process while political families continued to control the resources necessary for campaigning. The difference between electoral administration and political equality remains important for interpreting the first Commonwealth elections and the later presidential contest.

In November 1941 Quezon won reelection against Juan Sumulong, the principal opposition candidate, while Osmeña retained the vice presidency. The campaign occurred under the shadow of expanding Japanese power in East and Southeast Asia. It affirmed constitutional continuity, but did not settle questions of agrarian reform or the balance between president and legislature. Political competition remained possible, though the Nacionalista Party possessed powerful organizational advantages. A narrative centered entirely on electoral results would miss the broader social constraints and rising external threat that shaped the final months of the prewar Commonwealth.

The 1940 constitutional changes show that the Commonwealth was not static. Its leaders revised institutions in response to political concerns and ambitions, demonstrating genuine capacity for constitutional action. At the same time, U.S. approval remained part of the legal process, confirming that Philippine sovereignty had not yet been transferred. The political system was becoming more elaborate and more visibly Filipino, but questions about executive concentration, elite dominance, and effective popular representation persisted. Its final prewar test would come from a threat that parliamentary design alone could not meet: the approach of a wider Pacific war.

National Defense, American Sovereignty, and the Philippines on the Eve of War

The National Defense Act of December 21, 1935, created a Commonwealth army, with Douglas MacArthur as military adviser. Preparations were incomplete as Japanese expansion advanced. Japan attacked in December 1941; invasion and occupation belong to Chapter 137.

Commonwealth leaders faced an especially demanding defense problem: they were preparing a future independent state while the United States still controlled the country's international position and bore responsibility for major strategic decisions. Japanese military expansion in China and the western Pacific increased fears that the Philippine Islands could become a target. The archipelago had long coastlines, dispersed islands, vital ports, and a limited industrial base. No constitutional declaration could by itself supply ships, aircraft, artillery, communications systems, or trained personnel. National defense therefore became both an institutional project and a measure of the limits of Commonwealth autonomy.

Commonwealth Act No. 1, the National Defense Act of December 21, 1935, established a framework for a Philippine Army with regular and reserve components. The government intended to use training and mobilization to build a force capable of defending the islands after independence. Douglas MacArthur served as military adviser, drawing on American military experience and working with Filipino officers. The program's ambitions were considerable, but equipment, funding, training facilities, and the challenge of coordinating forces across many islands placed major constraints on implementation. Building a defense institution required years of administration and logistics; the calendar of Japanese expansion did not wait for those plans to mature.

Military planning raised difficult questions about sovereignty. Philippine officials sought a force identified with their own government, while American military policy still treated the islands as strategically linked to U.S. interests. Decisions about naval bases, weapons procurement, and wartime command structures were therefore entangled with the broader relationship between the two governments. The Tydings–McDuffie framework had promised a transfer of sovereignty, yet the Pacific security environment gave American commanders and policymakers strong reasons to maintain influence. The Commonwealth could legislate a national army but could not determine American strategy independently.

Economic dependence constrained defense as well. The Philippine economy remained oriented toward agricultural exports and trade with the United States, and the resources available for military investment were finite. Money spent on equipment competed with demands for schools, public health, agricultural support, infrastructure, and relief. Quezon's administration also faced expectations that it would reduce inequality and modernize public services. Choosing defense priorities was therefore not a matter of military expertise alone; it involved political judgments about which needs could be postponed, which risks were credible, and how much help the United States would provide.

The military program cannot be judged solely by hindsight. In 1935 the timetable for independence extended a decade into the future, leaving room for plans based on gradual training and institution-building. By 1940–1941 that horizon appeared more fragile. Japan's war in China, conflict over Asian resources, and worsening relations with the United States increased the danger of an immediate crisis. American military measures expanded, and in July 1941 President Franklin Roosevelt ordered the mobilization of organized military forces of the Commonwealth into the service of the U.S. armed forces in the Far East. Command and legal authority became still more entangled as war approached.

The human implications were considerable. Recruits and reservists entered a force whose training and equipment varied, while civilian families were asked to trust that authorities could protect towns, ports, crops, and communication routes. Schools, offices, and newspapers discussed national defense as a duty linked to eventual independence. Yet the risks were uneven: people living near strategic installations or major cities faced different immediate concerns from distant farming communities. Military history should not erase the ordinary social world that an army was expected to defend. Nor should it treat the approaching Japanese invasion as inevitable from the viewpoint of every Filipino in the 1930s.

In December 1941 war abruptly transformed the Commonwealth's circumstances. The Japanese attack on the Philippines began on December 8 local time, following the wider outbreak of hostilities across the Pacific. The invasion, occupation, collaboration, resistance, and destructive struggle for liberation are the subject of Chapter 137. Chapter 131 ends instead at the threshold, where its main argument becomes clear. Filipinos had gained an elected government, a constitution, expanding citizenship, and a legally scheduled date for independence; but land inequality, elite domination, economic dependence, contested national inclusion, and limited defense capacity remained unresolved under continuing American sovereignty. The Commonwealth was an important political achievement without being a completed national settlement.

Historical Debate III — Independence, State Capacity, and Defense in 1941

Institutional-achievement interpretation: By 1941 Filipinos had conducted national elections, written a constitution, expanded women’s suffrage, and established a domestic government and national defense framework. These provided a strong foundation for eventual sovereignty.

Limits-of-transition interpretation: U.S. control over key external matters, dependence on American markets, unequal access to land, contested inclusion in parts of the archipelago, and inadequate military resources made readiness uncertain. The Japanese attack demonstrated vulnerabilities that no political declaration could immediately remedy.

Balanced assessment: Preparedness should be evaluated using institutions, resources, and contemporary information. Do not confuse the later military catastrophe with proof that all prewar choices were irrational or that a particular result was inevitable.

Chapter Summary

American rule generated the institutions through which Filipino nationalism increasingly pursued independence. The civil government took form in 1901, before Congress enacted the Organic Act of 1902, and municipal elections and the Philippine Assembly of 1907 provided arenas for political representation. These institutions were created under American sovereignty and restricted suffrage, so greater Filipino participation did not mean that Filipinos could determine the colony’s international position or appoint every leading official.

The Nacionalista Party and leaders such as Sergio Osmeña and Manuel Quezon turned legislative organization and overseas lobbying into a durable national strategy. The Jones Act of 1916 strengthened the elected legislature and declared a conditional American intention to withdraw sovereignty, but without a fixed timetable. Nationalist politicians expanded constitutional experience while preserving electoral advantages inherited from provincial social hierarchies.

Preferential trade legislation in 1909 and 1913 drew Philippine export sectors more firmly into the American market. Sugar, coconut products, abaca, and other commodities generated substantial business opportunities, but gains varied sharply among mill owners, landholders, merchants, tenants, and workers. Trade dependency mattered when American commercial groups helped shape the conditions of independence legislation and Philippine exporters sought to preserve access to the market.

Landownership and tenancy continued to structure rural politics. Rice share agreements, sugar estates, credit relationships, and uneven enforcement of land rights made economic opportunity unequal across regions. The expanding school system and civil service created new social groups with political ambitions, while union organizers and rural activists challenged employers and local elites outside the established national parties.

Filipino delegations repeatedly argued in Washington that the Jones Act promise should be fulfilled. The Hare–Hawes–Cutting Act of 1933 offered a ten-year transition but was rejected in Manila amid disputes about bases, trade, and political leadership. The revised Tydings–McDuffie Act of 1934 established the accepted constitutional route to a Commonwealth and recognition of independence in 1946.

Popular movements challenged the assumption that constitutional agreement would solve social problems. The Colorum and Tayug rebellions combined religious, agrarian, and political grievances in differing ways. Benigno Ramos’s Sakdal movement rejected the decade-long delay and criticized elite control. The May 1935 uprising was suppressed, but the inequalities and competing visions of independence expressed by many participants persisted.

The 1935 Constitution established a Filipino presidential government and unicameral legislature within the American transition. Quezon and Osmeña won the Commonwealth elections, and the government was inaugurated on November 15, 1935. Filipino autonomy grew substantially, but foreign affairs and ultimate sovereignty remained with the United States. Later constitutional amendments in 1940 restored the Senate, revised presidential tenure, and strengthened election administration.

Citizenship and inclusion were continually renegotiated. Women’s organizations helped secure the 1937 plebiscite that enfranchised qualified women. Commonwealth social-justice programs addressed labor and tenancy while confronting powerful local interests; Socialist and Communist organizers offered alternative programs. Language policy selected Tagalog as the basis of a national language, and Mindanao settlement schemes generated questions about land rights and the political claims of Muslim and indigenous communities.

By late 1941 the Commonwealth had important national institutions and an independence timetable but continued to face inequality, trade dependence, contested membership, and inadequate defense resources. The approaching Japanese invasion exposed vulnerabilities without making the preceding political achievements meaningless. Independence remained a project whose constitutional form was increasingly defined but whose social and strategic meaning was unresolved.

Chapter Reflection

The Philippine Commonwealth represents a major constitutional achievement achieved under conditions of unequal power. Filipino politicians developed national institutions and secured an independence timetable, yet the United States retained ultimate authority over international status. The formal transfer of responsibility to Filipino leaders placed new demands on them without dissolving the social hierarchies through which government had long operated.

The experience of tenants, industrial workers, women, and political communities outside the dominant Christian lowland centers broadens the meaning of national independence. Each raised questions that could not be answered simply by fixing a future date for sovereignty. A tenant might ask whether an independent government would protect an equitable harvest share; a woman organizer might ask who could vote; a Maranao petitioner might ask whether the new political community would respect religious and customary authority.

These questions did not undermine the significance of the Commonwealth’s achievements. Instead, they reveal the difficult work of state-building and citizenship in a changing society. Constitutional laws, political campaigns, and social movements should be studied together, because their interaction produced both reform and conflict. Historical actors did not possess a settled blueprint for the independent republic, and many priorities competed for resources and legitimacy.

In 1941 the Commonwealth stood at the threshold of an external crisis that would disrupt its expectations. A responsible account neither praises the period as inevitable progress nor judges every decision solely through knowledge of the invasion. Its lasting historical interest lies in the coexistence of substantial Filipino political agency, continuing imperial constraints, and social conflicts over what freedom was supposed to accomplish.
