Indonesia under Japanese Occupation

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Between January 1942 and August 1945 the archipelago that Europeans called the Netherlands East Indies passed through the most violent and politically consequential three and a half years of the colonial era. Japan destroyed Dutch rule in a matter of weeks, governed the islands through three separate military administrations, drew heavily on the labor, rice, and raw materials of Indonesian society, and, as its war effort failed, allowed nationalist leaders a degree of organization and public voice that the Dutch had long denied them.

In March 1942 a colonial order that had taken more than three centuries to assemble, from the first trading posts of the Dutch East India Company to the vast territorial state of the early twentieth century, ceased to function in the space of a few weeks. The speed of the collapse is itself a central historical fact. The Dutch administration, the colonial army, the European civil service, and the plantation and oil companies that had underpinned the colony's wealth were swept away across an archipelago stretching some five thousand kilometers from Sumatra to the Moluccas. Millions of Indonesians who had been told that Dutch power was permanent watched Europeans marched into internment camps. What replaced that power was not liberation, but a new and in many ways harsher form of foreign rule.

This chapter studies that rule and its contradictory legacy. Japanese authorities came to the archipelago with strategic aims that had little to do with Indonesian welfare. They wanted petroleum, rubber, tin, rice, and labor for a war economy, and they wanted order behind the front. To obtain these things they dismantled much of the Dutch political superstructure, but they also relied on Indonesian administrators, religious leaders, and nationalist politicians whose cooperation made the occupation workable. The same authorities that imposed rice requisitions and conscripted laborers also released Sukarno and Mohammad Hatta from Dutch control, promoted the Indonesian language in public life, and trained tens of thousands of young Indonesians in military and paramilitary organizations.

The chapter's central problem is therefore the relationship between Japanese imperial exploitation and the strengthening of Indonesian nationalism. Two simple interpretations must be avoided. One holds that Japan deliberately liberated Indonesia from the Dutch; the other holds that Indonesian independence in 1945 was essentially a Japanese creation. Neither is supported by the evidence. Japanese policy was driven by military necessity and was repeatedly cautious, reversible, and contradictory. Yet Indonesian nationalists were not passive instruments either. They used the organizations, the platforms, the language policies, and the military training that the occupation made available, and they pursued objectives that were not those of their Japanese sponsors.

A second caution concerns the unit of analysis. There was no single "occupied Indonesia." Japan divided the former colony among the Sixteenth Army on Java and Madura, the Twenty-Fifth Army on Sumatra, and the Navy in the eastern archipelago. These jurisdictions pursued different priorities, permitted different degrees of political activity, and imposed different burdens. Experiences in Java, where the most elaborate nationalist mobilization took place, were not typical of West Kalimantan, where Japanese forces destroyed much of the local elite in 1943–1944, or of Sulawesi and the Moluccas under naval rule. Much of the historical literature is weighted toward Java, and this chapter tries to correct that tilt without pretending that the evidence is evenly distributed.

The chapter draws on several kinds of evidence, each with limits. Japanese military and administrative records survive unevenly and reflect official purposes. Indonesian nationalist writings, memoirs, and speeches, including those of Sukarno and Hatta, were composed under occupation censorship or afterward in the service of political memory. Dutch colonial and postwar investigative materials often emphasize European internment and Dutch suffering. Oral testimonies, notably from former romusha laborers, supply perspectives absent from official files, but are fragmentary and recorded long after the events. Modern scholarship has used all of these, together with agricultural and demographic data, to reassess the occupation's costs. Wherever casualty figures are given, they are estimates, and the chapter says so.

Chapter 138 fits within a division of labor across the textbook. Prewar nationalist organizations and ideologies belong to Chapter 126, and the Depression to Chapter 133. The comparative histories of coerced labor, resistance, and wartime atrocities appear in Chapters 141 to 143, and the Allied return and Japanese surrender in Chapter 144. The detailed account of the proclamation of 17 August 1945, the formation of republican institutions, and the opening of the revolution belongs to Chapters 146 to 148, especially Chapter 147. This chapter establishes the conditions that made a declaration of independence possible; it does not narrate the declaration itself, and it does not suggest that effective sovereignty was already won.

Students should finish this chapter able to explain how Japan overthrew Dutch authority, how occupation was organized, what Indonesians were asked to give and what they suffered, what opportunities nationalist leaders, religious figures, and youth did and did not obtain, and why the collapse of Japan's position in 1945 turned those opportunities into a struggle over who would hold political authority in the archipelago.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the political, economic, and social structure of the Netherlands East Indies in 1941 and identify which nationalist structures survived Dutch repression.
  2. Trace the Japanese conquest of the archipelago in 1942 as a multi-island campaign shaped by oil, sea routes, and airfields, rather than a single invasion of Java.
  3. Explain the division of occupied Indonesia into Army and Navy jurisdictions and its consequences for regional experience.
  4. Analyze how the military government used Indonesian officials and neighborhood associations without granting political autonomy.
  5. Evaluate the choices of Sukarno, Hatta, and Sjahrir, avoiding both condemnation and apology as default positions.
  6. Compare the Three-A Movement, PUTERA, Jawa Hokokai, and the Central Advisory Council as instruments of mobilization.
  7. Assess Japanese policy toward Islam and the varied responses of Muslim leaders and organizations.
  8. Explain the economic purposes of occupation and distinguish resources nominally controlled from resources actually delivered.
  9. Interpret the evidence for the Java famine of 1944–1945 and the limits of mortality estimates.
  10. Analyze romusha recruitment, the role of Indonesian intermediaries, and the problems of counting recruits and deaths.
  11. Distinguish PETA, Heiho, Seinendan, and Keibodan, and assess their roles in producing leaders and organizations without assuming institutional continuity.
  12. Explain how Japanese defeat, the Koiso promise, BPUPKI, the PPKI, and the surrender created the circumstances for the August 1945 declaration.

Historical Context

By 1941 the Netherlands East Indies was one of the most valuable colonial territories in the world, with a population commonly estimated at about seventy million, of whom well over half lived on Java and neighboring Madura. It exported rubber, tin, sugar, tea, coffee, palm oil, and, above all, petroleum from fields in Sumatra and Borneo. The colony was governed by a Governor-General at Batavia through a dual administration: European civil servants in the Binnenlands Bestuur ruled alongside Indonesian aristocratic officials, the bupati or regents, who held rank in a hierarchy that ultimately answered to Dutch authority. The colonial army (KNIL) and police enforced order. A partly elected Volksraad offered limited consultation without legislative power.

Indonesian nationalism was already decades old. Budi Utomo, Sarekat Islam, the Indonesian Communist Party, Sukarno's Indonesian Nationalist Party, and the Youth Pledge of 1928, which asserted one homeland, one nation, and one language, are examined in Chapter 126. After the early 1930s the Dutch jailed or exiled the movement's principal leaders. Sukarno was imprisoned and then exiled to Flores and Bengkulu; Hatta and Sutan Sjahrir were sent to Boven Digul and Banda Neira. The Depression, discussed in Chapter 133, had undermined export incomes and rural livelihoods and made the colonial state financially defensive. Moderate organizations survived and legal parties continued to function, but the radical nationalist leadership had been removed from public life.

The German conquest of the Netherlands in May 1940 had already changed the colony's strategic position. The Dutch government-in-exile in London could not reinforce the Indies, and the colony's security depended on the uncertain commitments of Britain, Australia, and the United States. When American, British, and Dutch embargoes in 1941 cut Japan off from oil, the East Indies became a primary Japanese objective. Chapter 134 treats the road to the Pacific War; this chapter begins when its consequences reached the archipelago.

Two further contexts matter. First, the geography of the colony: Java's density and its roads, railways, and administrative apparatus contrasted with the thinly populated, resource-rich Outer Islands, which supplied much of the export wealth. Second, Japan's own institutional structure: Army and Navy competed for control of occupied territory, and the wartime division between them directly shaped Indonesian political life. The interpretive task of the chapter is to explain how decisions made in Tokyo, in Army and Navy headquarters, and in local Japanese offices interacted with Indonesian choices that were never merely reactions.

Section 138.1 · Division I — The Japanese Conquest, 1941–1942

The Netherlands East Indies on the Eve of the Japanese Invasion

The colonial state that Japan attacked in 1942 was a layered and uneven structure. At its head stood the Governor-General in Batavia, advised by departments of the central government. Beneath him, European residents and assistant-residents supervised Indonesian regents and their subordinate officials. This dual administration meant that the Dutch ruled through, rather than around, much of the Javanese and Sundanese aristocracy, and that many Indonesian officials had a career stake in the colonial order. Outside Java, the Dutch relied more heavily on arrangements with local rulers, especially in the many "self-governing territories" governed under short-form contracts with sultans and princes.

The administrative map concealed considerable variation in authority. In parts of Java, a resident could issue instructions through a densely staffed chain of regents, district officers, and village heads. In territories administered indirectly, Dutch officers negotiated with rulers whose standing rested on older courts, ritual obligations, and regional loyalties. The arrangement let a small number of European officials direct a vast colony, but it also meant that the language of a single Netherlands East Indies overstated the degree of everyday integration. Japanese officers encountered these differences when they tried to demand grain, workers, and obedience. Where local personnel remained in place, directives could circulate quickly; where relations had been mediated by rulers or trading networks, military requisition could undermine existing accommodations. The diverse organization of the colony is essential to explaining why the occupying authorities did not impose identical institutions everywhere.

Economically the colony was a producer of raw materials for world markets. Java supported intensive smallholder rice cultivation and a large sugar industry, while the Outer Islands, particularly Sumatra, Borneo, and the islands off the Malay Peninsula, produced rubber, tin, tobacco, palm oil, and petroleum. Oil was the strategic center of gravity: fields and refineries around Palembang in southern Sumatra, and at Balikpapan and Tarakan on the east coast of Borneo, were owned by Dutch-British and American companies and ranked among the largest in Asia. The Depression had cut prices and incomes, hurting smallholders, estate workers, and colonial revenues; recovery was partial and the late 1930s were years of rearmament rather than reform.

Politically the nationalist movement had been fragmented by repression. Sukarno's Indonesian Nationalist Party had been broken up, and Sukarno was in exile in Bengkulu in 1941. Hatta and Sjahrir, who had led the smaller education-oriented New PNI, were held in Banda Neira until early 1942. Moderate nationalists participated in the Volksraad and in cooperative organizations such as Gerindo and the Indonesian Political Federation (GAPI), which demanded an Indonesian parliament in the late 1930s and received only token Dutch concessions. Muslim organizations, above all Muhammadiyah and Nahdlatul Ulama, ran schools and welfare institutions and had mostly avoided direct confrontation with the state. The Youth Pledge of 1928 and the spread of the Malay-derived Indonesian language had established a shared political vocabulary, but nationalism remained largely an urban and educated phenomenon.

Politically, the Dutch had denied sovereignty while inadvertently helping create national institutions. Schools, the press, railway travel, inter-island associations, and the use of Malay as a common language brought some activists into contact with a territory broader than their native region. The Youth Pledge expressed an aspiration to an Indonesian nation, not evidence that all islands had already accepted a shared program. Nationalist parties disputed the pace of change, relations with the colonial government, the place of Islam, and the distribution of power between Java and other regions. Sukarno's imprisonment and exile, Hatta's efforts to promote disciplined political education, and Sjahrir's socialist internationalism represent different currents rather than a single leadership bloc. Their disagreements would matter again when Japanese promises became instruments of wartime politics.

The strategic position was weak. The KNIL was a long-service colonial army officered chiefly by Europeans and manned largely by Indonesian soldiers drawn disproportionately from Ambon, Minahasa, and Java; its equipment was dated, and its air force and navy were small. Dutch planning depended on British naval power based at Singapore, which was unavailable once the Pacific War began. Dutch officials also planned a scorched-earth policy for oil installations, which proved only partially effective.

The student should note what 1942 destroyed and what it did not. It destroyed the Dutch administrative and military hierarchy and the prestige that supported it. It did not destroy the Indonesian regents and village officials, Muslim schools and networks, the Indonesian language, or the memory of nationalist organization. These survivals shaped what Japan could and could not do.

Section 138.2

Japan's Invasion: Oilfields, Naval Warfare, and the Conquest of the Archipelago

Japan's offensive in the East Indies followed from its need for petroleum. After the oil embargo of 1941, Japanese planners calculated that stockpiles would last only a limited time, and the conquest of the Indies, together with Malaya and the Philippines, was an integral part of the Southern Operation. Japanese forces advanced in a sequence of amphibious and airborne operations designed to seize oil centers, then airfields from which air cover could be extended, then Java itself.

Campaign planning depended on successive advances rather than an indiscriminate sweep across the islands. Japanese naval forces escorted landings while aircraft operating from recently seized bases threatened the next Allied defensive position. The capture of an airstrip could therefore be as important as occupation of a town. As Japanese air cover moved southward, Allied ships had to fight increasingly far from secure repair facilities and dependable reconnaissance. The landings on Tarakan and Balikpapan tested the defenders' ability to destroy petroleum facilities before capture. Even successful demolition could not substitute for a coherent naval and air defense, because Japanese engineers might restore installations if the territory remained secure for months.

The campaign opened in January 1942. Japanese forces landed at Tarakan on 11 January, and moved against Balikpapan later in the month, where a naval action off the port and Dutch demolition of installations marked the first major clashes. Operations followed across Celebes (Sulawesi), including Manado and Kendari, and in Ambon and Timor. In mid-February Japanese paratroopers dropped on Palembang, while seaborne forces advanced up the rivers of southern Sumatra, securing the principal oil region before the Dutch could complete denial measures. Bali was taken in February, depriving Java of a southeastern buffer.

At Palembang the attack combined airborne troops, river approaches, and the movement of larger formations across southern Sumatra. Its design sought to capture refinery equipment before Dutch destruction parties could do irreversible damage. This emphasis showed both Japanese dependence on oil and the limits of destroying installations as a delaying strategy. Some equipment was damaged, and output could not instantly return to its prewar level; Japanese commanders nonetheless gained strategically valuable territory. In the eastern islands, operations against Ambon and Timor also mattered for maritime communications with Australia. These were not marginal diversions from a Java-centered war but part of the wider attempt to secure Japan's southern perimeter and deprive the Allies of forward bases.

The Allied response was improvised. The American-British-Dutch-Australian Command (ABDA), set up in January under General Sir Archibald Wavell, attempted to coordinate defense, but its component forces had different doctrines, languages, and priorities, and it was dissolved in late February as the situation collapsed. Allied naval forces under the Dutch Rear Admiral Karel Doorman were destroyed in the Battle of the Java Sea on 27 February and in subsequent actions in the Sunda Strait, removing the defenders' main surface force. Historians generally attribute Japanese success to superior air power, experience, coordination of naval and ground forces, and the dispersion of Allied resources, rather than to any single factor; Japanese forces also made mistakes, and the outcome was not predetermined.

The ABDA command embodied an alliance assembled under emergency pressure. Its Dutch naval commanders knew the local waters, but units under their direction were short of common communications procedures, air reconnaissance, and opportunities for coordinated training. The Battle of the Java Sea revealed the disadvantage of engaging a more integrated opponent while dependent on mixed ships, limited intelligence, and deteriorating air cover. The deaths of sailors and the loss of cruisers and destroyers narrowed the options for the soldiers on Java. Yet tactical failures should not erase the efforts made by local defenders, including Indonesian servicemen whose subsequent experiences rarely receive the attention devoted to European officers. A balanced campaign history explains constraints and decisions, not merely its final outcome.

It is essential to see the conquest as geographically broad. Java was the last major objective, not the first. By the time Japanese troops landed there, Borneo, Celebes, much of the eastern archipelago, and southern Sumatra were already in Japanese hands. This sequencing explains the later administrative division: forces that had conquered different regions remained responsible for them, and the separate Army and Navy commands that emerged had their origins in the operational plan.

Section 138.3

The Fall of Java and the Collapse of Dutch Rule, March 1942

Japanese forces of the Sixteenth Army under Lieutenant-General Imamura Hitoshi landed on Java on 1 March 1942 at several points on the north coast and in the Sunda Strait region. Allied ground resistance, drawn from KNIL units, Australian, British, and American contingents, and local forces, was uncoordinated and short-lived. Within about a week the Japanese had taken key airfields and moved on Batavia and Bandung, where the colonial government and army headquarters had withdrawn.

The Java campaign moved faster than the colonial administration could organize a defense in depth. Japanese columns moved toward the capital and the Bandung plateau, using captured airfields and road junctions to separate defending contingents. Some Australian, British, Dutch, and American detachments fought hard in particular localities, but they did not share a reserve large enough to reverse the advance. Ter Poorten's command faced collapsing communications and the possibility of continued fighting amid densely inhabited regions. Military surrender therefore reflected a deteriorating operational position as well as a political decision about the costs of further resistance. Describing the campaign as simply a Dutch collapse obscures the different circumstances of units dispersed across the archipelago.

The principal capitulation was negotiated at Kalijati on 8 March, when Governor-General Jonkheer Tjarda van Starkenborgh Stachouwer and Lieutenant-General Hein ter Poorten, commander of the colonial army, were compelled to accept Japanese terms. Ter Poorten’s surrender order for forces on Java was broadcast on 9 March, explaining the two dates cited by historians. The surrender applied to Java. Fighting and later capitulations continued elsewhere in the archipelago: guerrilla and small-unit resistance persisted in Sumatra and in parts of the eastern islands for some time, and Dutch and Allied forces in Timor and elsewhere resisted into the spring. The date should therefore be understood as the collapse of the central colonial command, not as the end of all fighting.

The Kalijati negotiations on 8 March were followed by the broadcast of capitulation orders on 9 March. This sequence explains why histories use both dates; they do not necessarily disagree about the same event. Governor-General van Starkenborgh Stachouwer did not acquire freedom to negotiate an Indonesian political settlement as a condition of surrender, and Japanese military government replaced the colonial executive. However, administrative continuity operated at a lower level. Many Indonesian regents and officials continued performing their duties, now subject to a different superior. Ordinary residents could experience the change as both the visible humiliation of Europeans and the persistence of familiar officials demanding tax, labor, or compliance.

The immediate consequences for the European population were severe. Officials, soldiers, and civilians of Dutch nationality were interned in camps, with a large part of the military personnel becoming prisoners of war; estimates of the total number of internees run to the order of one hundred thousand or more. Indo-Europeans, people of mixed European and Indonesian descent, occupied an ambiguous position. Some were interned, while others, particularly those of lower status or with Indonesian ties, were left at liberty under restrictions; the Japanese treated them with suspicion and their social position, once secure within the colonial hierarchy, deteriorated sharply. Chinese communities, long prominent in commerce, were subjected to Japanese control, extortion, and at times violence, even as they were courted for their economic networks.

Prisoner experiences also varied over time. Military personnel were disarmed and sent to camps or later labor destinations; many European civilians were segregated and interned in phases rather than all on one day. Indo-European families confronted classifications that could depend on documents, parentage, perceived political loyalty, and local Japanese decisions. Japanese authorities often used the presence of mixed-descent residents for competing aims: surveillance, a claim to include fellow Asians, or the removal of people identified with the Dutch order. Chinese communities, affected by Japan's war in China, faced additional suspicion. The occupation's racial arrangements differed from those of Dutch colonialism without creating a society free from hierarchy, discrimination, or coercion.

For Indonesians, responses to the Japanese arrival ranged widely. Some welcomed it with enthusiasm, drawn by prophecies of liberation from foreign rule and by anti-Dutch resentment. Others feared the soldiers, especially after reports of violence by the Japanese military in areas it had taken. Many adopted a cautious wait-and-see attitude. Local crowds sometimes attacked Europeans and their property in the interval between Dutch collapse and Japanese consolidation; in a few places there was disorder. The longer-term effect was psychological: the experience of seeing the European regime humiliated shattered an assumption of permanence on which colonial authority had rested.

Section 138.4

Three Military Jurisdictions and the Fragmentation of Occupied Indonesia

Japan did not govern the former colony as one territory. Java and Madura came under the Sixteenth Army, whose military administration (gunsei) was centered in Batavia. Sumatra came under the Twenty-Fifth Army, headquartered at first in Malaya and from 1943 at Bukittinggi, and administered as part of a broader command that also included Malaya, so that Sumatra was at times connected more closely to Singapore than to Java. The eastern archipelago, including Borneo, Sulawesi, the Moluccas, the Lesser Sunda Islands, and western New Guinea, was assigned to the Imperial Navy, which governed through naval administrative offices based at Makassar and elsewhere. The boundaries and responsibilities were adjusted over the course of the war.

The term military jurisdiction requires institutional precision. The Sixteenth Army's command on Java was not equivalent to the Twenty-Fifth Army's arrangements for Sumatra, and the naval administration in eastern Indonesia reported through a different armed service. Orders issued on Java could not automatically be assumed to apply at Makassar or in Borneo. Different reporting chains affected the treatment of associations, the authority of local administrators, the severity of surveillance, and the extent to which Indonesian nationalist figures could speak publicly. This variation is vital when historians use records produced in Java to describe the wartime experience of a much wider archipelago.

The administrations differed in purpose and style. Java, with the largest population, the densest bureaucracy, and the greatest political activity, was treated as a source of food and labor and, increasingly, of manpower for local defense. Sumatra's importance lay in oil, rubber, and strategic location. The Navy areas were valued for oil, minerals, and forest products and for their strategic role, and the Navy was generally more restrictive about political activity there, forbidding nationalist organizations that the Army tolerated on Java. Telephone, telegraph, shipping, and travel between the three zones were limited, and Indonesians could not easily move between them; the Indonesian national movement, which had depended on press, schools, and travel, found its communications fractured.

Transportation reinforced the divisions. Before the invasion, inter-island shipping linked producers, markets, administrators, and newspapers. Under occupation, ships and fuel were needed for military operations, Japanese authorities controlled travel, and Allied submarines increasingly threatened coastal traffic. The inability to move people and commodities meant that a shortage in one district could coexist with stores elsewhere that could not safely be transported. It also reduced the chances for nationalist politicians to coordinate campaigns across different jurisdictions. This was a paradox of occupation: Japanese rule spread an Indonesian political vocabulary in important centers while its wartime transport restrictions made national coordination more difficult.

The consequences were lasting. Java became the center of nationalist mobilization and of the most developed Indonesian military organizations, a fact that would shape the revolution. In the Navy areas, by contrast, the political experience of the occupation was much thinner, and the Navy tended to rely on local rulers and administrators rather than mass organizations. Sumatra saw some mass mobilization and Indonesian volunteer units, but under different arrangements. The pattern also shaped later regional resentments over the Java-centered character of Indonesian nationalism, and it is a reason that this chapter treats the Outer Islands separately in Section 138.16.

Jurisdictional differences did not mean that the eastern islands lacked political history. Local rulers, Islamic leaders, church communities, merchants, and young activists confronted military demands and tried to defend their constituencies. The Navy often regarded the islands primarily as an operational zone and source of material support, with less interest in allowing Jakarta-style political mass movements. Sumatra's ties to the Malay Peninsula also shaped military administration and economic priorities in ways that made comparisons with Java misleading. A single narrative of Japanese rule is therefore possible only if it continuously identifies whose experience it describes, which authority made the decision, and where that decision had force.

Military Government, Indigenous Officials, and Administrative Restructuring

The Japanese military government on Java retained the shell of the Dutch bureaucracy while removing its European head. Dutch officials were interned and replaced, where possible, by Japanese officers and civilian specialists at the top, and by Indonesians at the middle and lower levels. The regents, who had been subordinate to European residents, now reported to Japanese officials, and Indonesians were promoted to positions that had previously been closed to them. The staff of the provincial and district administrations, the police, and the technical departments expanded accordingly. For many educated Indonesians, the occupation offered career advancement that Dutch colonialism had denied.

Military administration rested on a hierarchy of command rather than consent. Japanese military officers controlled decisive questions of policing, security, transport, political association, and the movement of supplies. Indonesian personnel were important precisely because the occupation could not function without them. Regents and village heads knew which households cultivated land, which families could be ordered to provide workers, and where locally stored food might be found. By retaining these intermediaries, Japanese authorities gained access to social knowledge that a foreign military force could not quickly acquire. The arrangement increased the practical responsibilities of indigenous officials, but the power to refuse an order or revise its purpose remained limited.

Language and symbols were changed. Dutch was banned from official use, and Indonesian and Japanese took its place; the Dutch names of cities were replaced, so that Batavia became Jakarta. Time was reckoned by the Japanese calendar, and public ceremonies emphasized obeisance to the Japanese emperor, including bowing toward Tokyo (seikerei), a practice that offended many Muslims. Radio sets were sealed to receive only authorized broadcasts, and press and publication were controlled by a propaganda department.

The Japanese also changed the symbols and language of government. Dutch names and public commemorations were suppressed, and official communications increasingly used Indonesian and Japanese rather than Dutch. Formal ceremonies emphasized loyalty to the emperor and Japan's claim to Asian leadership. These changes exposed wider sections of the population to a public Indonesian language, but military orders and neighborhood surveillance remained coercive. Administrative change should thus be assessed at two levels: the staffing and vocabulary of government became less visibly Dutch, while the mechanisms of discipline and centralized direction often became more intrusive. This distinction prevents students from equating indigenization of personnel with self-rule.

The most consequential local innovation was the tonarigumi, neighborhood associations of roughly ten to twenty households, extended widely on Java in 1944 on a model drawn from Japan. Each group had a leader responsible for distributing rations, collecting rice and contributions, organizing air-raid precautions, reporting on residents' behavior, and supplying labor quotas. The tonarigumi gave the state an unprecedented ability to reach every household. It also drew on existing Indonesian forms of village cooperation, and after independence the neighborhood structure survived under Indonesian names.

The neighborhood association was especially consequential. Tonarigumi leaders helped report household populations, distribute rationing instructions, organize air-raid precautions, identify labor recruits, and collect deliveries. They could also become intermediaries through whom villagers negotiated for relief or challenged an unfair quota. A structure designed for surveillance might enable cooperation among neighbors, yet it exposed households to scrutiny and placed local leaders in difficult positions when they were ordered to impose penalties. The resulting record is morally complicated: a village head who selected a worker for compulsory labor participated in coercion, even when the choice was made under threat from superiors. Individual responsibility should be examined without assuming that every local official possessed equal freedom of action.

The expansion of Indonesian officialdom did not equal political autonomy. Decisions were made by Japanese officers, who sat beside or above Indonesian officials as advisers, and who could override them. Indonesian officials were often caught between Japanese demands for rice and labor and their communities' resistance, and those who carried out the demands acquired local resentment. Political activity outside Japanese sponsorship was banned and enforced by the military police, the Kempeitai, whose reputation for arbitrary arrest and torture was widespread.

Section 138.6

Sukarno, Mohammad Hatta, and the Politics of Wartime Cooperation

The Japanese needed Indonesians who could speak to the population, and the leading nationalists were the most obvious candidates. Sukarno had been moved by the Dutch from Bengkulu toward Padang in the last days of Dutch rule and was left behind when they withdrew. Japanese officers in Sumatra, aware of his reputation, met him and arranged his transfer to Java in mid-1942. Hatta, held at Sukabumi after leaving Banda Neira, was approached by Sixteenth Army officers who wished to employ him as an adviser. Sjahrir, who also returned to Java, declined public involvement.

Sukarno's and Hatta's prominence under occupation did not emerge from nothing. Sukarno had already built a recognizable nationalist political language before his Dutch exile. Hatta had emphasized organization, disciplined leadership, and economic independence. Japanese authorities needed the appeal and credibility of such figures to speak to a large, politically complex population. The leaders, in turn, gained an audience denied to them by Dutch censorship and imprisonment. Their cooperation operated within constraints: a public speech could discuss national capacity and solidarity, but direct appeals for immediate sovereignty risked repression. Historians should ask which statements represented nationalist ambition and which were the compulsory language of wartime sponsorship.

The leaders' reasons for cooperating were stated in different ways in later memoirs, which must themselves be read as accounts written with hindsight. Sukarno, in particular, reasoned that Japan was a force that could remove the Dutch, that open opposition would be crushed, and that organizations with Japanese sanction could be used to reach the population and to build a movement. Hatta recalled hoping to use his position to protect Indonesians from the worst Japanese demands and to prepare for independence, and to keep open channels with those working underground. They were also concerned about the possibility of renewed Dutch rule, which they associated with the repression they had suffered.

The consequences of cooperation were not merely rhetorical. Appeals for work, savings, food deliveries, and sacrifice could increase the ability of Japanese authorities to extract labor and materials. Nationalist leaders later faced difficult questions about their responsibility for mobilization campaigns whose human costs became obvious after the war. The historical task is to reconstruct what the speakers knew, what alternatives were available, and whether their actions amplified coercive policies. The claim that cooperation advanced independence explains motive but does not automatically settle responsibility. Conversely, evidence that a leader promoted a Japanese program does not establish that he had abandoned Indonesian independence as a goal.

Sukarno's strengths were oratory and mass appeal; the Japanese rapidly learned that his speeches could draw crowds, and he became the most prominent Indonesian figure in public life. Hatta's strengths were administration and analysis, and he developed working relationships with Japanese officials, particularly staff of the Army's administrative offices and, later, of the Navy liaison office headed by Tadashi Maeda, who was sympathetic to Indonesian nationalism. Their styles differed, and so did their relations with the Japanese, but they maintained an effective, if unequal, partnership.

Sjahrir's different course illustrates the diversity of nationalist strategy. Drawing on socialist contacts and an awareness of international developments, he regarded a Japanese military victory as dangerous for the prospects of an independent democratic Indonesia and avoided serving as a prominent Japanese-sponsored leader. Underground politics, however, offered few opportunities for open national mobilization and carried risks of arrest. Hatta, Sukarno, and Sjahrir were not simply personal opposites; their positions reflected judgments about how international war, mass politics, and political legitimacy should interact. The tensions among their strategies would become visible in August 1945 when they disagreed over the timing and sponsorship of the transition to independence.

Cooperation had costs. Sukarno and Hatta lent their names to appeals for rice deliveries, to recruitment for the auxiliary and military organizations, and to the recruitment of labor, including the romusha drives; Sukarno made public speeches encouraging labor service. These appeals aided Japanese mobilization, and the leaders were aware of the human costs even if the scale was not fully clear to them. At the same time, cooperation gave them access to a public that the Dutch had barred, a legitimacy that no underground leader could match, and positions in organizations from which they could shape events. The ethical and political assessment remains disputed.

Historical Debate

Strategic Nationalist Cooperation or Wartime Collaboration?

The question: Was the public cooperation of Sukarno and Hatta with Japan a shrewd strategy that advanced independence, a form of collaboration that aided an oppressor, or both?

Interpretation A — strategic cooperation: Some historians stress that open opposition was impossible, that the leaders used Japanese platforms to build organization and national consciousness, and that they tried to moderate Japanese demands.
Interpretation B — collaboration with costs: Others stress that the leaders' propaganda and recruitment appeals contributed directly to forced labor and to the burdens on villagers, and that their cooperation helped legitimize Japanese rule.
Interpretation C — a false binary: Still others argue that the choices facing Indonesian leaders cannot be reduced to resistance versus collaboration, and that Sukarno, Hatta, and Sjahrir were pursuing a coordinated or at least complementary division of tasks, an interpretation supported by some memoirs but treated cautiously because memoirs are self-interested.

Evidence: Speeches, memoirs, Japanese records, and later testimony support different readings; no single source settles the question.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns motive, effect, and the standards used to judge decisions made under duress. Neither cooperation nor non-cooperation was risk-free: Sjahrir's underground work was dangerous, and those who refused or were suspected of resistance faced arrest and torture.

Why it matters: The question shapes how Indonesians and others remember the occupation and the founders of the republic.

Section 138.7

The Three-A Movement, PUTERA, and Jawa Hokokai

The Sixteenth Army tried successive vehicles to mobilize Indonesian society. The first was the Three-A Movement, launched in April 1942 under the slogans of Japan as the protector, leader, and light of Asia. Led by a Japanese propagandist and an Indonesian figurehead, it was meant to spread loyalty to Japan, but it was run by Japanese officials with little consultation, lacked appeal among educated nationalists, and was dissolved within a year.

The Three-A Movement depended on slogans portraying Japan as Asia's protector, leader, and light. Its appeal was limited partly because Japanese propaganda sought enthusiastic obedience before offering Indonesians meaningful political authority. The occupation regime then turned to better-known nationalist and religious figures. PUTERA offered Sukarno and his colleagues a stage from which they could speak in an Indonesian political idiom, even when the ceremonies were designed to serve the Japanese war effort. Japanese officials expected the leaders' prestige to improve mobilization; nationalist leaders could use the same audiences to reinforce a sense of belonging to a broader political community. The organization therefore illustrates competing purposes within a coercive institution rather than a simple transfer of political control.

Its successor, PUTERA (Pusat Tenaga Rakyat, Centre of the People's Power), was founded on 9 March 1943 and was led by four prominent Indonesians: Sukarno, Hatta, Ki Hadjar Dewantara, and K. H. Mas Mansur. Their combination of nationalist, educational, and Muslim leadership was meant to give the body authority. PUTERA's official purpose was to mobilize the population for the war effort, but its leaders hoped to use it to build a nationwide network and to raise national consciousness. It gave them speaking platforms, branches, and a public presence, and it was more popular than the Three-A Movement. The Japanese, however, came to feel that it served Indonesian more than Japanese ends, and they restricted its activity.

The replacement of PUTERA with Jawa Hokokai reflected frustration with this ambiguity. Japanese military officers increasingly wanted an organization whose work could be measured in contributions, collections, labor participation, and discipline. As the war moved closer to the islands, appeals to abstract Asian fraternity yielded to demands for concrete sacrifice. Jawa Hokokai operated through local administrative structures, making it easier to combine village pressure with public ceremonies and campaigns. Women's associations, neighborhood groups, schools, and youth formations could be drawn into these efforts. Nationalist participation continued, but the space for using mass organizations for independent political education became narrower.

In early 1944 the Army replaced PUTERA with Jawa Hokokai, the Java Service Association, a body under tighter Japanese supervision and with local branches built on the tonarigumi and village administrations. Its tasks were explicit: increase production, collect rice and other goods, raise donations, and mobilize labor. Indonesian leaders remained prominent, but the body was a transmission belt for orders rather than a nationalist forum. Sumatra and the Navy areas had their own, more limited, organizations.

Advisory councils require a third category of analysis. The Chūō Sangi-in on Java allowed selected Indonesian figures to discuss matters presented by the occupation government and gave participants experience speaking and negotiating in an official forum. Yet a council that could advise was not a legislature with authority to compel the military to accept its decisions. Students should compare its membership, agenda, and recorded discussions with the real location of coercive power. An institution may shape political habits and future networks even when it fails to provide sovereignty in the present. That combination was characteristic of several Japanese-sponsored structures later remembered as part of Indonesia's preparation for independence.

In September 1943 the Japanese established the Central Advisory Council (Chūō Sangi-in) in Jakarta, chaired by Sukarno, with regional advisory councils in the residencies. These bodies could answer questions put to them by the military government and make suggestions, but they could not legislate. They nonetheless gave Indonesian politicians experience in debate and representation and created a framework in which questions about the future could be discussed. The pattern, advisory bodies without sovereign power, illustrates the central contradiction of Japanese policy: it created political experience while withholding authority.

Islamic Organizations, Religious Authority, and Wartime Political Mobilization

Japanese officials recognized that Indonesian Muslims were a majority, that they were organized in schools and associations, and that their leaders had influence in villages that nationalist politicians did not reach. They established an Office of Religious Affairs (Shūmubu) and sought the cooperation of ulama and kyai. Policy toward Islam was initially cautious; the Japanese suspended the Dutch-era federation of Muslim organizations and, in 1943, established Masyumi, the Consultative Council of Indonesian Muslims, as a single supervised body representing both Muhammadiyah and Nahdlatul Ulama.

Japanese religious policy was shaped by military need rather than a consistent commitment to Islamic autonomy. In predominantly Muslim regions, Japanese administrators could not govern effectively while alienating teachers, mosque networks, and respected scholars. Officials therefore sought endorsements from prominent religious figures, supported certain public Islamic organizations, and allowed some religious educational activities. These concessions were selective and reversible. Matters touching military loyalty or resource extraction could produce conflict even where official speeches praised Islam's place in Asian life. A scholar's willingness to attend an occupation-sponsored meeting did not mean that every congregation approved the regime.

The relationship was uneasy from the start. K. H. Hasyim Asy'ari, the founder of Nahdlatul Ulama and one of the most respected religious scholars on Java, was detained in 1942, apparently in connection with his refusal of seikerei and with the arrests of others, and was released later in the year. He subsequently led Masyumi, and his son Wahid Hasyim played a prominent role. Muhammadiyah's leaders, including K. H. Mas Mansur, took part in PUTERA. Japanese authorities also adjusted some practices, such as exempting Muslims from seikerei in certain contexts, in response to protests.

The establishment of Masyumi in 1943 brought several influential Muslim associations into a single wartime framework. It helped Japanese authorities communicate with religious communities and seek assistance, but it also familiarized activists with coordinating beyond a single school, town, or organization. Muhammadiyah and Nahdlatul Ulama retained distinct doctrinal, educational, and social orientations. Kyai in local pesantren possessed authority that did not always translate into the political ambitions of leaders in Jakarta. Consequently, students should distinguish the positions of national religious figures, regional ulama, youth, and villagers rather than treating Islam as a single party or set of orders.

Muslim leaders evaluated the opportunities in different ways. Some saw the institutions as a means to protect schools, strengthen religious networks, and gain a role in the political order that would follow. The authorities offered religious training for ulama, and Muslim youth organizations such as Hizbullah were formed in late 1944 as a volunteer corps attached to the military system. Others, particularly in rural pesantren, resisted demands such as the rice levy, the bowing ritual, and the recruitment of young men. The Singaparna uprising in 1944 and the Aceh revolt of 1942 (Section 138.15) arose from religiously motivated opposition to Japanese demands.

Conflict demonstrated the limits of accommodation. Religious obligations, the Japanese salute toward the imperial palace, requisitions, and military discipline could collide with beliefs and customary authority. Opposition associated with Zainal Mustafa at Singaparna and Abdul Djalil in Aceh shows that anti-Japanese action sometimes arose from defense of religious practice and local dignity rather than from the program of a nationwide nationalist committee. The Japanese also recruited through Muslim networks where officials thought religious legitimacy would make demands more acceptable. For historians this creates a dual task: assessing the actual benefits organizations obtained and documenting their participation, willing or coerced, in a military regime that extracted food and labor from the population.

Visions of independence differed. Secular nationalists favored a state based on a common nationality that did not privilege any religion, while many Muslim leaders argued for a role for Islamic law. These differences would be argued in BPUPKI in 1945 (Section 138.18). Regional visions also differed: some regional elites emphasized local autonomy, and Sumatra and the eastern islands were not equally represented in Java-centered organizations. The occupation thus intensified debates over the character of the future state rather than settling them.

Oil, Rubber, Tin, and the Japanese Wartime Economy

Japanese planners regarded the resources of the Indies as the economic prize of the Southern Operation. The oilfields and refineries of southern Sumatra and eastern Borneo were the most important, but the colony also produced rubber, tin from Bangka, Belitung, and Singkep, bauxite, copper, quinine from cinchona estates in West Java, sugar, and fibers. The aim was to exploit these as inputs to the Japanese war economy and to make the occupied territories as self-sufficient as possible, so that local needs would be met from local production with little diversion of Japanese shipping.

Japanese planners had entered the war hoping that control of Southeast Asian petroleum would relieve the empire's strategic shortage. But fields and refineries were parts of an integrated chain, and control of territory alone did not restore that chain. Skilled employees had been interned, evacuated, or displaced; repair materials were scarce; and Allied sabotage had damaged equipment. Japanese engineers attempted to restart production, yet output varied by installation and over time. Even when crude oil could be extracted, its usefulness depended on refining capacity and enough tankers to move it to consumers. This is why the story of occupation economics cannot be reduced to a map of captured resources.

The Dutch had carried out demolitions at oil installations before retreating, and the Japanese brought in technicians from the Army and Navy and from Japanese companies to restore them. Production recovered over 1942 and 1943, with refined products shipped to Japan and to Japanese forces in the region, but output fell short of the targets that planners had set, because of equipment losses, shortages of spare parts, labor problems, and, later, Allied attacks. Rubber and tin were affected more severely, because the prewar markets had been in the United States and Europe, and Japan's own demand for them was limited; estates were neglected, converted to food crops, or abandoned, and tin mining declined.

Rubber and tin posed a different problem. Before the war they had been sold through international markets, generating cash income for estates, smallholders, traders, and colonial revenue authorities. War disrupted commercial demand and left workers and producers without their established outlets. Japanese military needs generated some requisitioning, but the occupation did not reproduce the prewar export economy. Mines and plantations required equipment, specialized knowledge, spare parts, and workers who had other urgent needs. Some enterprises closed or operated at lower capacity; workers might shift to subsistence farming or be conscripted elsewhere. The collapse of market incentives interacted with direct military extraction to reduce civilian welfare.

Shipping was the decisive constraint. Japan entered the war with a merchant fleet that was not large enough for its needs, and from 1943 Allied submarines sank growing numbers of ships. By 1944 a substantial fraction of the tankers and freighters that carried Indonesian oil and other goods to Japan had been lost, and the home islands could no longer import what the East Indies produced. Resources remained theoretically under Japanese control but were stranded in the archipelago. Historians therefore distinguish between the resources Japan nominally held, the resources actually produced, and the portion that reached Japanese industry; the third was considerably smaller than the first.

Shipping became the decisive constraint as Allied offensives advanced. Tankers and merchant vessels were sunk, diverted to troops, or left idle for want of fuel and repairs. Maritime insecurity weakened connections among islands and between their producers and Japanese consumers. The same process also contributed to food shortages, because rice and other goods could not move reliably between areas that had previously traded. By 1944, Japanese commanders could still hold much of Indonesia while facing growing difficulties in moving its resources, reinforcing isolated garrisons, and sustaining the occupation bureaucracy. A resource-rich conquered archipelago had become increasingly fragmented by the logistics of total war.

For Indonesians the effects were mixed and mostly negative. Wage-earning estate and mine workers lost incomes as production fell, and the redirection of trade cut off imports of cloth, medicine, and consumer goods. The Japanese authorities introduced new currency in large quantities, which drove inflation. At the same time, the occupation encouraged some local manufacturing and food production to replace imports, and Indonesians were employed in technical and administrative positions formerly reserved for Europeans.

Rice Requisitions, Agricultural Disruption, and the Food Crisis of 1944–1945

Java before the war was a net importer of rice in some years and relied on Burma and Indochina to supplement domestic supply. The Japanese conquest cut these imports, while the military's own needs and those of the urban population added to demand. At the same time, production on Java faced growing obstacles. Rice farming depended on irrigation systems that needed maintenance by village labor and on a supply of fertilizer, seed, and draft animals; labor mobilization, the loss of fertilizer imports, the requisition of animals, and the neglect of canals all reduced yields. Wartime shifts of land to cotton, castor, and jute, encouraged by Japanese planners, reduced rice acreage.

A famine is not explained by harvest volume alone. Families require both physical food supplies and the ability to obtain them. A cultivator who surrendered a quota at a low official price might lack enough grain for the household even if the village's aggregate production appeared adequate. An urban wage-earner could face a different danger: food existed in the countryside, but transport failed or black-market prices rose faster than wages. Fixed prices, forced deliveries, rationing, and unofficial exchange therefore redistributed food and risk unequally. The poorer a household was, the fewer reserves, animals, ornaments, or relatives it could draw on in an emergency. Administrative measures intended to secure military supplies could intensify scarcity for people with the least bargaining power.

The state responded by tightening control over distribution. A system of compulsory deliveries, with quotas assigned to villages and collected through village heads and the tonarigumi, was introduced in 1943 and tightened in 1944, with government purchasing at fixed prices that were far below the real cost of goods. Farmers had little incentive to deliver and had reason to hide grain; officials who failed to meet quotas faced punishment, and enforcement often fell on village heads, who had to decide whose households to squeeze. Fixed prices and a black market that grew as the official one failed further undermined production. Transport between regions was disrupted by shipping losses, fuel shortages, and the deterioration of railways, so that surplus regions could not supply deficit ones.

The village officials who enforced procurement lived inside this conflict. A quota might be determined from unreliable acreage or crop estimates; the official responsible for it faced demands from Japanese superiors and pleas from neighbors. Some officials concealed shortages or used personal influence to relieve selected households, while others shifted the burden to families with little capacity to protest. Farmers hid stocks and turned to private transactions not solely out of profiteering but in attempts to preserve subsistence and secure goods that money no longer reliably purchased. Compulsory labor and work on nonfood crops also weakened the capacity to cultivate land. Famine policy operated through local social relations as well as national administrative decrees.

By 1944 the consequences were visible. A poor harvest in some regions in 1944, followed by further pressure in 1945, left rural households short of food while rice continued to be collected. Reports from the period and later testimony describe widespread hunger, a diet of cassava, maize, and other substitutes, clothing so scarce that people wore sacking, and an increase in disease. Malnutrition made populations more vulnerable to epidemic illness, and mortality rose, with some regions, notably parts of Java's south coast and the poorer districts of the interior, hit harder than others. Urban residents, who depended on rations, suffered shortages too.

Recent demographic research offers a more disciplined estimate of the human cost while underscoring its uncertainty. Pierre van der Eng's 2024 study estimates a net population loss in Java of roughly 3.3 million during occupation, incorporating approximately 1.8 million excess deaths and a substantial shortfall of births. Of the excess deaths, about 0.7 million are assigned to 1944 and 1.1 million to 1945. These are model-based historical estimates, not individual death registrations or an uncontested count of people who died solely from starvation. Malnutrition made disease more lethal, and late-war mortality reflected interacting causes. Net population loss must not be described as famine deaths: it includes people who were never born.

Modern research confirms that there was a major famine on Java in 1944–1945, but contemporary registration gaps complicate demographic reconstruction. Pierre van der Eng, writing in the Australian Economic History Review in 2024, estimates approximately 1.8 million excess deaths during the occupation (about 0.7 million in 1944 and 1.1 million in 1945), alongside a larger net population loss of about 3.3 million when births that did not occur are included. These are analytical estimates derived from population evidence, not individual death records or an uncontested count of people dying solely of starvation. Historians also assess Japanese requisition policy, labor mobilization, broken transport links, missing agricultural inputs, and local harvest variation as contributing causes. Population loss, excess death, and starvation mortality are distinct concepts that must not be used interchangeably.

Section 138.11

Romusha: Coerced Labor and the Human Cost of Mobilization

The Japanese war effort depended on labor for construction, mines, airfields, fortifications, railways, and agriculture. In occupied Indonesia, laborers recruited for such tasks were called romusha, a term that originally meant simply "worker" or "laborer" and later became associated with coercion, hardship, and death. Recruitment began in 1942 with the use of "volunteer" labor drives, and, as demand grew and voluntary supply fell, shifted to quotas assigned to districts and villages, which in practice meant compulsion.

Recruitment appears in official documents as an administrative process, but its consequences unfolded in family and village relationships. District officers could announce a quota while neighborhood and village leaders decided which men to send. People selected for transport often possessed little information about destinations, wages, medical provision, or the likely duration of service. The claim that a recruit had volunteered must therefore be read alongside the real power of the official who requested names. Some workers sought wages or opportunities and some initially agreed, but deceptive promises, social pressure, restrictions on departure, and punishments made the boundary between recruitment and compulsion unstable. A careful history does not erase this variation, yet it recognizes the profound inequality of power behind the labor system.

The recruitment chain involved many actors. Japanese military and civilian offices set quotas, regents and district officials allocated them, and village heads and tonarigumi leaders selected individuals. The selection process was open to abuse, since officials could exempt relatives and clients and send the poor, landless, and vulnerable. Nationalist organizations and leaders, including Jawa Hokokai and Sukarno personally, publicly urged service, often presenting romusha as heroes of the war effort, while the labor itself was frequently coerced. Recruits were sometimes taken by deception, told they would work nearby for short periods and then sent far away.

Local projects and transported labor differed sharply. Men on a nearby irrigation or road scheme might continue to return home, though their labor was diverted from farming and they could suffer hunger and beatings. Those sent by ship to mines and railways faced separation from family, exposure to unfamiliar diseases, and little practical possibility of leaving. On the Pekanbaru–Muaro railway in Sumatra, Javanese laborers worked alongside other coerced populations under punishing conditions. Sardi, whose later testimony was recorded by photographer Jan Banning, recalled being taken from a rice field as a teenager and sent to Sumatra. His account describes fatal illness, compulsory burial work, and the loss of ordinary human dignity. Such testimony conveys experience that aggregate recruitment statistics cannot capture.

The experience varied by destination. Many romusha worked on Java on road, rail, airfield, and fortification projects and in mines and plantations, living at home or in nearby camps and suffering from heavy work and poor food, but often returning. A smaller but significant number were shipped to Sumatra, Borneo, and other islands, and some to places outside Indonesia, including Malaya, Thailand, Burma, and New Guinea, notably for work on the Burma–Thailand Railway, treated in Chapter 141. Conditions in camps were severe: malnutrition, tropical disease, brutality by guards, lack of medical care, and exhaustion produced high mortality. Families lost contact with those sent away and often never learned their fates; after Japan's surrender many survivors had no means to return and remained stranded.

The numerical categories must remain separate. Takuma Melber's research discusses estimates of roughly 200,000–300,000 Javanese laborers sent beyond Java to other occupied territories, but those estimates are not a census of every person pressed into local work. Survivors, people who died overseas, workers who returned, and those retained for months in nearby labor details are different populations. Because records were damaged, locally inconsistent, or never compiled, scholars cannot calculate one definitive overall mortality percentage. The postwar challenge was equally substantial: surviving laborers often lacked transport or formal documentation, while families had spent years without reliable news. The history of romusha consequently includes repatriation, missing persons, and unresolved claims for recognition, not simply the wartime recruitment process.

No generally accepted figure exists for the total number of romusha or for deaths. Takuma Melber discusses estimates of roughly 200,000–300,000 Javanese laborers transferred to other occupied territories; this category should not be confused with the much larger and less reliably recorded pool of local workers mobilized within Java. Historical totals also differ because some refer to recruits, others to transfers or missing persons, and many records were incomplete or destroyed. The number of workers who died is even harder to determine. Students should distinguish these categories, identify each estimate’s source, and avoid presenting a transfer estimate as a census of every Indonesian subjected to forced labor.

Women, Families, Education, Language, and Everyday Survival

The occupation altered household life. Shortages of cloth, soap, medicine, kerosene, and imported goods forced families to improvise, to barter, and to rely on kin and neighbors. Inflation eroded wages and savings, and families of salaried officials and teachers, once secure, often struggled to eat. Informal trade, smuggling, and the black market grew, enriching some and ruining others. With men taken as laborers, soldiers, and auxiliaries, women and children took on more agricultural and household work, and children were often drawn into labor details, collecting scrap, planting castor, or digging air-raid shelters.

Household survival often depended on women's decisions and labor. When men were summoned for military projects or disappeared into distant camps, women negotiated with officials, cultivated small plots, traded produce, searched for rice, and cared for children and elderly relatives. Families moved between town and village to obtain food or shelter. Such work rarely appeared in the public record of Japanese mobilization, which emphasized rallies, production quotas, and male military service. Oral histories and local studies reveal instead a daily sequence of improvisations under scarcity. Survival strategies were not equal: a family with land or extensive kin connections had choices unavailable to landless workers, urban tenants, or displaced people.

Women were mobilized through organizations such as Fujinkai, women's associations established in 1943 that taught Japanese songs and exercises, organized collection drives, and prepared women for civil defense. Some Indonesian women found in them an opportunity for organization and public activity that built on earlier women's movements. Women also faced severe dangers. Documented cases of sexual violence by Japanese soldiers exist throughout the archipelago, and the Japanese military operated a system of sexual slavery in which women, including Indonesian, Dutch, and Indo-European women, were coerced or deceived into brothels. The scale and details remain subjects of research and continuing public debate, and survivors' testimony emerged slowly because of stigma.

Education was reshaped by language policy and war demands. Dutch-language schools closed or were transformed; Japanese instruction and ritual practices entered classrooms; children could be mobilized for drills, labor, and patriotic ceremonies. The Indonesian language acquired wider use in public administration and educational material because Dutch was suppressed and a common means of communication was required. But progress toward national linguistic integration was uneven. School attendance could decline because of hunger, family work requirements, and lack of teachers or supplies. The occupation did not invent Bahasa Indonesia, whose political significance dated at least to the Youth Pledge of 1928; it expanded certain uses of a language already central to nationalism.

Education was transformed. Dutch-language schools were closed or converted, Japanese-language instruction was introduced, and schools were required to teach loyalty to Japan, military drill, and songs; attendance fell and school resources shrank, but the system also opened teaching posts and gave Indonesian a new status as the language of instruction. Bahasa Indonesia became the language of administration, the press, and public ceremonies, and a commission worked on terminology and standardization. The Japanese promoted their own language, with limited success, but the practical effect of banning Dutch was to establish Indonesian as the common medium. Indonesian-language nationalism predated Japan, going back to the Youth Pledge of 1928, so the occupation accelerated a process already under way rather than creating it.

Women's associations such as Fujinkai combined public participation with coercive expectations. Their members could be directed toward sewing, relief work, collections, and support of soldiers, while their activities remained subordinate to the occupation government's priorities. A separate and much more violent history concerns sexual coercion by Japanese military personnel, including the forced sexual slavery of women in military-controlled facilities. Evidence comes from survivors, later investigations, and historical research, though documentation is uneven and many experiences were suppressed by stigma or fear. These experiences should not be submerged within a general description of women's war work. Nor should they be described with gratuitous detail. The central historical question is how military domination transformed the security, bodily autonomy, livelihood, and choices of civilian women.

PETA, Heiho, and the Creation of Indonesian Military Organizations

As the war turned against Japan, the Army needed local forces to defend Java against a possible Allied landing. In 1943 it created two kinds of Indonesian military organization. Heiho, established in April 1943, were auxiliaries recruited individually and attached to Japanese units, performing labor and support tasks, and later some combat duties, under direct Japanese command, and were stationed in many parts of the archipelago and beyond. Pembela Tanah Air (PETA, Defenders of the Homeland), established on 3 October 1943, was a different kind of force: a volunteer army organized in battalions (daidan) with Indonesian officers, intended for the defense of Java and Bali.

PETA's importance lay not only in its numerical strength but in its organization. Units recruited locally on Java and trained Indonesian officers under Japanese supervision; the design allowed Japan to conserve regular troops while preparing for an Allied landing. Training introduced recruits to drill, command procedures, small-unit organization, communications, and the discipline of coordinating personnel. Around 37,000 men are commonly cited for PETA on Java, but the precise count depends on the date and whether officers and support personnel are included. The organization served Japanese defense needs, and the influence it gave Indonesians should not be confused with authority over national military strategy. Nevertheless, individuals acquired practical skills and networks previously denied to many Indonesian nationalists under Dutch military recruitment policies.

The immediate background was a petition by Indonesian nationalists, including Gatot Mangkupradja, for a volunteer army, which Japanese officers accepted for their own reasons. Recruitment drew heavily on the sons of village officials, teachers, religious figures, and others of local standing; battalion commanders were often men of local prominence, appointed for their social authority rather than military experience, while platoon and company officers were younger. Training was conducted by Japanese instructors at Bogor and emphasized discipline, drill, and the "Japanese spirit," but it also produced organizational and tactical competence. Commonly cited estimates put PETA's strength on Java at around 37,000 by 1945, with additional units in Bali, though figures vary. Sumatra had a comparable volunteer force, the Giyūgun, and similar units existed elsewhere.

Heiho had a different relationship to command. Its personnel were auxiliaries integrated more directly into Japanese units and could be posted to dangerous tasks or locations chosen by Japanese officers. PETA's territorial-defense orientation gave some Indonesian personnel greater scope to develop a sense of unit leadership, even while Japanese authorities retained decisive control. Comparing the organizations requires attention to recruitment, deployment, discipline, and their relationship to Japanese regular formations. A uniform or weapon does not itself establish that a force possessed independent political command. Nor should later Indonesian military reputations determine how each participant understood his wartime service in 1943 or 1944.

PETA's Indonesian officers commanded Indonesian troops under their own flag and in the Indonesian language, a status unthinkable under the Dutch, who had recruited soldiers from specific ethnic groups and kept command in European hands. The experience supplied hundreds of future military leaders. Yet the assumption of institutional continuity between PETA and the later national army is incorrect. PETA was disbanded by Japanese order after the surrender and its units dissolved before the republic organized its own forces; the later army drew on PETA veterans, on Heiho and Dutch-trained KNIL veterans, and on youth militias, and its formation was shaped by the revolution rather than inherited from Japan.

Sumatra's Giyūgun formations belong to the same wider Japanese strategy of raising local defense forces, but they were developed under the Twenty-Fifth Army and had a distinct regional setting. They should not be counted automatically with Java's PETA formations or treated as subordinate branches governed from Batavia. After the Japanese surrender, veterans and weapons moved through new political circumstances; some trained men contributed to emerging Indonesian armed formations, while institutions themselves were dissolved or reorganized. There was no simple administrative handover from PETA to a national army. The deeper continuity was in human skills, networks, ambitions, and a belief among some young recruits that Indonesians could organize military power on their own behalf.

Seinendan, Keibodan, and the Mobilization of Indonesian Youth

Alongside the military forces, Japanese authorities organized the young. Seinendan, youth corps founded in 1943 for young men roughly between fourteen and twenty-two, trained members in physical fitness, discipline, drill, and the rudiments of defense, and used them for guard and labor duties. Keibodan, vigilance corps for somewhat older men, assisted the police and fire services and handled air-raid precautions. Students also received drill and ideological education in schools. By 1945 hundreds of thousands of young Indonesians had passed through such organizations, though numbers are uncertain.

Youth mobilization reached groups with little previous role in formal nationalist parties. Students and apprentices participated in physical drills, marching, neighborhood patrols, first aid, and public rituals. The stated purpose was to create disciplined subjects prepared to support Japan. Yet these programs also allowed participants from different localities and social backgrounds to meet, acquire responsibility, and learn organizational routines. A teenager who attended political instruction might accept some official claims while also recognizing that an Indonesian-speaking organization could mobilize its own members. Such ambivalence is more historically plausible than assuming either complete indoctrination or an immediate clandestine nationalist purpose among every recruit.

The official purposes were to discipline youth, prepare them for defense, and instill loyalty to Japan and the Asian order. The experience of members varied. Some enjoyed the discipline, status, and sense of purpose; others resented the regimentation, the compulsory labor, and the physical punishment imposed by instructors. Urban students and graduates often had more access to political discussion than villagers, and some formed informal circles in dormitories and hostels where nationalist ideas were debated. The Japanese also operated training courses for young leaders, and courses on Indonesian history and nationalism could be used for purposes the sponsors did not intend.

Seinendan and Keibodan must be kept distinct. Seinendan primarily mobilized young people for physical and ideological preparation, while Keibodan functioned as an auxiliary to local policing, surveillance, and public order. Both depended on the existing administrative hierarchy, and participation could range from eagerness to comply with a popular program to reluctant service under official pressure. Members sometimes helped enforce occupation rules; some later joined movements challenging Japanese authority. Their subsequent political trajectories cannot erase the coercive actions they may have performed during wartime. Nor can the later prominence of some activists justify generalizing about every student or village participant.

The result was the emergence of a politically active generation known as pemuda, a term meaning youth that acquired a political meaning in these years. Many pemuda were more radical and impatient than the older leaders, who had experience of Dutch repression and were cautious about Japanese power. Japanese policy aimed to discipline youth but also to harness their energy, and the sponsors lost control as the war ended. This organizational foundation explains the youth pressure on Sukarno and Hatta in August 1945 (Section 138.19).

The category pemuda eventually acquired a political resonance larger than the organizations that trained individual youths. It was not an institution with one membership roll but a term applied to young people who demanded a rapid break with colonial and Japanese tutelage. By the final months of occupation, rumors of Japanese defeat, contacts among students, and experience in military or civil associations gave some activists the confidence to challenge senior politicians. This did not produce an agreed nationwide youth program. Networks were uneven, especially beyond Java, and young nationalists differed in ideology and methods. The transformation is that Japanese authorities had helped develop habits of mobilization while failing to determine what all participants would do when the regime collapsed.

When analyzing these organizations, compare what participants learned with the rules they were ordered to enforce. The same trained youth might distribute a ration notice one month and join a demand for independent political action the next. Neither possibility should be assumed in the absence of evidence about that person, town, or unit. Regional differences in schooling and administration also limited how far the organizations reached.

Section 138.15

Underground Opposition, Local Rebellions, and the Blitar PETA Uprising

Resistance to Japanese rule took many forms and was not a single movement. Sutan Sjahrir and a network of socialist and student activists kept contact through small groups, listened to foreign radio broadcasts despite bans, and avoided open cooperation; Kempeitai arrests and torture made clandestine work dangerous, and several underground networks were broken. Other groups, including communists such as those associated with Amir Sjarifuddin, who was arrested in 1943, also operated underground. Public cooperation and clandestine opposition coexisted, and individuals sometimes did both, as when nationalists in official positions passed information to underground circles.

Resistance arose from several sources rather than one central underground. Sjahrir's circles maintained personal and political contacts and tried to follow international developments beyond Japanese censorship. Other groups were embedded in religious schools, local communities, or armed units. Their opposition could reflect anti-imperial nationalism, religious grievance, anger at forced labor, defense of local authority, or direct experience of military abuse. The relative importance of these motives varied among individuals and events. The absence of a single national guerrilla command is therefore not evidence that resistance did not exist; it means historians must reconstruct separate networks whose surviving sources differ in quality and purpose.

Rebellions arose mainly from religious communities that resisted specific Japanese demands. In Aceh, in November 1942, villagers around Cot Plieng under the religious leader Teungku Abdul Djalil fought Japanese troops after a dispute over religious practice and demands on the community, and were crushed with heavy loss of life. In February 1944 at Singaparna in West Java, Kyai Zainal Mustafa and his students rose after he refused the bowing ceremony and rice deliveries; Japanese forces suppressed the revolt, and Zainal Mustafa was captured and later executed. These rebellions showed the limits of Japanese religious policy and the willingness of rural communities to resist, but they were local and isolated.

At Singaparna in West Java, the confrontation associated with Zainal Mustafa emerged amid tensions over Japanese ritual demands and local religious authority. In Aceh, Abdul Djalil's opposition reflected a different regional political and religious setting in which ulama, chiefs, and Japanese officers competed over legitimacy. Military repression followed these conflicts and helped discourage more open challenges. Such episodes also demonstrate the danger of interpreting resistance only through the later constitutional politics of Jakarta. A village or pesantren could resist Japanese orders without participating in Sukarno's organizations or anticipating the exact political configuration of an independent Indonesia.

The most significant revolt arose within a Japanese-sponsored institution. On 14 February 1945, soldiers of a PETA battalion at Blitar in East Java rose against the Japanese. The leaders included young platoon commander Supriyadi and other officers, and the grievances included the mistreatment of Indonesians by Japanese instructors, the hardship inflicted on villagers by rice and labor demands, which the soldiers saw in their home districts, and nationalist resentment. The rebels attacked Japanese personnel and installations but did not receive support from other PETA units, and Japanese forces, aided by loyal PETA units, put down the revolt within days. Many rebels were captured; several leaders were tried and executed or imprisoned, while Supriyadi disappeared and his fate has never been established. He remained a symbol of resistance and was later named in the first republican cabinet, though he never took up the post.

The Blitar PETA revolt of 14 February 1945 provides a distinctive case because its organizers had been trained by Japan. Supriyadi and other officers drew on grievances over humiliating treatment, the suffering of local people, and the contradictions between promises of Indonesian dignity and the realities of coercion. The rising was poorly positioned to spread widely and was suppressed. Arrests, trials, and sentences created records from the viewpoint of Japanese military discipline, while later Indonesian accounts framed the rebellion as a nationalist achievement. Those sources cannot simply be combined without scrutiny. Supriyadi's ultimate fate remains unresolved. The revolt shows that military training could create the practical capacity and solidarity for rebellion even when the institution supplying that training had been established to defend Japanese rule.

The revolt demonstrates that Japanese military training could produce opposition to Japan, but it also shows the obstacles to coordination: the rebels lacked contact with other units, the Japanese retained overwhelming force, and the repression strengthened the Japanese resolve to control PETA. It did not lead directly to independence. Resistance did not develop as a nationally coordinated movement because of geographic fragmentation, Japanese security measures, the divisions among Indonesian groups, and the calculation of many leaders that open rebellion would fail.

Section 138.16

Sumatra, Kalimantan, Sulawesi, Bali, and the Eastern Archipelago

Occupation outside Java followed different paths. In Sumatra, the Twenty-Fifth Army reached agreements with local rulers and religious leaders and used mass organizations, including a Sumatran advisory council and volunteer units, though less elaborately than on Java. In northern Sumatra the Japanese initially relied on the traditional aristocracy (uleebalang) in Aceh, as the Dutch had, while religious scholars (ulama) expanded their influence, setting up tensions that would erupt in the revolution. Oil, rubber, and the transit of troops and laborers across the island made it strategically significant, and Sumatran laborers were recruited for projects including the Pekanbaru railway.

Sumatra's wartime history varied substantially by locality. The oil region around Palembang was central to Japan's strategic calculations, while northern districts including Aceh had strong Islamic and regional political traditions. Japanese officers negotiated with existing elites while demanding labor, supplies, and compliance, and the outcome depended partly on relationships formed before the war. Sumatran volunteer formations and administrative arrangements arose under the Twenty-Fifth Army, not through automatic extension of Java's system. In some districts, wartime disruption weakened established rulers; elsewhere, Japanese military appointments temporarily reinforced local authorities. These developments mattered for postwar arguments about legitimacy and control outside Java.

In the Navy areas, administration was typically more restrictive. The Navy banned nationalist organizations that the Army tolerated on Java, preferring to govern through local rulers and officials, and treated its territories as sources of resources and as strategic bases. As Allied air and sea attacks intensified from 1944, these areas, which were closer to the front, suffered bombing, blockade, and food and supply shortages.

West Kalimantan provides an especially stark contrast to nationalist mass mobilization on Java. Japanese repression in 1943–1944 targeted members of princely houses, community leaders, officials, intellectuals, and other suspected opponents in the Pontianak and Mandor region. Arrests and executions destroyed social and political leadership across communities. Estimates of the dead vary widely, in part because of incomplete lists, retrospective investigations, and the changing populations counted by memorial sources. The often-cited regional memorial figure of 21,037 should be identified as a commemorative claim rather than a verified census. The central historical fact is the deliberate destruction of much of the region's leadership and the lasting difficulty of rebuilding local institutions after occupation.

The most severe episode of Japanese violence occurred in West Kalimantan. In 1943–1944 the Japanese authorities, alleging a plot, arrested and killed large numbers of local elites, including the Sultan of Pontianak and other rulers, officials, Chinese community leaders, intellectuals, and others, in what are known as the Pontianak incidents, with killings at sites including Mandor. Estimates of the number killed vary widely, with some Indonesian sources and memorials giving figures above twenty thousand and other estimates lower; the destruction of much of the region's educated and ruling class had lasting consequences, and the events remain central to regional historical memory.

In Sulawesi, the Moluccas, Bali, and nearby islands, naval administration, shipping disruption, and proximity to military operations produced still other experiences. Indonesian-language nationalism did not carry exactly the same institutional opportunities as in Jakarta, while locally influential chiefs, religious figures, and traders often mediated contact with Japanese forces. Coastal populations could face bombing, naval requisitioning, population movements, or loss of livelihoods when fisheries and inter-island commerce were interrupted. Japanese power also weakened as the United States and its allies captured bases and advanced across the western Pacific. A regional comparison therefore tests the chapter's general argument: Japanese rule did not create identical political capacities or inflict identical burdens across the archipelago, and independence would have to address that inherited unevenness.

In Bali, Sulawesi, and elsewhere, local rulers and societies adapted in diverse ways. Bali, under the Navy, saw its own rice and labor demands and the creation of local volunteer units; Sulawesi's rulers, such as those of South Sulawesi, were drawn into the administration, and some experienced both cooperation and pressure. These regions did not follow Java's nationalist trajectory, and many local leaders in 1945 had limited contact with the Jakarta-centered movement. Their attitudes toward the independence proclaimed on Java, and toward a possible return of the Dutch, differed accordingly.

Section 138.17 · Division V — The Final Crisis and the Conditions for Independence, 1944–1945

Japan's Deteriorating Military Position and the Promise of Independence

By mid-1944 Japan's strategic position was collapsing. The loss of the Mariana Islands in June and July brought American bombers within range of the home islands and opened the way to the Philippines; Allied submarines and aircraft were destroying shipping; and Japanese garrisons in the archipelago were increasingly isolated, with later Allied bypassing leaving large forces cut off. Japanese planners expected an Allied advance toward the Indies and needed to ensure that the population would remain loyal, and that Indonesian forces would support defense.

Koiso's promise on 7 September 1944 must be placed in the changing military situation. Japan had already lost the strategic initiative at sea and was increasingly unable to reinforce or supply its far-flung island positions. The loss of vital naval capabilities and the advance of Allied forces through the Pacific made a future attack on the Indies more plausible. Political concessions became a means of maintaining Indonesian cooperation when the occupation authorities needed food, workers, fortifications, and local defense forces more urgently than before. The timing does not mean that Tokyo had suddenly accepted Indonesian sovereignty as an overriding principle; the statement was part of a strategy for surviving a deteriorating war.

On 7 September 1944 Prime Minister Koiso Kuniaki announced in the Japanese Diet that independence would be granted to the "East Indies" in the future. The announcement set no date and defined no territory, and it did not transfer sovereignty. The Army and Navy continued to control administration and to restrict nationalist activity, and the military authorities in the different regions interpreted the announcement differently. The Java administration, which had more experience with Indonesian organizations, moved to permit the display of the red-and-white flag and the singing of "Indonesia Raya," while the Navy remained more cautious.

The promise was also deliberately indefinite. Nationalist leaders gained a new argument in public discussions: the Japanese government had acknowledged that independence was on the agenda. But there was no immediate Indonesian executive able to override the military administration, no sovereign control over foreign affairs, and no settled timetable that Indonesians themselves could enforce. Some Japanese officers favored gradual concessions; others distrusted political mobilization or feared disorder. Local administrations were not always aligned with pronouncements from Tokyo. The gap between the political meaning Indonesians assigned to the promise and the military obligations Japan continued to impose became a growing source of tension.

The announcement nevertheless transformed expectations. Nationalists, who had regarded Japan's promises with suspicion, now had an official commitment that could be cited, and they began planning for the institutions of an independent state. Japanese authorities in turn saw the promise as a way to bind Indonesians to the war effort. Whether Japan would in fact have granted real independence, and on what terms, remains uncertain, because the war ended before the question was tested; many historians stress that the Japanese authorities intended Indonesia to remain closely tied to Japan.

By early 1945 the contradiction was plain. Japanese authorities asked Indonesians to prepare for autonomy while building defenses against Allied landings and continuing to requisition food and labor. Trained youths could interpret participation in state preparation as a step toward self-rule even as their families suffered from the economic crisis. The existence of parallel Japanese and Indonesian agendas meant that developments inside an officially sponsored committee could have effects beyond the intentions of its sponsors. The collapse of Japan's military position did not automatically produce a viable independent state, but it weakened the system that had prevented nationalists from turning a claim to sovereignty into a public political act.

A public pledge of future sovereignty could be powerful even when legally empty. It altered what Indonesian leaders could demand and gave committee participants a reason to prepare proposals. Yet Japanese forces retained weapons, censorship powers, and control of appointments. The contrast between public recognition and practical coercion should remain central when assessing later claims that independence was a Japanese grant.

Section 138.18

BPUPKI, Pancasila, and Constitutional Preparations in 1945

The Investigating Committee for Preparatory Work for Independence, BPUPKI (Badan Penyelidik Usaha-Usaha Persiapan Kemerdekaan Indonesia), was announced in March 1945, formally established in late April, and began its principal deliberations in Jakarta on 29 May, with Radjiman Wediodiningrat as chair. Its members, some sixty or so, included nationalists, Muslim leaders, regional representatives, and a small number of Chinese, Arab, and Dutch-descended members, together with Japanese observers. It was a Japanese-sponsored body, convened by the Sixteenth Army and operating only for Java, but its debates ranged over the future of the whole archipelago.

The constitutional discussions did not begin in an institutional vacuum. Since the 1920s Indonesian leaders had debated representation, religion, territorial boundaries, and the form of a postcolonial state, but the Dutch administration had prevented them from deciding these questions nationally. BPUPKI gave selected leaders a formal setting in which competing ideas could be recorded and compared. Its status nevertheless remained limited: membership was approved within a Japanese-sponsored process, and no committee possessed authority to end military occupation. Students should distinguish an opportunity to formulate a constitution from the possession of constituent sovereignty.

The committee debated the territory of the future state, with Mohammad Yamin and others proposing a large territory including the Malay Peninsula and parts of Borneo and Timor, while Hatta and others were wary of such claims; the structure of government, with Soepomo arguing for an "integralist" state in which ruler and people were united, and others, including Hatta and Yamin, emphasizing individual rights and representative government; and the place of religion, a divide between those who wanted a state based on Islam and those who wanted a secular nationalist state. Sukarno's address on 1 June, in which he proposed five principles as a philosophical basis, offered a way to bridge these divisions.

The first session revealed multiple approaches to the ideological foundations of a future republic. Some speakers emphasized a unified national community and strong state institutions; others stressed representative government, Islam, or guarantees of social welfare. Sukarno's 1 June speech offered Pancasila as a way to frame nationalism, humanity, deliberation, social justice, and belief in God within a shared national project. The version and order of its principles developed in later drafting, and readers should not project the final constitutional formulation backward onto every sentence of the speech. The speech is historically important because it records a political proposal made in debate, not because it settled every unresolved question.

A smaller committee, the Committee of Nine, worked out a compromise preamble, signed on 22 June and known as the Jakarta Charter. Its first principle included an obligation for Muslims to observe Islamic law, a formula that satisfied many Muslim members but was later objected to by non-Muslims and some secular nationalists. A second session in July approved drafts of a constitution and addressed territory, though many issues remained unresolved. The formal adoption of the constitution and the alteration of the Jakarta Charter formula belong to Chapter 147.

The Committee of Nine's Jakarta Charter of 22 June reflected negotiation over religion and state. The draft placed an Islamic-obligation formula alongside a claim to Indonesian nationhood, but that wording was contested and would not survive unchanged in the constitutional settlement adopted after independence. Differences over the place of Islamic law, the status of religious minorities, and the geographic scope of the country had implications far beyond the committee room. The BPUPKI proceedings consequently provide a window into the diversity of Indonesian nationalism at the moment of Japanese defeat. Their importance cannot be reduced to a simple sequence of founding dates: the discussions disclosed political disagreements that independence would have to address rather than dissolve.

BPUPKI's work created a constitutional foundation and a common vocabulary, and gave Indonesian leaders experience in drafting a state. But it operated in a Japanese-sponsored process whose future depended on Japan's military fortunes, and its deliberations were overtaken by the surrender. It also reflected the limited representation of the Outer Islands and the lack of popular participation.

Section 138.19

PPKI, Japanese Surrender, and the Crisis of Political Authority

In August 1945 BPUPKI was replaced by the Preparatory Committee for Indonesian Independence (PPKI), a smaller body with representatives from beyond Java, chaired by Sukarno with Hatta as vice-chair. Sukarno, Hatta, and Radjiman flew to Dalat in southern Indochina to meet Field Marshal Terauchi Hisaichi, commander of the Southern Army, who told them that preparations for independence could proceed and indicated a possible timetable. They returned to Jakarta around 14 August expecting a Japanese-supervised transfer.

The Preparatory Committee for Indonesian Independence, or PPKI, was authorized on 7 August 1945 as the next stage in the Japanese-sponsored process. Its composition and authority were shaped by the wartime regime, even though participating Indonesian leaders intended to use the committee for national purposes. Sukarno, Hatta, and Radjiman departed on 9 August for a meeting with Field Marshal Terauchi at Dalat in Indochina, where the audience took place on 12 August. A chronology that dates the meeting to 9–11 August confuses the journey with the meeting itself. The distinction matters because Japan's military position changed radically during those same days.

Events overtook the plan. The atomic bombing of Hiroshima on 6 August, the Soviet declaration of war on 8 August, and the bombing of Nagasaki on 9 August led Japan to accept surrender terms, announced by the emperor on 15 August. In Indonesia, the news arrived by foreign radio and through informal channels, and Japanese authorities were ordered to maintain the status quo pending the arrival of the Allies, who had promised to restore order. Japanese officers in Java were uncertain whether they could allow an independence declaration at all.

Events outside Indonesia made the committee's intended timetable increasingly precarious. The atomic bombing of Hiroshima on 6 August, the Soviet declaration of war and offensive against Japan beginning 8–9 August, and the bombing of Nagasaki on 9 August accelerated the Japanese leadership's decision to surrender. Different officials in the Indies received information at different times, and censorship, military discipline, and uncertainty limited what could be said openly. By 15 August the public surrender announcement undermined the political legitimacy of independence granted as a future Japanese concession. Indonesian leaders now had to decide whether to act through a body created by Japan or assert independence on a different basis.

The surrender created a crisis of authority. Younger activists, including students and former members of youth organizations, wanted an immediate declaration by Indonesians themselves, free from any appearance of Japanese sponsorship. Sukarno and Hatta, more cautious, wished to proceed through the PPKI and to avoid a bloody confrontation with Japanese troops who were still armed. This disagreement over timing, authority, and the political meaning of an independence declaration set the stage for the events of 16–17 August, which belong to Chapter 147. The announcement of surrender on 15 August should be distinguished from the sequence that led to the declaration on 17 August.

The final crisis brought political generations and strategies into sharper conflict. Some senior figures wanted a declaration that minimized bloodshed and provided an orderly route to government; younger activists feared any procedure that allowed independence to be portrayed as Japan's gift. Contacts among student and military networks made rapid action more thinkable, while warnings from Japanese officers created practical uncertainty. Rear Admiral Tadashi Maeda, the naval liaison official in Jakarta, maintained relationships with nationalist leaders, but his role should be set within the conflicting positions of Japanese army and navy authorities rather than presented as a unified Japanese decision. The detailed negotiations of 16–17 August belong to Chapter 147. The essential point here is that Japanese surrender transformed the question from when Japan would authorize independence to who possessed the authority to proclaim it.

The calendar of these days must be handled precisely: a delegation’s departure, an audience in Dalat, the announcement of surrender, and the Indonesian declaration are distinct events with different political consequences. Treating them as one compressed sequence risks attributing decisions to an authority that had already lost the capacity to make them effective.

Section 138.20

Indonesia on the Threshold of Independence: Occupation, Nationalism, and Historical Transformation

The occupation produced contradictory results. On one side it destroyed Dutch colonial prestige, removed European administrators, expanded the participation of Indonesians in administration, gave Sukarno and Hatta public stature, created organizations such as PUTERA and Jawa Hokokai that reached into villages, trained tens of thousands of young Indonesians in PETA and youth corps, made Indonesian the language of administration, and set up committees that began constitutional planning. These changes enlarged Indonesian capacity to organize and to assert claims.

The impact of occupation cannot be measured by its final political outcome alone. The collapse of Dutch military authority removed a powerful barrier to nationalist organization, but it did not settle the claims of different regions, religious communities, or social classes. Java emerged as the organizational center largely because Japanese authorities developed there a particularly extensive administrative and mobilization apparatus. Elsewhere, repression, naval priorities, or disrupted communications limited comparable opportunities. The future republic would inherit unequal experiences of wartime government, along with different expectations about who should exercise authority and how national resources should be distributed.

On the other side the occupation imposed heavy human costs: forced labor and the death and displacement of romusha, rice requisitions, a famine on Java that killed a very large number of people, inflation and shortages, the repression of political activity by the Kempeitai, sexual violence and sexual slavery, and mass killings such as those in West Kalimantan. The same networks that provided political opportunities, the tonarigumi, the Hokokai, nationalist leaders, and local officials, also carried out extraction and recruitment, and Indonesians suffered at the hands of their own leaders and officials as well as of Japanese. Any interpretation that treats the occupation merely as a stage in the road to independence must account for these costs.

Political capacity grew through institutions designed for purposes other than national liberation. Administrative officials learned to manage large programs without European supervisors; public leaders addressed crowds; committee members debated constitutions; military recruits acquired techniques of command; and youth groups practiced collective mobilization. These experiences were real, yet they should not be treated as evidence of benevolent Japanese intention. Japan created organizations to obtain compliance, manpower, and resources for its war, and it could repress members who ceased serving those goals. After surrender, people and skills could be redirected, but this redirection depended on Indonesian choices made in a changing international situation.

The consequences varied by region and group. Java saw the greatest mobilization and the heaviest rice and labor demands; the Navy areas saw less political organization and harsher security measures; religious communities experienced both cooperation and clashes; women, laborers, and farmers faced burdens that differed from those of urban nationalists. These variations affected how different parts of the archipelago would respond to the proclamation of independence and to the return of the Dutch.

The human cost of these developments was profound. Families suffered from food shortages, coercive labor selection, disease, and the disappearance of relatives. Women confronted new burdens and dangers; communities in West Kalimantan and other regions endured repression that damaged local political leadership. For many survivors, the war was remembered through hunger and separation rather than constitutional committees. Any claim that occupation accelerated independence must therefore be evaluated alongside the suffering imposed by the same system and should not imply that the consequences were somehow necessary or justified.

By mid-August 1945, therefore, Indonesian leaders had organizational experience, military and paramilitary cadres, a language and a draft ideology, and a promise of independence, and the sudden collapse of the occupying power had opened a vacuum. They did not have recognized sovereignty, secure control of the territory, or a unified armed force; Japanese troops remained in place, Allied forces were expected, and the Dutch intended to return. On 17 August 1945 Sukarno and Hatta proclaimed independence. That act, and the struggle to make it effective, belong to Chapters 146 to 148.

Finally, the approaching declaration was not equivalent to effective sovereignty. Japanese military forces remained on the islands, the Allies had not yet recognized a new state, and the Dutch government intended to reestablish authority. Indonesian leaders faced the task of converting a political claim into institutions, territorial control, and international acceptance. The proclamation on 17 August would be a decisive opening, not the end of conflict. By stopping at that threshold, this chapter explains both the organizational resources that independence advocates could mobilize and the fragmentation, violence, and uncertainty that made the subsequent Indonesian Revolution such a difficult struggle.

Historical Synthesis and Debate

Did Japanese Occupation Accelerate Indonesian Independence despite Its Coercive and Destructive Character?

The question: To what extent did the occupation make independence possible, and at what cost?

Interpretation A — acceleration: Some historians argue that the destruction of Dutch authority, the mobilization of the population, military training, and the language and administrative changes created conditions that would not have existed otherwise, and that the revolution drew directly on them.
Interpretation B — exploitation and disruption: Others emphasize that Japanese rule was exploitative, that its institutions were designed to serve Japanese purposes, that it brought famine and forced labor, and that nationalists would have continued to organize without it; they caution against crediting the occupation with benefits.
Interpretation C — Indonesian agency: Others stress that Indonesians used the opportunities for their own ends and that independence was proclaimed and defended by Indonesians, not conferred by Japan.

Evidence: Organizational records, memoirs, demographic data, and the pattern of the revolution inform each position.

Points of disagreement: Counterfactual claims about what would have happened without the occupation cannot be tested; disagreement also concerns weighting of the costs and benefits.

Why it matters: The question shapes national memory, in which the occupation is remembered both as a time of suffering and as a time of preparation.

Student task: Write a response that weighs both sides of the culminating question: how did the occupation exploit and devastate Indonesian society while creating conditions that helped make independence possible?

Chapter Summary

Japan's conquest of the Netherlands East Indies in early 1942 was a multi-island campaign driven by the need for oil. Beginning at Tarakan in January, Japanese forces seized Borneo's and Sumatra's oil regions, cut off Java by sea and air, destroyed the Allied naval force in the Java Sea, and forced the capitulation of the Dutch and Allied command on Java on 8–9 March. The speed of the collapse destroyed the prestige of Dutch rule, though fighting continued elsewhere.

The occupation was not administered as a unit. The Sixteenth Army governed Java and Madura, the Twenty-Fifth Army Sumatra, and the Navy the eastern archipelago. These jurisdictions had different priorities, permitted different degrees of political activity, and imposed different burdens, and the fragmentation of communications reinforced regional differences.

Japanese military government kept much of the Dutch bureaucratic structure while replacing its European head, expanding Indonesian participation in administration without granting autonomy. The tonarigumi extended state reach to the household level and were used for rationing, surveillance, and labor recruitment, while the Kempeitai enforced a ban on independent political activity.

Japanese authorities released and employed Sukarno and Hatta, and mobilized society through the Three-A Movement, PUTERA, the Central Advisory Council, and Jawa Hokokai. The leaders' cooperation gave them public platforms and organizational resources, but also tied them to propaganda and labor recruitment. Historians disagree about how to judge these choices, and the alternatives, including Sjahrir's underground work, carried risks of their own. Japanese policy toward Islam produced Masyumi and Hizbullah, while religious leaders responded in varied ways, from cooperation to rebellion.

Wartime economic priorities reshaped the archipelago. Oil production was restored but fell short of targets, while rubber, tin, and plantation exports declined and shipping losses stranded resources. On Java, compulsory rice deliveries, labor mobilization, input shortages, and transport collapse contributed to a famine in 1944–1945 whose mortality remains disputed, with estimates ranging from several hundred thousand to over two million.

Romusha labor was central to the occupation experience. Recruitment worked through Japanese officials, Indonesian administrators, village heads, and nationalist organizations, and workers faced malnutrition, disease, and violence, especially when sent to other islands and overseas. Totals of recruits and deaths are uncertain, and the categories of local labor, interisland transfer, and overseas transfer must be distinguished. Women and families bore heavy burdens, including shortages, inflation, sexual violence, and sexual slavery, while Indonesian became the language of public life.

Militarization produced PETA, the Heiho, and youth corps such as the Seinendan and Keibodan, which trained tens of thousands of Indonesians and helped create the politically active pemuda generation. Opposition arose from religious rebellions in Aceh and Singaparna, from underground networks, and from within PETA itself at Blitar in February 1945. Resistance was fragmentary, and PETA was not the direct ancestor of the later national army.

As Japan's position collapsed, the Koiso announcement of September 1944 promised future independence without transferring sovereignty. BPUPKI debated territory, government, and religion in 1945, producing Sukarno's Pancasila address and the Jakarta Charter. The PPKI and the Dalat meeting prepared a Japanese-supervised transfer, but the surrender on 15 August produced a crisis in which youth pressure and uncertainty shaped the leaders' choices.

The occupation was a contradictory transformation. It enlarged Indonesian organizational capacity and political expectations while inflicting famine, forced labor, repression, and violence, and its effects varied by region and social group. It made a declaration of independence possible, but sovereignty had still to be won, a story told in Chapters 146 to 148.

Chapter Reflection

The Japanese occupation matters because it joined the end of one imperial order to the opening of a struggle over the character of the state that would replace it. It removed the Dutch hierarchy, but substituted a harsher foreign regime whose demands fell most heavily on villagers, and it created political and military capacities that its sponsors never meant to place in Indonesian hands.

It also shows how political opportunity can arise from conditions of suffering without justifying them. Indonesian leaders used Japanese institutions to build organization and to define a national ideology, but some of those same institutions carried out the extraction and recruitment that killed and impoverished many Indonesians. Honest history keeps both facts in view, and does not allow either the opportunities or the costs to cancel the other.

The chapter's themes recur. The Java-centered character of mobilization shaped the geography of the revolution, the debate over religion and state returned in 1945 and later, the pemuda and former PETA and Heiho members were central to the armed struggle, and the memory of famine, romusha, and killings shaped postwar politics. Chapters 141 to 145 will place these experiences in a regional context, and Chapters 146 to 148 will show how the conditions described here were tested when independence had to be defended.
