Johor-Riau and the Malay Successor World

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Johor-Riau's power rested less on a fixed capital than on royal legitimacy, maritime alliances, commercial relationships, and the loyalty of mobile people across the straits.

When the Portuguese took Melaka in 1511, the Melakan royal family lost its port but not its claim to rule. Over the next two centuries its descendants rebuilt authority along the Johor River and among the islands of the Riau and Lingga archipelagos, moving their courts repeatedly as war, commerce, and faction required.

This chapter examines that successor world in three stages: the reconstruction of Melakan kingship after 1511; the crisis of 1699, when Sultan Mahmud Shah II was killed and the Melakan line was effectively ended; and the Malay–Bugis settlement of 1722, which created a shared government of a Malay sultan and Bugis Yang di-Pertuan Muda.

The story is told from Johor's side. Chapter 80 examined Aceh's expansion under Iskandar Muda, and Chapters 69 and 70 the Portuguese; here they appear as rivals and occasional partners of a kingdom that was itself an active political and commercial power. Historians rely on Malay court chronicles, Dutch and Portuguese records, and surviving royal letters, all of which carry the interests of their authors. South Sulawesi and the Makassar War belong to Chapter 82; Bugis migration is treated here only as it shaped Johor.

The geography matters to the plot. The Johor River could shelter a court from hostile shipping, but the islands around Bintan, Riau, and the Singapore Strait were also homes of experienced sailors, fishers, traders, and chiefs. A ruler who claimed authority across those waterways had to maintain relationships with people capable of navigating them. The Orang Laut were indispensable, but their obligations, opportunities, and loyalties differed among communities. Royal authority was therefore mobile and negotiated, even where court language presented it as enduring and sacred.

A second thread is commerce. Johor was an entrepôt because merchants exchanged goods drawn from many regions, not because the court possessed all the lands where those goods originated. Pepper, tin, rice, cloth, forest products, and maritime goods passed through ports sustained by brokers, pilots, craft workers, and a degree of security. Diplomacy with the Dutch East India Company could help remove Portuguese competition, but arrangements that benefited Johor at one moment could produce disputes about rights, taxes, and shipping later.

The history also includes ruptures within the Malay political world. The Johor-Jambi wars showed that Southeast Asian states had ambitions and rivalries independent of the European empires. The killing of Mahmud Shah II in 1699 generated a disagreement about the legitimacy of kingship itself, because the bendahara who became sultan could not simply inherit the old dynastic prestige by decree. Raja Kecil then asserted a rival claim, and the Bugis military and commercial networks that supported Raja Sulaiman in 1722 acquired a lasting place in government.

The documentary record permits several ways of approaching these problems. The treaties of 1606 show what Johor and Dutch negotiators agreed to record, while a royal letter of 1713 reveals specific commercial disputes from the viewpoint of the Johor ruler. Later Malay histories preserve remembered arguments over descent, obligation, and political partnership but were written after the events they narrate. This chapter identifies those different evidentiary positions rather than treating all surviving accounts as interchangeable testimony.

Historical Context

Melaka (Chapter 64) had been a leading port of the fifteenth century, ruled by a Muslim sultanate whose court traditions shaped Malay ideas of kingship. After the Portuguese conquest (Chapter 69), Melaka's merchants and rulers dispersed rather than vanished: networks moved to Johor, Aceh, Patani, and Brunei (Chapter 70). The Dutch East India Company (Chapter 77) arrived in the early seventeenth century seeking trade and enemies of Portugal, and built its base at Batavia (Chapter 79). Johor therefore operated among Aceh, Portugal, the VOC, and neighbouring Malay and Sumatran polities, none of which could fully dominate the straits.

The history of Johor is inseparable from the geography of the western Indonesian archipelago and the Malay Peninsula. The distinction between peninsular and island spaces became sharper in later colonial maps than it was in many early modern political relationships. Traders, rulers, and communities operated across waterways that now divide countries. The word Johor can therefore refer to a court, a riverine region, a trading system, or a larger political sphere depending on the date and source.

Evidence is particularly rich where Johor met Dutch commercial interests, yet this archival abundance has consequences. Company reports record negotiations, customs complaints, and military threats more often than the lives of ordinary households. Malay texts center rulers and noble lineages. The historian must read them together, retain uncertainty about uncorroborated genealogies and stories of regicide, and avoid using later descriptions of Riau commerce to supply numerical details for a much earlier period.

Division I · Section 81.1

The Johor River, the Singapore Strait, and the Riau Archipelago

The southern Malay Peninsula and nearby islands form a web of sheltered channels linking the Strait of Malacca with the South China Sea. Rivers brought forest goods to the coast; islands such as Bintan offered anchorages; monsoon winds set the rhythm of visiting traders. A ruler here governed waterways and settlements, not a bordered territory. Johor Lama, Batu Sawar, Bintan, and Riau were centres at different times, not simultaneous capitals.

The landscape looks compact on a modern map, but distance for a seventeenth-century sailor was measured in tides, currents, weather, safe anchorages, and the availability of people who knew each channel. The Johor River offered an interior refuge accessible from the coast but not easily seized by a warship unfamiliar with its approaches. Islands off its mouth provided alternative landing places, routes for dispatching messengers, and vantage points from which vessels could be observed. Bintan and nearby islands linked those river settlements to the broader traffic of the Singapore Strait. This physical environment allowed political centers to shift without the whole commercial and social system disappearing. It also meant that the ability of a court to direct movement depended on local expertise rather than on possessing a continuous strip of land.

The strait itself was not a single road with one gate. Ships could pass south of Singapore toward the South China Sea, turn through the Riau islands, stop at mainland rivers, or seek passages toward western Sumatra and the wider Indonesian archipelago. The pattern varied by vessel size and season, and captains balanced speed against water, provisions, protection, and information. Johor's rulers therefore pursued influence over stopping places and maritime groups who supplied services to shipping. Controlling a useful harbor meant more than collecting a toll at its entrance: it meant arranging trustworthy exchange, ensuring that imported cloth could be purchased, and making it worthwhile for a merchant to return on the next monsoon. Rival courts could contest these relationships without necessarily occupying the same land.

The islands were connected to the mainland by ordinary work as well as royal expeditions. Boat crews brought fish, preserved seafood, and other marine products into exchange with rice, cloth, pottery, and iron tools. River settlements received timber, forest products, and agricultural supplies from communities further inland. Court officials needed such provisions to support retainers and visiting traders. Some residents depended on a particular village or garden, while others maintained ties across several anchorages. These differences matter for political geography: a Malay ruler's following might include people whose main residence lay many sailing days from the palace. The center of political life was the capacity to summon, reward, protect, and coordinate those people, not an uninterrupted military presence at every point on the map.

Modern place names may conceal the changes students need to investigate. The historical Johor River court should not automatically be identified with present-day Johor Bahru, and the early political importance of Riau should not be confused with the later nineteenth-century Riau-Lingga sultanate. Some names describe a settlement, some an island group, and some a court or political jurisdiction whose extent changed. Cartographers working for Dutch or British patrons might depict a major trading port accurately while representing competing claims of sovereignty only imperfectly. Contemporary maps also omitted many small settlements important to sea-oriented communities. A useful comparison therefore puts a historical chart beside an account of a voyage or a letter listing places where a ruler claimed to have subjects.

The geography helps explain both resilience and vulnerability. Multiple safe anchorages allowed a ruler to evade an enemy fleet, but they also gave dissatisfied chiefs places to shift their allegiance. If a war destroyed warehouses, merchants could seek another market; if it drove away skilled pilots and trading partners, the political damage might last much longer. The river mouth did not guarantee security against Portuguese artillery, Acehnese campaigns, or neighboring Malay rivals. Nor did islands naturally belong to whichever ruler occupied the largest capital. The exercise of authority rested on recognition by particular communities and their leaders, often renewed through visits, marriage alliances, gifts, and protection. The chapter's map should therefore be read as a network of waterways and relationships rather than an unchanging shaded territory.

Section 81.2

From Melaka to Johor: Dynastic Exile and Reconstruction, 1511–1540

After 1511 Sultan Mahmud Shah withdrew, eventually basing himself around Bintan. His son Alauddin Riayat Shah II built a new centre on the Johor River at Kota Batu, later called Johor Lama. Johor kept Melakan titles, claims, and commercial connections but never recovered Melaka. It was a successor reconstructed through migration and alliance, not an unbroken government of Melaka.

The Portuguese conquest transformed a political center without extinguishing its connections. The exiled Mahmud Shah continued to claim the authority of the Melakan dynasty, and his movements through the region showed that an expelled ruler could still attract followers and alarm rivals. The island of Bintan became one center for attempts to renew resistance to Portuguese Melaka. Portuguese forces attacked Bintan in 1526, further restricting the possibilities for reclaiming the old port by force. When Mahmud Shah died in 1528, the question facing his successors was not simply where to build another palace. They needed to recreate a court able to organize armed followers, recognize nobles, receive merchants, and offer protection to populations already accustomed to several competing rulers.

Alauddin Riayat Shah II's establishment along the Johor River was a political response to this challenge. The early center at Pekan Tua and the movement toward Kota Batu, later called Johor Lama, are commonly placed across the later 1520s and 1530s; the exact institutional sequence is reconstructed from later accounts and outside notices, not a continuous palace archive. The river gave the new dynasty access to the Singapore Strait while allowing the court to withdraw inland if threatened from the sea. Around it could assemble a mosque, royal residence, landing places, markets, retainers' houses, and facilities serving visiting ships. These arrangements did not reproduce every institution of Melaka, but they gave recognizable form to a revived Malay royal center.

Claims of succession served practical purposes. A merchant who recognized the Johor ruler as an heir to Melakan authority might expect familiar rules for customs, contracts, safe conduct, and disputes. A subordinate chief might seek titles or confirmation of an inherited privilege. Royal marriage linked households that could supply vessels and military assistance. Courtly language about descent therefore connected to trade and governance. Yet the court's ability to make these claims meaningful depended on resources it did not simply inherit: ships, men, allies, and favorable relations with neighboring ports. The politics of survival required negotiation as well as appeals to memory. The fact that the dynasty moved made continuity more important as an argument, but it also exposed how much had to be reconstructed.

The Portuguese remained an active adversary. Their fortress in Melaka was a substantial strategic obstacle and a rival commercial center, but Portugal lacked complete control over trade moving through the surrounding seas. Johor could attract traders seeking alternative routes and sometimes cooperate with opponents of Portuguese policies. Still, being a successor to Melaka did not mean controlling every commercial relationship once associated with that port. Other centers, including Aceh and various peninsular and island ports, expanded their own claims. The old Melakan commercial world did not transfer intact to Johor; it was redistributed among several increasingly competitive states and companies.

For historians, the problem is distinguishing a ruler's claim from the reach of his government. A court narrative might describe the realm in terms of traditional loyalty, while Portuguese dispatches noticed only the fleets threatening Melaka and merchants looked for whichever harbor offered profitable conditions. Each perspective identifies something real and something partial. Describing Johor as an adapted Melakan successor captures two truths at once: its dynasty and vocabulary gave it continuity, and its location, alliances, commercial opportunities, and institutions were reassembled under altered circumstances. This historical distinction prepares students to understand why Johor could survive repeated loss of settlements over the next century without instantly losing its identity.

Historical Vocabulary
Daulat and Derhaka

Daulat is the sacral authority attached to a legitimate ruler; derhaka is disloyalty to such a ruler, regarded as gravely wrong. Together they shaped expectations of loyalty in Malay courts. They are ideals expressed in court texts, and actual behaviour varied. They should not be read as proof of unquestioned obedience.

Daulat and derhaka were historical concepts used in arguments about power, not mechanical descriptions of conduct. The 1699 regicide and the acceptance or rejection of Abdul Jalil IV demonstrate that different groups could use inherited ideas of loyalty to reach different political conclusions.

Additional inquiry: How could two groups disagree about loyalty while invoking the same royal tradition?

Section 81.3

The Orang Laut and the Maritime Foundations of Malay Sovereignty

Orang Laut communities served as pilots, navigators, messengers, defenders, suppliers of marine products, and crews for royal fleets. Their ties to rulers rested on protection, service, gifts, and inherited loyalty. They were politically diverse, with different degrees of autonomy; they were not a uniform navy, and the stereotype of all seafarers as pirates misreads the evidence, much of which comes from outside observers hostile to or dependent on them.

The term Orang Laut describes many sea-oriented communities, not a unified ethnic body with one leader or identical occupations. Different groups maintained home waters in the Riau-Lingga islands, the Singapore Strait, and more distant islands and coasts. Their knowledge of reefs, currents, weather, and seasonal anchorages could be decisive for both commerce and war. They served as boat crews, pilots, watchers, messengers, collectors of maritime products, and escorts for royal parties. These functions brought the ruler into contact with a world of movement and intelligence that officials living near the palace could not fully direct. A court without trusted sailors might possess noble titles and written claims but struggle to defend the channels through which merchants approached it.

Relations of service were not necessarily those of salaried soldiers. Some communities furnished crews or products in fulfillment of inherited obligations, and rulers reciprocated with protection, recognition, access to trade, and gifts. The arrangement could be reinforced through personal relationships between a ruler and group leaders, and the consequences of failing to honor them could be severe. Status was unequal, but it was not uniform: some maritime communities enjoyed considerable room to choose their movements, while others faced closer control or obligations resembling corvée. European descriptions often compressed these variations into broad categories such as subjects, slaves, or pirates. A careful account asks what each term meant to the observer and whether it corresponded to obligations recognized by the people concerned.

Maritime expertise linked different kinds of political activity. A vessel carrying news of an incoming fleet could also carry a royal invitation or a message to merchants. Pilots guiding a ruler's war boats could later guide cargo vessels through unfamiliar waters. Families gathering sea cucumbers, shellfish, or other maritime products depended on trading relationships that connected distant islands to royal markets. The same mobility that made these communities valuable could be used to avoid excessive demands or seek the protection of another ruler. Thus the ability of the Johor court to command sea services was continuously negotiated, not a permanent resource guaranteed by the state's name.

The history of regicide and succession makes the significance of these choices especially visible. When the direct royal line was disrupted in 1699, attachment to the prestige of Melakan descent did not automatically transfer to the Bendahara who became sultan. Some Orang Laut groups resisted that succession and considered other overlords, while many remained connected to Johor because of trade and the benefits of continuing relationships. A later claimant, Raja Kecil, appealed to old dynastic loyalties and attracted significant maritime backing. These responses were neither simple ethnic solidarity nor evidence that every Orang Laut community acted alike. They show how beliefs about legitimate kingship could affect the availability of ships, crews, and intelligence.

Terms such as piracy demand similar care. Raiding, wartime seizure, tribute collection, and unlawful attack were not always separated in the same way by Malay rulers, Dutch company officials, independent captains, and modern historians. A ruler might reward armed action against an enemy's shipping that the victim classified as piracy. Conversely, predation could threaten the commerce on which a court depended. Assigning the word indiscriminately to maritime communities obscures the choices of rulers who organized violence and the claims of those who defended maritime livelihoods. Oral traditions and later ethnography can illuminate remembered affiliations, but they must not be treated as transparent records of seventeenth-century events.

This history restores agency to groups too often presented as a background workforce for court-centered narratives. Orang Laut leaders could preserve a maritime alliance, leave a failed patron, gather information about a rival court, or help create a new political center. Their children inherited navigational skills and social networks, but not necessarily a fixed political allegiance. Johor's sovereignty therefore included more than the Sultan's authority over landholding nobles. It depended on relationships with people whose home was a maritime landscape and whose participation was essential to making royal claims effective across that landscape.

Section 81.4

Malay Kingship, Court Officials, and Melakan Political Tradition

Legitimacy rested on lineage, patronage, marriage, and diplomacy. The bendahara (chief minister), temenggung (official responsible for security and order), and orang kaya (wealthy leaders) formed the governing circle. The Sejarah Melayu (Sulalat al-Salatin), associated with Johor's court and Tun Seri Lanang in the early seventeenth century, preserves political ideals and memories while advancing dynastic claims. It survives in several manuscript versions and must not be treated as a single contemporary record.

A court claiming descent from Melaka inherited a powerful language of rulership. Daulat referred to a sacral quality of legitimate authority, while derhaka named a profound offense against the ruler. These concepts did not produce universal obedience; they provided a moral vocabulary through which subjects judged both loyalty and the limits of royal conduct. The bendahara helped coordinate elite affairs and could advise or act for the sultan; the temenggung was associated with order, security, and aspects of policing; and other officeholders had responsibilities shaped by court practice and circumstance. Their roles were neither fixed civil-service job descriptions nor identical in every generation. Local chiefs and maritime leaders retained influence that the palace needed to recognize.

Political authority was demonstrated through visible acts. A royal audience announced the relative standing of visitors, and gifts or titles could confirm a chief's relationship with the court. A dispute over succession might be framed not only as a question of force but as one of rightful descent and appropriate conduct. The obligations of nobles were strengthened by benefits they expected from a ruler: protection, a share of commercial wealth, access to marriage alliances, and confirmation of status. When these ceased, the meaning of fidelity became contested. This tension helps explain why royal ideology mattered intensely in 1699 but did not prevent elite conspirators from killing the sultan.

The Sejarah Melayu, preserved in several related versions, is an especially important witness to this political imagination. Accounts connect the text's early-seventeenth-century recension to Johor and to the court figure Tun Seri Lanang, although the manuscripts and composition history raise questions that resist a simple single-date attribution. Its stories about Melaka's rulers, officials, diplomats, and subjects made the past into a resource for later political thought. The narrative contains memories of court life, moral examples, stories of origins, and legitimating claims. Reading it critically requires asking what later writers wished to remember, how versions differ, and where Portuguese or Dutch evidence supplies alternative viewpoints.

Islamic identity formed part of this inherited world, but local practice cannot be reduced to abstract law. Judges, teachers, ritual specialists, and court advisers could invoke religious authority in matters concerning marriage, contracts, inheritance, and the conduct of rulers. Malay adat provided customary expectations shaped by kinship, rank, and political setting. The two were not simply competing codes; they could be invoked together or interpreted differently by participants in a dispute. A commercial port required practical mechanisms for settling debt, shipping, and property questions involving people from several communities. The extent to which the court directly adjudicated such cases varied with its resources and relations with local intermediaries.

The language of dynastic continuity also linked royal households with distant places. A noble family in Pahang or an island chief might recognize Johor because of historical connections to Melaka, but recognition still required ongoing diplomacy and mutual advantage. A ruler's authority might be acknowledged at a ceremonial audience while daily management remained in the hands of regional leaders. Distinguishing these layers prevents the textbook from mistaking a court's ideal world for an administrative map. It also clarifies the significance of marriage: women in ruling households and the ties created through their marriages could help secure loyalties across regions even when the written record speaks mostly of male officeholders.

The succession to the Bendahara line after 1699 was consequently a constitutional problem in a premodern sense, though not a modern constitutional case. The issue was whether long-serving officials could transform delegated royal authority into kingship after the accepted direct line appeared exhausted. Supporters could argue that a functioning ruler was necessary; opponents could interpret the change as derhaka against inherited sovereignty. The Sejarah Melayu did not dictate one unambiguous answer, but its political language helps historians reconstruct the stakes of the argument. This is why §81.4 belongs at the beginning of the chapter, even though its consequences culminate much later.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Melakan Kingship and Its Johor Successor

Changed: the fixed port capital gave way to mobile river and island courts; royal power depended more on maritime followers. Continued: titles, the language of daulat and derhaka, Islamic court culture, and claims to Melakan descent. Change was gradual and uneven, and most visible to court officials and maritime communities who had to relocate. The pattern matters because it shows how legitimacy could outlast territory.

Johor, Aceh, the Portuguese, and the Dutch

Division II · Section 81.5

Between Aceh and Portuguese Melaka

Johor competed with Aceh and Portuguese Melaka for traffic, pepper, and recognition. Aceh attacked Johor in 1564, and the Portuguese destroyed Johor Lama in 1587. Johor fought, negotiated with, and sought help from both rivals as circumstances changed. Rebuilding elsewhere was a survival strategy, not simply defeat.

The rivalry was part of a larger contest over the political inheritance of Melaka. Johor claimed descent from the displaced rulers, Portuguese officials held the fortress and sought to regulate traffic, and Aceh's sultans presented themselves as powerful Muslim rulers capable of challenging both. None of these states possessed unchallenged authority over the entire strait. Merchants could move between ports, local leaders could negotiate with more than one suzerain, and warfare could unexpectedly redirect trade. Pepper and other commodities made the competition profitable, but religious language, dynastic claims, prestige, and strategic security mattered too. The struggles therefore cannot be understood as a simple ethnic or religious confrontation between Europeans and Asians.

Aceh's assault in 1564 demonstrated the military peril facing Johor from another Muslim polity. Contemporary and later accounts differ in emphases, but the capture and removal of members of the royal leadership underscored that a dynastic capital could be attacked directly. The Portuguese attack on Johor Lama in 1587 was another severe blow. It destroyed an important royal center, yet the court and its networks survived and regrouped elsewhere. Such setbacks shaped the practical value of rivers, islands, and maritime followers. A court prepared to evacuate records, precious goods, retainers, and boats had a better chance of reconstructing authority than a dynasty whose entire political identity depended on defending one walled town.

Cooperation and hostility changed as circumstances changed. Johor might need Portuguese restraint against an Acehnese attack while seeking Dutch assistance to dislodge Portugal from Melaka. European observers were prone to describe such changes as inconsistency or betrayal, but from Johor's perspective alliances were instruments for preserving autonomy. The key questions were what each partner could supply, what concessions would be demanded, and whether aid to one rival would provoke another. The arrival of armed Dutch shipping added leverage without ending the earlier triangle. Political actors in Johor weighed the possible benefit of a distant European partner against the danger of granting a commercial corporation special privileges.

Conflict also altered social life beyond the court. War could interrupt rice shipments, drive artisans and market sellers away, damage landing places, and compel boat crews to perform military service. People associated with the royal household might follow it to new settlements, but farmers and communities tied to specific lands faced different choices. A ruler who relocated had to persuade merchants that the new location was safe and worth visiting. Recovery could thus take longer than a court's formal re-establishment. The experience of non-elite households is fragmentary in the record, yet it should shape the questions historians ask of campaigns commonly narrated as contests between sultans and admirals.

The rivalry with Aceh belongs in the larger chronology of Islam in Southeast Asia, but Islamic identity alone did not determine alliances. Aceh, Johor, and the Muslim traders who served both could have conflicting economic priorities and competing ideas of rightful leadership. Some Acehnese claims over Johor were asserted through dynastic appointments and capture rather than permanent military occupation. Conversely, Johor's diplomacy might involve the Portuguese or Dutch without implying abandonment of its own religious or dynastic tradition. Political survival required a court to separate religious legitimacy from the immediate choice of military partner. This complexity is an indispensable transition to the treaties of 1606 and the Acehnese invasion of 1613.

Students should use the chronology to distinguish destruction from disappearance. Portuguese forces could destroy a settlement without destroying the dynastic claim; Aceh could remove a ruler without obtaining lasting control over every maritime follower; and Johor could sign a treaty with a European company without becoming its dependent colony. Each event changed the conditions of the next, but no single campaign decided who would command the southern strait for the century. The pattern of conflict and recovery helps explain why the later kingdom developed flexible strategies and why disagreements over legal and dynastic authority could remain so consequential.

Section 81.6

The Johor–VOC Alliance and the Diplomacy of 1603–1613

Johor made contact with the new Dutch company around 1603. Two treaties were concluded in 1606, on 17 May and 23 September, with Admiral Cornelis Matelieff de Jonge, in the context of joint campaigns against Portuguese Melaka. Raja Bongsu and Sultan Alauddin Riayat Shah III were important figures. The Twelve Years' Truce between the Dutch Republic and Spain, whose news arrived after 1609, complicated Johor's position; research by Peter Borschberg shows how it aggravated factional disputes in the court and helped push Johor toward accommodation with the Portuguese in 1610.

The agreements of 1606 are unusually instructive because their dates and clauses survive in Dutch treaty collections. The first was concluded on 17 May between Cornelis Matelieff de Jonge and Johor's rulers, including Sultan Alauddin Riayat Shah III and Raja Bongsu. The second, on 23 September at Batu Sawar, followed the failure of the combined attempt to capture Portuguese Melaka and supplemented the earlier arrangement. The distinction matters. An alliance negotiated before a military campaign could express broad expectations; a revised agreement after defeat could address the more immediate problems of continuing cooperation, access, and obligations. Treating the two documents as an interchangeable pair loses evidence of political bargaining over time.

The arrangements concerned cooperation against the Iberian powers, trading privileges, and practical provisions such as wood needed for ships. The surviving treaty material also addresses matters of exclusion and jurisdiction. The parties were not equals in military technology, financial scale, or overseas reach, but neither did the negotiations reduce Johor to a passive recipient of Dutch policy. Johor's rulers saw the VOC as an ally capable of providing ships, artillery, and commercial leverage against Portugal. The Dutch saw access to a strategically placed Malay court and its maritime allies as valuable. Their overlapping interests could produce detailed promises even when the participants did not share a consistent understanding of how those promises should operate.

Johor's embassy to the Dutch Republic in 1603 illustrates its diplomatic initiative. The journey placed Malay representatives in direct contact with the emerging Dutch maritime state and its commercial leadership. Communications between the republic and Asia were slow, however, and later events exposed the problems created by this distance. A decision made in Europe might alter relations with Spain and Portugal before a ruler in Johor or a Dutch admiral received any reliable account of it. A treaty signed in Asia could likewise be reinterpreted by directors whose priorities differed from those of captains facing Portuguese ships. Diplomatic history therefore requires reconstructing communication delays instead of assuming that everyone acted on the same information at once.

The Twelve Years' Truce concluded in Europe in 1609 was a central test. News reaching Johor complicated expectations that the VOC would continue military cooperation against Portuguese Melaka. Peter Borschberg's study shows how this uncertainty sharpened existing differences at Johor's court. Raja Bongsu's pro-Dutch position could be challenged by men who saw no advantage in remaining committed to an expensive anti-Portuguese struggle without dependable Dutch assistance. A peace with the Portuguese was arranged in October 1610, while the court's internal balance shifted. This was not simply a change of mind about trade. It showed how an international agreement made far away could change the distribution of political influence within a Southeast Asian court.

The diplomatic record also reveals the limits of what a written treaty can prove. A Dutch version preserves clauses that officials wanted recorded and later enforced, but it does not reproduce every oral understanding or the conversations through which Malay rulers interpreted their commitments. Dutch reports about compliance might emphasize failures of Johor's partners while understating company conduct that caused resentment. A critical reading compares the treaty with later correspondence and the actual course of relations. It is especially important to distinguish formal promises from the capacity to deliver ships, ammunition, provisions, and protection in any specific season.

In the larger narrative the alliance marks Johor's strategic agency and vulnerability together. The court could negotiate with a powerful new maritime corporation because Johor controlled valued routes, relationships, and commercial opportunities. But the costs of depending on foreign ships were visible as soon as European diplomacy took an unexpected turn. The 1606 arrangements therefore illuminate a regional political system in which royal courts and chartered companies had to bargain across unequal resources and incompatible assumptions. They also foreshadow Johor's later disputes with the VOC over trade and jurisdiction, documented particularly well in the royal correspondence of 1713.

Section 81.7

Acehnese Invasion, Dynastic Captivity, and Johor's Recovery, 1613–1640

In 1613 an Acehnese attack, part of Iskandar Muda's expansion (Chapter 80), captured Johor's leaders and displaced the court. Raja Bongsu, later Sultan Abdullah Ma'ayat Shah, and successive rulers worked to restore autonomy amid Acehnese influence, Portuguese pressure, and Dutch commercial ambition. Johor recovered because its networks of followers and anchorages survived the loss of particular settlements.

The Acehnese attack of 1613 was not an isolated raid; it was part of Iskandar Muda's attempt to make Aceh the leading power around the Strait of Malacca. Johor's position threatened that project because its court controlled important maritime relationships and could cooperate with Portuguese or Dutch enemies of Aceh. The seizure of Batu Sawar and the capture of prominent Johor leaders were instruments of political reorganization. Removing a ruler or senior prince disrupted succession, while carrying captives to Aceh allowed the conqueror to impose conditions and shape new dynastic relationships. Such practices are examined from Aceh's perspective in Chapter 80; here their consequences for Johor's court and subjects are central.

Raja Bongsu became an important figure in this unstable situation. He had played a leading part in diplomacy with the Dutch and subsequently emerged as Sultan Abdullah Ma'ayat Shah. His path illustrates the difficulty of recovering authority when a court's members have been seized, reinstated, or made dependent on a more powerful neighbor. Dynastic marriage could create an apparent reconciliation while continuing to limit freedom of action. A ruler might be recognized by one outside power and opposed by another, and his followers might disagree over whether compromise preserved the kingdom or endangered it. This makes it misleading to reduce the period to a single uninterrupted Acehnese occupation of Johor.

The consequences extended across maritime communities. An invading fleet could reach and burn a major royal center, but it could not easily compel the loyalty of every boat crew, island headman, and river community that had served the older dynasty. Some groups might withdraw to distant islands, others bargain with Acehnese representatives, and still others continue exchanging goods with Portuguese or Dutch ports. These movements preserved possibilities for recovery but also fragmented political authority. Merchants likewise reassessed risk: a route newly exposed to warfare might be avoided, while a smaller anchorage could acquire value precisely because it lay outside the immediate battlefield.

The years after 1613 contained further shifts rather than a neat period of restoration. Portuguese, Dutch, and Acehnese relations fluctuated while Johor's leaders sought security and opportunity. In particular, the great Acehnese defeat before Portuguese Melaka in 1629 altered the relative capabilities of the regional powers, but it did not automatically eliminate Aceh's influence or make Johor secure. Political leaders had to judge what could still be gained from alliance, whether a rival's fleet was likely to return, and how much military help a European partner could reliably provide. The record is uneven on how such calculations unfolded within Johor's court, so a responsible narrative identifies the overall pressures without inventing a continuous sequence of meetings or decisions.

Changes in the royal household could also transform local social relations. Noble captives and hostages were sometimes treated differently from ordinary people carried away by armies. Political families might be incorporated through marriage or restored to positions under conditions set by the victor. Agricultural households and artisans confronted the loss of people or access to markets. The experiences of women and children often appear only indirectly in accounts focused on defeated princes. Recovering these consequences requires reading the campaign history in conversation with regional practices of captivity, service, and patronage, and acknowledging the limits of the available evidence.

By the time of the 1641 campaign against Portuguese Melaka, Johor had survived repeated military shocks and could again contribute to a major regional alliance. That capacity should not be mistaken for uninterrupted strength; it demonstrates the persistence of networks and institutions capable of being reassembled. Royal legitimacy remained useful, commercial partners could be attracted anew, and maritime followers gave the court means to act beyond a single defended site. The story of the 1613 conquest and its aftermath therefore shows that defeating a court was easier than permanently governing the relationships on which a maritime kingdom depended.

Section 81.8

Johor, the VOC, and the Fall of Portuguese Melaka in 1641

A renewed Dutch–Johor alliance besieged Melaka, which fell in January 1641. The VOC kept the city. Johor gained commercial advantage but not the old capital, and disputes over trading privileges continued.

The end of Portuguese rule in Melaka was a major turning point, but it was achieved through a partnership whose members expected different benefits. The VOC sought a strategically important fortress and a stronger position against the Iberian presence in Southeast Asia. Johor sought the removal of a rival occupying the former Melakan capital and hoped to improve access to trade, influence, and security in the strait. These aims could align militarily even if they did not imply agreement about who should control Melaka after victory. The longer history of Johor's dynastic claim made the alliance symbolically important in ways that a narrow Dutch account of commercial competition might overlook.

The Dutch-led siege began in 1640 and ended with the capture of Portuguese Melaka in January 1641. Company ships and forces were central to the operation, while Johor's maritime support and local cooperation mattered to the campaign and blockade. A careful narrative should avoid assigning precise troop or vessel totals without specifying the contemporary source, because accounts differed in what they counted and whose contribution they praised. Disease, shortages, and the difficulty of sustaining a siege were important realities for participants on both sides. The outcome was not merely an artillery contest; it depended on maintaining pressure on an established fortified port over time.

The decisive political fact was that the VOC retained Melaka. Johor's rulers did not re-enter the old capital as sovereigns, and Portuguese rule was replaced by company government rather than a restoration of the earlier Malay polity. Nevertheless, Johor gained from the weakening of a hostile power and from commercial arrangements arising from Dutch cooperation. A ruler could judge the result favorable even though it left the ancient dynastic capital in foreign hands. This distinction prevents students from equating the recovery of commercial opportunities with the recovery of territory. It also invites comparison with other regional alliances in which an Asian state participated actively but did not determine the ultimate settlement.

In the years after 1641, Johor benefited from changing trade patterns. Merchants did not have to direct all business through VOC Melaka, and some found the Johor-Riau system attractive because its officials and commercial intermediaries offered useful conditions. The company simultaneously tried to protect its own customs and shipping revenues. Dutch and Johor officials could therefore cooperate in defense or diplomacy while disputing the movement of goods. Trade privileges were not permanent guarantees of harmony, and the status of private traders, coastal settlements, and Sumatran dependencies remained contentious. A commercial partner could become a competitor as the economic consequences of victory unfolded.

The new relationship was especially consequential for the social geography of the strait. A prosperous court needed royal officials, customs collectors, merchants, port laborers, shipwrights, brokers, and Orang Laut escorts. Its authority over these people relied on workable arrangements for profit and security. Expanded commerce could increase a ruler's ability to reward followers and strengthen political influence; the same increase could empower merchants and regional notables whose interests did not always coincide with the palace. The political significance of the 1641 victory lies partly in this growth of resources and partly in the more complicated internal bargaining that growth made possible.

The conquest also belongs to a longer process of readjustment among Malay polities. Aceh remained a regional factor, Jambi and other Sumatran ports retained independent ambitions, and merchants moved between markets. Johor's growing prominence did not create a monopoly over all shipping in the southern strait. The chapter should compare competing historical views: one emphasizes Johor's recovery through a successful Dutch alliance; another asks whether dependence on company cooperation created new constraints. Both can be partly correct. By the later seventeenth century Johor had recovered commercial importance, yet it remained exposed to war with Jambi and the enduring problem of how to keep a politically dispersed maritime world together.

Commerce, Maritime Society, and the Seventeenth-Century Malay World

Division III · Section 81.9

The Changing Capitals of Johor

Courts moved among Johor Lama, Batu Sawar, other river sites, and later island centres for reasons of war, security, faction, and commerce. Movement did not mean state failure: loyal maritime communities and supply links could be maintained from a new anchorage.

The phrase moving capital can suggest a ruler wandering without institutions, but Johor's relocations usually involved a whole political establishment. A court included noble households, religious figures, retainers, scribes, armed followers, craft specialists, and people who supplied food and transport. Some could travel with the ruler, while others managed property and trading relationships left behind. When the palace settled at a new river bend or island harbor, officials needed to re-establish landings, secure water and food, receive envoys, and make the location recognizable as a place where agreements and disputes could be handled. A royal capital was thus a concentration of political relationships as much as a group of buildings.

Johor Lama, Batu Sawar, and later Riau centers became prominent at different moments because each solved particular problems. A river settlement could shelter a court from immediate naval attack, but an island harbor might lie closer to the routes favored by visiting merchants. The reputation of a ruler, the availability of loyal boat crews, and the presence of secure warehouses could matter more than the size of the settlement itself. Relocation often followed warfare, yet it could also reflect efforts to manage factional disputes or engage trading networks. We should therefore avoid a deterministic story in which each move represents a decline or each island site represents an advance toward an inevitable later capital.

Moving a court tested the depth of allegiance. Regional chiefs who had recognized the ruler at one location might withhold supplies when ordered to attend a new palace. A merchant with debts outstanding in the old port might prefer a rival market rather than seek repayment in the new one. Orang Laut leaders might support the journey because the royal household had protected their families for generations, or negotiate fresh terms for an escort. Even successful movement imposed costs in lost storage, interrupted harvests, and the departure of specialists. The sources generally notice the whereabouts of a ruler, not the resources required to take a community with him; historians must reconstruct this practical underside through the logic of maritime and household economies.

The river and island landscapes also allowed political authority to be distributed among several nodes. A senior official could hold a particular residence while another prince directed naval affairs from elsewhere. A port might serve as the principal commercial center without being the site of every royal ceremony. Consequently, documents naming Johor, Riau, Batu Sawar, or a river landing require attention to whether they refer to a court, an anchorage, a geographical district, or a wider political network. Chronology is especially important because some place names persisted after the center of government had shifted and later writers could refer back to older sites using familiar labels.

European records are useful but partial evidence for these movements. Dutch agents knew where they needed to send envoys, where warehouses or ships lay, and which officials could deliver an agreement. They might not understand why a settlement held particular symbolic importance or how royal relationships reached beyond their immediate trading contacts. Malay narratives can preserve genealogies and ceremonial precedence, but may narrate movements to emphasize divine favor or the wisdom of a ruler. Archaeological traces of river settlements and fortifications add evidence without automatically revealing which household or faction occupied a site at a given date. The strongest reconstruction compares these kinds of sources rather than taking one as exhaustive.

By the eighteenth century Riau's political and commercial importance became more conspicuous, yet the term Johor-Riau should not be used as though the eighteenth-century arrangement had always existed. Earlier Johor's court structures, the interruptions of the 1699 succession, and the alliance of 1722 all changed how and where power was exercised. Understanding this geographical history helps students distinguish continuity of a royal tradition from the survival of a fixed administrative center. In a maritime kingdom, effective sovereignty meant being able to relocate while retaining enough networks of trade, recognition, and service to remain a ruler worth following.

Section 81.10

Johor as a Maritime Commercial Power

After 1641 Johor traded in pepper, tin, textiles, rice, forest and marine products. Malay, Chinese, Indian, Bugis, and other merchants took part, some resident and some seasonal. Johor lacked unlimited freedom: the VOC pursued restrictive monopoly aims that constrained its trade.

Johor's economy was built around exchange between ecological zones. Pepper and forest products reached markets through Sumatran and peninsular ports, while tin from the Malay Peninsula circulated through merchants capable of connecting mining districts with coastal buyers. Rice, cloth, metalware, ceramics, salt, and everyday goods moved in the opposite directions or between local markets. The kingdom did not need to produce every valuable commodity within the royal heartland; it needed to provide an attractive and sufficiently secure place where diverse traders could meet. This was the logic of the entrepôt, a port whose prosperity came substantially from exchange, storage, transshipment, and the services supporting those activities.

A port's appeal depended on practical arrangements. Merchants needed pilots, interpreters, warehouses or safe storage, brokers who knew the value of goods, and officials willing to settle disputes. The shahbandar, a title inherited from the wider Persianate and Malay commercial world, was associated with the reception and regulation of foreign traders. Customs levies and gifts to the ruler could generate revenue, but excessive charges might redirect shipping toward a rival anchorage. Credit arrangements were crucial because voyages operated on seasonal schedules and cargoes might be bought before earlier debts were collected. Commercial success was therefore a political task: the court had to balance immediate revenue against the future willingness of merchants to return.

The people operating this system did not all share one identity. Malay and Orang Laut sailors worked alongside traders from various Sumatran and peninsular communities, Chinese merchants, Indian Ocean networks, and later Bugis commercial participants. Some visitors came for a season, others established households, and still others entrusted goods to resident intermediaries. Labels used in official documents can hide complex partnerships: an overseas merchant might rely on a local relative or broker, while a royal official could also trade on his own account. The distinction between public authority and private business was often blurred, making disputes over trade privileges especially politically sensitive.

Johor's relations with the VOC illustrate this tension. After the company's victory at Melaka in 1641, Dutch officials sought to secure customs revenues, control certain trades, and regulate movements through their establishments. Johor's merchants and rulers had reasons to resist commercial restrictions that disadvantaged their ports. A company agreement could formally recognize a sphere of access while its actual enforcement varied by captain, official, or year. The 1713 royal letter, studied in §81.14, is unusually revealing because it concerns rice shipments from Java, disputes over access to Siak, and a voyage on the Coromandel coast. It documents a ruler acting as an investor in long-distance commerce, not merely collecting tribute from others.

The commercial importance of Johor had a social cost as well as a benefit. Wealth concentrated around royal circles, influential brokers, and merchants capable of financing ventures, while laborers faced the danger of shipwreck, war, disease, and debt. A prosperous entrepôt required craft workers, boat builders, fishermen, porters, household servants, and cultivators whose names rarely survive in treaties. Royal demand for vessels or provisions could interrupt their ordinary work. The same commercial networks that brought luxury goods could transmit news, political factions, and claims of succession. It is therefore misleading to isolate economic prosperity from the court politics that merchants helped finance or influence.

Trade also had a spatial dimension that makes rigid territorial descriptions inadequate. A ruler's influence in a tin-producing district might be exercised through an allied chief rather than a permanently stationed governor. An island settlement might receive a formal privilege while local merchants retained much practical autonomy. Conflict with Jambi in the late seventeenth century revealed the vulnerability of such distributed connections, and the eighteenth-century Siak dispute showed that commercial rights could remain contested even between rulers who claimed to be allies. Johor's economy was neither an unrestricted free market nor a territorial monopoly. It was a changing structure of obligations, advantages, relationships, and negotiated access.

Historical Vocabulary
Shahbandar and Entrepôt

A shahbandar was a port official who handled foreign merchants, customs, and disputes. An entrepôt is a port whose wealth rests on exchange rather than its own production. Such ports offered protection, brokerage, and credit; their prosperity did not benefit all groups equally.

In a commercial kingdom the shahbandar’s effectiveness depended on persuading merchants that transactions and disputes would be handled predictably. An entrepôt earned its position through traffic and services rather than through possession of every producing district.

Section 81.11

Orang Laut Households, Maritime Labor, and Everyday Life

The maritime economy required households that could adapt to changing seasons and coastal conditions. In sheltered waters a family might fish, collect shellfish, process and dry a catch, repair a boat, or exchange a marine product for rice and cloth. In another season some members could join trading voyages or serve a royal expedition. These tasks were not necessarily divided into separate occupations. Practical knowledge of particular reefs, landing places, and weather conditions was transferred through daily work and kin relationships, making household continuity valuable to both local commerce and the wider political system. A palace could demand a fleet, but the experience required to move it through narrow channels was held among people whose ordinary life took place on those waters.

The physical form of settlement could vary. Some families lived for extended periods in boats or on the shore, others maintained relationships with island villages, and many moved according to work, marriage, or safety. The broad label Orang Laut should not obscure differences in residence, language, status, and connection to particular rulers. Nor should all maritime households be described as permanently wandering. Some cultivated stable ties with local places while retaining mobility as a practical resource. Women could be central to exchange, preservation of food, household finance, and the maintenance of kinship relationships, even when foreign observers wrote chiefly about male sailors and warriors. Children grew into the skills that gave families access to work and alliances.

Royal service altered this rhythm. When a sultan called for crews, men and sometimes whole households might have to travel away from ordinary fishing or trading activities. Some service brought rewards or access to protection; some was expected under inherited obligations that could become burdensome. A leader unable to meet a royal demand might face punishment or exclusion from favored trading arrangements. Historians must distinguish these kinds of dependency from enslavement, though the early modern world contained captives and slaves alongside dependent but legally free people. An outsider's use of the word slave does not automatically establish a community's own understanding of its status, and a ruler's claim that his subjects served willingly cannot establish the absence of coercion.

Conflict exposed household vulnerability in ways missed by military histories. A battle might kill pilots or seize vessels on which families depended. A rival court might try to compel people to change their loyalties, while the movement of a royal capital could shift demand for fish and boat services to a different harbor. War could also open opportunities for skilled sailors to bargain with several rulers, but that flexibility was not equally available to every household. A family with strong kin in another island group might withdraw from danger; one dependent on a particular fishing ground could face a harder choice. The economic costs of disruption therefore differed among people whom palace records collectively counted as maritime followers.

Trade offered channels of social connection beyond direct royal service. Exchange of fish, sea cucumbers, salt, timber, pottery, and cloth linked maritime groups with inland cultivators and urban merchants. Marriage relationships could cross these communities and help establish trust across distances. Knowledge of prices, cargo arrivals, political dangers, and safe passage was itself valuable. These networks helped Johor remain politically viable when a royal settlement was destroyed, because people could continue to communicate, provision ships, and direct travelers to an alternative center. Yet precisely because the networks were not wholly owned by the court, they could help rivals as well.

The historian's evidence is uneven. Dutch company letters, Malay chronicles, and later colonial ethnography describe maritime people from different positions of power and at very different dates. The first two often record their conduct only when it affected rulers or foreign merchants; the later sources may preserve traditions but cannot be carried back uncritically into the seventeenth century. A responsible everyday-life study should therefore explain patterns supported by comparative evidence without inventing the thoughts of a named family or treating a modern practice as unchanged for three centuries. The result is not a romantic portrait of freely mobile seafarers, but a social history of skilled work, unequal obligations, family strategy, and political choice.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households sailed, fished, built boats, gathered marine products, and moved seasonally; they also joined royal and commercial expeditions. Status varied among free households, dependent communities, captives, and people owing royal service, and scholars caution against labelling all obligation as slavery. War and shifting dynastic loyalty threatened safety and livelihood. Evidence comes mostly from court and company records, so these families' own views are largely lost.

Social relations could move between service, negotiated loyalty, commercial exchange, captivity, and direct coercion. Do not infer from a court record calling a group servants that all its households had the same legal status or choices. A historical reconstruction must distinguish documented activity from illustration.

Section 81.12

Court Society, Islam, Law, Gender, and Political Relationships

The court combined royal households, political marriages, the bendahara and temenggung, religious scholars, and commercial elites. Islamic law, Malay adat, ceremony, and negotiated authority interacted. Women in ruling families influenced marriages, inheritance, household management, and alliances, though the evidence is uneven.

Court society was sustained by households that conducted politics as well as family life. The sultan's immediate kin had claims on property, honor, and marriage alliances, and the composition of a royal household could affect which ministers or regional chiefs gained influence. Palace marriages were rarely simply personal unions: they could connect a court with a rival lineage, settle a dispute, or guarantee a military arrangement. A prince related through several marriages to different ruling houses might become useful as a negotiator and dangerous as a claimant to the throne. Women's influence is often more visible through these relationships than through formal office titles, which makes the reading of genealogies an important part of social history rather than an exercise in memorizing names.

The bendahara, temenggung, and other senior officials occupied positions that combined administration, patronage, and dynastic responsibility. A minister might assemble forces, organize an embassy, advise a ruler, or manage commercial privileges. How far any officeholder could act independently depended on the sultan's authority, the court's factions, and available resources. It is misleading to present these roles as ministries with sharply separated portfolios. A powerful noble could command followers because of kinship and wealth as much as because of a royal appointment. The rise of the Bendahara dynasty after 1699 is especially revealing: an office that had long served royal government became the basis for a claim to royalty itself.

Islam offered public languages for legitimacy, learning, and judicial conduct. Rulers maintained connections with religious teachers and were expected to support mosques, scholarship, and aspects of proper governance. At the same time, Malay adat shaped conduct concerning marriage, rank, inheritance, obligations, and ceremonial precedence. Real disputes could involve several bodies of expectation at once. A royal court might appeal to Islamic norms while defending a customary order of noble privileges; local people could interpret those same norms differently. The resulting legal world was not an undifferentiated fusion, and surviving sources rarely document enough proceedings to reconstruct one universally enforced code across all Johor settlements.

Commercial society intersected with this social order. A courtier might finance a ship; a merchant might seek a marriage connection to a powerful family; and a royal official might use his authority to guarantee debts or secure a cargo. Port authorities had to handle foreign merchants whose languages and practices differed. Interpreters, brokers, and trusted household servants could exercise real influence without occupying the formal positions celebrated in court chronicles. The household and the market were therefore not separate realms. A change in the ruler could affect commercial credit, while a dispute over trade revenue could deepen a conflict among princes or officers.

The experiences of ordinary women and families are harder to reconstruct. Warfare and migration could separate spouses and children, while the movement of capitals shifted markets for food, cloth, labor, and housing. Families tied to river cultivation may have faced different pressures from those making a livelihood by fishing or transporting goods. Some residents possessed property and negotiating power, while captives and dependent workers had sharply limited choices. General statements about social norms should therefore be qualified by questions of wealth, legal status, location, and access to patrons. The sources seldom allow us to give a continuous account of one ordinary household across the century.

Court culture also depended on performance. Audiences, gifts, titulature, ceremonial dress, and the seating of guests affirmed differences of rank and recognition of political relationships. A visitor who received honors might understand that as recognition of an alliance, whereas another could see a form of subordination. Diplomatic protocols thus had practical political effects. These questions become particularly important when the succession of 1699 and the Bugis partnership of 1722 forced Johor's elites to negotiate who should sit above whom. Royal legitimacy was not merely an abstract doctrine; it was created and contested in everyday acts of deference, exchange, household alliance, and public ceremony.

Division IV · §§81.13–81.16

War, Regicide, and the Transformation of Malay Dynastic Power

Division IV · Section 81.13

The Johor–Jambi Wars

Johor and Jambi, a Sumatran Malay polity, contested commerce, precedence, and marriage alliances. Jambi's major attack of 1673 damaged Johor's court and its hold on the Riau islands, and encouraged shifting capitals and competition among officials. These were regional ambitions, not merely a European–Asian contest.

Jambi was a major Sumatran Malay polity with its own royal establishment, merchant networks, and connections to the lower Batang Hari River. Its disputes with Johor were not a side story to European expansion. Both kingdoms sought influence over ports and traders, and each court understood relations through questions of prestige, marriage, and political precedence. Commercial rivalry grew sharper when pepper markets shifted and when officials tried to shape the terms on which merchants could trade. Conflicts over royal kinship could carry economic consequences because marriage alliances linked claims of honor with access to resources and followings. Historians should resist translating the rivalry into an imagined struggle between two fixed nation-states with clearly surveyed borders.

The war was not one uninterrupted campaign. Relations moved between confrontation, attempted settlement, and renewed hostility, while factions at each court assessed the advantage of alliance. The Dutch at Melaka and Batavia monitored these events because disruptions affected commerce and could change opportunities for intervention. Yet the principal motivations were grounded in Johor's and Jambi's own political calculations. The great Jambi attack of 1673 was a particularly damaging blow to Johor, forcing the court into crisis and altering the distribution of power in the southern strait. Some summary accounts name the site of attack differently; the safest description connects the campaign with Johor's principal riverine establishment rather than treating later place-name conventions as conclusive.

A court in retreat needed more than a new location. It required the allegiance of nobles, crews, and merchants who might have reconsidered their commitments after defeat. Rivals could exploit a moment of weakness to demand better trading terms or to claim influence over districts that had previously acknowledged Johor. At the same time, the island geography provided refuges and routes by which royal supporters could regroup. The war thus brought the central contradiction of a maritime kingdom into view: a flexible geography allowed survival, but the looseness of political control also made it easier for followers and dependent ports to shift relationships when confidence declined.

Economic circumstances changed the terms of the struggle. The price and movement of pepper could strengthen or weaken rulers who depended on it for revenue and gifts. If regional merchants were unwilling to visit a dangerous port, a court's ability to reward followers diminished. Jambi too could experience declining income and political instability, and conflicts over legitimate succession later divided its own subjects. The National Archives of Indonesia publishes a 1714 report about Johor war vessels in the Batang Hari area and discusses how Orang Laut communities were affected by competition between Jambi and Johor. Although that document is later than the major war, it demonstrates that the conflict's relationships and memories had not disappeared with a single battle.

The rivalry sheds light on the role of the VOC without making it the controlling cause. Dutch officers could issue threats, negotiate agreements, or profit from a rival's weakness, but their commercial and military capacity was not sufficient to dictate every Malay political outcome. Company reports sometimes portrayed local conflicts as disorders impeding trade; from the viewpoint of Johor and Jambi they were struggles over legitimate authority and the conditions under which regional commerce would operate. Bringing these perspectives together produces a more convincing picture of the strait than a narrative centered exclusively on Portuguese and Dutch warships.

By the late seventeenth century Johor had recovered substantial commercial importance, but warfare contributed to tensions within the governing elite and weakened the assumption that dynastic prestige alone ensured obedience. The political challenges emerging in the 1680s and 1690s cannot be attributed mechanically to Jambi's attack: royal conduct, elite rivalries, commercial changes, and individual decisions mattered. Nevertheless, repeated crises made disputes over who could legitimately speak for the kingdom more consequential. The next section turns to the exceptional rupture of 1699, when Johor's ruling circle did not merely relocate its sultan but replaced the basis on which the throne was claimed.

Section 81.14

The Regicide of 1699 and the Rise of the Bendahara Dynasty

In September 1699 Sultan Mahmud Shah II was killed, with no generally recognized direct heir of the Melakan line. Bendahara Abdul Jalil became Sultan Abdul Jalil Riayat Shah IV, raising the problem of making a senior official sovereign. Nobles, religious figures, regional rulers, and Orang Laut communities judged the succession differently; accounts of the dead sultan's conduct come from later and partisan sources.

The murder of Sultan Mahmud Shah II, conventionally dated 3 September 1699, was an extraordinary rupture because it challenged both a ruler and the theory of rulership. Later narratives portray Mahmud as cruel, erratic, or incapable of fulfilling the obligations of a just sovereign. Some recount elaborate stories of personal insult and revenge. Such accounts cannot all be treated as independent testimony: many survive in later court histories shaped by a need to justify or condemn the regicide. What can be said with greater confidence is that powerful nobles participated in or supported the killing, and that the absence of an uncontested direct successor created an opening for the bendahara Abdul Jalil to assume the throne. The chronology of events is more securely established than the precise motives of every participant.

For supporters of the succession, Abdul Jalil possessed experience, influential connections, and an established place at the heart of Johor's court. A kingdom whose ruler had been killed needed somebody able to receive foreign envoys, settle disputes, command followers, and guarantee order. The office of bendahara made its holder a plausible candidate in practical politics. But the inherited language of Malay legitimacy complicated that claim. A chief minister was not automatically a ruler of Melakan descent, and some subjects understood loyalty to the old royal line as a duty surviving even the death of its last generally recognized direct representative. The problem was not whether the new sultan could administer a court; it was whether administrative competence could replace inherited royal sanctity.

The consequences were observable in the loyalties of maritime groups and regional leaders. Some Orang Laut communities would not follow the new sultan, while others remained because Johor's commerce offered rewards and security. The National Archives of Indonesia's discussion of a 1714 report identifies both resistance and continued loyalty among these communities, cautioning against a uniform story of defection. The court also faced challenges from chiefs and places previously attached to its commercial system. Some of those disputes could be settled through negotiation and gifts, but the sense that sovereignty had been broken gave later claimants a language in which to mobilize dissatisfaction.

The political thought involved was more complex than the opposition of superstition to necessity. Daulat expressed inherited and sacral claims; derhaka identified disloyalty against rightful authority. Yet Malay narratives also judged rulers against expectations of justice and proper treatment of subjects. A noble could argue that killing a tyrannical ruler was necessary for the survival of the polity, while an opponent could regard the act as the very violation that undermined the new dynasty's legitimacy. Kanji Nishio's study of political culture emphasizes tensions between different ideas of authority and agreement in the Malay world. These ideas should be presented as historical arguments with consequences, not as a timeless rule that explained every person's conduct.

Abdul Jalil's government had to engage rivals as well as defend its reputation. Trade and diplomacy continued, including relations with the VOC; the surviving letter of 26 April 1713 gives unusually concrete evidence of how a ruler in the new dynasty articulated sovereignty. It was addressed to Governor-General Abraham van Riebeeck and recorded in the Batavia Castle daily journal as a Dutch translation of a Malay original. The sultan invoked longstanding friendship, sent envoys and gifts, and raised disputes over commerce and political authority. The fact that he could operate a functioning diplomatic and trading establishment did not settle the deeper issue of dynastic acceptance, but it is evidence against describing the period as an immediate collapse of all Johor institutions.

A close reading of the 1713 letter reveals how commerce and politics were intertwined. Abdul Jalil asked about access to Siak trade, requested gunpowder and bullets, and sought permission for merchants to bring rice from Java to Johor. He also complained about a shipment carried on a voyage to ports on the Coromandel coast whose cargo had been seized by VOC officers, and sought restitution. The published translation identifies the captain and gives a monetary valuation, but these should be cited to that record rather than treated as typical of every trading venture. The letter is not neutral: its elaborate protestations of friendship were part of a persuasive diplomatic strategy. Yet it makes clear that the Johor ruler was directly involved in long-distance commerce and negotiation.

The transition after 1699 thus had several different timescales. In one sense the succession was immediate: the court needed a ruler and named one. In another it remained unresolved for decades, because claims of Melakan descent and debates about proper sovereignty could be revived in new circumstances. The emergence of Raja Kecil in 1718 made that unresolved conflict unmistakable. The historical lesson is not simply that an oppressive sultan was killed or that a minister seized power. It is that a maritime kingdom's institutions, commercial networks, and ideological foundations could persist while the legitimacy tying them together became contested.

Section 81.15

Raja Kecil, Siak, and the Contest for Johor's Throne, 1718–1722

Raja Kecil claimed to be a posthumous son of Mahmud Shah II. This is a contested political claim and not an established fact. Drawing on Siak and Minangkabau networks and on groups dissatisfied with the Bendahara dynasty, he took Johor in 1718 and displaced the ruling family, unsettling the Orang Laut and Johor's claims in Sumatra.

Raja Kecil's rise turned a problem of legitimacy into a struggle for control of the kingdom. Emerging through Siak and Minangkabau connections, he claimed to be a posthumous son of the murdered Mahmud Shah II. If accepted, that genealogy would make him more than another rival noble: he would embody a possible restoration of the old Melakan royal line. The claim was therefore politically powerful even though historians cannot establish its truth from the later narratives that celebrate or deny it. Assessing it requires separating the political effectiveness of an assertion from the evidence that would prove biological descent. A convincing history describes the claim's reception without endorsing it as fact.

The networks that supported him were spread across eastern Sumatra and the island world. Siak was strategically placed for connections between riverine production and maritime trade, while Minangkabau communities supplied relationships reaching inland and along the coast. Raja Kecil gained assistance from people dissatisfied with the Bendahara dynasty, including significant Orang Laut groups that retained attachment to the idea of the old royal house. This coalition was broader than one ethnic faction: political grievances, hopes for patronage, and the promise of legitimate kingship could reinforce one another. The degree of participation varied among communities, and accounts from later supporters have to be read alongside company correspondence and other evidence.

In 1718 Raja Kecil displaced the ruling family and asserted authority over Johor. The event was a victory, but not the conclusion of the conflict. The Bendahara household retained powerful relatives and allies, while regional leaders assessed the prospects of a ruler whose claim rested on contested descent and military success. Raja Kecil also faced the practical challenge familiar to earlier conquerors: winning a capital or forcing a court's submission did not guarantee the obedience of merchants, islands, and chiefs throughout Johor's sphere. Those who had welcomed him might disagree over appointments or demands once he began governing. Political legitimacy had to be sustained in daily relationships after it had been announced in a campaign.

The older dynasty suffered grave losses in this struggle, including the killing of Abdul Jalil IV during the ensuing conflict; the exact stages are narrated differently in Malay and Dutch traditions and should not be compressed into an invented single encounter. Surviving members of his family sought allies capable of challenging Raja Kecil. Their search demonstrates that the politics of succession did not take place solely among people born within the old royal court. Trade diasporas, mobile military specialists, and leaders of neighboring regions could become decisive participants. The dynasty's vulnerability thus invited an external alliance that would eventually transform Johor's institutions more deeply than a simple return to the pre-1718 order.

From Raja Kecil's perspective, the struggle also involved control over Siak and routes into eastern Sumatra. A ruler able to claim Johor's royal title might gain commercial and diplomatic influence across both sides of the strait, while opponents could continue to contest individual ports. The relationship between genealogy and trade is important. Followers might support a claimant because they accepted his descent, because they anticipated better treatment, or because his victory promised commercial security. Later narratives sometimes rank these motives according to the sympathies of their authors. A sound analysis asks what evidence exists for particular groups rather than assuming that an entire population shared one purpose.

Raja Kecil's eventual loss of Johor's principal royal center did not end his political influence. He remained a significant figure in the Siak political world, and conflicts involving his lineage shaped later developments beyond the chapter's main chronological endpoint. The episode of 1718–1722 is therefore not the story of a false claimant briefly interrupting a legitimate line. It is a struggle among competing interpretations of legitimacy, backed by maritime and Sumatran networks, whose outcome depended on a new coalition of interests. That coalition came together around Raja Sulaiman and Bugis leaders and is examined in the next section.

Section 81.16

Bugis Migration, Military Alliance, and the Restoration of Raja Sulaiman in 1722

Bugis migrants had settled in the western Malay world by this time; their origins in Sulawesi (Chapter 82) lay in several movements, and they were not a single faction. Brothers including Daeng Parani, Daeng Marewa, and Daeng Chelak allied with members of the displaced Bendahara family against Raja Kecil. In 1722 Raja Sulaiman became sultan. The alliance was a negotiated exchange of political rewards, not simply hired soldiering.

Bugis participation in Johor's politics arose from migration, commerce, and warfare across a much wider maritime world. People from South Sulawesi had long connections with western Indonesian ports, and the upheavals of the later seventeenth century created further opportunities and pressures for movement. Different Bugis communities did not act as one organization. Some settled as merchants, some maintained links to relatives and patrons in Sulawesi, and particular leaders gathered armed followers able to intervene in regional struggles. The detailed history of South Sulawesi and the Makassar War belongs in Chapter 82. Here the crucial point is how Bugis networks became a resource that rival claimants in Johor could mobilize.

Accounts of the five Bugis brothers commonly identify Daeng Parani, Daeng Marewa, Daeng Chelak, Daeng Menambun, and Daeng Kemasi or Kamasi. Their names and alliances appear in later Malay-Bugis narratives as well as in discussions grounded in other evidence. They brought naval and military capabilities, kinship relationships, and commercial connections that could give a court more than an immediate victory. For members of the displaced Bendahara family, an alliance with these leaders offered a route back to power after Raja Kecil's success in 1718. For the Bugis leaders, support for a Malay claimant could yield a recognized place in a commercially important political system rather than merely wages for a temporary campaign.

The resulting conflict was not resolved in one uncomplicated engagement. Accounts compress multiple movements and shifting alliances into a dramatic story of restoration, while the broader historical process involved bargaining over where authority would be exercised, what titles would be granted, and how supporters would be rewarded. By 1722 Raja Sulaiman had been installed as sultan with Bugis assistance. His claim rested on the Bendahara family, whose legitimacy had been disputed after 1699. Bugis intervention helped overcome an immediate rival, but it also made the reinstated household dependent on partners whose influence could not simply be dismissed once the fighting ended.

The settlement involved formal recognition of a powerful Bugis officeholder. Daeng Marewa became the first Yang di-Pertuan Muda, the junior ruler, within a new relationship between Malay dynastic authority and Bugis political strength. This was not equivalent to a European mercenary captain becoming a subordinate civil servant. The office represented access to the machinery of rule and the ability to shape appointments, military decisions, and commercial relationships. Marriage alliances reinforced the settlement, joining the families through obligations that could survive an individual leader's death. The precise ceremonial forms and later claims attached to the alliance must be read with attention to the retrospective character of some surviving accounts.

The coalition did not suddenly erase all older Johor institutions. Malay court titles, religious authority, diplomatic habits, Orang Laut relationships, and trading settlements remained important. Nor did the Bugis newcomers become a unified ruling caste controlling every maritime community. Negotiated relationships with Malay elites and island groups still mattered, and other Bugis factions pursued their own interests elsewhere. Conflict remained possible when parties disagreed over the distribution of wealth or recognition. The new arrangement was therefore both a restoration of a sultan and a significant redistribution of political power. These two elements are not contradictory; each explains why the settlement could be made and why it required continued maintenance.

The episode also demonstrates the importance of regional mobility. Alliances between a Johor prince and Bugis leaders connected a political conflict in the Riau islands to households, fleets, and commercial routes extending across the archipelago. A ruler in this environment could rebuild his following with assistance from far beyond his immediate homeland. But that assistance came with political claims. In examining the 1722 settlement, students should therefore ask not only who won the struggle for the throne, but what kind of kingdom emerged from the bargain. The answer leads directly to the shared political authority of the Malay sultan and the Yang di-Pertuan Muda.

Division V · §§81.17–81.20

Johor-Riau, Malay–Bugis Government, and the Changing Straits

Division V · Section 81.17

The Malay Sultan and the Bugis Yang di-Pertuan Muda

After 1722 Sultan Sulaiman Badrul Alam Shah held dynastic legitimacy, while Bugis officeholders exercised much political and military authority. Marriages and appointments bound the groups together.

The office of Yang di-Pertuan Muda is best understood through the circumstances that created it. Raja Sulaiman's claim to the sultanate drew on the Bendahara ruling house, court titles, and recognition from Malay elites. The Bugis leaders who helped establish him possessed military strength, commercial networks, and supporters whose loyalty they could mobilize. A political arrangement recognizing both sources of power gave the coalition a chance to endure. The result was sometimes described as a dual structure, but it did not specify two permanently separated areas of competence like those in a modern written constitution. Authority was continuously shaped by the personalities, resources, marriages, and alliances of the officeholders.

Daeng Marewa held the junior rulership from 1722 until his death in 1728, and Daeng Chelak succeeded him, holding the position until 1745. The dates are important because they mark the transition from the initial settlement to a period when the new institution had to survive ordinary political problems rather than only the immediate defeat of Raja Kecil. A powerful Bugis officeholder could command maritime followings and influence trade, while the Malay sultan continued to embody a recognized dynastic and ceremonial center. Cooperation could strengthen both, but the balance shifted when one party possessed better access to military resources, merchants, or influential relatives. Neither office automatically made its holder supreme in every setting.

Marriage was an essential part of this political system. Alliances between Bugis leaders and Malay royal households created relationships of obligation and claims to status that could be invoked in future disputes. Such marriages also affected succession, because the children and relatives of several court lineages acquired connections to both sides of the partnership. Treating marriage solely as a ceremonial appendix to warfare would miss the way early modern political coalitions were reproduced over generations. Women within these households could become central to alliance making even though later court histories often privileged the deeds of male princes and warriors.

The distinction between formal title and effective authority had commercial consequences. A merchant might need protection from a Bugis military leader and an agreement with a Malay official responsible for court recognition. Tin-producing districts and coastal trading communities could bargain with different elements of the ruling coalition. Competing claims over appointments, collections, and shipping rights created room for conflict even as the enlarged network brought opportunities for commerce. In some cases the Bugis presence attracted new trading contacts; in others, established local leaders could resent the growing influence of migrants or try to shift relations elsewhere.

The principal nineteenth-century Malay-Bugis narrative, the Tuhfat al-Nafis, offers an influential account of how this arrangement was legitimized. Raja Ali Haji wrote as a member of a later courtly milieu deeply invested in understanding the rights and ancestry of Bugis-Malay rulers. His work is invaluable for studying what that political world remembered, but it was composed around 1866, more than a century after the first settlement. It cannot be used as if its precise speeches and accounts of private motives were transcripts from 1722. Students should compare its narrative with surviving eighteenth-century correspondence and ask what an author writing after later conflicts wished to establish about rightful authority.

By the middle of the eighteenth century the partnership had become a defining feature of Johor-Riau politics, yet disagreement was never absent. The death of Daeng Chelak in 1745 posed questions about leadership, patronage, and the relationships among families that the earlier agreements had not permanently settled. A strong historical interpretation avoids both extremes: it neither portrays the sultan as an empty figurehead from the outset nor treats the Bugis junior ruler as an ordinary official under an unchanged Malay monarchy. The arrangement was a negotiated political technology that preserved elements of the old court while creating a new basis for exercising power.

Section 81.18

Riau as a Commercial Entrepôt

Under the new system Riau grew as a centre of tin, pepper, textiles, marine products, and shipbuilding, linked by Bugis commerce to other archipelago ports; Malay, Bugis, Chinese, and Minangkabau traders interacted. The extensive British private-trader commerce of the 1760s–1780s belongs to later decades and should not be read back into the 1720s–1740s.

Riau's commercial importance grew from its position within a dense island network and from the ability of its rulers and merchants to connect several trading worlds. Ships from the Straits of Malacca, peninsular rivers, the Sumatran coast, and the wider archipelago could exchange cargo in its waters. The goods included tin, pepper, textiles, forest and marine products, rice, and supplies for vessels. No single item explains the entire economy, and some commodities became more important at different dates. The fundamental advantage was that a merchant could meet partners with different supplies and destinations in a relatively accessible anchorage, provided political conditions remained sufficiently favorable.

Bugis commercial connections were an important part of this expansion. Migrants and their descendants maintained relationships with other ports and could recruit sailors, capital, information, and goods through networks extending beyond the narrow territory of a particular court. Malay traders, Chinese merchants, Minangkabau groups, and sea-oriented communities also contributed. The existence of several commercial populations did not create an egalitarian society. Powerful court households could claim privileges, and merchants sometimes needed protection from political patrons. Ordinary laborers handled boats, loaded cargo, repaired vessels, and supplied food; their work made visible wealth possible but seldom won them extended attention in written political sources.

The court's economic policies had to balance revenue and attraction. Taxes, fees, gifts, and monopolistic privileges could finance military activity and noble households, yet harsh demands might encourage merchants to relocate. Security mattered too. A vessel's captain needed to know whether rival groups would recognize passes or promises issued by the ruling coalition and whether supplies would be available during a stay. Political legitimacy and port reputation therefore reinforced one another: a ruler acknowledged by influential traders could attract more traffic, while steady exchange gave the ruler means to reward the allies whose support made government possible.

Riau's prosperity should not be imagined as a simple linear rise. Wars over succession, conflict with Siak, Dutch interventions, and competition among merchant groups could interrupt business. The commercial relations of the 1720s and 1730s are also less continuously documented than the much more extensive British private-trader networks of the later eighteenth century. Detailed accounts of trade from the 1760s to 1780s must not be projected backward as though they described the city immediately after the 1722 settlement. This is a methodological lesson: a later source may reveal the possibilities of a port, but only dated evidence can establish how those possibilities were realized in an earlier generation.

The physical and social organization of a commercial center extended beyond a single shoreline. Storage sites, residential clusters, markets, boatbuilding places, freshwater sources, and connections to fishing and provisioning communities formed an interconnected urban landscape. Women and men could participate in exchange at different scales, from household production to voyages organized by leading merchants. Political changes influenced which groups had access to favorable locations or court protection. Even the term Riau may refer in different records to islands, settlements, or a larger political center; historians should be explicit about the geographical unit being described.

What made this maritime economy distinctive was its dependence on negotiated access. The Malay sultan's inherited prestige, Bugis military and commercial strength, regional Malay chiefs, and Orang Laut expertise each contributed something that could not easily be replaced by another actor. Such interdependence did not prevent coercion or rivalry, but it created incentives for partnership. The broader history of Johor-Riau thus challenges both a purely European-centered account of trade and a romantic portrait of harmonious regional cosmopolitanism. The port was an arena in which people pursued commercial opportunity while living under political arrangements that distributed resources and risks unequally.

Section 81.19

Siak, Terengganu, the VOC, and Rivalry within the Malay–Bugis World

Disputes over sovereignty and commerce involved Siak, regional Malay rulers, Bugis leaders, and the Dutch at Melaka, especially over tin-producing areas. Tensions became more visible after Daeng Chelak's death in 1745 and shaped conflicts later in the eighteenth century.

The settlement of 1722 did not end the political problem created by the 1699 regicide. Raja Kecil and his followers retained influence around Siak, while communities in eastern Sumatra and the Malay Peninsula continued to assess which ruler could provide advantage or protection. The Bugis leaders in Riau possessed strong claims to reward for their intervention, but their growing authority also altered relationships with established Malay families. Political competition could therefore emerge along several lines at once: disputes within a court, rival claims from Siak, disagreements between Malay and Bugis leaders, and contests for commercially valuable ports or access to tin-producing districts.

Terengganu and other peninsular political centers entered this world through kinship, commerce, and diplomacy, not as permanent subordinate provinces on a modern administrative map. A ruler's alliance could be strengthened by marriage or challenged by a rival's gifts and promises. Control of a coastal anchorage might be more immediately useful than a formal claim to extensive inland territory. Regional chiefs sometimes negotiated between neighboring powers, and even well-established courts could have limited ability to enforce a decision at a distant river mouth. This flexibility explains why political titles in Malay chronicles and Dutch records must be interpreted in their local setting rather than assumed to signify uniform territorial administration.

The VOC at Melaka remained an important participant. Company officials wished to secure trade and limit competition that threatened their revenues, but their military resources were not limitless and they had to operate among powerful Asian partners. Treaties could assign privileges in particular districts while merchants and rulers disagreed about who was included. The 1713 correspondence had already shown that Johor interpreted commercial rights in Siak differently from Dutch officials, and later struggles continued to combine legal claims with economic interests. Company reports are indispensable but their complaints about disorder or broken promises should be read alongside the political calculations of Malay and Bugis rulers.

Tin held particular significance because merchants in the strait sought access to mining and collecting districts and the means to transport their output. Competing courts could attempt to influence chiefs who controlled passage between the producing areas and maritime markets. The resulting disputes were not merely commercial in a narrow sense: revenue from tin and related trade financed ships, retainers, gifts, and political alliances. A dispute over an anchorage or trading contract could consequently affect the status of a prince or the capacity of a military leader to reward followers. This linkage between trade and political authority is one of the major continuities connecting the older Johor kingdom to the Malay-Bugis system.

Daeng Chelak's death in 1745 made questions of succession and patronage especially pressing within the Bugis side of the arrangement. His career had helped consolidate the Yang di-Pertuan Muda office after the initial period under Daeng Marewa. The next generation had to sustain relationships made through earlier campaigns and marriages while facing opponents with their own claims. Accounts of later eighteenth-century confrontations are useful for seeing what tensions developed, but they should be kept outside the chapter's detailed chronological endpoint. By about 1750 the important fact is the persistence of a commercially active yet politically contested coalition, not an assumption that a later conflict was inevitable.

The wider consequence was an increasingly complex political geography. Riau, Johor, Siak, Terengganu, Dutch Melaka, and smaller communities all participated in a field of shifting loyalties and overlapping claims. Some rulers portrayed their relationships as ancient bonds of obligation, whereas others emphasized practical bargains made under new conditions. The later division of the region into colonial territories was not already written into this arrangement. The chapter should therefore conclude this section by returning to the fundamental analytical question: how could a kingdom preserve royal continuity when its merchants, armed allies, and territorial connections were negotiated across several centers of authority?

Section 81.20

From Melaka to Johor-Riau: Continuity, Maritime Power, and Change

The Melakan inheritance survived conquest, warfare, and dynastic displacement through titles, alliances, and Orang Laut loyalty, though the Bendahara and Malay–Bugis arrangements were significant departures from the older lineage. The political geography of 1750 had not yet been fixed by later British and Dutch boundaries such as those after the 1824 treaty.

Johor-Riau's history from the sixteenth to the mid-eighteenth century demonstrates that political continuity need not mean unchanged institutions or territory. The exiled descendants of the Melakan sultans built new centers and reassembled commercial followings after 1511. They invoked inherited titles and political ideals while adapting to the practical geography of rivers and islands. Their authority was therefore both remembered and recreated. The Portuguese destruction of Johor Lama and later Acehnese attacks damaged royal settlements, but the ability to withdraw, negotiate, and attract followers gave the kingdom resources for recovery. These developments cannot be reduced to a single story of decline after the fall of Melaka.

The Orang Laut were essential to this pattern because they connected political authority to maritime labor and knowledge. Their leaders and households supplied crews, intelligence, products, and access to waterways, often through relationships developed over generations. But their loyalties were not automatic or uniform. When royal claims became contested after 1699, some groups reconsidered whom they would serve. This revealed a limit built into Johor's strength: reliance on mobile followers allowed the kingdom to recover from the loss of a capital, yet followers could also redirect the networks that gave royal authority substance. Sovereignty was a practice sustained through reciprocal relationships, never merely a title held at court.

Commerce was equally central. Johor's value as an entrepôt lay in its ability to connect goods, merchants, brokers, and shipping routes that no single ruler produced or controlled alone. Cooperation with the VOC in 1606 and again in the campaign ending in 1641 showed that Johor could act diplomatically and militarily alongside a powerful foreign company. Yet the Dutch retention of Melaka and later disputes over trading rights revealed the unequal possibilities of such partnerships. A maritime court could gain security and access while accepting new constraints. Johor's commercial vitality did not depend on excluding all rivals, but it did require political authority capable of making trade secure and profitable enough to sustain loyalty.

The years 1699–1722 changed the foundations of rule. Mahmud Shah II's killing discredited the idea that continuity of the accepted direct dynasty was assured, while the Bendahara succession prompted arguments about the meaning of legitimate kingship. Raja Kecil turned those unresolved arguments into a military and diplomatic challenge, and the eventual alliance with Bugis leaders reshaped the distribution of authority. Raja Sulaiman's accession in 1722 retained the Malay sultanate while recognizing a powerful Yang di-Pertuan Muda. The outcome was simultaneously dynastic restoration, political compromise, and institutional transformation. Calling it only one of these obscures the process by which people with different claims built a workable, though contested, government.

Source criticism is indispensable to this interpretation. Dutch treaties tell us about negotiated obligations but not always their subsequent enforcement. A Malay royal letter written in 1713 reveals commercial grievances and arguments about sovereignty, but its language of friendship was also diplomatic persuasion. The Sejarah Melayu supplies traditions of kingship and dynastic memory; the later Tuhfat al-Nafis explains how a nineteenth-century Malay-Bugis milieu represented earlier political changes. These texts are not interchangeable. Their differences in date, author, audience, and purpose can be used productively: what one presents as ancient legitimacy another records as a contested bargain, and what a foreign company calls disorder may be a dispute over rightful authority.

The principal historical lesson is that the Malay successor world remained an active force in early modern Southeast Asian politics. Johor's rulers made decisions, fought wars, negotiated with Europeans and Asian rivals, and drew upon diverse maritime communities to sustain power. Bugis migration introduced new military and commercial networks, while royal alliances reshaped what it meant to govern Johor-Riau. By about 1750 the region had not been divided into the later territorial states familiar from modern maps. It was a changing network of courts, islands, ports, trading routes, and mobile people. The next chapter turns to Makassar and South Sulawesi, where related questions of migration, commerce, indigenous state formation, and European intervention emerge from a different regional history.

Chapter Summary

The Portuguese conquest of Melaka in 1511 displaced the ruling dynasty without ending its political aspirations or the networks through which its members could seek support. Mahmud Shah’s exile and the establishment of Johor under Alauddin Riayat Shah II demonstrate how a Malay court could use descent and remembered authority to reconstruct government in another setting. The Johor River and surrounding islands offered safety and access to shipping, but the political system had to be assembled through practical relationships, not merely proclaimed by royal title.

Johor’s geography encouraged flexible authority. Its river settlements and island anchorages served different functions at different times, while merchants and maritime followers connected them. Movement of a royal court after war could preserve political life, but it imposed new costs and tested loyalties. A map showing one administrative center at a given date cannot capture the whole domain of people and relationships that sustained Johor’s rulers.

Orang Laut communities were essential to royal fleets, navigation, intelligence, and maritime commerce. Their service was shaped by differentiated obligations, benefits, and loyalties. Some groups supported the ruling court for generations; others renegotiated or redirected their allegiances during political crises. Describing all Orang Laut as pirates, slaves, or a single royal navy obscures both their diverse experiences and the political choices that made them consequential.

Johor’s kingship combined inherited Melakan titles and ideas of daulat with shifting arrangements among the sultan, bendahara, temenggung, regional chiefs, and commercial groups. The Sejarah Melayu preserves influential conceptions of royal authority, but its manuscript traditions are literary and political, not an unmediated record. Royal legitimacy depended on performance, patronage, and recognition as well as ancestry.

Johor survived formidable rivals. Acehnese campaigns and Portuguese attacks destroyed or disrupted important settlements, but did not permanently eliminate the court’s alliances or commercial networks. Cooperation with the VOC was a calculated effort to change the balance of power. The treaties of May and September 1606 show Johor acting diplomatically, while the impact of the European truce of 1609 reveals the danger of relying on distant allies whose priorities could change.

The Dutch–Johor capture of Portuguese Melaka in January 1641 removed a major adversary and enlarged commercial opportunities for Johor. The VOC nevertheless retained the city. Johor’s position improved through trade and regional influence, not through restoration of direct rule in Melaka. Later commercial disputes showed that military cooperation did not eliminate competing claims over ports, shipping, and jurisdiction.

Johor’s commercial importance rested on exchanges among maritime and inland economies: pepper, tin, food, textiles, forest products, and marine goods. Brokers, shahbandars, captains, craft workers, and ordinary households made these transactions possible. Prosperity strengthened a ruler’s ability to reward followers while also raising the stakes of disputes with merchants, foreign companies, and neighboring Malay polities. The wars with Jambi in the later seventeenth century were independent expressions of these regional rivalries.

Mahmud Shah II’s murder in 1699 brought a crisis of succession and legitimacy. Abdul Jalil IV’s accession from the bendahara’s office maintained a government but did not produce unqualified acceptance by all elites or Orang Laut groups. His letter of 1713 to the VOC reveals the continued operation of diplomacy and trade alongside unresolved disputes over recognition, resources, and commercial rights.

Raja Kecil exploited the unresolved problem in 1718 by claiming descent from Mahmud Shah II and attracting support from Siak, Minangkabau, and maritime networks. His genealogy remains contested, but the claim was politically influential. The displaced Bendahara family then forged an alliance with Bugis leaders, who helped place Raja Sulaiman on the throne in 1722. Daeng Marewa and Daeng Chelak consolidated the new Yang di-Pertuan Muda office, changing the distribution of political and commercial power.

By about 1750 Johor-Riau preserved significant parts of the Melakan inheritance while operating through a transformed Malay–Bugis political partnership. Its history illustrates the resilience of maritime sovereignty and its dependence on shifting allegiances, commercial opportunity, and negotiated authority. Dutch treaties, contemporary Malay royal correspondence, and later court histories must be read according to their dates and purposes. The later division of the region by European colonial treaties had not yet determined the political landscape of this chapter.

Chapter Reflection

Johor-Riau’s history challenges the assumption that a kingdom must be defined primarily by a stable capital or territorial border. The descendants of Melaka rebuilt a political center after conquest and then relocated repeatedly under military pressure. Their ability to keep a following depended on recognition, commercial opportunity, maritime skills, and alliances. State survival was a process undertaken by rulers and communities, not an automatic property of a dynasty.

The story also gives prominence to people usually left at the edge of court chronicles. Orang Laut pilots, households, and maritime leaders sustained the practical connections through which royal authority was exercised. Merchants, craft workers, and regional chiefs shaped the resources available to courts. Their different responses to the crisis of 1699 reveal that political ideas about legitimacy influenced actual access to ships, labor, markets, and security.

The shifts from Melakan descendants to the Bendahara dynasty and then to a Malay–Bugis partnership should be evaluated as both continuity and transformation. Some political forms survived while power was redistributed. The treaties of 1606, the letter of 1713, and the later Tuhfat al-Nafis demonstrate why historical sources must be situated in the moment of their creation and not collapsed into one authoritative voice.

As the narrative moves to Makassar and South Sulawesi, it carries forward a broader question: how did Southeast Asian maritime societies create political authority in the presence of migration, economic opportunity, external intervention, and competing dynastic claims? Johor-Riau shows that such authority was made and remade through relationships that crossed the waterways later divided by colonial boundaries.
