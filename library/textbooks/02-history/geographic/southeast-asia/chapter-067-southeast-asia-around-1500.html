Chapter Opening

Around 1500, no ruler, religion, port, or commercial system governed all of Southeast Asia. The region was a field of interacting courts, river valleys, harbors, upland communities, and maritime networks, each with its own rhythms of power, and it entered the sixteenth century as a set of functioning societies rather than as a passive stage awaiting outside arrival.

Three documents, produced across roughly eighty years, allow a historian to stand at different points in the fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries and look at the same regional world. The first is the account of Ma Huan, a Chinese Muslim interpreter who sailed with the Ming admiral Zheng He and who completed a description of the "ocean shores" in 1433. The second is a set of stone inscriptions set up at Pegu, in lower Burma, between about 1476 and 1479 on the orders of the Mon king Dhammaceti (Dhammazedi), recording a reform of the Buddhist monastic order. The third is the Suma Oriental, written by the Portuguese apothecary Tomé Pires in the years after he reached Melaka in 1512. A Chinese court observer, a Southeast Asian king and his monks, and a European commercial agent each described what they saw or what they intended, and none saw the whole region.

Read together, the three sources suggest a region that was already densely connected. Ma Huan described ports in Champa, Java, Sumatra, and the Malay Peninsula where merchants from several seas met. The Pegu inscriptions show a Southeast Asian ruler using relations with Sri Lanka to define the orthodoxy of his realm. Pires, writing soon after the Portuguese had entered the Strait of Malacca, recorded in detail a spice trade that had been moving cloves, nutmeg, and mace through Javanese, Malay, Indian, and Chinese hands long before any European ship reached Maluku. They also show how unevenly the region can be reconstructed. One source is early, one is local and royal, and one is retrospective and written from the standpoint of a newcomer.

This chapter surveys Southeast Asia in the period historians commonly center on the year 1500, though the evidence compels a wider window, roughly from the mid-fifteenth century to the second decade of the sixteenth. It opens Volume V, but it does not begin a new story. The preceding chapters examined the Theravada transformation of the mainland (Chapter 53), the rise of Ayutthaya, Lan Na, and Lan Xang (Chapters 56 to 58), the political reorganization of Burma (Chapters 59 and 60), the transformation of Angkor (Chapter 61), and the Islamic commercial and political order from Samudera Pasai to Melaka, Brunei, and Sulu (Chapters 62 to 66). Chapter 67 draws these regional histories into one comparative picture.

The chapter has a particular responsibility: to describe the political, commercial, religious, and social order of the region on the eve of sustained European intervention without allowing that intervention to organize the description. The Portuguese appear only toward the end, in Section 67.19, as one element of a widening Indian Ocean system. Their maritime entry into Asian waters belongs to Chapter 68, and the conquest of Melaka in 1511 to Chapter 69. What precedes them is the subject here: kingdoms of the Irrawaddy, Chao Phraya, Mekong, and Red River systems; port-states of the Strait of Malacca and the Java Sea; the clove and nutmeg islands of the east; and the farmers, boatmen, laborers, merchants, monks, and householders whose work sustained all of them.

Two cautions run through the chapter. First, 1500 is an analytical convenience, not a threshold. The mainland polities of 1450, 1475, and 1500 were not the same, and several decisive events, such as the Vietnamese conquest of Vijaya in 1471 and the Pegu reforms of the later 1470s, fall before the date. Second, commercial integration should not be confused with political unity. Merchants moved goods across the whole region, but this created neither a single state nor a single administration nor a single faith. Modern national borders likewise cannot be read back onto fifteenth-century realms whose authority radiated from courts and shifted with alliances, tribute, and the movement of people.

Finally, the chapter asks students to treat the region's own historical capabilities as the foundation for what followed. When Europeans arrived in strength, they encountered political institutions, merchant communities, monastic establishments, legal traditions, and agricultural systems that had been shaped by Southeast Asians over centuries. Those institutions determined much of how the encounter unfolded. Understanding them is the purpose of this chapter.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how historians reconstruct Southeast Asia around 1500 from Chinese, Southeast Asian, archaeological, and early Portuguese evidence, and distinguish contemporary from retrospective testimony.
  2. Describe the geographic relationships among river valleys, uplands, monsoon seas, straits, and archipelagic passages that shaped mainland and maritime political systems.
  3. Trace the principal political and commercial changes between about 1450 and 1500 on the mainland and in the archipelago.
  4. Compare the political organization of Đại Việt, Ayutthaya, Lan Na, Lan Xang, the Khmer kingdom, Ava, Hanthawaddy, and Arakan, distinguishing claims of supremacy from direct administrative control.
  5. Analyze the causes and consequences of the 1471 Vietnamese conquest of Vijaya while explaining the continuity of Cham polities and communities.
  6. Assess the standing of Melaka, Pasai, Javanese ports, the Malukan sultanates, the Banda communities, Brunei, and Sulu within regional commercial networks.
  7. Explain how production centers, collecting ports, intermediaries, and international markets were linked in the circulation of rice, pepper, spices, tin, gold, forest products, ceramics, and textiles.
  8. Reconstruct the social life of port cities and their agricultural hinterlands, including merchants, laborers, women, households, and enslaved people.
  9. Compare religious institutions and royal patronage across Theravada, Islamic, Confucian-Buddhist, and Hindu-Buddhist settings, using the Kalyani inscriptions as evidence.
  10. Evaluate historiographical arguments about whether the decades around 1500 mark the beginning of an early modern era in Southeast Asia.
  11. Contextualize the beginning of Portuguese expansion within an existing Asian commercial and political order.
  12. Interpret primary sources by identifying authorship, date, perspective, and limits, and by avoiding the projection of later conditions onto earlier ones.

Historical Context

By the middle of the fifteenth century the region had passed through a long series of political reorganizations. On the mainland, the great classical centers had given way to new configurations. Pagan had collapsed in the late thirteenth century, and Burmese political life had fragmented among Upper Burma, the Shan states, the delta, and the Arakan coast before reconsolidating around Ava and Hanthawaddy (Chapters 59 and 60). Tai-speaking courts had risen in the Chao Phraya basin and the northern valleys, producing Sukhothai, Ayutthaya, and Lan Na, while the Mekong corridor saw the formation of Lan Xang (Chapters 54 to 58). The Khmer kingdom had shifted its political center away from Angkor in a process this textbook treats as transformation, not sudden collapse (Chapter 61). In the east, an independent Đại Việt had survived the Mongol-Yuan wars and a brief Ming occupation in the early fifteenth century before the Lê dynasty restored independence (Chapters 44 and 45).

In maritime Southeast Asia, the transformations were no less deep. Srivijaya's older system had dissolved into competing Sumatran and Malay centers (Chapter 47), while Majapahit had reached its fourteenth-century prominence and then fragmented (Chapters 50 to 52). Muslim merchants and rulers had established themselves at Samudera Pasai and other ports, and in the fifteenth century the sultanate of Melaka, founded around the turn of the century, became one of the principal ports of Asia (Chapters 62 to 65). Muslim political formation also advanced in Brunei and Sulu (Chapter 66).

Beyond the region, the early Ming dynasty had sent the large maritime expeditions associated with Zheng He between 1405 and 1433, which brought Ming diplomacy into direct contact with many Southeast Asian ports and gave certain rulers, notably Melaka's, a powerful external patron. After 1433 these state-sponsored voyages ended, but Chinese private and tributary commerce continued, and Southeast Asian merchants and rulers remained deeply engaged with Chinese markets. Across the Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea, merchants from Gujarat, the Coromandel Coast, Malabar, and Bengal sustained connections with the Red Sea and Persian Gulf. These links predated any European presence by many centuries.

Historians have framed this period in different ways. Barbara Watson Andaya and Leonard Y. Andaya treat 1400 to 1511 as a phase of transformation driven by established maritime connections and changing political authority, followed by faster change after 1511. Anthony Reid's "age of commerce" emphasizes the growth of trade and urban life between about 1450 and 1680. Victor Lieberman stresses parallel mainland consolidation. This chapter draws on all three while examining, in Section 67.18, whether any of these periodizations should be accepted without qualification.

Historical Vocabulary
Tributary relations

Definition. In this chapter the term refers to relationships in which a ruler acknowledged the superior standing of another by sending envoys, gifts, or goods, and often by accepting titles or seals, in exchange for recognition, protection, or trading access.

Historical context. Tribute worked in several directions. Ming China recognized Southeast Asian rulers through its own tribute system, while mainland rulers such as those of Ayutthaya and Ava claimed or received submission from lesser rulers by means that varied from formal oaths to periodic military campaigns. A ruler could be a tributary of one court and receive tribute from another at the same time.

Why it matters. Tribute describes recognition, not necessarily administration. A tributary might keep its own officials, army, and revenue, so a claim of tribute does not establish direct control.

Potential misunderstanding. Students should avoid reading tribute as a modern "vassal state" with fixed borders, or assuming that the receiving court's records, which called missions "tribute," reflect how the sending ruler understood the exchange.

Historical Vocabulary
Age of Commerce

Definition. A period label proposed by Anthony Reid for the era from about 1450 to 1680, when Southeast Asia's participation in long-distance trade intensified and port cities, monetization, and commodity production grew.

Historical context. Reid's argument was that commerce, in pepper, spices, rice, textiles, and silver among other goods, shaped political and social life across the region, while the label itself is a modern scholarly convention, not a term used at the time.

Why it matters. It encourages historians to treat trade as central to state formation and household life and to see Southeast Asians as commercial participants rather than passive suppliers.

Potential misunderstanding. The label does not mean commerce was equally important everywhere or that agriculture was marginal. Section 67.16 shows that ports depended heavily on rural production and labor.

Division I — Southeast Asia around 1500: Evidence, Geography, and Regional Transformation
Section 67.1

Reconstructing Southeast Asia around 1500

A regional survey of any single year invites a false impression of precision. The sources for Southeast Asia around 1500 are scattered across more than a century, written in many languages, and produced for different purposes. Historians therefore reconstruct a window, roughly 1450 to 1520, and ask of each piece of evidence what moment it actually illuminates.

Chinese records are among the most continuous. The Ming court's annals, the Ming Shilu, record the arrival of envoys from Champa, Siam, Java, Melaka, and other polities, along with the titles and gifts the court bestowed. Travel accounts by participants in Zheng He's voyages, including Ma Huan, Fei Xin, and Gong Zhen, describe ports and peoples as seen in the early fifteenth century. These sources preserve valuable detail about trade goods and ruling customs, but they were written by outsiders interested in diplomacy and commerce, and they stop being detailed for many regions after the 1430s.

Southeast Asian sources are rich but uneven. Stone and copper-plate inscriptions in Mon, Pali, Burmese, Old Javanese, Thai, Khmer, and Cham record royal donations, religious reforms, and legal decisions. The Kalyani inscriptions at Pegu are a major example. Court chronicles, such as the Vietnamese Đại Việt sử ký toàn thư, whose original compilation dates to 1479 under Lê Thánh Tông, belong to the period but reflect the dynasty's own perspective. Many other chronicles, including Ayutthayan and Khmer royal histories, the Malay Sejarah Melayu, and Javanese babad traditions, were composed or reworked well after 1500 and mix earlier memory with later political purpose. They are essential but retrospective.

Material evidence offers an independent check. Excavated port sites, shipwrecks carrying Chinese and mainland Southeast Asian ceramics, coin finds, temple complexes, and the remains of the Javanese capital region at Trowulan show where goods and people moved, though archaeological dating rarely resolves events to a particular decade. Arab navigational literature, notably the work of the Indian Ocean pilot Ibn Majid composed around 1490, demonstrates that Southeast Asian waters were known in detail to western Indian Ocean navigators.

Finally, accounts by Portuguese writers, principally Tomé Pires and Duarte Barbosa, and the later reports associated with Afonso de Albuquerque, describe Southeast Asia from the years after 1509. They contain unusually detailed commercial information, collected from local merchants and officials, but they are retrospective with respect to 1500, are shaped by Portuguese commercial and political aims, and sometimes reflect conditions altered by Portuguese intervention itself. This chapter therefore uses them to illuminate structures that likely predated 1511, while flagging each use.

The distinction between kinds of evidence also governs how disagreements are handled. Where an inscription and a later chronicle conflict, historians generally give weight to the contemporary stone, but not automatically, since inscriptions record what a donor wished to be remembered. Where Chinese and Southeast Asian sources describe the same polity under different names, as with the many Chinese renderings of Southeast Asian ports and rulers, identification itself becomes a scholarly problem. Silence matters as well. The absence of a polity from Ming records does not show that it was unimportant, only that it did not engage the Ming court in ways that generated documents. A responsible reconstruction therefore presents conclusions in grades: what is directly attested, what is probable, and what rests on tradition.

Source and Method Study

Which "1500"? Dating the Evidence

Before using any source in this chapter, a historian should place it on a three-part scale: contemporary (produced within a few decades of 1500 by a participant or observer), retrospective (written later, drawing on memory or earlier records), and modern reconstruction (scholarly synthesis). Ma Huan (1433) is contemporary with the early fifteenth century but earlier than 1500 by two generations. The Kalyani inscriptions (later 1470s) are contemporary with the reforms they describe. Tomé Pires (c. 1512 to 1515) is close to 1500 but post-conquest. The Malay and Javanese chronicles are largely retrospective.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might a source written in 1433 be useful for understanding 1500 in some respects and misleading in others?
  2. What types of information are more likely to survive in inscriptions than in travel accounts, and vice versa?
  3. How could archaeological evidence be used to test a claim made in a retrospective chronicle?
Section 67.2

Mainland and Maritime Southeast Asia: Geography and Strategic Connections

The mainland's political geography was organized around river systems. The Irrawaddy linked the dry zone of Upper Burma, where the courts of Ava and later Toungoo drew on irrigated rice lands, to a delta that supported the Mon kingdom at Pegu and a coast open to the Bay of Bengal. The Chao Phraya basin joined fertile floodplains to the Gulf of Siam, favoring an Ayutthaya positioned between the sea and an extensive rice-growing hinterland. The Mekong linked the Lao valleys to the Cambodian plain and the Tonle Sap lake system, whose seasonal flood pulse sustained fisheries and rice cultivation. The Red River delta supported a dense population of wet-rice farmers in northern Đại Việt, while the narrow central Vietnamese coast, divided by spurs of the Annamite range into small plains, had historically supported the Cham polities and, after 1471, a growing Vietnamese frontier.

These valleys were separated and connected by uplands. Mountain corridors carried migrants, merchants, and armies, and upland communities practicing swidden agriculture traded forest products, minerals, and labor with lowland courts. Political boundaries followed rivers and passes only loosely. Authority radiated from courts and weakened toward frontiers, a pattern historians describe with the mandala model introduced in Chapter 1, which is a modern analytic tool and not a contemporary term.

The maritime world was organized around seas, straits, and seasonal winds (see Chapter 4). The southwest monsoon carried ships eastward and northward in the middle of the year, and the northeast monsoon reversed the pattern in winter, producing rhythms of arrival, waiting, and departure that made ports in the Strait of Malacca natural meeting points. The strait itself, with its sheltered anchorages and river mouths on the Sumatran and Malay coasts, concentrated commerce. The Sunda Strait offered an alternate passage, the Java Sea linked Javanese ports to Borneo, Sulawesi, and Maluku, and the South China Sea tied the Malay Peninsula, Champa, the Philippines, and Brunei to the Chinese coast.

Mainland and maritime systems met in several ways. Rivers brought mainland products, such as forest goods, rice, and metals, to coastal ports. The Malay Peninsula's narrow waist allowed overland and river portages that shortened some routes between the Bay of Bengal and the Gulf of Siam, and ports on both coasts, including those under Ayutthaya's influence, traded with Melaka. Ayutthaya, Pegu, and the ports of Champa and Đại Việt all participated in seaborne exchange, while Javanese courts drew heavily on irrigated rice. The categories used in Chapter 1, mainland and maritime, are therefore useful regional distinctions and not walls.

Distance and seasonality should be handled cautiously. Travel times between ports depended on wind, vessel type, cargo, and the decisions of captains, and the sources rarely give reliable figures, so historians avoid precise claims about sailing durations. What the evidence does show is regularity: merchants timed arrivals to the monsoon, remained in port for months while awaiting the change of wind, and thereby created the seasonal populations that gave ports like Melaka their character. On land, the rainy season slowed movement and campaigning, while the dry season opened roads, river fords, and military operations. The geography of 1500 thus consisted of calendars as much as of places, and political life followed both.

Section 67.3

The Maritime World before 1500 in Chinese Travel Accounts

Ma Huan's Ying-yai Sheng-lan ("Overall Survey of the Ocean's Shores") is the most detailed single description of fifteenth-century Southeast Asian ports from a participant's pen. Ma Huan was a Muslim interpreter from the Zhejiang region who accompanied three of Zheng He's voyages. The surviving work dates in its main form from 1433, and its translation by J. V. G. Mills for the Hakluyt Society in 1970 remains the standard English edition. The account moves systematically from country to country, describing climate, customs, rulers, food, trade goods, and religion.

On Java, Ma Huan reports a populous island with several kinds of inhabitants: Muslim merchants from the west, Chinese settlers, many of whom were also Muslim and some of whom had formed communities at ports such as Gresik, and the native population. His account of the inland court and the coastal markets suggests a society that mixed court-centered authority with active maritime commerce. On Melaka, he describes a settlement that had grown quickly under the patronage of the Ming court, relied on trade rather than farming, and whose ruler and people observed Islam. He gives similar descriptions of ports in Sumatra, Champa, and Siam, with attention to the commodities each exported.

For students of 1500, Ma Huan's value lies in what he shows about structures that persisted: the multiethnic composition of ports; the importance of Chinese and Muslim merchant communities; the combination of royal courts and commercial specialization; and the way several goods, such as pepper, aromatic woods, tin, and camphor, moved through regional ports. His account cannot establish political conditions at 1500. Melaka's rulers, Java's politics, and Ayutthaya's reach all changed in the decades between 1433 and the end of the century. Like all travel accounts, it also reflects what a Chinese official could observe and ask, and his information about inland societies is usually secondhand.

The end of the Zheng He voyages after 1433 affected Southeast Asia without ending its Chinese trade. The Ming court's formal tributary missions continued at a reduced scale, and Chinese private shipping, though officially restricted at times, remained active. Ports in Fujian and Guangdong and the Ryukyu Kingdom sustained connections with Melaka, Siam, and Java throughout the fifteenth century. In this sense, Chinese demand continued to shape the region's commercial order, though not through large state-run fleets.

Ma Huan's own position deserves attention. As a Muslim, he was alert to Islamic communities and rulers and sometimes described them with sympathy, which may have shaped what he noticed and recorded. As an interpreter, he depended on informants and on the languages he could use, probably Chinese, Arabic, and Persian rather than the local languages of every port. Modern scholars, including Mills in his notes, have compared his descriptions with other Chinese accounts and with later evidence to identify errors and uncertain identifications. Students should therefore treat the account as a carefully produced but partial window onto the early fifteenth century, valuable above all for the structures it reveals.

Section 67.4

From 1450 to 1500: A Regional Chronology of Political and Commercial Change

The half century from 1450 to 1500 was not a static plateau. On the mainland, Đại Việt under Lê Thánh Tông expanded militarily and administratively, defeating Vijaya in 1471 and undertaking campaigns against upland and Lao neighbors. Ayutthaya under Borommatrailokanat fought a long contest with Lan Na for the upper Chao Phraya region and reorganized its government, though the surviving law texts are later compilations whose dating is debated. In Burma, the Mon kingdom of Hanthawaddy under Dhammaceti implemented a religious reform and presided over a prosperous coast, while Ava weathered Shan pressure with uneven success, and Arakan established an independent court at Mrauk-U in the 1430s.

In the maritime world, Melaka grew in commercial stature. Later Malay tradition associates the reigns of Mansur Shah and then Mahmud Shah with expanding diplomacy and trade, and Pires later described a port with extraordinary merchant communities. Pasai continued as a pepper and Islamic learning center. In Java, the political order associated with Majapahit fragmented, with competing centers and the growing importance of coastal Muslim ports such as Gresik, Tuban, and Demak. In the east, the clove-producing polities of Ternate and Tidore consolidated their positions as trade with Javanese and Malay merchants expanded, and Islam gained a foothold at their courts. Brunei under Bolkiah grew in influence, and Sulu's court took shape in the southern Philippines (Chapter 66).

These trends were not synchronized. The 1450s were years of conflict and reorganization on the mainland, while the 1470s saw major reforms and conquests; the 1480s and 1490s combined stability in some courts, such as Hanthawaddy, with growing strain in others, including Ava. By 1500 the political map differed significantly from that of 1450, which is the reason the chapter treats 1500 as a vantage point and not a snapshot.

The chronology also shows that political change and commercial change often moved in different directions. Melaka's commercial preeminence did not require conquest of the entire strait, yet its rulers also asserted power through military expeditions and subordinate relationships, including the later Malay traditions about Pahang. Đại Việt's territorial expansion, meanwhile, was driven chiefly by continental statecraft rather than maritime commerce. Hanthawaddy combined a flourishing coast with religious reform, while Java's political fragmentation coincided with the growth of coastal trade. Students should therefore treat the later sections of this chapter as comparisons among differently structured societies.

A further lesson of the chronology concerns the speed of change. Some transformations, such as the Vietnamese conquest of Vijaya, occurred within a single campaign, while others, such as the conversion of coastal rulers to Islam or the fragmentation of Majapahit, unfolded across decades and are poorly dated. Historians use precise dates for events recorded in contemporary documents, but approximate or traditional dates for processes known mainly from later sources. Readers should note which kind of date they are given. A chronology that mixes secure and traditional dates without comment gives a false impression of uniform certainty, and this chapter flags the difference wherever it matters.

Section 67.5

Đại Việt, Champa, and the Eastern Mainland around 1500

Đại Việt around 1500 was shaped by the reign of Lê Thánh Tông (1460 to 1497). The Lê dynasty had restored independence after the Ming occupation of the 1400s, and Lê Thánh Tông strengthened the court through an expanded bureaucracy recruited by examinations in the Neo-Confucian tradition, a legal code associated with the Hồng Đức reign period, a census-based system of provincial administration, and measures to regulate land and village affairs. Buddhism, popular religion, and local cults continued alongside the court's Confucian orientation. The Đại Việt court's chronicles and legal materials, however, represent official aspirations and cannot be assumed to describe practice in every village.

Militarily, Lê forces campaigned against upland peoples to the west and in 1479 and 1480 against the Lao kingdom of Lan Xang, reaching Luang Prabang, after which Lan Xang recovered. The most consequential campaign was the conquest of the Cham capital Vijaya in 1471, in which the Cham ruler was captured and much of the Cham northern territory was absorbed into new Vietnamese provinces. Vietnamese settlers, soldiers, and officials began to occupy lands formerly under Cham rule, a process whose pace and character are examined in Chapter 88.

The conquest did not end Cham political and religious life. Cham polities survived to the south, at Kauthara and Panduranga, and Cham communities, Hindu, Muslim, and local in religious practice, continued in the coastal plains and highlands, maintaining trade and diplomatic contact with neighboring courts. Many Cham in former Vijaya territory lived under Vietnamese rule and gradually adapted, while others migrated. Describing 1471 as the "end of Champa," as some older narratives do, misrepresents this persistence.

The relationships between lowland and highland societies were also changing. Vietnamese court expansion into the highlands met resistance and led to arrangements of tribute and local autonomy. Highland societies traded forest products, minerals, and labor, and they maintained their own leadership structures. The eastern mainland, then, was a zone of overlapping authority, with the Lê court's capital Thăng Long at the center of a state that was expanding and consolidating, but whose expansion depended on military force, migration, and local accommodation.

The Lê administrative program also had social consequences. Registers of population and land, intended to support taxation and service, made households more visible to the state, and the examination system opened careers to scholars whose loyalty lay with the court. Village communities, however, retained their own leaders, customs, and common lands, and the court negotiated with them as much as it commanded them. The conquest of Vijaya brought captives, land, and tribute to the Lê court, and it also created a frontier where officials, soldiers, and settlers encountered Cham farmers, traders, and religious communities. Vietnamese historical writing later portrayed this frontier as a story of steady advance, but contemporary conditions were more uneven, as Chapter 88 examines.

Section 67.6

Ayutthaya, Lan Na, Lan Xang, and the Khmer Political World

Ayutthaya around 1500 was the leading power of the Chao Phraya basin, with a court that combined Theravada patronage, Khmer-influenced royal ritual, and a commercial position on the Gulf of Siam. Under Borommatrailokanat, the court reorganized government in ways later recorded in law texts that distinguish civil and military branches and assign rank values expressed in nominal units of cultivated field area, conventionally termed sakdi na. Such numbers ranked status and legal standing; they cannot be read literally as an individual’s acreage or number of laborers. Because the surviving laws were compiled later, historians debate which provisions and institutions can securely be dated to Trailok’s fifteenth-century reign. Ayutthaya's ports sent trade and tribute missions to China and traded with Melaka and with Muslim and Chinese merchants, and Ayutthayan campaigns against Melaka in the fifteenth century, reported in later sources, failed to bring it under direct control.

To the north, Lan Na, centered on Chiang Mai, was a distinct Tai kingdom with its own royal tradition. Under Tilokarat, Lan Na fought Ayutthaya over the Sukhothai region and the upper valleys; by the 1470s the contest had reached an uneasy settlement. Tilokarat was also a major Buddhist patron, and a council traditionally dated to 1477 at Chiang Mai reflects the kingdom's scholarly activity. Lan Na's political life rested on irrigated valley rice and upland trade and was linked to Burma and the Shan states as well as to Ayutthaya.

Lan Xang, the "Kingdom of a Million Elephants," occupied the middle Mekong around Luang Prabang, and it combined Lao and Tai political traditions with Theravada Buddhism. Its fifteenth-century history includes the Vietnamese invasion of 1479 and 1480 and a succession of rulers whose dates are uncertain. Lan Xang looked toward both the Mekong's trade and the highlands, and its relations with Đại Việt, Lan Na, and Ayutthaya were shaped by diplomacy and war (see Chapters 58 and 90).

The Khmer kingdom had relocated its main political activity toward the Phnom Penh and lower Mekong region, and Angkor continued as a major religious site. Later Khmer chronicles credit a king commonly named Ponhea Yat with the shift in the fifteenth century, but the chronicles are retrospective and their chronology is uncertain. In the decades around 1500 the Khmer kingdom was a participant in Mekong trade, engaged with Ayutthaya, Đại Việt, and maritime merchants, and it maintained royal institutions and Buddhist patronage. The Khmer world did not disappear. It reorganized, and its later history, including pressure from Ayutthaya and Đại Việt, belongs to Chapter 89.

Relations with the sea differed among these courts. Ayutthaya's port functions gave it direct access to Chinese, Malay, and Muslim merchants and to the goods of the Malay Peninsula, and its kings used trade revenue and foreign specialists to strengthen their courts. Lan Na and Lan Xang were inland kingdoms whose commercial horizons ran along rivers, caravan paths, and upland routes toward Burma, Yunnan, and the Mekong. The Khmer court's shift toward the lower Mekong brought it closer to maritime commerce and to Cham, Malay, and Chinese traders. These differences in orientation affected each kingdom's revenue, diplomacy, and military options and help explain why the mainland cannot be described as a single agrarian bloc.

Section 67.7

Ava, Hanthawaddy, Arakan, and the Political Geography of Burma

Burma around 1500 was divided among several kingdoms. Ava, in the dry zone of Upper Burma, claimed the legacy of Pagan and drew its strength from irrigated rice lands and a Burmese-speaking service population. Its fifteenth-century history was marked by conflict with Hanthawaddy and by recurrent pressure from Shan polities to the north and east. Kings such as Minkhaung II (1481 to 1502) maintained the court, but the kingdom's cohesion was fragile, and the Shan overthrow of Ava in 1527 (Chapter 84 discusses the consequences) showed the limits of its strength.

Hanthawaddy, with its capital at Pegu on the delta, was a Mon-centered kingdom that had been a major commercial and religious center since the fourteenth century (Chapter 60). Under Dhammaceti, who reigned from about 1471 to 1492, Hanthawaddy was prosperous and politically stable, and its ports exported rice, lac, and other goods while receiving merchants from India and elsewhere. Its religious reforms, recorded in the Kalyani inscriptions, are examined in Section 67.15.

Arakan, along the coast of the Bay of Bengal, was a distinct political and cultural world. The court at Mrauk-U, established in the 1430s, was linked to the Bengal Sultanate, which had supported its founder's restoration, and Arakanese rulers adopted aspects of Bengal's court culture while maintaining Buddhist kingship. The Arakan-Bengal frontier around Chittagong was contested in the later fifteenth century. Arakan's position gave it access to Bay of Bengal trade and to the Burmese interior across the Arakan Yoma.

At the margins of Ava, the upland town of Toungoo on the Sittang valley gained importance as a frontier settlement receiving refugees from Shan pressure. Toungoo's leaders asserted independence in the early sixteenth century (c. 1510), after the period of this chapter, and the state they founded would reshape Burma in Chapter 84. Its rise reminds students that peripheral regions in 1500 were the seedbeds of later consolidation.

Monastic institutions were a significant feature of Burmese political geography. Monasteries held land and laborers donated by rulers and elites, mediated disputes in some communities, and educated the sons of the literate and wealthy. A king who controlled monastic appointments and endowments gained influence over a major institution, while a king who lost the support of the sangha risked a loss of legitimacy. Rivalry among Burmese courts therefore included competition for prestigious monks and for recognition as defenders of religion. The Shan polities that pressed on Ava had their own princes and Buddhist institutions, and their relations with Ava ranged from alliance and intermarriage to open warfare, making them participants in Burmese politics rather than simply external raiders.

Section 67.8

Mainland Statecraft: Warfare, Tributary Relations, Administration, and Local Autonomy

Mainland courts shared several instruments of rule, but they combined them differently. Military mobilization usually depended on levies from the population registered under the court or its provincial lords, supplemented by retainers, mercenaries, and, increasingly, firearms by the early sixteenth century. Campaigns were seasonal, tied to the rains and the agricultural calendar, and a principal aim was often to capture population as well as territory, because labor was scarcer than land.

Revenue came from taxes in kind, labor service (corvée), trade duties, and royal monopolies, though the mix varied. Đại Việt's court relied on registers, provincial officials, and a growing scholar-official class. Ayutthaya's system tied service obligations to ranks. Burmese courts drew on hereditary service groups and grants of revenue to nobles, officials, and monasteries. Religious institutions were also economic institutions, holding land and labor donated by rulers and elites, and monastic legitimation helped bind court and society.

Tributary relations structured relations among courts. A ruler might send gifts to a stronger neighbor and might also demand them from weaker ones. The court's own chronicles often described these exchanges in terms flattering to itself. Historians therefore distinguish carefully among a claim of overlordship, a tribute relationship, an alliance sealed by marriage, military occupation, and direct administration. Ayutthaya's influence over certain Malay Peninsula ports, for example, was real but intermittent, and Lê Thánh Tông's provinces in former Cham territory were more directly administered than any Lao or highland tributary.

Local autonomy was therefore normal. Provincial governors, hereditary lords, monastic communities, and upland chiefs often retained substantial discretion. Rulers kept power by balancing these actors and by rewarding loyalty, and their power could dissolve when rivals offered better terms. For this reason the chapter does not imagine fifteenth-century mainland states as steadily centralizing. Some, like Đại Việt, moved toward more intensive administration, while others, like Ava, struggled to keep the allegiance of provincial and Shan lords.

Warfare also had a human dimension that inscriptions and chronicles often pass over. Campaigns drew men from fields, disrupted planting, and exposed civilians to requisition, flight, and capture. Resettlement of captured populations could enrich a victor's court and devastate the defeated region, and the movement of craftsmen, monks, and scholars as captives helped transfer skills and texts among courts. Villagers responded by fleeing to uplands or to rival jurisdictions when demands became severe. Rulers who wanted stable revenue therefore had to weigh the benefits of extraction against the risk of losing people, a calculation that shaped both law and policy in the mainland courts.

Section 67.9

Melaka, Northern Sumatra, and the Strait of Malacca around 1500

Melaka's institutional history belongs to Chapter 64. Here the task is to place it among its neighbors. By the later fifteenth century it was among the most prominent ports of the strait, a status that rested less on a large territory than on its harbor, its rulers' diplomacy, and the commercial services it offered. Its sultans claimed authority over Malay settlements along the peninsula and across the strait in Sumatra, relying on alliances with local chiefs and with the Orang Laut, seafaring communities who guarded sea lanes and provided naval strength.

Pires, writing after 1511, described Melaka's merchant organization in a way that probably reflects older practice: four shahbandars, port officials, each responsible for the merchants of a broad group of origins, including Gujarat, the Coromandel and Bengal coasts together with Pegu and Pasai, Java and the eastern islands, and China and the Ryukyus. This arrangement let a ruler regulate trade and collect duties while giving merchants familiar courts and languages. Pires's reconstruction should be read as retrospective, but the commercial pattern it describes is consistent with earlier evidence from Ma Huan and Chinese records.

Pasai, in northern Sumatra, remained a significant Muslim port and center of Islamic learning (Chapter 63), exporting pepper and other goods and maintaining ties with Bengal, Gujarat, and the Red Sea trade. It was not simply eclipsed by Melaka. Other ports, including Pedir on Sumatra's northern coast, Aru, Kampar, and Siak on the Sumatran side, and Pahang and Patani on the peninsula, supplied pepper, gold, tin, and forest goods and often sold directly to Gujarati and Javanese merchants. These ports competed and cooperated, and rulers shifted allegiances according to advantage.

Melaka's relationship to inland producers was indirect. It did not typically control mining or farming districts, but depended on local intermediaries who delivered tin, pepper, gold, and forest goods to the coast. This arrangement made its prosperity vulnerable to shifts in alliances and to the choices of merchants, who could take their goods elsewhere. It also meant that Melaka was one commercial center among several. The chapters that follow show how that structure shaped Portuguese strategy and Malay responses (Chapters 69 and 70).

Chinese and Ryukyuan connections added another dimension. Melaka's rulers cultivated ties with the Ming court, which provided recognition and a measure of diplomatic protection, while Ryukyuan ships carried Chinese goods and Southeast Asian products between the islands, Siam, and Melaka. Gujarati merchants, among the most prominent in later descriptions, supplied textiles that were essential to the exchange for spices and pepper. These connections meant that Melaka's prosperity depended on the continued flow of ships from several directions, and that its rulers had reasons to treat merchants with care. They also meant that a disruption of any one commercial partnership could be absorbed, to a degree, by the others.

Section 67.10

Java, Bali, Majapahit's Legacy, and the Rise of Coastal Muslim Centers

Java's political landscape around 1500 is among the hardest to reconstruct. Majapahit had been the dominant court of the fourteenth century (Chapters 50 to 52), but by the later fifteenth century it was divided among competing branches of the royal house. Javanese tradition gives 1478 as the year of its end, tied to a chronogram, but inscriptions of Girindrawardhana, including several dated 1486, attest continuing royal grants and claims in the Majapahit tradition. Some scholars connect the late court with Daha (Kediri), but the inscriptions do not securely establish the court’s location or the full sequence of rival rulers. Later Javanese and Portuguese-period accounts describe inland rulers who retained prestige, and Pires, around 1512 to 1515, still described a non-Muslim Javanese court in the interior alongside Muslim ports on the coast. No single secure date can therefore mark the end of Majapahit.

On the north coast, ports such as Tuban, Gresik, Surabaya, and Demak were centers of trade and Islam. Ma Huan had already described Muslim and Chinese communities at some of these ports in 1433, and by 1500 their rulers exercised substantial local autonomy, controlling shipping, grain exports, and access to the eastern spice trade. Demak's emergence as a Muslim court is associated by later tradition with a ruler called Raden Patah, but the date of its foundation and the nature of its early relationship to Majapahit are debated. Demak's growth belongs more to the early sixteenth century than to the decades before 1500, and it would be a mistake to project its later power backward.

Islam in Java spread unevenly. Coastal merchant communities, rulers seeking commercial and diplomatic advantage, and religious teachers associated by tradition with the "nine saints" (wali) all contributed. Many Javanese rulers and villagers combined Islamic practice with earlier Hindu-Buddhist and local ritual traditions, and conversion did not simply erase the older cultural world (Chapter 65). West Java's kingdom of Sunda, with its center at Pakuan and pepper port at Sunda Kelapa, remained outside this northern coastal Muslim network in 1500.

Bali followed a distinct path. It retained a Hindu-Buddhist religious tradition and a close political and cultural relationship with Java, and Balinese courts and communities maintained commercial and cultural links with Javanese ports, although the precise commodities, scale, and social relations of exchange around 1500 are much less securely documented than in later centuries. Later Balinese tradition holds that Javanese elites and religious specialists moved to Bali as Majapahit declined, strengthening its court culture, but this tradition, preserved in later texts, needs cautious handling. What is clear is that Bali did not follow Java's religious trajectory and became a major repository of Javanese-derived literary and ritual practice.

The question of Majapahit's final decades is complicated by the character of the evidence. Inscriptions are scarce for the late period, and much of what is known comes from later Javanese chronicles written in Islamic Java and from Portuguese-era reports that transmit information already filtered through political change. Scholars have therefore proposed differing reconstructions, some stressing a violent overthrow of the inland court by a rival branch or by Muslim coastal forces, others a gradual dispersal of authority among several centers. The state of the evidence supports caution. What can be said securely is that Hindu-Buddhist kingship in inland Java persisted into the early sixteenth century and that coastal Muslim ports were growing in importance alongside it.

Section 67.11

Maluku, Banda, and the Trading Networks of the Eastern Archipelago

The eastern archipelago supplied goods that travelled farthest. Cloves came from a small group of islands in northern Maluku, including Ternate, Tidore, Moti, Makian, and Bacan. Nutmeg and mace came principally from the Banda Islands to the south. Both commodities entered a trading system that carried them through Javanese and Malay ports to Melaka, India, the Middle East, and China, long before any Portuguese ship reached Maluku (Chapter 71).

Political organization in the two areas differed. In northern Maluku, polities headed by rulers, called kolano in local usage and later sultans, centered on Ternate and Tidore, with neighboring Bacan and Jailolo. Traditional accounts speak of four principal kingdoms, and the rivalry between Ternate and Tidore shaped regional politics. Islam reached these courts in the late fifteenth century, probably through Javanese and Malay merchants and teachers, though the dating of conversion rests on later chronicles. Ternate’s ruler around 1500 is traditionally identified as Zainal Abidin, followed by Bayanullah, but the succession and conversion dates are reconstructed chiefly from later chronicles and early-sixteenth-century European reports. They do not document uniform adoption of Islam among the inhabitants of the clove-producing islands by 1500.

Banda was different. Pires and later writers describe a group of small islands governed not by a king but by communities of leading men, the orang kaya, who negotiated with merchants and with one another. John Villiers's study of Banda emphasizes this oligarchic structure and the dependence of the islands on imported rice, cloth, and other goods, obtained in exchange for nutmeg and mace. Banda's merchants and village leaders had considerable bargaining power, but the islands' dependence on imported food made them vulnerable to disruption, a point of enormous later consequence (Chapter 78).

Javanese and Malay merchants, many based at Gresik, Tuban, and Melaka, typically carried spices westward. Indian cloth, especially from Gujarat and the Coromandel coast, was a principal import, as were Chinese goods, and local communities used these imports to build status and cement alliances. Producers were thus participants in a sophisticated exchange and not isolated suppliers, though their dependence on a few trading partners shaped their vulnerability.

The spice trade also illustrates how commodities shaped local societies. Clove cultivation shaped village life on the producing islands, where families tended trees, harvested during particular seasons, and exchanged cloves for imported rice, cloth, and metal goods. The need for imports tied these communities to merchants, and rulers on Ternate and Tidore sought to regulate trade and to control access to visiting ships. In Banda, harvests of nutmeg and mace involved large numbers of workers, and the leading families mediated between producers and traders. In each case, the same global demand produced different local arrangements, which is why this chapter avoids the generic label of spice kingdoms.

Section 67.12

Northern Borneo, Sulu, Mindanao, and the Other Maritime Communities

The northern maritime world, introduced in Chapter 66, provides a contrast with both the mainland kingdoms and the major spice ports. Brunei, on Borneo's northern coast, grew in influence in the later fifteenth century under rulers such as Bolkiah. Its trade linked Borneo's forest products, camphor and others, to the South China Sea, Melaka, and the Philippines, and Pires later described Brunei merchants trading with Luzon. Brunei's authority was exercised through coastal alliances and maritime dependencies and not through fixed territorial borders.

Sulu's court took shape in the later fifteenth century, with later tradition crediting a Muslim religious leader from the Malay world with its establishment. Sulu's wealth came from pearls, sea products, and trade with Brunei and China, and its political organization combined kinship networks, maritime mobility, and Islamic institutions. Mindanao's communities were diverse, including coastal Muslim groups and upland societies, and Muslim polities at Maguindanao took shape later, in the early sixteenth century.

Further north, Luzon's coastal communities, including those around Tondo and Manila Bay, traded with Chinese, Bruneian, and other merchants, and Chinese sources record contacts with Luzon. The Visayas and other regions contained chiefdom-based communities known as barangay (Chapter 72). These were not "weaker" versions of the mainland kingdoms. They were differently organized societies whose leadership rested on kinship, ability to attract followers, and control of trade and seafaring.

Across the archipelago, sea peoples such as the Orang Laut and the Sama-Bajau moved between coasts, serving as navigators, boatmen, fishers, and sometimes as naval forces for coastal rulers. Their mobility is a reminder that maritime authority did not follow lines on a map. It followed networks of people, harbors, and obligation, and a ruler could claim a coast and control little of the interior behind it.

Chinese records provide a valuable external check on these northern societies. Ming sources record missions from Brunei and other polities, as well as contacts with Luzon, and these records, together with archaeological finds of Chinese ceramics in the Philippines and Borneo, show regular long-distance contact. At the same time, the sources reflect how Chinese officials classified foreign rulers and cannot show internal arrangements. For Sulu and Mindanao especially, later tradition and later colonial-era records dominate, so historians treat early details with caution. What the evidence clearly supports is a maritime world in which authority rested on kinship, commerce, and mobility, linking northern Borneo, the Sulu zone, and the Philippines to wider networks.

Division IV — Commerce, Society, Religion, and the Foundations of Regional Power
Section 67.13

The Commercial Geography of Southeast Asia around 1500

Trade moved through a hierarchy of places. Production centers, such as the pepper districts of Sumatra and the clove islands of Maluku, supplied goods. Collecting ports, such as Pedir and Pasai in northern Sumatra and coastal markets linked to the older Banten Girang polity in western Java, gathered commodities from producers. The later independent Muslim sultanate of Banten and its subsequent commercial preeminence must not be projected unchanged backward onto 1500. Intermediary entrepôts, above all Melaka, concentrated cargoes and offered credit, storage, and market information. International markets in India, the Middle East, and China consumed the goods and supplied textiles, ceramics, and other commodities in return. The same port could serve several functions.

Rice moved in the opposite direction from many luxury goods. Java, Pegu, Siam, and parts of the mainland coast exported rice to ports that could not feed themselves, and Melaka depended on such imports. Pepper, grown on Sumatra and increasingly in other regions, was a major commodity, while tin from the Malay Peninsula and gold from Sumatra and the peninsula entered regional trade. Forest products, including aromatic woods, resins, camphor, and hornbill casques, came through upland and coastal intermediaries. Chinese ceramics and Indian textiles arrived in return, with cloth from Gujarat, the Coromandel Coast, and Bengal in particular serving as a common currency of exchange.

Merchants of many origins made this system work. Gujarati, Tamil, Bengali, Persian, Arab, Chinese, Ryukyuan, Javanese, Malay, Cham, and Siamese traders all appear in sources, and many settled in ports for long periods. Credit, partnerships, and kinship ties underlay commercial activity, as did legal arrangements recognized by local rulers, who protected merchants in return for duties and gifts. None of this required a single commercial administration.

The scale of trade should not be exaggerated. Reid’s retrospective demographic reconstruction proposes roughly 23 million inhabitants across Southeast Asia around 1600, not 1500. This is an estimate inferred from uneven later records, not a regionwide census; it nonetheless reinforces how strongly population and production were concentrated outside the celebrated international ports. Trade nonetheless shaped state revenue, elite consumption, and the lives of those who worked in ports and shipping, and it connected producers across the region to distant markets.

Currency and credit also shaped commerce. Chinese copper coins circulated in many ports, and local coinage in gold and tin appeared in some, while textiles, pepper, and other goods functioned as stores of value and media of exchange. Merchants extended credit to producers and to one another, and agents resident in distant ports carried cargoes and market information. Rulers taxed trade through duties and gifts, and some sought to attract merchants with reputations for fair treatment. Because prices responded to the arrival of fleets and to political disturbances, commerce was both profitable and risky, and the commercial geography of 1500 included an unseen layer of trust, kinship, and reputation on which transactions depended.

Section 67.14

Merchants, Laborers, Women, Households, and Port-City Society

Port cities were socially diverse. A major port might house resident merchants from several regions in separate quarters, local elites and officials, artisans, boatbuilders, porters, fishermen, and a large floating population of sailors and visiting traders. Ma Huan and Pires both emphasized the variety of languages and peoples, and both depended on interpreters and local brokers. Many port residents were not rulers' subjects in a simple sense, but members of communities with their own leaders and customs who accepted a ruler's protection.

Labor in ports and hinterlands relied on several systems: free wage and piece work, labor obligations owed to rulers, debt bondage, and enslavement. Debt bondage, in which a person worked for a creditor in exchange for the debt, was widespread, and enslaved people, taken in warfare, purchased, or born into servitude, worked as domestic servants, laborers, and sailors. The status of such people varied across societies and was often less rigidly hereditary than in later plantation systems, though conditions could be severe. Slavery and debt bondage are examined comparatively in Chapter 91.

Women held important economic roles. Reid and others have emphasized that women in many Southeast Asian societies were prominent in markets, textile production, and trade, and that they often managed household finances and negotiated with foreign merchants. Foreign merchants frequently formed temporary or lasting marriages with local women, which gave them local connections and kin networks. Evidence for women comes largely from outsiders' accounts and legal and literary texts, and it should be treated with care, but it consistently suggests more economic autonomy than in several neighboring societies.

The prosperity of ports rested on rural labor. Farmers supplied rice, villagers supplied boats and goods, and upland communities supplied forest products. Rulers extracted revenue through duties, labor service, and monopolies, which could burden producers. Urban prosperity was thus uneven, and its benefits accrued disproportionately to merchants, rulers, and officials.

Port society also generated law and custom. Rulers recognized the right of merchant communities to settle disputes among themselves according to their own norms, and the authority of port officials helped to resolve conflicts between communities. Codes associated with Melaka and other ports, preserved in later manuscripts, address shipboard authority, commercial disputes, and debt, though their dating is uncertain and their use as evidence for 1500 requires care. Religious life added further variety, with mosques, temples, and shrines serving different communities. Ports were not harmonious, and fires, riots, and rivalries are recorded, but they also functioned as places where cooperation across cultural lines was routine.

These commercial relationships carried risks that cannot be reconstructed from the perspective of rulers alone. A shipowner needed reliable crews, interpreters, boat repairers, and creditors; a market woman needed predictable access to customers and supplies; rural households could be exposed to price changes without ever meeting a foreign trader. A season of poor harvests could increase a port’s demand for rice and intensify the leverage of suppliers. Conversely, coercive taxation or warfare could redirect merchants to a rival harbor. The social history of commerce therefore involves bargaining power at several scales, not simply the total volume of cargo handled at Melaka or another famous port.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households and Work in a Fifteenth-Century Port

A household in a port might include a trading family, kin and clients, enslaved or indebted workers, and occasionally a foreign guest or partner. Women might sell cloth and food in the market, manage storage and credit, and negotiate with visiting merchants, while men worked in shipping, trade, or royal service. Rice was the staple, supplemented by fish, vegetables, and imported goods in wealthier households, and housing was typically of wood and thatch raised on posts, with fires a constant danger in crowded ports.

These details come from travelers' accounts, legal texts, and archaeology, which describe elites and foreigners far better than ordinary residents. Historians must therefore infer ordinary life cautiously, and they should not treat the court or the merchant quarter as representative of the whole society.

Section 67.15

Religious Plurality, Royal Patronage, and Institutional Authority

Religious life around 1500 was plural. On the mainland, Theravada Buddhism was the dominant tradition of courts in Burma, Ayutthaya, Lan Na, Lan Xang, and the Khmer kingdom (Chapter 53), organized through monastic communities, the sangha, whose members depended on lay donations and whose institutions held land and labor. Alongside it, Brahmanical court rituals, spirit cults, and local practices continued. Court institutions do not tell the whole story, however: village religion often combined Buddhist, spirit, and local elements that texts describe poorly.

In Đại Việt, the Lê court promoted Neo-Confucian education and examinations while Buddhism, Daoism, and popular cults persisted among the population. Cham religious life included Hindu, Muslim, and local traditions. In maritime Southeast Asia, Islam was established at port courts and among merchant communities in Sumatra, the Malay Peninsula, north-coast Java, Brunei, and parts of the east, administered through rulers, scholars, and teachers, and spreading unevenly into hinterlands. Java and Bali retained substantial Hindu-Buddhist institutions and ritual. Labeling whole regions by a single faith on the basis of court practice would therefore be misleading.

Royal patronage connected religion and authority. Rulers gave land and labor to monasteries and mosques, sponsored construction, and presented themselves as upholders of orthodoxy. The Hanthawaddy king Dhammaceti offers a clear example. A former monk, he reorganized the monastic order, sending monks to Sri Lanka in the 1470s to receive ordination in lineages regarded as pure and establishing a consecrated ordination hall (sima) near Pegu. The Kalyani inscriptions, named for the river in Sri Lanka associated with the ordination site, recorded the reform in Pali and Mon.

The reform had political as well as religious meaning. By setting a single ordination standard for his kingdom, Dhammaceti strengthened the king's role as guarantor of the sangha, linked Pegu to a prestigious Sri Lankan Buddhist center, and projected his legitimacy beyond the delta. The reform differs from Islamization: it was a top-down institutional reorganization within an existing Buddhist society, while Islam's spread in the archipelago proceeded through commerce, scholarship, courts, and marriage.

Lived religion extended beyond courts and monasteries. Villagers on the mainland maintained shrines to local spirits, sought the protection of guardian figures, and made merit through gifts to monks, while in maritime ports Muslim traders, teachers, and Sufi-influenced communities shaped devotional life in ways that varied by locality. In Java and Bali, temple ritual, ancestor veneration, and court ceremonies sustained earlier traditions, and in the Philippines and eastern Indonesia, local religious specialists continued to mediate with spirits and ancestors. The evidence for such practices is uneven and filtered through elite writers or outside observers, which is a reason to avoid sweeping statements about the beliefs of whole populations.

Section 67.16

Agriculture, Population, Environment, and the Material Foundations of States

Behind every court and port stood farmers. Wet-rice cultivation, in the Red River and Irrawaddy deltas, the Chao Phraya plain, the Tonle Sap region, and the volcanic plains of Java, supplied the principal food and the main base of labor and taxation. Irrigation works, from small village dams to larger canals, depended on community labor, sometimes organized by rulers and sometimes by local groups. Other farmers practiced swidden cultivation in the uplands, growing rice, millet, and tubers while gathering forest goods.

Population was low relative to land. Scarcity of labor shaped warfare, which sought captives, and rulers competed to attract settlers with land and tax relief. It also made rulers dependent on the cooperation of village headmen and provincial lords. Settlement patterns clustered along rivers and coasts, leaving large forested uplands in which communities could remain beyond close court control.

Rivers carried bulk goods and troops, and seasonal floods set the rhythms of planting and campaigning. Forest and sea products added to livelihoods. The environment was also hazardous: volcanic eruptions in Java and elsewhere, floods and droughts, and endemic diseases such as malaria affected settlement and mortality (Chapter 5). Environmental conditions constrained possibilities but did not mechanically determine political outcomes.

Commercial success therefore rested on agricultural and labor foundations. Melaka imported rice, Banda imported food, and Javanese ports exported grain, so food supply shaped political strategy. Rulers who could secure labor, grain, and boats had advantages over those who relied on trade alone. For this reason, the chapter treats prosperity around 1500 as the product of ports, plains, forests, and the people who worked in each.

Agricultural change was also under way. Rulers encouraged the clearing of new land and the settlement of captives, and in some regions the extension of cultivation created frontiers where court authority was thin. In the Chao Phraya region, the management of floodwater and the extension of rice land supported population growth, while the Red River delta, where dikes and water control had a long history, supported dense settlement. Sources for such changes are limited, and estimates of population and production for 1500 are highly uncertain, so historians rely on regional patterns and later figures with caution. What remains clear is that political power, in every region, ultimately depended on the capacity to feed people and mobilize labor.

Grain production was also a political problem of storage and movement. A court could claim extensive territory yet struggle to provision troops across forests, hills, or floodplains, especially when campaigning coincided with planting or harvest. River craft, portage labor, granaries, and obligations negotiated with local headmen mattered as much as royal orders. In an island entrepôt, reliable food imports could be a diplomatic asset, while interruption of a rice route could make a prosperous port vulnerable. This comparison helps explain why control over people and transport was often more consequential than drawing a boundary around land on a map.

Division V — Regional Comparison, Historical Interpretation, and the Opening of the Early Modern Era
Section 67.17

The Regional Balance of Power around 1500

Comparing the principal states shows why no one controlled the region. Đại Việt had the most intensive administration and a strong military but faced upland and southern frontiers and was oriented toward the Red River delta. Ayutthaya had commercial reach and agricultural resources but contended with Lan Na and Khmer neighbors and could not impose lasting control on the Malay Peninsula. Hanthawaddy had wealth and religious prestige but sat beside the competing courts of Ava and Arakan. Melaka had a commanding harbor, diplomacy, and relationships of military and subordinate authority, but its reach was uneven and its capital depended heavily on imported food and merchant goodwill.

Military capability varied by type. Land-based armies relied on levies, elephants, and by the early sixteenth century increasingly on firearms, while maritime powers relied on ships, crews drawn from seafaring communities, and control of anchorages. Neither type could easily conquer the other's domain. A mainland court could march against a river port, and a naval power could blockade a coast, but sustained occupation across the boundary of land and sea was difficult and rare.

Strategic geography reinforced this balance. Mountains, forests, and seasonal rains limited campaigns, while monsoon timing limited naval operations. Rulers consequently preferred alliances, marriages, and tribute to outright conquest, except where a sharp advantage presented itself, as in Đại Việt's campaign against Vijaya. The resulting balance was unstable, with rivalries that could shift quickly, but it did not give any single power the means to dominate.

This pattern must be distinguished from commercial integration. Trade linked the courts, but political competition and religious difference continued. No kingdom of 1500 was positioned to unify the region, and no institution did so. Understanding that fragmentation is essential to understanding how outside powers, when they arrived, could find allies, rivals, and openings in many places at once.

The balance of power also depended on diplomacy with outside powers. Ming China provided recognition to several rulers, including those of Melaka, Champa, Siam, and Java, and Chinese attitudes affected a ruler's standing in disputes with neighbors. Sri Lanka, Bengal, Gujarat, and the Ryukyu Kingdom all played roles in the diplomatic and religious networks of Southeast Asian courts. These external relationships offered rulers resources, but rarely control: no outside court commanded the routine administration of the region as a whole, and rulers often exploited rivalries among outsiders as they did among local neighbors. The region's political system was therefore open to the wider world without being subordinate to it.

Power also changed with scale. At the level of an imperial title, a ruler could claim precedence over many ports or tributaries; at the level of one river mouth, authority might depend on a headman, merchant consortium, or local naval leader whose cooperation was conditional. A court’s effective power could expand during a successful campaign and recede after a succession dispute, while commercial relationships survived both changes. Comparing the region therefore requires at least three measures: the reach of a ruler’s claims, the institutions available to enforce them, and the willingness of communities to continue cooperating. None can be inferred from a dynastic map alone.

Section 67.18

Was Southeast Asia Entering a New Historical Era? Historiographical Debates

Historians have long asked whether the decades around 1500 mark a turning point. The Andayas present 1400 to 1511 as a distinct phase in which commercial connections and shifting authority transformed the region, followed by accelerated change after 1511. Reid argues that the period from about 1450 to 1680 constitutes an age of commerce, with growing trade, urbanization, and monetization. Lieberman identifies parallel mainland trends of territorial consolidation, administrative intensification, and religious integration across the fifteenth to eighteenth centuries, a pattern he places within a global comparison.

Each periodization highlights real developments. Trade expanded, large courts consolidated, Islam and Theravada Buddhism deepened their institutional presence, and firearms spread. But each also has limits. Reid's commercial emphasis can understate agricultural and mainland dynamics, and Lieberman's pattern fits the mainland better than the archipelago. The 1511 boundary treats a Portuguese conquest as a hinge, though changes before and after it often owed more to local dynamics. Critics also point out that "early modern" is a European label whose usefulness elsewhere is contested.

The evidence therefore supports a nuanced view: continuous long-term transformation, with local turning points, such as 1471 for Cham history or the 1470s for Hanthawaddy's reforms, that do not align with a single date. European arrival intensified some processes and disrupted others, but did not initiate long-distance commerce or political competition.

The debate also has practical implications for how chapters are organized. If the decades around 1500 are treated as a break, the narrative will tend to emphasize arrivals and conquests, and to look backward to 1500 as an origin. If they are treated as part of a longer process, the narrative can follow local chronologies, such as the Vietnamese southward movement or Burmese reconsolidation, across the supposed boundary. This textbook uses 1500 as a convenient point for survey while insisting that readers follow each thread back into the fifteenth century and forward into the sixteenth, as the chapters of Volume V do.

These approaches can be tested against different categories of evidence rather than accepted as competing slogans. Reid’s emphasis on commerce is especially illuminating for Melaka, north-coastal Java, and the spice islands, but a court such as Đại Việt also derived capacity from examinations, tax administration, and agrarian settlement. Lieberman’s argument about mainland integration directs attention to the durability of courts and religious institutions, but fits less neatly with mobile seafaring populations and commercially linked islands that did not form one territorial state. The Andayas’ 1511 turning point captures the dramatic consequences of the seizure of Melaka, yet that conquest neither ended Asian trade nor immediately transformed every mainland kingdom. Historians should ask which historical processes each periodization makes visible and which it pushes to the margins.

Section 67.19

European Expansion on the Horizon: What Changed, and What Had Not Yet Changed?

In 1498 Vasco da Gama's fleet reached Calicut on India's Malabar coast, having rounded Africa with the help of pilots and information from East African and Indian Ocean sources. The voyage opened a direct maritime route between Portugal and the Indian Ocean. In the years that followed, Portuguese fleets, armed with ship-mounted artillery and supported by crown investment, began to contest trade in the Arabian Sea and along the Indian coast, and in 1509 a Portuguese expedition reached Melaka. These events are the subject of Chapters 68 and 69 and are noted here only as a boundary.

What the Portuguese entered was an established system. Merchants from Gujarat, the Coromandel Coast, Bengal, the Red Sea, and China already moved goods through Southeast Asian ports. Rulers maintained treaties, tributary ties, and merchant regulations. Local shipbuilders, pilots, and intermediaries supplied the knowledge on which any newcomer would depend. The Portuguese did not create long-distance Asian commerce, and for some time they were participants, rivals, and allies within it.

What had not yet changed in 1500 is as important as what soon would. Southeast Asian courts remained the principal political authorities, agricultural production remained overwhelmingly local, and the great majority of the region's people had no contact with Europeans. Portuguese artillery and ships would matter in particular places, but the political fragmentation described in this chapter set the context in which they operated, and the consequences, from conquest at Melaka to the later development of Spanish and Dutch power, unfolded over generations.

The Portuguese presence also raises the question of how Southeast Asian rulers would perceive newcomers. Rulers accustomed to hosting merchants of many origins were prepared to negotiate with foreigners, to grant them quarters and privileges, and to seek their goods and expertise, including firearms and shipbuilding. They were also alert to behavior they judged hostile. Reports reaching Southeast Asian courts from India in the early 1500s gave them some prior knowledge of the Portuguese. This chapter leaves the detailed course of those responses to the following chapters but stresses that Southeast Asian rulers entered them with experience, information, and options.

The political choices open to Asian rulers before Portuguese arrival were substantial. They could encourage one merchant network over another, offer protected markets and anchorage, tax overseas cargo, request diplomatic recognition, or use commercial revenues to negotiate with neighboring courts. None of these decisions guaranteed security: attracting trade might increase dependence on imported food, while coercing merchants could encourage them to sail elsewhere. For that reason, the arrival of armed Atlantic ships should be understood as an additional strategic pressure on existing institutions rather than an event that instantly erased them. The detailed military and diplomatic consequences require the separate narratives in Chapters 68 and 69.

Section 67.20

Southeast Asia around 1500: A Regional Historical Synthesis

The chapter's portrait is of politically diverse but deeply interconnected societies. Mainland courts such as Đại Việt, Ayutthaya, Lan Na, Lan Xang, Hanthawaddy, Ava, and Arakan competed through warfare, tribute, marriage, and religious patronage, each shaped by its own river valley and frontier. Maritime port-states and island polities, from Melaka and Pasai to the Javanese coastal ports, the clove-producing courts of northern Maluku, the Banda communities, Brunei, and Sulu, engaged in a commerce that moved goods across the region and beyond.

Beneath these courts and ports lay farmers, boatmen, forest gatherers, laborers, and women traders whose work sustained revenue and consumption, and whose experience of political change was often one of continuity. Religious institutions, whether Theravada monasteries, Islamic courts and schools, Confucian-influenced administration, or Hindu-Buddhist temples and ritual, tied rulers to communities and to wider networks. Local agency was visible throughout: Dhammaceti's reform, Lê Thánh Tông's administrative program, Melaka's merchant governance, and the Banda notables' bargaining all show Southeast Asians shaping their own institutions.

These systems existed before European maritime expansion and continued to shape its terms. The Portuguese appeared in a region where no power could command all others, where merchants and rulers had long practiced diplomacy across cultural lines, and where agricultural and commercial bases gave many societies the means to resist, adapt, or ally. The chapters that follow examine how those capabilities met a new kind of maritime power.

The synthesis can be tested against the evidence of this chapter. Each of the central claims rests on sources of differing quality: the political fragmentation of the mainland on inscriptions and chronicles, commercial structures on Chinese and Portuguese accounts and archaeology, religious change on royal inscriptions and later tradition, and social life on scattered observations. Where the evidence is thin, the synthesis is cautious, and where the evidence converges, as with the multiethnic character of ports and the dependence of ports on agriculture, it is firmer. Students should treat the synthesis not as a final judgment but as the best-supported present reading of a region whose history continues to be revised.

An integrated comparison also exposes several different meanings of historical strength. For Đại Việt, documented administrative and military mobilization mattered; for Ayutthaya and Hanthawaddy, control of productive lowlands combined with court diplomacy and religious patronage; for Melaka, port governance, negotiated alliances, and maritime connections were vital; and for Banda, independent notables could exercise bargaining power through a highly valued commodity without maintaining an expansive royal bureaucracy. These contrasts are not a ranking from primitive to advanced. They demonstrate that political organization was adapted to distinct resources, ecologies, and social relationships. The region’s coherence came from exchange and recurring interaction, not institutional sameness.

Historical Synthesis
Five findings about Southeast Asia around 1500

1. Political diversity without a hegemon. Many courts and port-states coexisted, and none controlled the region.

2. Commercial integration without political unity. Trade linked societies without producing one state, administration, or faith.

3. Agricultural foundations. Ports, courts, and armies depended on rice, labor, and rural production.

4. Religious plurality. Theravada, Islamic, Confucian-influenced, and Hindu-Buddhist institutions coexisted, with beliefs richer than court institutions suggest.

5. Local agency. Southeast Asian rulers, merchants, monks, and communities actively shaped the conditions of the encounter with Europeans.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 67 has surveyed Southeast Asia around 1500 as an integrated but diverse historical field. The date is a vantage point, not a threshold: the evidence, drawn from Chinese records, Southeast Asian inscriptions and chronicles, archaeology, and early Portuguese accounts, spans roughly 1450 to 1520, and each source illuminates a different moment.

Geography shaped the region's political systems without determining them. Mainland river valleys supported courts whose authority radiated across farmland and frontiers, while monsoon seas, straits, and archipelagic passages favored port-states. Rivers, portages, and coastal routes joined the two zones, and many mainland states traded by sea while Java combined a large agrarian base with active ports.

On the mainland, Đại Việt under Lê Thánh Tông consolidated administration and conquered Vijaya in 1471, though Cham polities and communities persisted. Ayutthaya, Lan Na, Lan Xang, and the Khmer kingdom competed through tribute and warfare. In Burma, Ava, Hanthawaddy, and Arakan occupied different positions, and Toungoo's later rise began at the margins. Claims of supremacy differed from administrative control, and local autonomy was normal.

In the maritime world, Melaka was highly influential but not the sole gateway, and Pasai and other ports remained significant. Java's politics fragmented after Majapahit's peak, with no single date for its end, while coastal Muslim ports grew. Ternate, Tidore, and Banda supplied cloves, nutmeg, and mace through differently organized polities. Brunei, Sulu, Mindanao, and Philippine communities illustrate maritime authority based on networks and not on fixed borders.

Commerce moved rice, pepper, spices, tin, gold, forest products, ceramics, and textiles through production centers, collecting ports, intermediaries, and international markets. Port societies were multiethnic, relied on free, obligated, indebted, and enslaved labor, and gave women prominent economic roles. Prosperity rested on agricultural and rural labor and carried costs for producers.

Religious institutions varied. Theravada Buddhism structured mainland courts, Islam spread through ports and courts, the Lê state promoted Neo-Confucian administration, and Java and Bali preserved Hindu-Buddhist traditions. Dhammaceti's reform, recorded in the Kalyani inscriptions, shows a king using links with Sri Lanka to assert legitimacy, a process distinct from Islamization.

Comparison shows a balance of power with no hegemon. Land and sea powers each had strengths that could not easily overcome the other's domain, and commerce did not create political unity. Historiographical debates over an "early modern" era, as in Andaya and Andaya, Reid, and Lieberman, each capture real trends but should be applied with regional and chronological care.

The Portuguese appear at the edge of the chapter, following da Gama's arrival at Calicut in 1498 and the arrival at Melaka in 1509. They entered an established Asian commercial and political order, and their later impact was shaped by it. The conclusion is that Southeast Asia around 1500 consisted of politically diverse, deeply interconnected societies whose institutions and capabilities conditioned the terms of European encounter.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Entrepôt
A port whose prosperity derived chiefly from concentrating and redistributing goods rather than from producing them, as at Melaka.
Collecting port
A port that gathered products from inland or island producers for sale to larger entrepôts and foreign merchants.
Tributary relations
Relationships of acknowledged seniority marked by envoys, gifts, or titles, which did not necessarily imply administrative control.
Mandala
A modern scholarly model of political authority radiating from a center and weakening toward overlapping peripheries.
Composite polity
A realm combining communities and territories with varying degrees of integration under a ruler.
Age of Commerce
Anthony Reid's label for c. 1450 to 1680, when trade and urban life expanded; a modern periodization.
Shahbandar
A port official, known especially at Melaka, who managed merchants of a particular origin and regulated trade.
Orang kaya
Malay term for "rich" or leading men; in Banda, the village notables who governed and negotiated with merchants.
Orang Laut
Seafaring communities of the Strait of Malacca and Riau region who served as navigators, fishers, and naval support to coastal rulers.
Monsoon
Seasonal wind reversal that governed the timing of sea voyages across the Indian Ocean and South China Sea.
Pepper
A spice grown in Sumatra and elsewhere, one of the region's major export commodities.
Cloves
Spice from a small group of northern Maluku islands including Ternate, Tidore, Moti, Makian, and Bacan.
Mace and nutmeg
Spices from the tree native to the Banda Islands, the principal source of both before the sixteenth century.
Sangha
The Buddhist monastic community, an institution holding land, labor, and religious authority.
Ordination lineage
The chain of valid ordinations by which Buddhist monks claim the authenticity of their order.
Sima
A consecrated boundary or ordination hall within which valid monastic ceremonies are performed.
Kalyani inscriptions
Pali and Mon stone inscriptions of the later 1470s at Pegu recording Dhammaceti's monastic reform.
Neo-Confucianism
A philosophical and political tradition emphasizing moral cultivation and bureaucratic service, promoted by the Lê court through examinations.
Hồng Đức
The reign period of Lê Thánh Tông, associated with a legal code and administrative reorganization.
Swidden
A system of upland cultivation in which plots are cleared, planted, and later left fallow.
Wet-rice cultivation
Irrigated or flood-fed rice farming that supported dense lowland populations.
Corvée
Labor service owed to a ruler or lord as a form of taxation.
Debt bondage
A condition in which a person works for a creditor to repay a debt, widespread across the region.
Sakdi na
A Thai system of ranking by notional allotments of controlled labor, whose fifteenth-century origins are debated.
Periodization
The division of the past into named eras, which are analytical tools and not natural facts.
Retrospective source
Evidence composed after the events it describes, such as many chronicles and Pires's account for 1500.
Historiography
The history of historical interpretation, including debates over how to periodize the era.
Kolano
Local northern Maluku term for a ruler, later replaced in many cases by the title sultan.
Barangay
Kin-based community, headed by a leader, characteristic of many Philippine coastal societies.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it fixes a baseline. Every later account of European, colonial, and national history in Southeast Asia depends on an assumption about what the region was like before sustained intervention, and Chapter 67 insists that it was a world of capable states, commercial networks, and religious institutions, not a blank space.

It also reveals larger processes that continued after 1500: the interplay of agriculture and trade, the competition among courts without a dominant power, the negotiation of religious authority, and the dependence of political power on labor and food supply. Many of these shaped Portuguese, Spanish, Dutch, and later colonial strategies, as the following chapters show.

Continuities extend far beyond the chapter. The diversity of religious traditions, the importance of ports and rivers, and the long-term interplay between lowland states and upland communities remain visible in later centuries. At the same time, the transitions begun in the fifteenth century, from Vietnamese southward expansion to Burmese reconsolidation, would shape the early modern states of Volume V.

Finally, the chapter contributes to the textbook's central argument that Southeast Asia has never been merely a crossroads. Its societies selected, adapted, resisted, and transformed what reached them, and understanding that agency at the opening of Volume V prepares students to read the encounters ahead as interactions among active historical actors.
