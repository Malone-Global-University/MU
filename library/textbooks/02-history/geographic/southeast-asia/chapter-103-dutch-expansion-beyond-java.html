Chapter Opening

A line on a colonial map was a claim, not a conquest. Between 1816 and 1900 the Netherlands turned scattered coastal posts, commercial contracts, and unequal treaties into assertions of sovereignty over much of the archipelago, but each assertion had to be tested against rulers, religious reformers, mining federations, river communities, and armies that had their own purposes.

In 1830, when the Java War ended and the Cultivation System was being introduced (Chapters 101 and 102), the Dutch colonial state was a Java-centered enterprise. Beyond Java it held a patchwork of coastal establishments inherited from the Dutch East India Company (VOC), a number of treaties of uncertain force, and a set of claims on maps. By 1900 the Netherlands Indies government presented the Indonesian archipelago, from northern Sumatra to western New Guinea, as a single colonial territory. Examined closely, that presentation conceals as much as it reveals. This chapter asks how a claim became a state, where it failed to do so, and who made the difference.

The narrative begins with a vocabulary problem. Colonial officials spoke of the "Outer Islands" (Buitengewesten), a term that describes the archipelago from the viewpoint of Batavia and Java's revenues. It tells us little about how the peoples of Sumatra, Borneo, Sulawesi, Bali, or the eastern islands understood their own political worlds. Palembang's sultans, Minangkabau religious scholars, Chinese mining federations, Banjarese princes, Bugis and Makassarese kingdoms, Balinese courts, and Sasak village leaders were not peripheral to their own histories. The chapter therefore treats them as decision-makers, whose alliances, refusals, and wars shaped the pace and form of Dutch expansion.

A second task is to separate four conditions that colonial documents often blur. Nominal sovereignty was a legal assertion, often resting on a treaty. Treaty-based influence allowed the Dutch to shape a ruler's foreign relations or trade without governing his subjects. Military occupation placed troops and forts in a territory, which was not the same as administering it. Effective civil administration meant collecting taxes, appointing officials, enforcing law, and being obeyed in villages. In nineteenth-century Indonesia these conditions arrived at different times, sometimes decades apart, and sometimes never.

The chapter follows the geography of expansion. Division I explains the motives, diplomacy, and instruments of expansion, including the Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824. Division II examines Sumatra: Palembang, the Padri War, the coffee economy of West Sumatra, Jambi, and the plantation coast of Deli. Division III turns to Borneo, where Malay sultanates, Chinese gold-mining federations, and Dayak communities met Dutch residents and British rivals. Division IV covers South Sulawesi, Minahasa, Bali, and Lombok. Division V reaches the Lesser Sunda Islands, the Moluccas, and western New Guinea, and closes with a comparative synthesis.

Historians approach these events through an uneven body of evidence. Dutch treaties, military reports, and administrative memoranda survive in abundance and are indispensable, yet they record the colonizer's categories. Indigenous chronicles, genealogies, letters, and oral traditions preserve different understandings of rank, loyalty, and obligation, but they survive unevenly and were often written or copied later. Missionary reports, merchants' accounts, and British records supply further perspectives. Modern scholarship by historians of Jambi, Minangkabau society, western Borneo, Minahasa, and Bali has shown how much the colonial archive can distort when read alone.

The period also falls into two phases that historians commonly distinguish. Before about 1870 Dutch policy was cautious, commercial, and selective, aiming to secure trade and prevent rivals from settling. After the 1870s, and especially in the 1890s, the colonial government and army intervened more systematically, driven by a combination of economic investment, strategic anxiety, and military ambition. The shift was gradual rather than abrupt. Chapter 104 will treat the Aceh War, which began in 1873, in full; here it appears only as part of the wider context.

The chapter's governing argument is that Dutch territorial expansion was not the automatic spread of colonial government across an empty political landscape. It was a contested process in which independent rulers, indigenous communities, trading networks, and military resistance determined how far European claims could become effective authority. The colonial state that existed in 1900 was an uneven achievement, and some of its largest campaigns still lay ahead.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the political geography of the archipelago outside Java after the Napoleonic Wars and why the term "Outer Islands" reflects a colonial viewpoint.
  2. Distinguish nominal sovereignty, treaty-based influence, military occupation, and effective civil administration.
  3. Analyze the Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824 and its consequences, including its limits as a settlement made without Indonesian rulers.
  4. Identify the economic, strategic, and political motives for expansion and how they varied by region and period.
  5. Describe the instruments of expansion: treaties, recognition of rulers, expeditions, forts, naval patrols, residents, concessions, and annexation.
  6. Trace Dutch intervention in Palembang, the Padri War, Jambi, and Deli, and explain the role of local alliances and resistance.
  7. Evaluate competing interpretations of the Chinese mining federations of western Borneo and the wars fought against them.
  8. Explain how interference in dynastic succession contributed to the Banjarmasin War and the abolition of the sultanate.
  9. Compare the negotiated integration of Minahasa with the military campaigns in Bali, Lombok, and South Sulawesi.

  11. Interpret treaties as primary sources, identifying the contracting parties, their differing expectations, and the evidentiary limits of each document.
  12. Evaluate historians' debates over the causes of Dutch imperial expansion and the gap between claimed and effective authority around 1900.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the Netherlands extend its claims beyond Java after 1816, and why did the pace and character of expansion change after about 1870?
  2. What is the difference between a sovereignty claimed in a treaty and authority exercised in a village, and why did the gap often last for decades?
  3. How did indigenous rulers use Dutch power, and how did the Dutch use indigenous rivalries, in particular regions?
  4. Why did religious, commercial, and dynastic conflicts within Indonesian societies so often become occasions for Dutch intervention?

  6. Why did Dutch intervention in a succession dispute sometimes destroy the political authority it claimed to protect?

Historical Context

The VOC, dissolved in 1799, had never governed the archipelago as a territorial state. Its power rested on fortified ports, monopoly contracts for spices and other commodities, alliances with rulers, and naval force, concentrated in Java's northeast coast, Ambon and Banda, Makassar, Malacca, and a handful of western Sumatran and other posts (Chapters 77 to 79). Beyond these footholds, many rulers dealt with the Company through treaties that obliged them to deliver goods or exclude competitors, which gave the VOC leverage over trade rather than authority over people. This inheritance mattered in 1816. Dutch officials often treated older treaties as proof of continuing rights, while indigenous rulers might regard them as lapsed, personal to a previous generation, or limited to commerce.

After the Napoleonic Wars, as Chapter 100 explains, the British returned Java and the other possessions to a restored Dutch state. The Netherlands then had to rebuild a government with limited finances and military means. Java's revenues, and from 1830 the Cultivation System, had priority. The colonial government at Batavia was therefore reluctant in the 1820s and 1830s to commit large forces to distant conquests, except where local crises threatened trade or where a rival power might fill a vacuum.

The political landscape outside Java was highly diverse. Sumatra contained sultanates such as Palembang, Jambi, Siak, and Aceh, the Minangkabau highlands with their dispersed village republics and royal court at Pagaruyung, and Batak and other highland societies with their own institutions. Borneo's coasts and river mouths held Malay sultanates, while its interior was inhabited by Dayak and other communities who were not simply subjects of the coasts. South Sulawesi had the great Bugis and Makassarese kingdoms; Bali had rival courts; Lombok combined Balinese rulers with a Sasak majority. Eastern Indonesia mixed Muslim sultanates, Christian communities, small chiefdoms, and zones where no state authority reached the villages at all.

Trade networks tied these societies to China, India, the Malay world, and Europe. Chinese migrants worked in mines and ports, Bugis and Makassarese ships carried goods across the seas, and Arab, Indian, British, American, and Dutch merchants all operated in the region. The British presence in Penang and Singapore (treated in Chapter 105) offered alternative markets and sometimes political support to rulers who were pressed by the Dutch. In this setting, Dutch expansion competed with other forms of political and commercial power.

Finally, historians stress that the term "Indonesia" was not yet a political entity. The colonial territory later called the Netherlands Indies was assembled by treaty and force over the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Its final shape was fixed by agreements among European powers, such as those with Britain, Portugal, and Germany, as much as by anything indigenous societies had experienced as a single polity.

Division I — Colonial Expansion and the Geography of Sovereignty

Central question: Why did Dutch colonial authorities pursue expansion beyond Java, and how did they attempt to turn political claims into territorial control?

Section 103.1

The Outer Islands after the VOC: A Fragmented Political Geography

In 1816 the Dutch regained a set of possessions whose extent was far smaller than their later colonial maps suggest. On Sumatra they held Padang and a few other posts on the west coast, though some British-held posts, including Bencoolen, remained until 1825. In Borneo they had little more than a presence on the coasts. In Sulawesi they held the fort at Makassar and an old treaty framework. In the Moluccas they held Ambon, Banda, and the Ternate area, where the VOC's spice policies had once concentrated European power. Elsewhere, from Bali to New Guinea, the Dutch had limited or no practical presence.

Colonial administrators later grouped all this territory under the name Buitengewesten, or Outer Islands, in contrast to Java and Madura. The expression is revealing. It makes Java the center and defines everything else by distance from it. Yet from the standpoint of Palembang, Banjarmasin, or Gowa, Java was the distant island, and from the Moluccas the Pacific and Philippine seas were equally close. Historians therefore use the term in quotation or with explanation, as a colonial category and not a geographic or political reality.

The political units outside Java varied in kind. Sultanates such as Palembang, Jambi, Siak, Pontianak, Banjarmasin, and Ternate had courts, titles, and claims to tribute that extended along rivers and coasts. Others, such as the Minangkabau villages, relied on councils of elders and customary law. Highland and forest peoples often owed only loose or occasional allegiance to any coastal ruler. Even where a sultan claimed a river basin, his effective power over inland communities usually depended on negotiation, trade, and kinship, a pattern compatible with the mandala model introduced in Chapter 1 but not identical to it.

Colonial maps flattened this complexity. A line around "Dutch Borneo" or "the Residency of Palembang" implied a continuous space under a single authority. A better reading distinguishes three things: the territory the Dutch claimed, the territory where they had forts and officials, and the territory where villagers actually obeyed Dutch orders. Throughout this chapter, students should read each claimed boundary as an assertion to be tested by evidence of how authority operated on the ground.

One practical way to test a colonial claim is to ask who could enforce a decision away from the port. A Dutch resident might correspond with a sultan, receive tribute, and record a district in an annual report. But if the resident could neither appoint a village chief nor protect a trader traveling upriver, administrative sovereignty remained limited. A Bornean river basin could contain several overlapping circles of allegiance: downstream Malay courts, Chinese mining societies, Dayak communities with their own longhouses and ritual authorities, and mobile merchants. None necessarily recognized the territorial boundaries assumed by European cartographers. The same analytical caution applies to Minangkabau villages, Balinese kingdoms with interspersed dependencies, and the courts of the Moluccas.

Restoration did not even return all former Dutch stations at once. Transfers from Britain occurred under the arrangements of 1814 and 1816, while the later exchange of Melaka and Bencoolen was governed by the 1824 treaty and subsequent handovers. Personnel, records, finances, and local political agreements did not move together. Colonial officials could invoke an eighteenth-century VOC contract whose signatories were dead and whose economic purpose no longer existed. Local rulers might remember an alliance as an exchange of protection and trading privileges, rather than an irrevocable transfer of sovereign authority. The meaning of continuity was therefore disputed on both sides.

The islands also differed in their population distributions and routes of communication. In Java, much colonial administration could extend from linked agrarian districts. On Sumatra, Borneo, and western New Guinea, rivers and coasts were often more consequential than straight-line distances. Monsoon seas limited voyages, rapids obstructed steamships, and mountain passes were controlled by communities not represented at coastal courts. An imperial map should consequently be read alongside a map of navigable waterways, seasonal routes, settlements, and garrisons. Only the second begins to show what officials could actually do. These distinctions explain why later Dutch reports celebrated the possession of territories that remained effectively self-governing.

Section 103.2

Treaties, Trade, and Imperial Rivalry after 1824

British and Dutch interests collided across the Strait of Malacca. Britain's founding of Singapore in 1819 had angered Dutch officials, who saw it as a breach of their commercial position. After lengthy negotiation, the two governments concluded the Anglo-Dutch Treaty of London in 1824. The agreement exchanged territories (Britain gave up Bencoolen, the Netherlands withdrew from Malacca and its Indian establishments) and divided the region into two spheres of influence: the Malay Peninsula and Singapore on the British side, Sumatra and the islands south of the Singapore Strait on the Dutch.

The treaty gave the Dutch the diplomatic assurance that Britain would not set up rival posts in much of the archipelago. It also committed both powers to trade provisions and to cooperation against piracy, which became a justification for naval operations. For the Dutch, this was an opportunity: a European recognition of their sphere in much of the archipelago, even though they were far from controlling it. For Britain, it secured Singapore and Malaya while retaining commercial access to Dutch territories on specified terms.

The agreement's limits are as important as its content. No Sumatran sultan, Bornean ruler, or Balinese king was a party to it. It created European obligations toward each other and did not transfer rights over indigenous societies. Dutch officials could, and did, use it to argue that British intervention in their sphere was illegitimate. They could not claim from it a right to govern Palembang or Pontianak. Where Britain's earlier commitments, such as those concerning Aceh's independence, constrained the Dutch, it took the Sumatra Treaty of 1871 to loosen them, a development that belongs to Chapter 104.

The treaty's sphere also shaped Britain's role elsewhere. In northern Borneo, British interests, notably James Brooke in Sarawak and later the North Borneo Company, remained outside the Dutch sphere, a pattern examined in 103.12. A further consequence was commercial. Singapore became the market on which many Indonesian ports depended, which made Dutch efforts to regulate trade in their own ports a continuing source of conflict with merchants and rulers who preferred the free port.

The treaty's articles reveal a more intricate settlement than the simplified expression “division of the archipelago.” Article VIII concerned Dutch establishments on the Indian mainland. Article IX transferred Britain's possessions on Sumatra and prohibited new British settlements or treaties there. Article X ceded Melaka to Britain and restricted future Dutch settlements and treaties on the Malay Peninsula. Article XII removed Dutch objections to British Singapore, while excluding new British settlements on specified islands south of the Singapore Strait. Article XIII set March 1, 1825, as the date for delivering the ceded establishments. Those dates should not be confused with ratification: the treaty was signed in March 1824 and ratifications were exchanged in June 1824.

Commercial provisions complicate the familiar image of an exclusive partition. Articles I–IV protected defined trading rights for each power's subjects in the other's possessions and in ports belonging to indigenous rulers. The treaty's authors were therefore trying both to limit political rivalry and to maintain access for commerce. Article VII preserved a temporary exception for Dutch spice-monopoly interests in the Moluccas. Students should distinguish the legal transfer of a European fort from a hypothetical transfer of an entire indigenous kingdom: the former was a concrete transaction between contracting parties; the latter was not granted by a treaty whose rulers and subjects had not consented.

Consider a Sumatran ruler maintaining commercial links to Singapore after 1824. The treaty did not prevent traders from sailing there; indeed commercial articles anticipated international exchange. Yet officials in Batavia could invoke the exclusion of new British political treaties to warn that the ruler had no acceptable alternative protector. This asymmetry gave Dutch diplomacy leverage without creating an effective local administration. Nor did the agreement settle all later disputes. British expansion in northern Borneo, the varying status of Aceh, and the continuing significance of independent shipping networks demonstrate that European “spheres” were practical arguments repeatedly renegotiated, not timeless boundaries.

Section 103.3

Commerce, Resources, and the Strategic Motives for Expansion

Why expand? The motives changed over time and varied by region. Early in the century, pepper in Sumatra, tin on Bangka and Belitung, gold in western Borneo, spices in the Moluccas, and forest products across the islands were attractive commodities. Control of ports and rivers offered customs revenue and a way to restrict British and other competitors. Naval and military posts protected shipping lanes that connected Java to the Straits, China, and the eastern islands.

Coal added a strategic dimension. Steamships, which became increasingly important from the mid-century, needed fuel, and the coal seams of southeastern Borneo became a reason to secure the Banjarmasin region (103.11). After 1870 the Liberal reforms, which opened the colonies to private investors, strengthened the pressure. Tobacco planters in Deli, oil prospectors, and mining and timber concessionaires sought legal security and labor. Investors petitioned for protection, and officials in Batavia, aware of the capital at stake, were more willing to endorse expeditions that could secure it.

Protecting merchants and investments could easily become a justification for intervention, but historians caution against assuming that economics explains everything. Military officers sought careers and prestige, and the colonial government feared that a vacuum of authority would invite a rival power, whether Britain, Germany, or later the United States, to claim territory. Missionaries and some Dutch officials argued that expansion would end slavery, piracy, and what they called misrule, though their claims coexisted with extractive policies and could mask other aims.

Motives also differed among regions. West Sumatra's coffee mattered for revenue, western Borneo's mines for the trade in gold and for Chinese migration, Banjarmasin for coal, Bali for shipping and prestige, and the eastern islands more for exclusion of competitors than for production. This variation warns students against a single explanation of "Dutch imperialism" and prepares the historiographical discussion in 103.20.

Tin provides an early example of how commodity geography shaped intervention. Bangka's deposits attracted commercial rivalry and made the island strategically important to Palembang and to the Dutch. A colonial government concerned with tin could exert pressure at river mouths, warehouses, and ports rather than immediately occupy every producing district. Pepper and forest products presented different problems: merchants often moved between several outlets, and producers could redirect sales when a monopolist's terms became disadvantageous. Colonial policymakers therefore repeatedly faced the question of whether controlling a harbor was enough or whether authority had to be extended into the hinterland to make commercial regulation effective.

The development of steam navigation changed the calculation but did not abolish its obstacles. Steamers could move troops and officials with less dependence on seasonal winds, while coal deposits and coaling stations became strategic resources. Rivers still presented shifting channels, sandbanks, and rapids; a steamer's reach was not the same as a resident's jurisdiction. In the later century, plantation concessionaires and mineral prospectors sought not simply security against attack but enforceable contracts, access to labor, and official assistance in settling land disputes. A private investment might thus generate new colonial commitments that exceeded its initial commercial value.

Expansion also required an allocation of scarce state resources. Before the profits of compulsory cultivation and the growth of colonial revenues, Batavia struggled to finance distant expeditions. After the Java War, maintaining garrisons and replacing casualties still imposed heavy costs. Later officials calculated whether administrative expenses could be offset by customs, monopolies, or concession fees. These calculations could be optimistic: a mining area that looked profitable on paper might need years of campaigning, road construction, and subsidized troops. Economic motive should not therefore be reduced to immediate profit, just as strategic motive should not be treated as wholly separate from commercial protection. Officials, business interests, and local allies often presented overlapping justifications for interventions whose costs and consequences proved difficult to control.

Section 103.4

The Instruments of Colonial Expansion: Diplomacy, War, and Indirect Rule

The colonial government used a limited set of tools in many combinations. Treaties came first: a ruler might acknowledge Dutch suzerainty, promise to exclude other Europeans, grant trade privileges, or accept a Dutch resident at his court. Recognition was a second tool. By confirming or installing a ruler, the Dutch could shape a succession, though the ruler's legitimacy among his own people might then depend on his foreign backer, which cost him authority. Military expeditions, usually combining the KNIL, naval vessels, and auxiliary troops from allied rulers, were a third tool; forts and garrisons a fourth.

Another major tool was indirect rule: leaving indigenous rulers and institutions in place while supervising them through residents and controllers. It was cheap, and it spared the colonial state from learning every local custom. It also made Dutch authority dependent on the cooperation of local elites. Where those elites were weak, hostile, or divided, the Dutch might replace them, annex the territory, or establish direct administration. The history of expansion is partly the history of such transitions.

A formal treaty relationship is not the same as colonial administration. In a typical treaty relationship, the Dutch had the right to appoint a resident and regulate foreign affairs, while the ruler collected taxes and administered justice. In direct administration, Dutch officials did these things. Several regions in this chapter moved from the first to the second over decades, and some, such as parts of Bali, stayed in an intermediate state for long periods.

The participants in any intervention did not share a single aim. A Dutch commander might seek a decisive victory; a resident in the field might prefer negotiation; a Batavia official might want to contain costs; merchants might want a protected trade; an indigenous rival of the reigning sultan might want help against him; and village leaders might want to avoid the conflict altogether. Because each pursued his own objectives, interventions often developed in ways none of them intended.

A political contract could regulate foreign relations, customs, succession, and jurisdiction in quite different combinations. The Dutch later used short declarations of submission in some territories, but earlier agreements might preserve far more visible autonomy. Translators working between Malay, Dutch, and local legal idioms were crucial. A word meaning protection or acknowledgment of seniority could be read by a European administrator as a legal subordination. Disputes over those meanings were especially acute when a newly appointed resident sought powers that an earlier ruler had never exercised or granted. In each case, the date of signature is only the beginning of the history of implementation.

Naval coercion often preceded the visible act of conquest. A cruiser could intercept shipping, guard an estuary, or blockade a port where local rulers depended on customs revenue. Expeditionary forces frequently recruited auxiliaries from rival kingdoms. Such allies did not simply take orders: they brought their own grievances, objectives, and expectations of territorial reward. An intervention described in Dutch records as a restoration of order might therefore be remembered locally as one faction's victory in a succession struggle. Later official narratives often flattened these multiple contests into a single war between the colonial state and an indigenous opponent.

Occupation demanded another layer of decisions. A garrison needed food, transport labor, interpreters, local guides, and dependable communication with the coast. If those could only be secured through coercion, “pacification” might generate new resistance. Dutch officials sometimes preserved dynastic titles and rituals to save administrative costs, while transferring judicial or financial control to a resident. Other campaigns abolished royal office altogether, but the people who had served a sultan might continue to recognize his relatives or religious authority. Such continuities help explain recurrent unrest after formal annexations. A useful historical test is to ask whether an intervention changed who could collect taxes, adjudicate disputes, mobilize labor, and prevent a rival ruler from exercising those powers. Without such evidence, terms like annexation and protectorate remain largely diplomatic labels.

Historical Vocabulary
Sovereignty and Indirect Rule

Definition. In European international law, sovereignty meant supreme authority over a territory and its people. Indirect rule describes a system in which a colonial power governs through existing indigenous rulers and institutions under supervision, instead of replacing them with its own officials.

Historical context. Dutch treaties often described rulers as acknowledging Dutch sovereignty or suzerainty. Indigenous rulers might have understood the same words as a ritual acknowledgment, a commercial alliance, or protection against rivals. European governments, for their part, used the language of sovereignty to exclude one another, so treaties could assert authority that local circumstances did not support.

Why it matters. The distinction explains why a colonial map and a village's experience could diverge so widely. It also shows why "conquest" in this period was frequently a process of converting treaty relationships into direct administration.

Potential misunderstanding. Indirect rule did not mean gentle rule. It could be coercive, extractive, and violent, and it often strengthened some indigenous elites at the expense of others.

Why the Dutch Expanded beyond Java

Long-term causes

VOC-era treaties and posts; the commercial importance of pepper, spices, tin, and forest goods; the strategic value of the straits; and Java's revenues, which funded armies and ships.

Short-term causes

The 1824 treaty's division of spheres; local succession disputes and rivalries; shipwreck, piracy, and trade grievances; requests by rulers for help; and investors' demands for security after 1870.

Immediate triggers

Specific incidents, such as an attack on a mine or a disputed succession, which officials and officers treated as grounds to act.

Treaties, forts, abolition or replacement of rulers, and the posting of residents, with warfare and displacement in many regions.

Long-term consequences. A territorial colonial state across the archipelago, uneven in its authority and dependent on military power and indigenous intermediaries. Historical complexity. No single motive explains expansion; its causes differed by region and period.

Division II — Dutch Expansion in Sumatra

Central question: How did the Dutch establish authority over Sumatran kingdoms and communities, and why did the process involve both local alliances and prolonged resistance?

Section 103.5

Palembang and the Struggle for Control of Southern Sumatra

The Sultanate of Palembang, on the Musi River in southeastern Sumatra, was one of the wealthiest polities of the island. Its sultans controlled pepper and tin exports and the river routes that linked the highlands to the sea, and they were heirs to a long tradition of Malay courts in the region (Chapters 28 and 29). The VOC had held a post there, but in the early nineteenth century Palembang's rulers were politically independent in most respects and engaged in trade with Britain and others.

Sultan Mahmud Badaruddin II, who ruled several times between 1803 and 1821, resisted the restored Dutch government's attempts to impose supervision and monopolies. The Dutch sent expeditions in 1819 and again in 1821. The first was repulsed; the second, a larger force, took the capital and the sultan was removed and exiled to Ternate in the Moluccas, where he died in 1852. Members of the royal family were installed in his place for a short time, but they proved unreliable allies. An agreement of 18 August 1823 transferred the remaining sultan’s governing authority to Dutch officials while preserving ceremonial privileges; the last vestiges of titular sultanate authority were removed in 1825. The Dutch thereafter strengthened residency administration.

The capture of the capital illustrated a recurrent feature of expansion. Controlling a river port gave the Dutch leverage over trade, but the interior beyond the Musi was another matter. Highland communities, headmen, and the old networks of tribute and kinship continued to operate, and the colonial government for decades relied on a mix of local officials, taxation experiments, and punitive patrols. The establishment of a Resident did not instantly produce a functioning administration, and some inland resistance and banditry continued through the century.

Mahmud Badaruddin II was not simply a casualty of colonial power. His decisions, including his trade arrangements, his military preparations, and his diplomacy with the British, represent a deliberate effort to preserve Palembang's autonomy. In later Indonesian memory he is honored as a national hero, a retrospective evaluation that students should distinguish from the ways his contemporaries in Palembang and Java viewed him.

Control of Bangka and the river approaches was closely entangled with the contest over Palembang. The sultanate's court occupied a fortified position near the meeting of waterways, and its political reach depended on negotiated ties with upstream communities. Mahmud Badaruddin II's resistance drew on these geographical advantages. Dutch expeditionary forces learned that ships carrying artillery were formidable along navigable channels but could still encounter defensive barriers, riverbank positions, and unfamiliar local supply networks. The failure of the 1819 expedition reflected more than a temporary military reverse: it showed that removing a ruler required a major commitment of force and local intelligence.

The larger expedition of 1821 secured the court and removed Mahmud Badaruddin II. The subsequent political transition was not a single act in 1825. Dutch commissioner Jan Isaac van Sevenhoven concluded an agreement with Sultan Ahmad Najamuddin III on August 18, 1823. In the documented arrangement, the sultan transferred governing authority while retaining specified titles, insignia, ceremonial honors, and financial support. This distinction between the transfer of effective governing power and the survival of titular sovereignty is important. The final removal of the remaining sultanate arrangement followed in 1825. Historians who simply state that the dynasty was abolished in 1821 or 1825 conceal the intermediate steps through which Dutch institutions displaced court authority.

Dutch occupation affected more than elite titles. Revenue collection and customs duties shaped the lives of river merchants and households whose produce moved toward the capital. Officials tried to replace negotiated payments to a ruler with standardized colonial claims; local intermediaries could resist or exploit that change. Highland communities retained their own leaders and often viewed a resident stationed in the city as a distant figure. The case therefore demonstrates both the political uses of a defeated dynasty and the limitations of coastal power. The commemorative prominence later given to Mahmud Badaruddin II is also historically meaningful: it reflects an Indonesian assessment of anti-colonial resistance that should be examined alongside contemporary debates among Palembang's nobles and traders about which alliances could preserve their livelihoods.

Section 103.6

The Padri War: Islam, Adat, and Dutch Intervention, 1821–1837

The Minangkabau highlands of central Sumatra were an agricultural and commercial zone with a matrilineal social order, a network of village-states governed by councils of elders, and a royal court at Pagaruyung whose authority was largely symbolic. Highland farmers produced rice, and from the late eighteenth century increasingly coffee, gold, and other goods that moved to ports on the west and east coasts. Christine Dobbin's work shows how this commerce, together with changing patterns of landholding and village politics, formed the setting for religious reform.

In the early nineteenth century a movement of Islamic reformers, known as the Padri, sought to enforce stricter observance, including prayer, dress, and prohibitions on gambling, cockfighting, tobacco, and alcohol. Several returning pilgrims from Mecca were among its leaders, though local scholars and village figures were equally important. The reformers came into conflict with the defenders of established adat, many of them nobles and leading village figures, who saw the new rules as an attack on custom and their own status. The conflict was initially a civil war among Minangkabau, not a war against Europeans.

The Dutch became involved in 1821, when adat leaders, including members of the Pagaruyung royal family, appealed for help and signed an agreement with the colonial authorities at Padang. The Dutch hoped to extend their position inland and to benefit from the highlands' trade, and they sent troops. The war was interrupted after 1825, when the Java War (Chapter 101) absorbed Dutch resources, and resumed in earnest after 1830. By the 1830s many adat leaders had grown wary of Dutch aims, and some Padri and adat leaders reached a degree of rapprochement. The war thus changed from a conflict within Minangkabau society to a broader struggle against Dutch expansion.

Tuanku Imam Bonjol was the most prominent Padri leader in the later phase. His fortified base at Bonjol held out against repeated Dutch assaults until it fell in 1837. He surrendered or was captured later that year, exiled to western Java, then to Ambon and finally to Minahasa, where he died in 1864. Dutch forces and their auxiliaries also fought other Padri strongholds, and their victory required both force and the exploitation of divisions among Minangkabau leaders. In 1833 the Dutch had publicly pledged, in a declaration known as the Plakat Panjang, not to rule the highlands directly, a promise that was soon compromised in practice.

For historians, the war shows how religious reform, local economic change, and colonial ambition could converge. The Padri movement was not simply a precursor of nationalism, as later accounts sometimes portray it, nor merely a fanatical sect, as Dutch accounts often described it. It was a reform movement with social and economic roots whose conflicts with adat provided the Dutch an opening.

The struggle arose within a society whose customary institutions were neither uniform nor static. Minangkabau inheritance practices located important rights over ancestral property with women's matrilineal kin groups, while political discussion involved lineage elders, religious scholars, and heads of local settlements. Reformers criticized gambling, drinking, and other practices, but also challenged arrangements of authority and trade. Padri leaders differed among themselves; some promoted stricter observance through persuasion, while others used armed coercion against communities that resisted. The labels “Padri” and “adat” should not imply two permanently fixed armies, because people and villages shifted positions as local conflicts developed.

Changes in highland commerce were integral to the conflict. Gold had long connected communities with ports on both coasts; expanding coffee cultivation created new demands for land and routes to markets. Trade profits could finance religious schools, armed retainers, and the purchase of weapons. Returning pilgrims contributed ideas circulating through the wider Islamic world, but local circumstances determined their reception. As a result, neither a purely theological explanation nor a narrowly commercial explanation suffices. Village political competition and disagreements about the proper place of religious law intersected with changing incentives for agriculture and commerce.

Dutch intervention was initially presented as assistance to adat leaders. The colonial government gained footholds through those alliances, and European officers at first treated the Padri as the principal enemy. The prolonged fighting changed this arrangement. As Dutch administration expanded, some former allies worried that military assistance would be converted into permanent external authority. The declaration commonly called the Plakat Panjang, associated with Van den Bosch in the early 1830s, promised respect for Minangkabau institutions and limits on coercion; scholarly treatments differ in dating and interpreting the sequence of proclamations, so students should use an identified text rather than a single unexamined date. The renewed attack on Bonjol and its fall in 1837 were military turning points, not the immediate creation of unanimous acceptance.

Imam Bonjol's later reputation illustrates the difficulties of historical memory. Anti-colonial commemoration emphasizes resistance to the Dutch, while contemporary Padri campaigns also involved coercion against other Minangkabau and neighboring populations. A rigorous account can acknowledge both without accepting either Dutch descriptions of religious fanaticism or later heroic narratives as complete records. The source problem is particularly serious because the surviving reports from commanders emphasized military objectives, while regional histories preserve distinct memories of village violence, diplomacy, and religious reform.

Historical Vocabulary
Adat and Suzerainty

Definition. Adat is a term widely used in the Malay-Indonesian world for customary law, tradition, and the social order they uphold. Suzerainty is a relationship in which a dominant ruler or state claims overlordship over a subordinate one that retains internal autonomy.

Historical context. In Minangkabau, adat governed inheritance, village authority, and land. Dutch officials, who later made the study of adat a branch of colonial law, often treated it as a fixed legal code, while local actors used it flexibly and argued over its meaning. Suzerainty was a common treaty formula for Dutch overlordship of sultans.

Why it matters. Both terms show how colonial vocabulary reshaped indigenous institutions and how such institutions could be mobilized by different parties.

Potential misunderstanding. Adat was not unchanging "tradition," and in the Padri War the Padri themselves appealed to Islamic norms they considered the true custom.

Section 103.7

West Sumatra after the Padri War: Political Control and Commercial Agriculture

After 1837 the Dutch consolidated their position around Padang and in the highlands, establishing military posts, building roads and bridges, and creating an administrative structure of residents, assistant residents, and controllers who supervised local headmen. Existing village leaders were retained and made salaried officials in many areas, so the colonial state grew by absorbing the existing hierarchy. The Dutch also asserted rights over the old royal court at Pagaruyung, whose symbolic authority they used and then largely dismantled.

Coffee became the central instrument of economic control. From the 1840s the government required highland villages to deliver coffee at fixed prices to government warehouses. The system resembled the Javanese Cultivation System (Chapter 102) in its use of compulsory deliveries and local headmen, but it differed in scale and in organization: Minangkabau farmers kept their own landholdings, delivered a crop they already grew, and were paid, though at low rates. The detailed fiscal analysis of compulsory cultivation belongs to Chapter 102; here the point is that the Dutch tied political control to agricultural extraction in West Sumatra as in Java.

Dutch control remained dependent on local cooperation and on transport. Roads and, later, a railway reduced the cost of moving coffee to the coast and troops to the highlands, but district headmen remained essential intermediaries, and their loyalty had to be bought with salaries, honors, and tolerance of local custom. Resistance did not disappear: tax revolts and local uprisings occurred in later decades, and older networks of religious teachers and traders persisted. The Minangkabau highlands also became a major source of Islamic scholarship and of later reform movements, a continuity with the pre-colonial era that Chapter 125 takes up.

A colonial officer's map of West Sumatra might show a road between an inland post and Padang; for villagers, that road could mean new commerce, easier travel, military surveillance, and demands for transport labor simultaneously. The government needed paths broad enough to carry coffee, troops, and administrative communications. Road construction and protection were therefore instruments of economic and political integration. At the same time, mountain geography gave settlements options to evade or negotiate with lowland officials. Control of one route could redirect trade toward another rather than extinguish it.

The compulsory coffee system did not reproduce a single Javanese template. Coffees in the Minangkabau highlands were grown on plots shaped by household and village claims, and local producers could possess knowledge of the crop predating closer Dutch supervision. Government purchase prices and mandatory deliveries nonetheless deprived growers of the ability to negotiate freely with merchants. Official collection points linked production to customs and revenue and made it easier for administrators to judge district heads by apparent output. This gave both officials and village elites incentives to conceal, shift, or inflate figures, a source-critical difficulty when using colonial statistical reports.

Religious institutions also continued to shape political authority. Teachers and students linked settlements by routes of pilgrimage and scholarship; some opposed armed resistance to the Dutch while maintaining a critical distance from colonial policy. Others participated in local protests against new levies. The surviving distinction between religious and customary authority cannot be treated as an absolute separation: Minangkabau political life increasingly incorporated negotiated accommodations between Islam and adat. For social historians, the decades after Bonjol are therefore not simply an interval between wars. They reveal an uneven remaking of taxes, transport, property disputes, schooling, and local leadership as the colonial state attempted to turn military gains into predictable administration.

By the later nineteenth century, West Sumatra was both more commercially connected and more tightly supervised. Its agricultural products moved in larger volumes through regulated channels, yet customary institutions remained crucial to everyday decisions. This combination of administrative penetration and continued local initiative illustrates an important outcome of expansion: incorporation rarely erased earlier institutions; instead, it altered who could call upon them and for what ends.

Section 103.8

Jambi, Deli, and the Uneven Expansion of Colonial Power in Eastern Sumatra

Jambi and Deli show two contrasting patterns. The Sultanate of Jambi, on the Batang Hari River, controlled pepper and other trade, and its ruling house was divided among rival lines and ministers. The Dutch concluded agreements with Jambi in 1833 and 1834 that recognized the sultan's position in return for Dutch rights to trade and political supervision. Elsbeth Locher-Scholten's study shows that the two parties read these agreements differently: for the Dutch they created a protectorate; for the sultan and his court they were treaties between a ruler and a friendly power.

Relations deteriorated over decades. After a series of disputes over trade, border regions, and Jambi's relations with its neighbors, the Dutch in 1858 deposed Sultan Taha Saifuddin and installed a rival. Taha withdrew into the interior, where he retained the loyalty of many inland communities and led resistance for decades until his death in 1904. Dutch forces occupied the capital, but they could not govern the upriver districts. Jambi was incorporated into the Residency of Palembang in 1903; Sultan Taha died resisting colonial forces in 1904; a separate Jambi Residency was organized in 1906. These dates describe different stages of incorporation, resistance, and administrative reorganization.

Deli, on the east coast of Sumatra, took a different course. Its sultan and neighboring rulers, nominally under the Sultanate of Siak, which the Dutch pressured to accept a treaty in 1858, welcomed European planters. From the 1860s, Dutch and other European planters began growing tobacco on land granted by local rulers on long leases. Dutch companies expanded quickly, and the area became one of the most productive plantation regions in the world. The rulers drew income from concession fees, and the Dutch government acquired a stake in protecting the planters, resulting in the establishment of a Residency in 1873.

The plantation economy depended on labor migration. Planters brought workers first from China and the Straits, then increasingly from Java, and relied on labor contracts enforced by penal sanctions that exposed workers to severe punishment for leaving their employers. The best-known regulation, the coolie ordinance of 1880, made desertion a criminal offense. Workers' experiences included harsh conditions, debt, violence, and high mortality in some periods. The subject returns in Chapters 112 and 122. For this chapter, Deli shows that colonial authority could grow from commercial concessions and the demands of investors, with indigenous rulers participating willingly and, later, losing autonomy.

The Batang Hari River gave Jambi's rulers a strategic advantage because its lower reaches could be supervised from coastal posts while important settlements and routes lay further upstream. Dutch officers hoped to secure the river's mouth, control customs, and limit competing foreign influence. Sultan Muhammad Fakhruddin accepted agreements in the 1830s under military pressure. Regional documentary histories identify an 1833 political agreement and a later agreement commonly dated December 1834, with confirmation reported in 1835; these sources do not always agree on the precise signing and approval dates. The later terms assigned important customs powers and a salt monopoly to Dutch authorities while professing respect for the sultan's internal administration. This was not a simple surrender of all domestic authority, and it should be read as a contested contract rather than as a modern constitution.

Sultan Taha Safiuddin's resistance exposes the weakness of formal colonial claims. Following his displacement in 1858 he moved inland, retained political and economic relationships, and complicated efforts to control the river's trade. The Dutch could install another sultan in the capital without eliminating the claims of a ruler whose authority survived upstream. The decisive changes came much later: Jambi was incorporated into the colonial administrative structure under Palembang in 1903, Taha was killed in 1904, and a separate Jambi residency was organized in 1906. Those are different events, not three dates for a single annexation. Locher-Scholten's work makes this prolonged conflict central to understanding Dutch state formation beyond Java.

Deli's path was different because commercial land concessions and expanding plantation capital often preceded more direct rule. Tobacco estates required land concessions from rulers, financing from investors, access to export facilities, and workers recruited across seas. Local sultans could derive substantial revenues and prestige from these contracts; they were not passive victims at the beginning of the process. Yet growing dependence on planters and the protection afforded by colonial officials narrowed their ability to manage land and disputes independently. The plantation frontier also intersected with the rights of Karo and other communities, whose relationships to land could not be resolved simply by a coastal ruler's grant.

The most serious social consequences appeared among migrant laborers. Contracts enforced with penal sanctions limited workers' freedom to leave, and debt, illness, punishments, and unequal access to justice made plantation work especially harsh. Chinese and Javanese migrants experienced these conditions differently according to recruitment networks and estate organization. Comparing Jambi and Deli therefore yields a sharper explanation than treating eastern Sumatra as one administrative unit: in one case, commercial and dynastic resistance prolonged a contested treaty relationship; in the other, private enterprise helped pull a coastal polity into a denser colonial state.

Division III — Borneo: Sultanates, Mining Communities, and Colonial Warfare

Central question: Why did Dutch sovereignty expand so unevenly across Borneo, and how did different political communities respond?

Section 103.9

Borneo's Sultanates, River Systems, and the Limits of Dutch Authority

Borneo is a very large island whose principal highways were its rivers. Along the Kapuas in the west, the Barito and Martapura in the south, and the Mahakam in the east, coastal sultanates grew up at the river mouths. Pontianak, founded in 1771 by an Arab-Malay dynasty, and Sambas were prominent in the west; Banjarmasin in the south had a long history of pepper and trade; Kutai on the Mahakam in the east was another important court. These states linked the interior with the sea, trading forest products, gold, diamonds, and pepper for cloth, salt, iron, and imported goods, with connections to China, Java, Singapore, and the wider Malay world.

Dutch political agreements with the sultanates multiplied from the 1820s. In 1846 the colonial government organized two administrative units, the Residency of Western Borneo, with its seat at Pontianak, and that of Southern and Eastern Borneo, based at Banjarmasin. These residencies were bureaucratic frames more than functioning governments. Residents had a small staff, a few troops, and treaties with rulers, and their effective reach was largely limited to the main rivers and ports.

It would be a mistake to assume that interior communities, including the many peoples collectively called Dayak by outsiders, were already subordinate to the coastal sultans. Coastal rulers claimed tribute and sometimes obtained it, but the relationship might be trade, intermittent payment, or ritual deference rather than rule. Some inland groups were closely tied to the coast, others lived beyond easy reach, and their relations changed with trade conditions, warfare, and migration. Dutch claims to rule the interior through the sultans therefore rested on a presumption that was often not true.

The physical character of Borneo explains why European claims were difficult to enforce. Rivers provided the main transport corridors, but their courses changed with flooding, shoals, and seasonal rainfall. Dense forests, mountain watersheds, and distances between settlements prevented coastal officials from supervising the interior simply by placing a flag at a river mouth. Malay trading courts could claim authority over upstream populations without possessing continuous coercive power. Dayak communities maintained their own systems of kinship, ceremonial exchange, farming, and political negotiation; reducing those relationships to tributary subordination would repeat the assumptions embedded in many colonial maps.

Western and southern Borneo offered contrasting opportunities for intervention. In the west, gold mines attracted migrants from southern China, whose mining associations and federations eventually assumed governmental functions of their own. Colonial officers could negotiate with sultans and still confront political organizations not subordinated to those rulers. In the south, Banjarmasin's court controlled a commercially important zone of river commerce, while disputes over succession and coal concessions offered openings for intervention. In the east, Kutai and other kingdoms negotiated with Dutch authorities across a vast region in which actual administration remained sparse. Describing a single “Dutch Borneo” conceals these divergent political trajectories.

Indigenous rulers often used Dutch recognition to strengthen their position against rival branches of a dynasty or competing communities, while Dutch agents sought to transform recognition into durable jurisdiction. This reciprocal calculation created instability. A prince who accepted support against a rival might later resist controls on taxes or foreign relations. Conversely, Dutch officers could describe a rival's claims as illegitimate simply because he opposed a colonial appointee. To understand these developments, students should distinguish titles conferred by treaty, influence exercised through river trade, and the actual ability to compel village cooperation.

The British presence in Sarawak and North Borneo sharpened these difficulties. Dutch claims to the southern and eastern portions were partly shaped by fears that delay would permit another imperial government to negotiate with indigenous rulers. The island's boundary thus became an international diplomatic issue well before it became a line administered across forests and rivers. The subsequent sections investigate what happened when commercial federations and dynastic courts resisted the conversion of such claims into territorial government.

Section 103.10

Chinese Mining Federations and the Kongsi Wars in Western Borneo

From the eighteenth century, Malay rulers invited Chinese miners, many from Hakka and Teochew communities in southern China, to work the gold fields of western Borneo. The miners organized themselves into kongsi, cooperative associations that held mining rights, ran villages and markets, raised armed forces, and maintained their own rules and officers. Some federated into larger units. Mary Somers Heidhues's research shows that these groups combined economic, social, and political functions, and in some districts exercised a degree of territorial authority comparable to a small state, while still paying nominal tribute to the nearby sultans or recognizing their formal precedence.

The kongsi also had to deal with Dayak communities, whose land they worked and with whom they traded, married, and sometimes fought, and with the Malay sultans of Pontianak and Sambas, who saw their income and sovereignty affected. When the Dutch reasserted themselves in the region in the 1820s, they sought to impose supervision, taxation, and opium and other revenue farms on the kongsi. Conflicts occurred through the 1850s, including fighting among kongsi themselves.

The largest Dutch campaign took place between 1850 and 1854 around Montrado, where the Dutch, with allied Malay and other forces, besieged and defeated the federation, which had resisted their demands. Many miners fled or were dispersed, and the Dutch tightened administration. Other federations survived for longer. The Lanfang kongsi, centered at Mandor, retained a form of self-government under successive headmen until its last leader died in 1884, after which the Dutch ended its independence in 1884–1885.

The Dutch justified their campaigns by pointing to disorder, tax evasion, and challenges to their sovereignty. The kongsi were not merely commercial associations, however: they possessed governing institutions, military resources, and territorial influence. Their destruction removed one of the major alternatives to Dutch and Malay rule in western Borneo, and it reshaped the region's economy and demography, as miners turned to farming, trade, and plantation work. The Chinese communities of western Borneo remained and developed new forms of association under colonial rule.

The federation's institutional structure matters as much as its mining output. Mining settlements required rules governing access to claims, water, labor, disputes, and the distribution of profits. Associations called kongsis developed councils, officers, armed guards, common halls, and mechanisms for coordinating mining communities. Some operated within wider federations, and their members formed relationships with local Malay rulers and Dayak communities that included tribute, trade, alliance, and conflict. The term “republic” is sometimes used for particular federations, especially Lanfang, but it risks importing assumptions about modern representative government into institutions with distinctive commercial and ritual foundations.

By the middle of the century Dutch officials increasingly sought authority over the mining districts rather than merely a share of their commercial revenue. Conflict centered on the ability to police roads, collect duties, mediate disputes, and restrict independent armed organizations. The wars of the early 1850s around Montrado involved shifting alliances and considerable civilian disruption. Military defeat altered mining governance, but it did not immediately dissolve all Chinese communal institutions or erase networks of credit and association. Families who had relied on mining income increasingly considered agriculture, market gardening, and commerce, adding another dimension to the social consequences of conquest.

Lanfang around Mandor survived the destruction of several other federations. Its negotiated relationship with Dutch authorities allowed a degree of local autonomy into the 1880s. The final crisis followed the death of its leader Liu Asheng in 1884, when colonial intervention in its institutions provoked further conflict that continued into 1885. This sequence is significant because it separates the major Montrado campaigns of 1850–1854 from the later end of Lanfang's autonomy. It also cautions against depicting every federation as a single alliance or dating their suppression uniformly.

Historians face substantial evidentiary asymmetry. Dutch military records treat Chinese organizations as threats to sovereignty, while some Chinese accounts emphasize corporate self-government and community protection. Local Malay and Dayak interests are still harder to reconstruct. The more persuasive interpretation considers these organizations neither mere criminal associations nor uniformly egalitarian polities. They were adaptable institutions in which economic cooperation, political authority, ethnic identity, and coercion could coexist. Their defeat increased colonial power, but their members and descendants continued to reshape western Borneo's commercial and social geography.

Historical Debate

What Were the Kongsi, and What Were the Kongsi Wars?

The question: Were the kongsi essentially commercial mining companies, quasi-republican states, or something between, and were the wars against them campaigns against rebellious subjects or against independent polities?

Interpretation A — commercial and community associations: Emphasizes shared capital, labor, and mutual aid among miners, with political functions secondary and subordinate to Malay sultans.
Interpretation B — territorial polities: Emphasizes the federations' courts, officials, armies, and control of territory and people, suggesting that the wars were conflicts between states, not suppressions of rebels.

Evidence: Dutch reports, kongsi records and Chinese-language sources, accounts by European visitors, and archaeological and demographic evidence. Dutch texts often reflect hostility, while Chinese documents survive unevenly.

Points of disagreement: Mainly terminology and interpretation: how much weight to give nominal ties to Malay rulers against actual practice.

Why it matters: Treating the kongsi as states changes how the Dutch campaigns are classified, and it places Chinese migrants within the political history of Borneo instead of only its economic history.

Student task: Identify two kinds of evidence that would help decide between the interpretations, and explain what each could and could not show.

Everyday Life and Social History

Mining, Trade, and Warfare

For miners, daily life revolved around digging, washing, and channeling water to separate gold, as well as farming, fishing, and small trade to feed the settlements. Villages included shops, temples, and meeting halls, and supplies came by river from the coast. Many miners were men who migrated alone, and marriage with local women, including Dayak women, shaped later communities, though the extent varied by place. Dayak farmers supplied rice, forest goods, and labor, and might also be attacked or pressed by miners and coastal rulers.

Warfare brought requisitions, flight, burned settlements, and disruption of food supplies. Because the surviving evidence is dominated by officials and observers, the experiences of women, Dayak villagers, and ordinary miners must often be read indirectly, through demographic changes, place names, and later oral tradition.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Kongsi Districts to Colonial Residency

What changed? Self-governing mining federations gave way to Dutch-supervised districts with appointed Chinese officials and revenue farms. What continued? Chinese settlement, trade networks, and agricultural communities persisted, as did Malay and Dayak relations with them. When and how? The change came gradually and through war, mostly between 1850 and 1885. Who experienced it most directly? Kongsi leaders and miners lost political autonomy; many farmers and traders experienced greater continuity. Why it matters: The pattern shows how colonial conquest reshaped, but did not erase, local communities.

Section 103.11

The Banjarmasin War and the Destruction of Sultanate Sovereignty, 1859–1863

The Sultanate of Banjarmasin in southeastern Borneo was among the island's oldest and wealthiest states, with a court, a Muslim religious establishment, and trade in pepper, rattan, diamonds, and gold. In the early nineteenth century it became increasingly dependent on Dutch support, and the Dutch used a series of treaties to extend their influence, including rights over coal-bearing land. From the late 1840s the Dutch opened the Oranje-Nassau coal mine near Pengaron, drawing laborers and creating resentment among local communities.

The immediate cause of the crisis was a succession dispute. When Sultan Adam died in 1857, rivals contested the throne: Tamjidillah, whom the Dutch preferred, and Hidayatullah, whom many Banjarese considered the legitimate heir. The Dutch installed Tamjidillah, who proved unpopular, and Hidayatullah's supporters, among them Prince Antasari, a leader with ties to the interior, mobilized discontent. In April 1859 an attack on the Pengaron mine and its Dutch staff began major fighting. Dutch officials deposed Tamjidillah and, in 1860, proclaimed the abolition of the sultanate and the annexation of its territory.

The war was fought largely along rivers and in forests. Dutch steamers and gunboats moved troops, while Banjarese forces built fortified positions and relied on inland support. Hidayatullah surrendered under severe colonial pressure in early 1862, then was removed with members of his household and exiled to Cianjur in Java; the competing accounts of his surrender and detention require careful handling. Antasari, proclaimed a religious and political leader by his followers, continued resistance until he died of illness in 1862. Fighting did not stop, however, and resistance under other leaders persisted in the interior into the early twentieth century. Civilians suffered displacement, burned settlements, and food shortages, though reliable casualty figures are not available.

The case demonstrates how colonial intervention in dynastic politics could undermine the political order it claimed to preserve. By choosing a candidate and then abolishing the sultanate, the Dutch removed the institution that had given them a framework for indirect rule, and the colonial state had to govern an unsettled territory directly, at high cost.

The succession crisis developed after the death of a designated heir in the early 1850s. Sultan Adam's family included rival candidates whose claims depended on maternal lineage, elite patronage, and the acceptance of court and religious authorities. Colonial officials favored Tamjidillah, who offered cooperation over coal mining, while Hidayatullah commanded significant support among sections of the royal family and population. These rival claims were not an incidental cause grafted onto a war for coal: access to resources and arguments over legitimate kingship directly influenced which candidate officials supported. The Pengaron coal workings, attracting investment and expectations of transport improvements, became a tangible focus of anxiety and opposition.

The fighting that began in 1859 involved attacks on colonial property and mines, the mobilization of followers around rival princes, and the emergence of Pangeran Antasari as a leading figure of resistance. Dutch military superiority did not deliver immediate control because river transport, local knowledge, and political loyalties mattered greatly. The formal abolition of the sultanate in June 1860 removed a court institution but did not settle the issue of legitimate authority. By contrast with a European assumption that a sovereign office could be legally terminated, many local supporters continued to recognize claims to leadership rooted in lineage and religious commitments.

Hidayatullah's removal requires careful wording. Accounts record his surrender in early 1862 under intense colonial pressure, followed by deportation with members of his family to Java and exile at Cianjur. It is misleading to describe the event simply as his capture in open battle. Dutch records could use language of surrender while local narratives emphasized manipulation, coercion, or broken expectations. This distinction is precisely why historians must compare political correspondence, court traditions, and later military histories. Antasari and others continued resistance after Hidayatullah's removal; the conventional label “Banjarmasin War, 1859–1863” should not imply that all opposition ceased in 1863.

The consequences extended through villages and waterways. Requisitions, flight, disrupted markets, and the threat of military punishment altered everyday economic life. Dutch officials attempted to standardize courts, taxation, and local appointments, but enforcement depended on men whose authority came from the political order being dismantled. This case illustrates an especially sharp contradiction of indirect rule: colonial authorities sought indigenous legitimacy to govern cheaply, yet their involvement in succession could destroy the legitimacy on which that system depended.

Section 103.12

Northern and Eastern Borneo: British Rivalry, Trade, and Frontier Delimitation

While the Dutch consolidated western and southern Borneo, other powers established themselves in the north. In 1841 James Brooke, an Englishman who had helped the Sultan of Brunei suppress a rebellion, was made Rajah of Sarawak, founding a dynasty that ruled for over a century. His government was small, relied on local alliances, and extended gradually along the rivers. Later, the British North Borneo Company, chartered in 1881, took over administration of northern Borneo, and in 1888 the British declared protectorates over Sarawak, North Borneo, and Brunei. Chapter 109 covers these states in detail.

For the Dutch, the rivalry was a concern because British activity in the north threatened to extend southward. Dutch interests in East Borneo, including the sultanates of Kutai and others, and the coal and oil possibilities, gave reasons to demarcate their own sphere. Negotiations between London and The Hague through the 1880s led to the Anglo-Dutch boundary agreement of 1891, which defined the frontier across the island by reference to watersheds and rivers.

The agreement settled a European dispute, but it did not describe the way people lived. Communities in the interior traded, intermarried, and moved across the line, and the Dutch and British governments had little ability to enforce it in the highlands. The boundary became important later, when it provided the foundation for the frontier between Indonesian Kalimantan and Malaysian Sarawak and Sabah. As with the 1824 treaty, students should distinguish a diplomatic line from the organization of the societies it crossed.

James Brooke's position in Sarawak emerged from local political struggles involving the Sultanate of Brunei and the lower Sarawak River, not from a simple purchase of empty territory. His rule from 1841 rested on negotiated titles, armed alliances, and subsequent British diplomatic connections. The Brooke state developed its own administrative forms and sought influence beyond its initial holdings. Farther northeast, the British North Borneo Company obtained a royal charter in 1881 and administered claims derived from concessions negotiated with regional rulers. These institutions were not identical to direct Crown government, and their political ambitions affected relations with Dutch officials across Borneo.

The Dutch were concerned both with potential loss of territory and with the movement of people and goods across a frontier that was largely unmapped on the ground. River systems and watersheds made boundaries especially difficult to describe: the headwaters of a river could be separated from its mouth by territories populated by communities whose allegiance changed according to trade and marriage ties. An international boundary drawn through those areas could bisect customary travel routes and create new opportunities for smuggling or for seeking refuge on one side. For residents of longhouses, the practical question was often which official would tax or punish them rather than which European state possessed a map.

The 1891 Anglo-Dutch Convention is best understood as an attempt to settle an imperial diplomatic frontier, not as proof that the two administrations had measured and policed the entire border. Later survey work, legal interpretation, and local encounters would be needed to translate treaty descriptions into physical markers. Some disputes arose precisely because European negotiators relied on imperfect geographical knowledge. Thus a treaty could reduce one kind of interstate rivalry while generating a different set of issues for officials and local communities.

This frontier history also complicates a national reading of the island. Today's borders separating Indonesian Kalimantan from Malaysia and Brunei were shaped by such nineteenth-century diplomacy, but the peoples who lived there did not regard themselves primarily as inhabitants of separate nation-states. Trade, religion, kinship, and migration continued to cross boundaries long after they appeared on paper. The distinction between diplomatic demarcation and lived geography is therefore central to understanding both the limits of Dutch expansion and the later history of Borneo.

Section 103.13

South Sulawesi: Gowa, Bone, and the Renewal of Dutch Intervention

South Sulawesi's political history was shaped by the seventeenth-century conflict that ended with the Treaty of Bongaya (1667), through which the VOC and its Bugis ally Arung Palakka defeated Gowa and established Dutch commercial primacy at Makassar (Chapter 82). Bone then became the leading Bugis kingdom, and the region remained a federation of rival states, including Gowa, Bone, Wajo, Soppeng, and others, bound by alliances and marriage. The treaty tradition gave the Dutch a basis for claims, but the kingdoms saw it differently.

After the Dutch restoration, officials in Makassar sought to renew and enforce the older agreements. In the 1820s they attempted to secure the kingdoms' acceptance of Bongaya's terms, and expeditions against Bone, which refused, followed. These had mixed results: the Dutch could defeat forces in the field and extract promises, but could not maintain control. In 1859–1860 a new campaign against Bone, which had challenged Dutch influence in its relations with neighboring states, resulted in further agreements and some concessions.

Why did earlier treaties not produce stable authority? The kingdoms had their own councils and traditions, and the rulers' authority depended on consensus among nobles. Dutch demands could be accepted by a ruler and rejected by his followers, and the Dutch themselves lacked the troops to station garrisons widely. Rivalries among the kingdoms offered opportunities, but also unpredictability. The more comprehensive conquests of South Sulawesi occurred in 1905–1906, when the Dutch subdued Bone and other states in a military campaign that belongs to the early twentieth century and is mentioned here only to avoid implying that control had been completed earlier.

The fall of Gowa's maritime power after the seventeenth-century wars did not produce a politically unified Dutch-controlled South Sulawesi. Bone's Bugis rulers were powerful partners and competitors in a shifting landscape of kingdoms, federations, and local communities. Successive Dutch governments invoked older VOC treaties to demand obedience, while local rulers interpreted the same relationships through changing conditions of military strength and political legitimacy. Their interests included control of port dues, rice-producing territories, trade corridors, and the recognition of aristocratic rank. A treaty from the seventeenth century could scarcely settle an eighteenth- or nineteenth-century succession dispute without renewed bargaining.

The military conflicts of the 1820s reflected the instability of the post-Napoleonic restoration and the difficulty of imposing Batavia's authority on local courts. Dutch forces could enter a port and place a garrison there but remain dependent on alliances with Bugis and Makassarese leaders. During the 1859–1860 campaigns involving Bone, Dutch commanders again tried to enforce terms of subordination, while rulers sought to defend their autonomy and protect legitimacy at home. Students should distinguish the immediate political circumstances of each expedition from later, more systematic conquest. The campaigns were not stages on a universally accepted path toward incorporation.

The commercial setting deepens the analysis. Bugis and Makassarese merchants maintained maritime networks reaching Borneo, the Lesser Sunda Islands, and the Moluccas. Trade did not stop when political authority changed, and many merchants used ports outside the closest reach of colonial customs. Local shipowners, sailors, and merchants could prosper from exchange even while objecting to Dutch attempts to regulate it. Attempts to centralize commercial routes were therefore inseparable from military and diplomatic pressure on inland or coastal rulers.

South Sulawesi also reveals why nominally allied courts could retain considerable autonomy. Some rulers recognized Dutch precedence in external affairs while continuing to exercise authority over internal disputes, labor, and agricultural revenues. The administrative weakness of colonial agents limited the enforcement of stronger claims. The large conquest campaigns of 1905–1906 belong only in the epilogue of this chapter, but their very necessity demonstrates that nineteenth-century agreements had not amounted to complete territorial control. The chapter's broader argument about uneven imperial consolidation is especially visible here.

Section 103.14

North Sulawesi and Minahasa: Treaties, Missions, and Colonial Integration

Minahasa, in the northern peninsula of Sulawesi, followed a different path. Its communities, organized in territorial groups with local leaders, had long traded rice and other goods with the VOC at Manado. The Dutch government and its local officials established closer relations in the early nineteenth century, building on alliances with village heads and the recruitment of auxiliaries. David Henley's study shows how this relationship became a distinctive form of integration.

Compulsory coffee cultivation was introduced in the 1820s, and Minahasan headmen were responsible for organizing deliveries, giving them roles as intermediaries and salaried officials. Protestant missionaries, who began working in Minahasa from the 1820s and 1830s, established schools and congregations, and conversion to Christianity spread widely. Education gave Minahasans access to administrative and clerical posts and to military service, and many joined the colonial army (KNIL), where they gained a reputation as reliable soldiers.

This pattern contrasted with major military campaigns elsewhere. In Minahasa, the Dutch relied on negotiation, patronage, and the co-optation of local leaders and institutions. Integration was not painless or entirely voluntary: coffee quotas were burdensome, headmen's powers could be abused, and the colonial order reinforced hierarchies. Yet the Minahasan elite also gained from schooling and employment, and Minahasa became one of the most closely tied regions of the Indies to Dutch institutions. Later, as Henley emphasizes, this experience shaped a regional identity that interacted with Indonesian nationalism in complex ways (Chapter 126).

Minahasa cannot be adequately explained merely by the absence of a large nineteenth-century conquest. Its communities had their own leaders, alliances, settlement traditions, and forms of collective obligation before closer Dutch supervision. Relations with European powers had grown through trade and military cooperation since earlier centuries, and Dutch officials later built upon these networks. Indigenous heads were often recognized and incorporated into local government, which gave colonial administrators channels through which to collect information, organize labor, and enforce obligations. Such arrangements were negotiated, but the imbalance of power increased as Dutch institutions acquired more stable resources.

Coffee cultivation became one of the material foundations of the colonial relationship. Officials sought compulsory deliveries from Minahasan producers, drawing on local leaders to organize planting and collection. Labor demands and government prices could provoke dissatisfaction even where open warfare was less frequent than in Bali or Banjarmasin. This exposes the limits of describing Minahasa as a case of peaceful integration: the absence of a large expedition does not establish the absence of coercion. Social historians must investigate payments, village workloads, access to new markets, and the differing circumstances of prosperous and marginal households.

Christian missions and education shaped a further set of changes. Missionary schools expanded literacy and created new possibilities for teachers and church officials, while conversion and religious instruction influenced community relationships and debates over authority. Some local leaders used these institutions to gain status or cultivate wider contacts; others resisted the implication that older beliefs and practices should be abandoned. The new institutions were not solely a projection of Dutch policy. Minahasan students, teachers, and congregations increasingly made choices that transformed them from recipients of outside instruction into participants in local religious and educational life.

Military recruitment also tied communities to the colonial state. Men from northern Sulawesi entered colonial service, acquiring wages and experiences that connected their homes to campaigns elsewhere in the archipelago. Their participation complicates an overly simple division between Dutch conquerors and Indonesian subjects: members of colonized communities could become agents of conquest in other regions while experiencing constraints at home. Henley's work explains how these overlapping military, educational, economic, and religious institutions created distinctive forms of regional identity. Minahasa therefore belongs at the center of a comparative history of imperialism, not as a brief exception to a supposedly universal pattern of military conquest.

Section 103.15

Bali and the Dutch Expeditions of 1846, 1848, and 1849

In the mid-nineteenth century Bali was divided among several kingdoms, including Buleleng in the north, Karangasem in the east, Klungkung (whose ruler, the Dewa Agung, held ritual precedence), Badung, Tabanan, Gianyar, and others. Henk Schulte Nordholt's study shows that their politics combined ritual status, alliances, and competition, with rulers' authority depending on followers and ceremony as well as taxes. Coastal ports, notably Buleleng, traded rice, cattle, and other goods, and the island's rulers had connections to Lombok and Java.

The Dutch crisis arose from the Balinese custom of tawan karang, the right claimed by rulers to wrecked vessels and their cargo. The Dutch regarded it as plunder. In 1844 the wreck of a Dutch ship on the coast of Badung led to disputes, and the colonial government demanded that Bali's rulers renounce the right and accept Dutch authority. The rulers refused to be bound by such demands, and the Dutch dispatched an expedition to Buleleng in 1846.

The question of shipwreck rights illustrates how different legal understandings led to conflict. Balinese rulers asserted claims to the property of wrecked vessels under the practice commonly called tawan karang, although its application varied by kingdom and circumstances. Dutch representatives claimed to defend international shipping and challenged the seizure of cargo or ships. Yet the dispute was also about which government had authority to regulate coastal commerce and punish alleged violations. For Balinese courts, acceptance of Dutch definitions could undermine a long-established prerogative and signal wider political subordination.

After the first expedition in 1846, agreements imposed on Buleleng and Karangasem addressed recognition of Dutch authority, restrictions on foreign relations, abolition of tawan karang, and payment of expedition costs. A regional Buleleng historical account gives a combined indemnity of 300,000 guilders, but published educational summaries differ in how they describe particular payments and treaty provisions. The chapter should identify that figure as reported by a specific regional source, rather than present it as an independently collated transcription of the original treaty. The pressures of agreement did not secure stable compliance. I Gusti Ketut Jelantik helped organize resistance at Jagaraga, where terrain, fortified approaches, and cooperation among Balinese forces complicated Dutch attack.

The failed Dutch assault in 1848 made clear that a kingdom regarded as technologically inferior could use knowledge of local terrain and defensive preparations to defeat an expedition. The renewed operations in 1849 succeeded at Jagaraga and extended into eastern Bali. Jelantik's death is associated with the subsequent retreat toward Karangasem; the exact circumstances and date are treated differently in historical traditions, so an unsupported day should not be invented. Fighting then shifted toward Klungkung. At Kusamba, Balinese forces launched a night attack that mortally wounded Dutch commander Andreas Victor Michiels, who died on May 25, 1849. That event was not simply an afterthought following Dutch victory but evidence that Balinese resistance continued after major strongholds had fallen.

Political results varied by locality. Some rulers acknowledged Dutch precedence without losing their entire internal administration immediately. Northern Bali underwent later restructuring, and the direct-rule reorganization of Buleleng and Jembrana in 1882 must be distinguished from the military events of the 1840s. Large portions of southern Bali remained outside direct Dutch administration until the campaigns of 1906–1908. The chapter should therefore teach 1849 as a turning point in the extension of Dutch influence, not as a date when the whole island became a uniformly governed colonial province.

Historical Vocabulary
Tawan Karang

Definition. A custom, recognized by several Balinese rulers, that gave them rights over ships wrecked on their shores and their cargoes.

Historical context. Similar customary claims were known in other seafaring societies, including parts of Europe. For Balinese rulers it was a source of revenue and a mark of sovereignty over the coast. Dutch and other merchants regarded it as legalized piracy.

Why it matters. The dispute allowed the Dutch to present intervention as protection of trade and humanity.

Potential misunderstanding. The shipwreck issue was a pretext as well as a grievance; broader aims of extending Dutch influence over Bali also mattered.

The 1846 expedition landed at Buleleng, defeated its forces, and imposed a treaty. It was followed by a standoff in which the Balinese refused to carry out the obligations, notably the demolition of fortifications and payment of an indemnity. The key Balinese figure was I Gusti Ketut Jelantik, the patih (chief minister) of Buleleng, who had argued against concessions and organized defense around the fortress at Jagaraga. A second expedition in 1848 was defeated there, a rare and humiliating Dutch setback.

The Dutch responded with a larger force in 1849, which took Jagaraga after heavy fighting. Jelantik withdrew, and accounts of his later fate differ. The expedition then moved against other kingdoms, but at Kusamba the Dutch commander, General Michiels, was mortally wounded and died on 25 May 1849, and the campaign ended with negotiated arrangements. Dutch victories in northern Bali did not give them direct authority over the island: Buleleng and neighboring Jembrana came under tighter control, and Buleleng was placed under direct administration in 1882, but most southern Balinese kingdoms retained their rulers until further campaigns, including the famous puputan (ritual mass suicide-in-battle) at Badung in 1906 and Klungkung in 1908, which belong to the epilogue.

Balinese decisions in these years were not irrational resistance to modernity. They reflected rulers' judgments about sovereignty, ritual standing, and the consequences of accepting Dutch demands, as well as rivalries among the kingdoms that sometimes led them to remain neutral or to seek advantage.

Lombok in 1894: Balinese Rule, Sasak Communities, and Dutch Conquest

Lombok, east of Bali across a deep strait, was home to a majority Sasak population, mostly Muslim, and a minority of Balinese settlers whose rulers from the Karangasem dynasty had taken control in the eighteenth century. By the nineteenth century the island was divided among several Balinese courts, with a ruler in Mataram and Cakranegara, and a Sasak aristocracy that held lands and local roles under Balinese overlordship. Rice exports and trade with Singapore and other ports made the island prosperous, and the court was widely known for its wealth.

Sasak grievances grew in the late nineteenth century. They concerned taxation, land, forced labor, and the preferential position of the Balinese, as well as conflicts within the Sasak aristocracy. Some Sasak leaders, particularly in the east, appealed to the Dutch for support, seeking to use Dutch power to end Balinese rule. Dutch officials, for their part, were attentive to Lombok's wealth and strategic position, to British and other foreign commercial interest, and to the opportunity to resolve their earlier treaty relationship with the Lombok ruler.

In 1894 a Dutch expedition landed on Lombok and initially was successful, but a Balinese counterattack on the night of 25–26 August inflicted heavy casualties around Cakranegara and nearby positions and forced a Dutch withdrawal. Major-General P. P. H. van Ham died on 26 August during the retreat. Reinforcements and a renewed campaign secured Dutch victory later in the year. By the end of the year, Balinese rule had been broken, the court's treasures were seized, the royal family was killed, exiled, or surrendered, and the island passed under Dutch rule. The consequences for local society were substantial: Sasak elites gained some positions, but the colonial government took over administration, and many Balinese lost land and status.

The intervention was not a straightforward liberation. Sasak leaders hoped for autonomy and relief from the Balinese court, but they came under Dutch control instead. Balinese rulers acted to preserve their rule, using alliances and diplomacy. Dutch officials claimed to be responding to Sasak appeals, but they also pursued expansion and economic and strategic interests. Each party's aims must be analyzed separately.

Lombok's political order was shaped by the long expansion of Balinese power from Karangasem and by the relationships among Sasak communities with their own leaders and Islamic institutions. It is misleading to describe all Sasaks as united against a single foreign dynasty, because regional elites had different interests and relationships with Balinese rulers. Some Sasak leaders appealed to Dutch officials for assistance against oppression, while others sought to protect their autonomy from both the Balinese court and the prospective colonial administration. The Dutch treated those requests as part of their justification for intervention, but their ultimate goal included the extension of effective government authority over the island.

The expedition of 1894 unfolded in distinct phases. Dutch forces initially landed, secured coastal positions, and negotiated from what appeared to be a position of superiority. In late August Balinese forces launched a surprise counterattack against Dutch troops around Cakranegara and related positions. The fighting during the night of August 25–26 inflicted severe losses and forced a retreat. Major-General P. P. H. van Ham died on August 26 during the withdrawal, not during the later decisive Dutch assault. The defeat shocked officials and increased the political importance attached to a successful renewed campaign. Later reinforcements and operations achieved the conquest of the Balinese strongholds.

The collapse of Balinese rule should not be equated with the fulfillment of every Sasak expectation. Dutch officers entered a complex society where disputes over land, religious authority, taxation, and labor had long histories. Sasak leaders who had anticipated greater independence found themselves negotiating with another government that expected revenue and obedience. Some former Balinese officials and local elites adapted to the new regime. Military victory created opportunities for replacing royal administration, but also required the new government to classify local authorities and determine which groups would control disputed land and resources.

A balanced interpretation must recognize the violence of Balinese political domination where documented, the real agency of Sasak leaders who called for outside support, and the coercion of the subsequent colonial conquest. No single label—liberation, annexation, or punitive expedition—captures the perspectives of everyone involved. Dutch descriptions of humanitarian protection need to be tested against the consequences of the occupation. The campaign also shows that military superiority did not prevent catastrophic operational failures, and that the Dutch state sometimes responded to defeat by increasing, rather than reassessing, its commitment to conquest.

Section 103.17

Flores, Timor, and the Lesser Sunda Islands

East of Bali and Lombok, the Lesser Sunda chain includes Sumbawa, Sumba, Flores, Solor, Alor, and western Timor. Political life there was highly varied: Muslim sultanates in Sumbawa, small kingdoms and clan-based communities on Sumba and Flores, and chiefdoms (raja) on Timor and neighboring islands. The islands exported sandalwood, beeswax, horses, and other goods, and traders from Makassar, Java, and elsewhere visited them. Catholic communities, descended from Portuguese and Dominican missions, existed in Larantuka and nearby areas, and Protestant influence grew around Kupang.

The Dutch and Portuguese had long overlapped in this region. Dutch Kupang stood in western Timor, while the Portuguese held Dili and other places in the east and the Solor archipelago. Disputes over jurisdiction, trade, and the authority of local rulers continued for generations, and neither European power exercised close control over most of the interior. Local rulers, who made agreements with one or the other, treated European titles as resources in their own contests.

In 1859 the Netherlands and Portugal concluded a treaty, signed in Lisbon on 20 April 1859 and ratified in 1860, which attempted to resolve these claims. In general terms, Portugal gave up its claims over Flores and the Solor archipelago, while the boundaries on Timor were adjusted, with Portugal retaining the eastern part and the enclave of Oecusse. The treaty fixed European positions, and Portuguese Timor stayed outside the Dutch colonial state (Chapter 120). But it did not mean that the Dutch could govern the territories they gained. Missionary institutions, local rajas, and trading networks continued to matter, and effective Dutch administration in Flores and neighboring islands developed slowly, with significant military pacification campaigns occurring only in the early twentieth century.

The lesser Sunda islands were not a single political unit. On Flores, coastal courts and communities were connected to Portuguese and Dutch trading and missionary networks, while inland societies retained distinct political and ritual institutions. Larantuka's Catholic communities had relationships with Portuguese authorities and local rulers that could not be reduced to the flag displayed at a trading post. On Timor, numerous liurai and other rulers negotiated alliances, tribute, and military support in a landscape where neither European power possessed comprehensive inland administration. Sumba and Sumbawa presented different political conditions, commercial contacts, and indigenous forms of authority. A general reference to the Lesser Sundas should never erase these variations.

The diplomatic struggle with Portugal had roots in competing claims over Solor, Flores, and Timor that extended back centuries. The treaty signed in Lisbon on April 20, 1859, attempted to simplify the map. Its articles addressed territorial demarcation on Timor, the transfer of Portuguese claims involving Larantuka, Sikka, and Paga on Flores and territories on Adonara and Solor, and arrangements affecting Maubara, the Ambeno region, and Ataúro on the Timor side. The treaty also contained a financial settlement and provisions concerning religious practice. It was ratified in 1860. Students should distinguish the original 1859 agreement from subsequent treaties and boundary arbitration that further altered or interpreted the frontier.

For many communities, these diplomatic changes did not immediately transform the authorities encountered in daily life. A ruler who had communicated with a Portuguese missionary might still mediate taxation and adjudication after officials in Europe reassigned a claim to the Dutch. Missionary institutions could have their own local networks, and their political interests did not always coincide with either European state's administrative objectives. Disputes about which flags were flown, which court received tribute, and which places were classed as dependencies could therefore persist long after ratification.

The case is an important counterpoint to the large expeditions in Bali. Colonial expansion could occur on paper through diplomatic exchange before troops, courts, or customs posts were established. It could also produce new jurisdictional questions because the governments signing the treaty did not have the geographical knowledge or local legitimacy they assumed. Dutch and Portuguese competition did not end with the 1859 settlement; later boundary negotiations and the 1914 arbitration reveal the continuing difficulties of translating treaty descriptions into the political and geographical reality of Timor.

Section 103.18

The Moluccas: Sultanates, Maritime Networks, and Colonial Reorganization

The Moluccas, the original Spice Islands (Chapter 71), had been the VOC's most closely controlled region. Ternate and Tidore, the two clove-producing sultanates, and the islands of Ambon, Banda, and the Lease group were bound to the Company by monopoly arrangements, forced deliveries, and the extirpation of unauthorized clove trees (Chapters 77 and 78). By the nineteenth century the spice monopolies were declining in value. Spices were being grown elsewhere, prices had fallen, and compulsory systems were costly and unpopular.

The Dutch reorganized the administration, creating residencies at Ambon, Ternate, and elsewhere, and reduced the sultans' functions. The sultans of Ternate and Tidore remained as nominal rulers with Dutch supervision and stipends, and their historic claims over parts of Halmahera, northern Sulawesi, and western New Guinea became instruments in Dutch diplomacy. The Dutch relaxed the older monopoly regime through the middle of the century, and in the 1860s compulsory deliveries were reduced, a change that altered the economies of Ambon and the neighboring islands.

Society in the Moluccas included Christian communities, especially in Ambon and the Lease Islands, many of whom served in colonial institutions and the army, and Muslim communities in the north and elsewhere. The Pattimura revolt of 1817, introduced in Chapter 100, had shown the discontent produced by Dutch demands; its narrative is not repeated here. In the longer term, Dutch policies encouraged religious and regional distinctions that later shaped political identity.

The Moluccas' historical importance to the VOC did not mean that the nineteenth-century Dutch controlled the whole of eastern Indonesia. Their administration was concentrated in a few centers. Remote islands, the Kei and Aru groups, parts of Seram and Halmahera, and interior areas were reached irregularly by officials, traders, and missionaries. Maritime commerce continued through networks of Bugis, Makassarese, Chinese, and local traders, only partly under Dutch regulation.

The transition from VOC monopoly to colonial administration was uneven across the Moluccas. Ambon and Banda had been centers of coercive spice production, while the sultanates of Ternate and Tidore pursued their own diplomatic strategies in northern Maluku. The gradual decline of the old monopoly order changed the incentives of producers, merchants, and officials, but government institutions inherited divisions between districts long subjected to European supervision and islands where local rulers retained considerable autonomy. The central Moluccan uprising of 1817, associated with Pattimura, was one prominent response to Dutch restoration; its causes and consequences belong in the administrative background of Chapter 100 rather than being retold at length here.

The nineteenth-century growth of alternative trade routes complicated Dutch commercial control. Bugis, Makassarese, Chinese, Arab, and other merchants connected island communities to wider markets. Producers of cloves and other commodities could seek new buyers when colonial restrictions weakened, but access to capital, boats, and commercial credit remained unequal. Village heads and local trading elites could gain bargaining power while communities formerly tied to monopoly deliveries struggled with changing markets. The end of one type of colonial extraction thus did not necessarily mean freedom from all official obligations or from new commercial dependencies.

Ternate and Tidore also mattered for Dutch claims extending toward western New Guinea. Dutch officers referred to their older relationships with these courts and to reported tributary connections with Papuan coastal rulers. Yet such references were often politically convenient simplifications. Tidore's claims rested on a range of maritime alliances, ritual relationships, and intermittent collections that did not amount to routine administration of an enormous island. The Dutch attempt to derive territorial sovereignty from these relationships therefore needs to be analyzed as imperial legal reasoning, not simply reproduced as an indigenous map of power.

The diversity of religious communities must also remain visible. Muslim and Christian populations, local ritual traditions, and missionary networks coexisted across the islands in changing arrangements. European writings frequently presented these differences as fixed political categories, although alliances and identities were more fluid. The Moluccas illustrate an alternative path of state formation in which inherited commercial institutions, diplomacy with sultanates, religious organizations, and naval presence were often more important than major land campaigns during the nineteenth century. They also show that longstanding European contact could coexist with extensive limits on effective colonial rule.

Section 103.19

Western New Guinea: Colonial Claims, Fort Du Bus, and Limited Administration

Western New Guinea's coasts had long been touched by the trade of Tidore, Seram, and Chinese and Bugis merchants, who obtained bird-of-paradise plumes, nutmeg-like spices, slaves, and other goods. Tidore claimed some coastal areas, and Dutch officials, who regarded Tidore as their dependent, cited its claims as a foundation for their own. Papuan coastal communities had trading, kinship, and political relations of their own, and the interior highlands, whose large populations were unknown to Europeans until the twentieth century, lay entirely beyond any state's reach.

In 1828 the Dutch proclaimed sovereignty over the western part of the island and established Fort Du Bus, named for Governor-General L. P. J. du Bus de Gisignies, on Triton Bay. The post was meant to support the claim against British or other rivals, but it suffered from disease, isolation, and poor supply, and was abandoned in the mid-1830s (dated 1835 or 1836 in different accounts). For decades afterward the Dutch maintained their claim chiefly through diplomatic means and occasional patrols, while exploration was carried out by scientific and missionary expeditions.

Interest increased late in the century, as Germany and Britain established themselves in the eastern half and the question of boundaries arose; agreements in the 1880s and 1890s divided the island among the three powers. In 1898 the Dutch established posts at Manokwari and Fakfak, with small garrisons and officials. Even then, large areas remained beyond effective colonial control, and much of the interior was not explored or administered until well into the twentieth century. Western New Guinea thus provides the starkest example in this chapter of an assertion of sovereignty preceding administration by seven decades.

Fort Du Bus was established in 1828 at Triton Bay during the administration of Governor-General Léonard Pierre Joseph du Bus de Gisignies, after whom it was named. This correction matters because the outpost was not the personal venture of an unidentified commissioner: it was an official attempt to demonstrate the Netherlands' claim in a region where sustained rule was absent. The remote site created formidable practical difficulties. Disease, isolation, inadequate local supplies, and unreliable communications undermined the settlement, which was abandoned by the mid-1830s; sources differ on whether to describe the effective withdrawal as 1835 or 1836. A date for a fort's abandonment should not be mistaken for abandonment of the underlying European territorial claim.

European officials justified their claim partly by appealing to the Sultanate of Tidore's supposed relationships with Papuan coastal settlements. The reasoning assumed that a ruler's occasional receipt of tribute or recognition from coastal communities could furnish a European treaty partner with sovereignty over the western half of a vast island. This was an imperial legal argument rather than evidence of effective occupation. Papuan coastal communities traded across the region and maintained their own political relations, while inland populations had not been incorporated into Tidore's administration. Their circumstances cannot be reconstructed solely from the diplomatic correspondence of Ternate or Batavia.

Late-century strategic anxiety accelerated the creation of actual posts. Britain and Germany were establishing imperial positions farther east, and Dutch officials feared that an entirely nominal claim would be challenged. Permanent administrative stations at Manokwari and Fakfak in 1898 represented a significant shift, but they remained small coastal footholds. The breadth of the claimed hinterland was vastly greater than the region directly visited by officials. Investigations, exploration, missionary contacts, and later patrols gradually extended the reach of the state, but no nineteenth-century administration comprehensively governed the western New Guinea highlands.

This example should also encourage caution about the idea of “discovery.” European expeditions might describe a mountain valley as unknown, but the people living there possessed histories, trade networks, agriculture, and knowledge of their landscapes. Their lack of representation in Dutch reports is a limitation of the archive, not evidence of a political or social vacuum. Western New Guinea is therefore the clearest demonstration of the difference among a written claim, a coastal fort, a permanent post, and the much more demanding achievement of everyday administrative authority.

Section 103.20

Historical Synthesis: The Expansion and Limits of the Dutch Colonial State

Comparison across the regions shows that the instruments of expansion were similar but their weight differed. In Sumatra, military intervention and the manipulation of local conflicts were central, in Palembang and the Padri War, while commercial concessions mattered in Deli. In Borneo, warfare against kongsi and the Banjarese sultanate and diplomacy with Britain combined. In Bali and Lombok, expeditions justified by trade and protection arguments overthrew or subdued courts. In Minahasa, patronage, missions, and military recruitment served better than war. In eastern Indonesia, treaties among Europeans were more important than local conquest for most of the century.

Indigenous political actors shaped each outcome. Rulers allied with the Dutch or resisted them according to their circumstances: adat leaders in Minangkabau, Sasak nobles in Lombok, and Malay sultans in Borneo sought help against rivals, while Mahmud Badaruddin, Taha Saifuddin, Antasari, Jelantik, and the kongsi leaders organized resistance. Religious ideas, notably Islamic reform in Sumatra and Borneo, and social structures, such as the kongsi's institutions and Minahasan village leadership, influenced outcomes. The Dutch rarely faced an empty political field.

The gap between claim and control is the chapter's central finding. Palembang's sultanate fell in the 1820s but its interior remained difficult; Jambi had treaties from 1833–1834, was incorporated in 1903, and became a separate residency in 1906; Buleleng came under direct rule in 1882 while most of Bali waited until 1906–1908; western New Guinea was claimed in 1828 and first occupied in 1898. By 1900 the colonial state had extended across much of the archipelago on paper and in practice across substantial parts, but important regions, including Aceh, much of Sulawesi, Bali, and New Guinea, were contested or unadministered.

Chapter 104 turns to Aceh, where the struggle for effective control became exceptionally prolonged and destructive, and shows what happened when an independent state and its people refused to accept the claims discussed here.

Four comparative dimensions help students assess the regional case studies. First, the political starting point differed: a dynastic sultanate in Palembang, village and religious networks in Minangkabau, mining federations in western Borneo, layered kingdoms in Bali, and independent coastal communities in New Guinea offered entirely different forms of negotiation and resistance. Second, the incentives differed: tin, coffee, tobacco, coal, and gold were commercially attractive in some places, whereas diplomatic exclusion of rivals mattered especially in others. Third, the means of enforcement differed among formal treaty provisions, blockades, allied troops, large expeditions, and administrative recruitment. Finally, the outcomes varied from intensified indirect rule to the removal of dynasties and still thinner coastal claims.

The chronological distinction between military victory and civil administration should be made explicit in the final comparison. Palembang provides a case of political displacement followed by prolonged inland negotiation. Jambi demonstrates a treaty relationship surviving in contested form for decades before the events of 1903–1906. Western Borneo's mining federations were not all suppressed in the same campaign. Buleleng's military submission in the 1840s preceded the direct-rule changes of 1882, and the conquest of Lombok in 1894 did not settle every subsequent dispute over land and local government. Western New Guinea remained overwhelmingly beyond Dutch administrative reach even after the permanent posts of 1898.

Historians should also evaluate the language of progress and pacification in colonial reports. Officials regularly described suppression of rival rulers, restrictions on trade, and regulation of labor as the necessary creation of order. Those claims must be considered alongside economic opportunities, local requests for protection, and the coercion inflicted on communities. Conversely, resistance movements were not always socially inclusive or free of violence against their own neighbors. Indigenous political actors could ally with colonial armies for contingent reasons and later oppose the resulting regime. Explaining such choices is more useful than assigning every participant to an unchanging category of collaborator or patriot.

The nineteenth-century colonial state was thus neither an uninterrupted military advance nor a simple continuation of the VOC's commercial empire. It became a territorial project through a complex series of bargains and conflicts, backed by growing fiscal, naval, and military capacity. Its maps were partly statements of aspiration and partly records of achieved control. The start of the Aceh War in 1873, and its prolonged course treated in Chapter 104, is an especially important challenge to any claim that Dutch expansion had already produced an orderly and accepted political system. The broader comparative lesson is that sovereignty is a historical process to be demonstrated, not a fact to be assumed from color on a map.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Colonial Sovereignty, 1816–1900

What changed? Dutch claims turned from treaty-based commercial primacy into assertions of territorial sovereignty, with growing use of force and direct administration, especially after 1870. What continued? Reliance on local intermediaries, uneven reach into the interior, and persistent resistance. When? Gradually, with acceleration in the later nineteenth century. Who felt it most? Deposed rulers, communities in war zones, and plantation and mine workers experienced change most directly; many villagers in remote areas saw less. Why it matters: It explains why the colonial state of 1900 was an uneven achievement.

Epilogue. Many conquests lay after 1900. South Sulawesi's kingdoms were subdued in 1905–1906, southern Bali in 1906–1908, and Flores and other islands in the following years, within a more systematic policy of pacification. These later campaigns confirm that the territorial state of the early twentieth century was still being made.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how the Netherlands extended its claims across the Indonesian archipelago between 1816 and 1900, and has argued that expansion was a contested process, not an automatic spread of government. The political landscape outside Java consisted of sultanates, village republics, mining federations, and chiefdoms, and the Dutch inherited from the VOC a patchwork of posts and treaties.

The colonial government pursued expansion for varied reasons, including commerce, strategic competition, and later investment, and used diplomacy, recognition of rulers, expeditions, forts, and residents. The Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824 secured a European acceptance of a Dutch sphere but did not give the Dutch authority over indigenous societies, whose rulers were not parties to it.

In Sumatra, the Dutch took Palembang's capital in 1821, intervened in the Padri War from 1821, and tied West Sumatra to compulsory coffee deliveries. In Jambi, treaties of 1833–1834 produced a protectorate claimed by the Dutch and contested by the sultan, and annexation came only in 1906. In Deli, concessions to planters created a plantation economy based on migrant labor.

In Borneo, the Dutch organized residencies in the 1840s but could administer little beyond rivers and ports. Their campaigns against Chinese mining federations from 1850 to 1885 ended a form of self-government that historians debate how to classify. The Banjarmasin War resulted from interference in a succession and led to the abolition of the sultanate, while resistance continued.

In Sulawesi, Bali, and Lombok, the legacy of earlier treaties, shipwreck disputes, and appeals by local factions led to expeditions of varying success. Minahasa showed an alternative route of integration through coffee, missions, schooling, and military service. Bali's resistance at Jagaraga and the complex interests at play in Lombok show that indigenous decisions shaped outcomes.

In eastern Indonesia, treaties among Europeans, such as the Dutch-Portuguese treaty of 1859, assigned territories to the Dutch without creating administration. The Moluccas saw the decline of the spice monopolies, and western New Guinea saw a claim in 1828 but posts only in 1898.

The chapter's evidence base combines Dutch treaties and reports with indigenous, missionary, and other sources and with modern scholarship, notably by Dobbin, Locher-Scholten, Heidhues, Henley, and Schulte Nordholt. Each source type has limits, and treaties in particular record the drafting side's categories.

Social history appears in the experiences of plantation workers, miners, villagers in war zones, and communities caught between rulers and the colonial state. The costs of expansion fell heavily on civilians, though precise figures are rarely available.

The principal conclusion is that Dutch expansion transformed the political geography of maritime Southeast Asia, yet formal sovereignty often preceded effective control by decades. The colonial state that emerged was an uneven achievement of intervention, negotiation, coercion, and military force. Chapter 104 examines Aceh, where this contest was longest and most destructive.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a colonial territory was made, not given. The archipelago later governed from Batavia and then Jakarta emerged from an accumulation of treaties, wars, concessions, and negotiations whose outcomes depended on local as well as European decisions.

It also shows why the study of colonial borders requires care. Lines drawn in London, Lisbon, and The Hague in 1824, 1859, and 1891 shaped later states, yet they did not describe the societies they divided. Authority often rested on intermediaries and on military threat, and the distance between claim and control was a lasting feature of colonial government.

The developments here prepared later chapters: Aceh's prolonged war (Chapter 104), the British Straits Settlements (Chapter 105), the colonial economy and plural societies (Chapters 121 and 122), and the nationalist movements that would inherit and contest the colonial map (Chapter 126). Understanding how the Dutch colonial state was built, unevenly and by force, is necessary for understanding the region it left behind.
