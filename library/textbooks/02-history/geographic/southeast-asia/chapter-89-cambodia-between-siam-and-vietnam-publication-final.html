Cambodia between Siam and Vietnam

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

After Angkor, Cambodia did not become an empty space between two rising neighbors. It remained a Buddhist kingdom with active courts, river capitals, foreign merchant communities, and an energetic diplomacy, whose rulers and rival princes repeatedly turned to Ayutthaya and to the Nguyễn lords of southern Vietnam for help, and in doing so gradually narrowed their own room for maneuver.

Visitors who stand today at Angkor Wat often take the monument as the endpoint of a story: a great empire built it, and then the empire vanished. The evidence assembled for this chapter points in a different direction. Within a few generations of the fifteenth century, Khmer-speaking rulers had established courts farther south along the Tonle Sap and Mekong rivers, at places such as Srei Santhor, the Phnom Penh area, Longvek, and later Oudong. Meanwhile, Angkor itself continued as a Buddhist pilgrimage and monastic site. Seventeenth-century Japanese visitors, for example, left inscriptions at Angkor Wat, which some of them took to be a famous Indian Buddhist monastery. Cambodia after Angkor was therefore a landscape of continuing religious and political life, not a ruin.

Longvek, the principal royal center of the sixteenth century, shows how much this older narrative leaves out. Archaeological work by Martin Polkinghorne and colleagues has identified a large fortified settlement, with earthworks, ceramics from several Asian production centers, and evidence of continued occupation after the Siamese conquest conventionally dated to 1594. Dutch, Portuguese, Spanish, Japanese, and Chinese records show that merchants and adventurers came to the Khmer court because it controlled access to river routes and to the forest and agricultural products of the interior. A history that treated these centuries only as a prelude to later loss would have to ignore this evidence.

The chapter's central problem is nonetheless a real one. By the early eighteenth century the Khmer court could no longer manage its two powerful neighbors as it had in the early seventeenth. This chapter asks how that change occurred. It argues that the answer lies neither in a simple story of Siamese and Vietnamese aggression nor in a story of Khmer weakness alone. Rival royal lineages, ministers, and provincial leaders competed for the throne; claimants who lost at court sought armies abroad; foreign assistance created obligations; and, along the lower Mekong, Vietnamese and Chinese settlement transformed access to ports, customs revenue, and people. Each of these processes depended on the others.

Several developments were of particular importance. Longvek fell to Siamese forces in 1594, although the settlement remained active and even briefly returned to royal use in 1732; the Khmer court recovered, and under Chey Chettha II (r. c. 1618-1628) it established a new capital at Oudong. Cambodian chronicle traditions associate Chey Chettha II with a marriage to a daughter of a Nguyễn lord, while a related, contested tradition records permission for a Nguyễn trading presence at Prey Nokor, a development that gave the court a new partner and later created new constraints. In 1642 a ruler known as Ramadhipati I came to the throne, converted to Islam, and led a court tied to Malay and Muslim Chinese commercial networks. His reign included a violent confrontation with the Dutch East India Company (VOC) in 1643-1644. In 1658 a Nguyễn expedition, invited into a quarrel among Cambodian claimants, removed him from power.

The evidence is uneven and must be read with care. Cambodian royal chronicles, which narrate these events in detail, were compiled or copied in their surviving forms long after many of the events they describe. Thai and Vietnamese court histories give accounts shaped by their own dynastic purposes. Dutch trading records offer unusually dense, though partisan, contemporary information about the 1640s. Inscriptions, such as the 1747 text at Angkor Wat, give dated Khmer-language evidence from an official's own world. Archaeology adds material evidence for cities and exchange that no chronicle recorded. Throughout the chapter, students are asked to separate recorded events, retrospective tradition, and scholarly inference.

This chapter treats Cambodia's story from the Khmer side, as its title's phrase "between Siam and Vietnam" might otherwise obscure. Chapter 86 examines Ayutthaya's institutions, and Chapters 87 and 88 examine the Trịnh-Nguyễn division and the Nguyễn frontier. Here those histories appear chiefly as they affected, and were affected by, decisions made at the Cambodian court. Chapter 61 treats the earlier transformation of Angkor, Chapter 92 synthesizes the regional commercial world to which Cambodia contributed, and Chapter 93 opens the broader eighteenth-century story. The chapter closes around 1750, without narrating the later protectorate and colonial periods.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the relocation of Khmer royal courts after Angkor reflected political and economic adaptation rather than civilizational collapse.
  2. Describe Longvek as a fortified royal and commercial center, distinguishing chronicle tradition from archaeological evidence.
  3. Analyze how the Tonle Sap-Mekong river system shaped the location of capitals, agriculture, fisheries, and trade.
  4. Trace the sequence of royal centers from Srei Santhor and Phnom Penh through Longvek to Oudong, c. 1430-1620.
  5. Assess the role of officials, provincial notables, monasteries, and villages in sustaining Cambodian society through dynastic change.
  6. Compare the goals of Cambodia, Ayutthaya, and the Nguyễn lords, and explain why Siamese and Vietnamese influence was neither constant nor uncontested.
  7. Evaluate the evidence for Cambodia's participation in the Asian maritime trading world, including Japanese, Chinese, Malay, Portuguese, and Dutch connections.
  8. Interpret Ramadhipati I's conversion to Islam as a political, commercial, and religious event, without exaggerating its effects on the wider population.
  9. Explain the causes and consequences of the Dutch-Cambodian conflict of 1643-1644 and the Nguyễn intervention of 1658.
  10. Distinguish among contemporary inscriptions, retrospective royal chronicles, foreign court histories, and modern scholarly reconstruction.
  11. Evaluate competing interpretations of post-Angkorian Cambodia as a declining kingdom or a transforming early modern state.

Historical Context

The Khmer kingdom of Angkor, examined in Chapters 33 through 39, organized a large agrarian, temple-centered polity in the Tonle Sap basin between roughly the ninth and fourteenth centuries. Chapter 61 reassessed older accounts of a sudden "fall of Angkor," emphasizing a combination of warfare, ecological and infrastructural change, religious transformation, and shifting trade. Several of those processes are essential background here. Theravada Buddhism had gained ground across mainland Southeast Asia (Chapter 53), shifting royal patronage away from the monumental Hindu and Mahayana programs of earlier centuries. Ayutthaya, founded in the fourteenth century (Chapter 57), had become a powerful rival on the Chao Phraya, and Khmer-Siamese conflict, including Siamese attacks on Angkor in the fifteenth century, formed part of the background to the court's southward move.

Equally important, the trading world of Southeast Asia was expanding. From the fifteenth century onward, Chinese maritime commerce, the growth of Melaka and its successors (Chapter 64), the arrival of the Portuguese (Chapter 68), and later the Dutch VOC (Chapter 77) increased demand for tropical forest products, aromatic woods, hides, and rice. A kingdom with access to the Mekong and the Tonle Sap could supply these goods. The relocation of the Khmer court toward the river confluence near modern Phnom Penh, and downstream toward the sea, therefore put the monarchy in closer touch with this commerce than an inland capital at Angkor could have been.

On the eastern side, Vietnamese state history was changing. The Lê dynasty gave way to a divided Vietnam in which the Trịnh controlled the north and the Nguyễn ruled the south (Chapter 87). From the early seventeenth century the Nguyễn needed trade, labor, and agricultural land, and Vietnamese settlers moved gradually toward the Mekong delta (Chapter 88). That process, which Chapter 88 treats in detail, is considered here only for what it meant to Cambodian kings: a new potential ally, a new potential threat, and eventually a competitor for the delta's ports and revenues.

Students should bear in mind that the Khmer monarchy was not a bureaucratic machine. Authority depended on personal relationships among kings, royal relatives, ministers, provincial leaders, and monastic and village communities. A ruler's reach varied with his military strength, his revenue, and his success at distributing titles and offices. These features explain both the resilience of Khmer political life, which could continue through changes of ruler, and its vulnerability to factional rivalry, which could continue even when no foreign army was present.

Section 89.1

Cambodia after Angkor: Political Continuity and Regional Transformation

Chapter 61 explains why historians no longer picture a single catastrophe at Angkor. Here it is enough to recall the outcome: during the fifteenth century the principal residences of Khmer kings moved southward. Cambodian chronicles, written down in their surviving forms centuries later, associate this relocation with the reign of Ponhea Yat and with a sack of Angkor by Ayutthaya traditionally dated to the 1430s. Modern scholars treat both the date and the attribution of a single cause with caution. Warfare mattered, but so did trade, the maintenance of Angkor's water system, religious preference, and the attraction of the river confluence. The chronicles record a court that settled, at various times, at Basan, Srei Santhor, and the area of Phnom Penh, before sixteenth-century kings consolidated at Longvek.

The relocation of a court is not the abandonment of a place. Angkor Wat, in particular, continued as a Buddhist temple, and the wider Angkor region retained settlements, monasteries, and pilgrimage activity. Inscriptions and later visitors' graffiti show that the monument was used and remembered. Seventeenth-century Japanese visitors left writing there, and, as Section 89.19 shows, a Khmer inscription of 1747 records religious activity at the site. Students should therefore distinguish three things: the principal royal residence, which moved; the temple landscape, which remained a religious center; and the symbolic memory of Angkorian kingship, which later kings invoked.

Continuity also marked institutions. Khmer remained the language of court and religion, with Pali used in monastic learning. Kings continued to seek legitimacy through patronage of monasteries, ceremonial authority, and the claim to rule as a righteous Buddhist king. Provincial leaders continued to supply labor, goods, and soldiers. Agriculture and fisheries, which had always supported the Khmer population, remained productive in the floodplain. The fifteenth and sixteenth centuries were thus a period of reorganization in which many inherited practices were adapted to new capitals, rather than a break in which Khmer political life began again.

What changed was the orientation of the court. A royal center on the river corridor was closer to the Mekong and the sea, closer to the commerce examined in Section 89.3, and also closer to the corridors by which Siamese and, later, Vietnamese forces could arrive. The same location that gave Cambodian kings revenue and links to foreign merchants also exposed them. This tension between opportunity and vulnerability shaped the entire period examined by this chapter.

The change of capital must also be understood through the relationships between royal households and the local populations that provided their food, boats, porters, and armed followers. A succession of residences could distribute demands among districts or concentrate them near commercial stations. Court movement therefore changed who benefited from supplying the ruler and who bore the costs. A riverine center facilitated the collection of forest products from interior channels, but it could not replace the seasonal agricultural cycle. Floodplain households remained fundamental to state finance, whether a king lived in the Angkor region or nearer Phnom Penh. This helps explain why continuity at village level was not simply a passive survival from an earlier era: local production and local leadership enabled ambitious rulers to construct new courts.

The evidence is unusually vulnerable to retrospective storytelling. Accounts of the fifteenth-century move often give one named king, one attack, and a single decision to abandon Angkor. Archaeological evidence instead measures settlement activity and changes in the built environment over longer spans, while inscriptions reveal devotional uses that were invisible to the chronicles' focus on royal courts. Neither category can answer every question. Archaeology cannot automatically identify the political allegiance of all residents; a chronicle's royal sequence may preserve remembered claims rather than a contemporary diary. The chapter therefore treats political relocation, urban development, and religious continuity as related but analytically distinct processes. That distinction makes the history after Angkor intelligible without treating the long interval as a void.

Section 89.2

Ang Chan I and the Development of Longvek

Ang Chan I, who came to the throne around 1516 according to the chronicle tradition, is associated with the elevation of Longvek to a major royal center. Cambodian chronicles place the foundation of Longvek in the 1520s, and they present the move as an act of royal planning. Archaeological research complicates this tidy account. Surveys and excavations by Martin Polkinghorne and colleagues have revealed a large settlement with defensive earthworks and evidence of occupation extending earlier than the chronicle date. A reasonable inference is that Ang Chan I and his successors transformed and fortified a place that already had significance, making it a capital, rather than founding a city from nothing.

Longvek lay on the Tonle Sap River north of the Phnom Penh confluence, in a position that gave access to river transport, floodplain rice, fisheries, and the lake. The earthworks, which survey work has traced, show organized labor and a concern with defense. Foreign visitors later described a capital with a palace, monasteries, and a population that included merchants from several regions. Royal patronage of Buddhist monasteries linked the court to the Sangha, while the court's administrative needs drew officials, artisans, and traders to the city. Longvek was therefore simultaneously a fortress, a ritual center, and a market.

The sixteenth-century kingdom was not secure. Chronicles and Thai sources report attacks from Ayutthaya and, in the mid-sixteenth century, the shadow of the Toungoo wars (Chapter 84), which drew Siamese rulers' attention in several directions. The details of sixteenth-century campaigns differ among Khmer, Thai, and foreign accounts, and students should treat reported expeditions as claims requiring comparison. What is clear is that Longvek's kings maintained their court, fortified their capital, and survived repeated military pressure for decades. Ang Chan I's successors, including Barom Reachea I (r. c. 1566-c. 1576) and Satha I (r. c. 1576-c. 1594), continued to rule from the city, and Satha I actively pursued diplomatic contacts that extended beyond mainland neighbors.

Historical Vocabulary
Longvek and srok

Definition. Longvek is the name of the fortified royal and commercial center on the Tonle Sap River that served as the principal Khmer capital for much of the sixteenth century. Srok is a Khmer term for a district or territory, ordinarily a locality of villages under a local official, and it is often used in older texts for the administrative divisions of the kingdom.

Historical context. A "royal center" in this period was not a capital in the modern sense of a permanent seat of a bureaucratic government. It was a location where the king, the court, key officials, and the principal ritual institutions resided, and which a ruler might change when political, military, or economic conditions required. Territory was organized through relationships with local leaders in the sroks, not through a uniform provincial administration.

Why it matters. Khmer courts moved more than once, so a move does not by itself indicate weakness. Longvek's earthworks and trade evidence show how an early modern Khmer capital worked.

Potential misunderstanding. Students should not assume that the chronicle date for Longvek's foundation marks the beginning of occupation, nor that "capital" implies fixed borders or centralized administration.

The remains at Longvek invite a closer inquiry into how early modern rulers mobilized resources. Earthworks required organized labor, maintenance, access to timber and water, and coordination with surrounding communities. Defensive construction also shaped the social landscape: markets and monasteries could benefit from proximity to a court, while military obligations might burden the villages that supplied materials and labor. A king did not need modern-style territorial administration to concentrate such resources. Royal households, ministers, and district leaders could negotiate obligations and distribute privileges through personal networks. The extent of the fortified complex is thus evidence for institutional capacity, but not proof of uninterrupted royal control over every outlying district.

Imported ceramics at Longvek indicate connections with several Asian production centers, although an imported vessel by itself cannot establish who owned it, who brought it there, or how much profit the court received. Archaeologists examine the distribution and dates of finds alongside settlement structures. Written descriptions by foreign visitors raise a different problem: traders noticed warehouses, tolls, access to the king, and provisions, but their accounts often omit the labor and local exchange sustaining those activities. Reading material and documentary sources together produces a fuller picture of a commercial kingdom. Longvek mattered because it concentrated political and ritual authority within an already active transport landscape—not because commerce abruptly appeared when a particular ruler proclaimed a capital.

Section 89.3

The Geography and Commercial Economy of the Khmer Heartland

The Khmer heartland is organized by a distinctive hydrological system. The Tonle Sap, a large lake in the western part of the Cambodian plain, is linked to the Mekong by the Tonle Sap River. During the monsoon rains, the Mekong's flood rises, and the Tonle Sap River reverses its direction, carrying water into the lake and greatly expanding its area. In the dry season the flow reverses again, and the lake drains toward the Mekong. This annual cycle supports floodplain rice agriculture, extensive fisheries, and seasonal movement of people and boats. It also offered a transport network on which kings depended to move goods and soldiers.

The confluence of the Tonle Sap River with the Mekong, near modern Phnom Penh, gave that area a strategic importance that Khmer rulers recognized. Boats could move downstream along the Mekong toward the delta and the South China Sea, upstream toward the middle Mekong and the Lao kingdoms (Chapter 90), or northwest toward the lake. Srei Santhor, on the Mekong to the east of the confluence, and Longvek and later Oudong on or near the river to the north, all took advantage of this corridor. A king who held the confluence could collect tolls and supplies, receive foreign ships, and respond to threats from several directions.

Commerce was integral to this setting. Chinese junks, Japanese vessels, Malay and other Southeast Asian traders, Portuguese intermediaries, and, in the seventeenth century, Dutch ships called at Cambodian ports or traveled upriver. The interior supplied deer hides, lacquer, gum benzoin and other resins, beeswax, ivory, and sappanwood, along with rice and fish products. Ceramics excavated at Longvek and at other sites include Chinese and other Asian wares, evidence that the court and its merchants were participants in an interregional exchange system, not merely observers of it. Trade revenue, whether in goods, fees, or gifts, supported kings and their officials and also made the court dependent on the commercial decisions of others.

Geography Study

  • The Mekong River from the Lao frontier through the Phnom Penh confluence to the delta
  • The sites of Angkor, Srei Santhor, Phnom Penh, Longvek, and Oudong in relation to the river corridor

Geography Analysis Questions:

  1. How did the seasonal flow of the Tonle Sap River shape agriculture, fisheries, and transport?
  2. What advantages did the Phnom Penh confluence offer a ruler, and what vulnerabilities did it create?
  3. Why might the same river corridor that enriched the court also allow outside armies to reach it?
  4. How does the location of the delta relative to the Khmer court help explain later competition over ports and customs revenue?

The annual change in water levels imposed its own calendar on households and institutions. A river route navigable in one season could be hazardous or shallow in another; fisheries depended on predictable ecological cycles, while rice-growing households synchronized planting and harvesting with flooding. The court's demands for transport and supplies therefore interacted with environmental timing. A military expedition could not be planned merely by drawing the shortest line between two cities. Its boats, provisions, and draft labor had to be gathered from places facing their own seasonal needs. The productive environment was a political resource only when officials and local communities could organize access to it.

It is also important not to conflate the Tonle Sap–Mekong corridor with the entire kingdom. Upland communities supplied forest goods through chains of exchange that could include many intermediaries. Coastal and river markets were connected, but their routes and the authorities able to tax them changed over time. Merchant records tend to register highly valued exports rather than the much larger daily movement of food and small goods. This difference affects historical interpretation: a surge in recorded deer-hide exports does not measure the whole economy, nor does a decline in a European company's trade imply that Cambodian production had collapsed. The kingdom's commercial geography was layered, and no single port controlled every stage of exchange.

Section 89.4

The Fall of Longvek in 1594 and Its Historical Consequences

By the 1590s Satha I's court faced a new and more dangerous Ayutthaya. Naresuan, who had restored Siamese independence from Toungoo Burma (Chapter 86), commanded a resurgent army and had reasons to secure Siam's eastern frontier. Satha I, for his part, sought outside support. Portuguese and Spanish adventurers, notably Blas Ruiz and Diogo Veloso, appeared at the court and in correspondence with Spanish authorities in Manila, and Satha I seems to have hoped that European arms or alliances might counter Siamese pressure. These connections show the Khmer court acting diplomatically beyond the mainland, though no substantial European force arrived in time.

Thai and Cambodian sources agree that Naresuan's forces besieged and took Longvek, conventionally in 1594, though the chronology of the campaign differs among accounts. Satha I withdrew to Lan Xang. Siamese forces carried away captives, and Siamese chronicles treat the capture as a major royal achievement. Cambodian tradition recalls the loss in different terms, and legends associated with sacred figures named Preah Ko and Preah Keo link the loss of the capital to the removal of sacred objects or beings. Versions vary, and students should treat these narratives as cultural memory shaping how later Cambodians understood the event rather than as evidence for the siege's course.

Older accounts treated 1594 as the end of Khmer political and commercial vitality. The evidence now suggests a more nuanced conclusion. Research by Nhim Sotheavin and Martin Polkinghorne indicates that Longvek remained occupied and commercially active after the invasion. The fall ended its role as the main royal residence, but not its life as a settlement. Meanwhile, Khmer claimants continued to contest the throne, sometimes with Siamese backing and sometimes against it, and Spanish and Portuguese adventurers continued to intervene in the following years. Siamese influence was real but not uniform, and it had to contend with Cambodian resistance, rival claimants, and the movement of populations.

Division II — Oudong, Royal Institutions, and Cambodian Society (c. 1594–1650)
Section 89.5

Srei Santhor, Dynastic Recovery, and the Search for Political Stability

The decades after 1594 were politically unsettled. Satha I's flight and subsequent death left rival claimants, relatives of the former king, ministers, and provincial commanders competing for authority. Siamese forces and their supporters installed or backed some claimants, while others drew on Khmer military followers, Cham and Malay soldiers, and adventurers. In the mid-1590s and after, Spanish and Portuguese adventurers also took part in attempts to install a favored prince. Contemporary and later accounts differ about the order and details of these struggles, and students should avoid reading them as a clean sequence of reigns.

One consequence was that royal residences moved. Srei Santhor, on the Mekong, served as a principal seat for several rulers in the early seventeenth century, along with other temporary residences in the river corridor. The movement of courts was not merely flight. It could be a way of securing supply, relocating near loyal supporters, or removing a court from a threatened position. A king without a secure capital could still hold power if he held the support of key officials and military followers.

A second consequence was that ordinary social life continued despite political crisis. Villages planted rice and fished, monasteries kept their communities, and local markets operated even as princes disputed the throne. Historians sometimes underestimate this distinction between the court and the country. The chronicles, which concentrate on royal events, make it easy to equate dynastic disorder with general collapse. Dutch and other foreign accounts, by contrast, describe ports and river trade continuing under changing rulers. The recovery of the monarchy under Chey Chettha II drew on this underlying continuity.

A major difficulty in reconstructing these years is that a ruler's reign can be recorded differently according to which source considers him legitimate. An official claiming the throne at one residence might possess ritual regalia and supporters while another claimant controlled more soldiers or revenue elsewhere. Later compilers often arranged overlapping claims into a neat sequence, giving the appearance that one government wholly replaced another. European adventurers, meanwhile, described events through their own efforts to secure privileges, appointments, or protection. The result is not a lack of history; it is a requirement to examine several kinds of political authority instead of assuming that the title “king” always referred to a monopoly of power.

The foreign adventurers active after Longvek's fall offer an instructive case. Their correspondence preserves negotiations and military ambitions but is not a neutral chronicle of Cambodian motives. A prince who employed such men could seek weapons, maritime connections, or a small force for a succession struggle, without wishing to surrender control of the kingdom. Conversely, adventurers could overstate promises to attract sponsors abroad. The persistence of boats, markets, religious foundations, and district leaders through this disorder shows that the social fabric of Cambodia cannot be equated with the stability of its throne. That background is essential for understanding why a relatively successful restoration under Chey Chettha II was possible.

Section 89.6

Chey Chettha II and the Establishment of Oudong

Chey Chettha II came to power around 1618 and ruled until about 1628. His reign stands out because it combined military confidence, a new capital, and a significant diplomatic innovation. Chronicles report that early in his reign Cambodian forces achieved successes against Siamese forces, and that he sought to reduce Siamese claims on the Khmer court. Since the main narrative comes from retrospective Cambodian chronicles and Thai accounts that describe the same period differently, students should treat these reports as evidence of a Khmer ruler resisting Ayutthaya, not as a precisely documented military history.

Around 1620 Chey Chettha II established Oudong as his principal royal center. Oudong lay near Longvek, on the river corridor, in a region with rice lands, access to the lake and river, and defensible ground. The choice reflected both continuity and strategy. Staying near Longvek kept the court close to the agricultural and fishing base that had sustained earlier kings, while a new location allowed a fresh political start after the disorder of the preceding decades. Oudong remained the main capital, with interruptions, for much of the next two and a half centuries.

Students should use caution in relating the present-day site to the seventeenth-century court. Many of the monuments visible at Oudong today belong to later periods, particularly the nineteenth century, and archaeological research warns against assuming that surviving structures reproduce the original royal complex. The seventeenth-century capital is better reconstructed from chronicles, foreign visitors' descriptions, and archaeological work than from the visible monuments alone.

A marriage between Chey Chettha II and a daughter of the Nguyễn lord is reported in Cambodian chronicle traditions, but its dating and independent corroboration are disputed; the reported alliance has nonetheless played a major role in later explanations of Khmer–Nguyễn relations. That alliance, discussed in Section 89.10, gave the king a partner to the east at a time when Ayutthaya's pressure was the principal concern. It also established a precedent for Nguyễn involvement in Cambodian affairs, which later kings and claimants would use for their own purposes.

A capital in the Oudong area could draw on nearby agricultural and river networks while retaining access to Longvek's established commercial landscape. The sites were close enough that a transfer of the principal court did not necessarily entail the abandonment of previous installations or communities. Students should therefore distinguish a named royal residence from a wider urban and settlement zone. When chroniclers say that a king “founded” a city, they may be describing the construction of a palace or the ceremonial concentration of a court rather than the first occupation of the place. This distinction is especially important where buildings were made of materials that have not survived.

Chey Chettha II's foreign policy also needs to be seen as a set of decisions under uncertainty rather than as a prelude to inevitable loss in the delta. Cambodian chronicles remember victories against Siam and an alliance associated with a Nguyễn royal marriage, but the chronology of the marriage and the claims surrounding it differ among versions. As Michael Vickery emphasizes, accounts later used to explain Cambodian territorial losses cannot simply be treated as transcripts of early seventeenth-century agreements. Even a genuine marriage would not, on its own, prove that a king transferred territory. It could establish kinship, increase diplomatic options, or open a channel for trading privileges. The consequences depended on later political decisions and on settlement patterns that the king could not fully control.

Section 89.7

Khmer Kingship, Court Officials, and Provincial Administration

Post-Angkorian Khmer kingship drew on older ideas but operated under different conditions. A king was expected to be a protector of Buddhism, a patron of monasteries, and a source of order. Royal legitimacy rested on descent from earlier kings, on ritual and ceremonial performance, and on the support of leading officials and the Sangha. Succession did not follow a single fixed rule. Brothers, sons, nephews, and other royal relatives could all be credible candidates, and the choice often depended on which candidate secured backing from ministers, provincial leaders, and military followers.

The senior officials of the court, among them the holders of the title oknha, administered justice, led soldiers, collected revenue in kind and labor, and managed relations with foreign merchants. Provincial leaders, often local notables with their own followings, controlled the sroks, supplied the court, and could withhold support. A king therefore governed through bargaining and patronage as much as through command. Granting titles, arranging marriages, and distributing offices tied officials to the throne, but those same ties could also shift to a rival prince who offered better rewards.

The distinction between ideals and practice is essential. Texts and ceremonies present the king as the center of a hierarchical order. In practice, how much revenue, labor, and military force a king could obtain depended on the loyalty of intermediaries, and on his ability to prevent powerful officials from supporting rival claimants. When a court was divided, the same officials who maintained order in ordinary times could become the instruments of civil conflict. This helps explain why disputes within the royal house could repeatedly produce wider instability, and why claimants looked outside for resources unavailable at home.

Historical Vocabulary
Oknha, royal patronage, and tributary authority

Definition. Oknha was a high Khmer court title conferred by the king on ministers and senior officials, and in this period it designated men with administrative, judicial, or military responsibilities. Royal patronage refers to the king's distribution of titles, land or revenue rights, and gifts to secure loyalty, and to his support of monasteries to demonstrate piety. Tributary authority describes relationships in which a weaker ruler acknowledged a stronger one by sending gifts or missions, often in return for recognition or protection.

Historical context. These terms describe relationships rather than fixed offices. Titles could be created, granted, and withdrawn, and tribute relations varied in meaning: Cambodian kings might send missions to Ayutthaya to avoid attack, or to the Nguyễn to secure an ally, without conceding permanent rule.

Why it matters. Such vocabulary shows that political authority rested on personal ties and negotiated recognition, which explains both the flexibility and fragility of Khmer kingship.

Potential misunderstanding. "Tribute" should not be equated with colonial subordination, and "oknha" should not be read as an equivalent of a modern cabinet minister.

Royal service joined household life to wider administration. A title might recognize an official's military success or signify his claim to revenue from a district, but the exact resources associated with an office cannot be assumed to have remained identical across centuries. Senior men often depended on relatives, household dependants, and lesser officials to act in their name. When a royal claimant obtained power, he had to decide whom to confirm, whom to remove, and how to reward allies without alienating others. This made the distribution of honors a political mechanism rather than a purely ceremonial matter. Religious giving could reinforce these relationships: a donation to a monastery advertised status and created a lasting public association between its patron and a sacred institution.

The 1747 inscription examined in §89.19 permits an unusually concrete view of this system. Its unnamed narrator advances through several titles, commands men against opponents, and receives offers of provincial responsibility, while women in his extended family help restore his fortunes and participate in the patronage commemorated at Angkor Wat. The source does not provide a complete organizational chart, but it shows why an account of institutions must include kinship, military following, revenue, and Buddhist merit together. A court office was not simply a job in a modern ministry; it was embedded in relationships whose political importance could change rapidly when kings and royal claimants contested authority.

Section 89.8

Buddhist Institutions, Villages, Households, and Everyday Life

Most Khmer people in this period lived in villages and made a living from rice, fishing, and the products of forest and field. Because chronicles and foreign accounts concentrate on courts and ports, the daily life of cultivators must be reconstructed from scattered evidence: later ethnographic knowledge used cautiously, archaeology, visitors' remarks, and royal and monastic records. What can be said with reasonable confidence is that the rhythm of the flood and the dry season organized agricultural and fishing work, that households combined cultivation with fishing and gathering, and that monasteries were central community institutions.

Theravada monasteries, wats, provided religious services, education for boys and men, and a focus for merit-making. Villagers donated food, labor, and goods, and kings and officials gained prestige from sponsoring monasteries, repairing temples, and making offerings. Monks transmitted Pali and Khmer learning and acted as moral authorities. The Sangha therefore connected court and village, which allowed it to endure through changes of dynasty. Religious activity continued at Angkor Wat, which had been adapted to Buddhist use, and at other temples, showing continuity with Angkorian religious geography.

Labor was not uniformly free. Khmer society included dependent laborers, debt-bondsmen, and war captives, and the Khmer term khnhom covered a range of statuses, from hereditary dependants to persons in temporary bondage. Warfare produced captives, including those carried off by Siamese and later Vietnamese forces, and Cambodian rulers also took captives. Evidence about the conditions and numbers of the unfree is thin and varies by source, so students should resist generalizing from a single type. Chapter 91 compares these arrangements across the region.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Fisheries, Monasteries, and Labor

A household on the Tonle Sap floodplain might plant rice as the flood receded, fish as the lake expanded and contracted, and sell dried or fermented fish and surplus rice at river markets. Women commonly held significant economic roles in markets and household production, a pattern remarked on by many foreign observers of Southeast Asian societies, though the evidence for Cambodia in this specific period is limited. Boys might spend time in a monastery, learning to read and acquiring religious knowledge. Household labor obligations to officials, and the possibility of being drawn into royal service or military levies, shaped what families could keep.

Political crisis affected these households unevenly. Those near the capital or on invasion routes might see crops and property seized or be taken as captives. Others, farther from the river corridor, may have experienced a dynastic struggle chiefly as new demands for goods or labor. The chronicles rarely record these experiences, which is a reminder that the perspective of the evidence is overwhelmingly that of the court.

The annual cycle of merit-making connected very different social positions. Some sons entered monastic training temporarily, acquiring literacy and religious status; women played important roles as donors and organizers even where they were not ordained as monks. Beyond rice and fish, households worked in boatbuilding, weaving, pottery, salt-making, and exchange at periodic markets, although evidence for the relative scale of each activity is incomplete. Warfare imposed additional burdens through provisioning, transport, recruitment, and captivity. Later chronicles rarely record what these demands meant to individual families. By asking which sources overlook dependants, women, and smaller villages, students can recover an analytical place for ordinary people without inventing their voices.

Section 89.9

Cambodia and Ayutthaya: Warfare, Tribute, and Diplomatic Competition

After 1594, Ayutthaya remained the most persistent external presence in Cambodian politics. Siamese kings sought to control the Khmer court for several reasons: to secure the eastern frontier, to obtain labor and captives, to gain access to the Mekong corridor and its commerce, and to prevent a hostile power from using Cambodia as a base. These aims were not constant. Siamese attention shifted with campaigns against Burma, Lan Xang, and others, and with the internal politics of the Siamese court, so pressure on Cambodia rose and fell.

Relations took several forms. Raids and expeditions carried off people and goods. Diplomatic missions exchanged gifts and sought recognition. Dynastic ties and the movement of princes and officials between courts created channels of influence: a Khmer prince might live in Siam, or a Siamese-backed claimant might return to contest the throne. Populations also moved, voluntarily and by force, across the frontier. Each of these forms had different implications, and "tribute" could signal anything from genuine subordination to a tactical gesture.

Students should resist a narrative of continuous Siamese domination in the early seventeenth century. Cambodian chronicles report Khmer military successes under Chey Chettha II, and the Khmer court sought alternatives, among them the Nguyễn alliance and commercial ties with Japanese, Dutch, and other merchants. Ayutthaya's resources were considerable, but they were limited by distance, competing priorities, and Khmer resistance. At the same time, Khmer kings could not ignore Siam, and the memory of 1594 made Siamese power a constant factor in their calculations.

Chapter 86 treats Ayutthaya's institutions, trade, and foreign communities. The present perspective differs: it asks what Khmer rulers wanted from Ayutthaya, such as peace, recognition, or restraint, and what they were prepared to offer. A king who sent a mission to Ayutthaya while negotiating with the Nguyễn was practicing a diplomacy of balance, a strategy that worked while the powers remained distant and failed when factions made it impossible to speak with a single voice.

Diplomacy across the western frontier also depended on intermediaries. Princes and nobles who spent time at Ayutthaya could acquire allies, language skills, and knowledge of another court's military practices. On returning to Cambodia, they might serve as brokers or as rivals to a reigning king. Marriage and fosterage created connections that could be used for peace or for intervention; their effects were not determined in advance. A mission carrying gifts to Ayutthaya could acknowledge unequal status in ceremony while pursuing a practical advantage, such as the release of prisoners or a pause in warfare. Sources that describe such missions as submission may record a particular court's preferred interpretation rather than a stable constitutional relationship.

The availability of labor and transport made military intervention costly for both sides. Cambodian river defenses, the seasonal landscape, the difficulty of keeping troops supplied away from home, and Ayutthaya's commitments on other frontiers limited what its kings could achieve. Cambodian rulers could exploit these constraints, bargain for recognition, or seek alternative partners. But those strategies required relative unity at court. When several claimants competed, one could promise support or privileges that another rejected, allowing Siamese involvement to become a feature of the succession struggle itself. Cambodia's security problem was therefore not reducible to an imbalance of military power: it was tied to the monarchy's ability to sustain a consistent policy.

Section 89.10

Cambodia and the Nguyễn Lords: Marriage, Trade, and the Eastern Frontier

The Nguyễn lords of Đàng Trong, who governed southern Vietnam from the late sixteenth century (Chapter 87), had their own reasons to seek a relationship with Cambodia. They needed trade, rice, and labor in a state often at war with the Trịnh in the north, and they were interested in the lands and ports to the south and west of their coastal base. For the Khmer court, the Nguyễn offered a counterweight to Ayutthaya and a source of commercial and military support.

Dynastic marriage formalized the relationship. Cambodian chronicle traditions report that a daughter of Nguyễn Phúc Nguyên married Chey Chettha II, although their dates differ; the woman is often identified in later writing as Ngọc Vạn. The early Nguyễn histories examined by specialists do not independently document the marriage, and the identification and circumstances of the union therefore cannot be treated as confirmed by two matching court traditions. Marriage ties gave each side kin-based claims to consult and, potentially, to intervene, and they offered Khmer princes of mixed descent a potential foreign constituency.

A further tradition holds that around 1623 Chey Chettha II permitted the Nguyễn to establish a trading or customs presence at Prey Nokor, in the delta region later known as Saigon. The date and exact terms are traditional and not documented in surviving contemporary sources. Students should distinguish among three things: commercial permission to trade and collect customs, diplomatic alliance, and permanent territorial transfer. The tradition describes the first and perhaps the second. It does not describe a cession of sovereignty, and interpreting it as one reads the later outcome back into the beginning.

From the Cambodian court's perspective, the choice was rational. A friendly Nguyễn presence in the delta could protect trade, supply allies, and impress Ayutthaya. The danger lay in what such a presence could become. Chapter 88 explains how Vietnamese settlement and administration expanded in the lower Mekong region over the following decades. Here the point is that a decision which served one king's immediate needs created conditions that later rulers and claimants would find difficult to reverse.

The marriage tradition warrants exceptional care because later accounts often make it the beginning of a story of irreversible Vietnamese territorial advance. Cambodian chronicle versions differ over dates and the circumstances in which an alliance was proposed. Michael Vickery's close study of the “1620” narratives shows how later historians combined the marriage with a purported customs arrangement and projected subsequent territorial losses backward onto Chey Chettha II's reign. The early Nguyễn royal chronicles examined in specialist scholarship do not provide the same straightforward attestation of the marriage. A possible dynastic union must therefore be presented as a chronicle tradition with disputed dating and independent corroboration, not as a jointly verified Cambodian–Vietnamese record.

Even if customs access was granted near Prey Nokor, officials and traders faced different questions from those faced by kings. Who collected duties on cargo? Who settled disputes between visiting merchants and resident households? Could settlers obtain land, and which authorities could register it? None of these questions is answered by the bare claim that permission was granted in 1623. A customs outpost could serve trade without transferring sovereignty; increasing settlement could subsequently alter power without an explicit royal cession. Later Cambodian rulers might seek Nguyễn troops against one claimant while opposing the expanding influence of Nguyễn officials in the delta. This changing combination of alliance and competition should shape the chapter's analysis, rather than a simplistic story of an unfortunate marriage.

Section 89.11

Dynastic Factions and the Political Uses of Foreign Alliances

Throughout the seventeenth century Cambodia's ruling house contained several branches. Brothers, half-brothers, sons, and nephews of earlier kings held titles and followings. Since succession was not governed by one rule, each death or deposition created a contest among princes, supported by ministers, provincial commanders, and in-laws. After the death of Chey Chettha II in 1628, the chronicles record a series of short reigns marked by violence within the royal family, a pattern that illustrates the instability of the system.

Foreign partners offered tools in these contests. A claimant who lost the support of the court might seek soldiers from Ayutthaya or the Nguyễn, or merchants and mercenaries from other communities. A king who held the throne might invite outside troops to defeat a rival. This is the logic of what this chapter calls political dependence through intervention: aid given to one claimant could become the basis for later demands, such as payments, titles, trading rights, access to ports, or the installation of favored officials. The ally who helped a prince win the throne could expect a return, and the prince, once crowned, might find himself obligated.

It would be a mistake to label factions permanently pro-Siamese or pro-Vietnamese. Alliances shifted with circumstances. A prince disappointed by Ayutthaya might turn to the Nguyễn, and a prince who owed his throne to Nguyễn troops might later seek Siamese help against his patron's demands. Court officials, too, calculated their interests and moved between camps. Foreign rulers were not uniformly effective either; their intervention depended on distance, season, competing commitments, and the reliability of their Cambodian supporters. Such shifting patterns explain why intervention recurred but did not produce a single stable outcome.

The consequences for the monarchy were double-edged. Foreign support could rescue a king from defeat, but it also weakened the monopoly of legitimate authority on which Khmer kingship depended. Each invitation made it easier for the next claimant to do the same, and each outside army left behind obligations and precedents. The mid-seventeenth-century episodes examined in Division IV show these dynamics at their most intense.

Foreign assistance could solve one immediate problem while creating several new ones. A claimant supplied with troops might take a palace or a river crossing, but the soldiers still needed food, boats, pay, and permission to withdraw. A supporting ruler could then ask for tribute, commercial privileges, recognition of protégés, or influence over appointments. Khmer ministers and provincial notables had their own calculations: backing a powerful outside ally could secure a candidate's victory, but it could also place their revenues or positions at risk. In a politically divided court, the question was not only who would be king but what each faction was willing to exchange to obtain that result.

The mechanism also worked in reverse. Outside rulers depended on Cambodian guides, interpreters, supply arrangements, and allies with knowledge of local waterways. An army invited by one prince could be resisted by another, and an apparently successful expedition might fail to establish durable control after departing. A useful analytical exercise is to follow the same conflict from three viewpoints: the prince requesting aid, the neighboring ruler considering intervention, and the provincial community that must provision both armies. Their goals need not coincide. This comparison restores Cambodian choices to the story while acknowledging the growing leverage of Ayutthaya and the Nguyễn.

Section 89.12

Cambodian Commerce and the Asian Maritime Trading World

Seventeenth-century Cambodia was a participant in Asian commerce, supplying goods that distant markets wanted. Deer hides, in demand in Japan, were among the best-documented exports. Lacquer, gum resins, beeswax, ivory, sappanwood, and other forest products, along with rice, were also traded. In return, Khmer elites and merchants obtained Chinese ceramics and textiles, Indian cloths, metals, and other goods. Archaeological finds at Longvek and other sites, including ceramics from several Asian production centers, give physical evidence of this exchange.

Japanese merchants were prominent. Under the Japanese red-seal system, shogunal authorities licensed overseas voyages in the early seventeenth century, and ships sailed from Nagasaki and other ports to Cambodia, among other destinations. A Japanese community settled near Phnom Penh, and Cambodia's links with Nagasaki were important for the hide trade. This system ended in the 1630s, when Japan restricted overseas travel and foreign trade, removing a significant trading partner and altering the court's revenue prospects. Chinese junks, Malay and other Muslim traders, and Portuguese intermediaries continued, and the Dutch VOC increased its interest in Cambodian goods, particularly hides and rice.

Trade mattered to politics. Kings obtained revenue from fees, gifts, and monopolies, and they used commerce to reward followers and to gain allies. Foreign merchants could also become political actors: some provided advice, soldiers, or ships, and some took sides in succession disputes. Dutch factors, for example, wrote detailed reports on Cambodian court politics because their commercial prospects depended on it. Students should therefore treat trade and politics as interwoven rather than separate spheres.

Commerce also created vulnerability. A court dependent on a small number of exports and partners could be damaged by changes in distant markets and policies, as the end of Japanese red-seal voyages showed. And foreign merchants were not a uniform group: Japanese, Chinese, Malay, Portuguese, and Dutch traders pursued different interests and sometimes conflicted with each other. The tensions among these groups, and between them and the Khmer court, formed the setting for the mid-century crises examined next.

Historical Vocabulary
VOC, trading factory, commercial monopoly, and entrepôt

Definition. The VOC (Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie, or Dutch East India Company) was a chartered Dutch company with commercial and quasi-governmental powers in Asia (Chapter 77). A trading factory was a company or merchant establishment in a foreign port, staffed by agents who bought and sold goods, and not necessarily a manufacturing site. A commercial monopoly is exclusive control of the trade in a commodity, which companies sought through contracts or force. An entrepôt is a port where goods are collected and redistributed.

Historical context. The VOC sought exclusive or privileged access to valuable goods and often sought such terms from local rulers. Cambodian rulers granted or refused privileges depending on their interests, and many other merchants competed with the Dutch.

Why it matters. The Dutch records are an essential source for Cambodian history, and the VOC's difficulties at the Khmer court show the limits of European power in mainland ports.

Potential misunderstanding. A "factory" here was a trading post, and the VOC's power at Cambodia was far less than it exercised in the Indonesian archipelago.

Trade had a pronounced political dimension because many products passed through networks of brokers rather than through directly administered royal warehouses. Upland suppliers might exchange aromatics, hides, or resins for food, cloth, and metal goods before those items reached a river port. Local officials could collect tolls or demand gifts at several points; merchants might seek exemptions in return for loans or assistance to the court. The VOC's attempts to create more predictable privileges therefore interacted with systems of personal negotiation already in use. When a ruler changed, an arrangement negotiated with his predecessor might not survive, even if trade continued by another route or through other intermediaries.

Japanese participation illustrates the changing direction of these exchanges. Before restrictions on Japanese overseas voyages, ships operating under red-seal permissions connected Japanese buyers with Southeast Asian ports. After the restrictions of the 1630s, Dutch and Chinese merchants carried much of the trade to Japan, but demand for Cambodian forest products did not vanish. This matters for interpreting company records: a new distribution of carriers could change who appeared in the written archive without eliminating local suppliers. Archaeological evidence for imported ceramics adds another dimension, showing circulation and consumption over spans that do not match the brief tenure of a particular merchant factor. Commercial Cambodia should be understood as a set of linked inland and maritime networks, not a passive outlet for European companies.

Division IV — Ramadhipati I, Religious Change, and the Dutch Conflict (1642–1660)
Section 89.13

Ramadhipati I: Succession, Conversion to Islam, and Court Realignment

In 1642 a member of the royal house known in chronicles as Ponhea Chan took the throne as Ramadhipati I, after, according to the chronicle tradition, deposing and killing his predecessor. He is also known by the Muslim name Sultan Ibrahim, reflecting his conversion to Islam. The circumstances of his accession are told largely by retrospective Cambodian chronicles, which portray him as a usurper, and students should note that a chronicle compiled by the opponents or successors of a fallen king is not a neutral source.

Ramadhipati's supporters included Malay and Muslim Chinese merchants, along with Cham Muslims and others who had settled in Cambodian river ports. Masayuki Endo's research emphasizes the growth of these Malay and Muslim commercial networks in seventeenth-century Cambodia, and argues that they provide crucial context for the king's conversion and diplomatic policy. According to the accounts, the king also had a Malay wife, and Malay figures held influential positions at his court. His reign thus represents an alignment of the throne with a commercial community whose ties reached through the Malay world and the wider Muslim trading networks of the Indian Ocean (Chapters 62 through 66).

Conversion was not simply a private religious decision. For a Khmer king, adopting Islam altered his relationship to the Buddhist Sangha, which traditionally legitimated kingship, and created tensions with Buddhist officials and monks who might have preferred a different ruler. It also drew the court closer to merchants and soldiers who could supply wealth and force. The chronicles, written from the viewpoint of the Buddhist tradition that survived, condemn the conversion, and later narratives of Muslim persecution of Buddhists should be weighed against this bias. Evidence on the king's personal piety is limited, and historians generally interpret the conversion as inseparable from commercial and political alignments.

Ramadhipati's reign also shows the agency of Cambodian rulers in choosing foreign partners. He cultivated Malay and Muslim connections, sustained trade with Chinese and other merchants, and, as the next section shows, took a hard line toward the Dutch. His decisions made sense given his base of support, but they also increased the hostility of rival Cambodian claimants, who looked to other allies. The court was split not only by dynastic rivalry but by differing commercial and religious orientations.

A Buddhist monk: A king who left the Buddhist path challenged the tradition on which royal legitimacy rested. A royal official: Court officials had to weigh the king's commands, their own standing, and the chance that a rival might replace him. Some might gain from a new coalition, others lose, and many would hedge, since officials' careers depended on the throne and on their local followings.
A provincial notable: A local leader might care less about the king's religion than about whether demands for labor, goods, and soldiers increased, and which claimant offered better terms. Evidence of such views is largely indirect.

The political setting of the conversion was extraordinarily complex. Ramadhipati gained the throne through a violent dynastic contest, and the networks that supported him did not align neatly with religious categories. Malay and Muslim Chinese merchants had cultivated routes through the Gulf of Siam and Mekong hinterland, while Portuguese interests and local Japanese merchants were differently situated in the competitive trading environment. Endo's use of VOC records stresses that the king's conversion occurred amid commercial realignments already under way; it cannot adequately be explained as a sudden response to a single Dutch grievance. The kingdom's ties with inland producers and coastal partners remained central to the resources available to the court.

Nor did the conversion erase Cambodia's Buddhist institutional landscape. Monasteries continued to exist, Khmer religious practices remained diverse, and surviving testimony must be examined before making claims about conversion among ordinary subjects. A reigning king's religious identity could profoundly affect patronage and succession politics without being an accurate proxy for the population's beliefs. Carool Kersten's comparison of Khmer and Dutch evidence shows that different sources attach different meanings to the episode. In a Khmer chronicle, religious change may help explain political disorder; in a VOC report, it may be linked to the company's difficulty obtaining favorable commercial treatment. These are perspectives to analyze, not interchangeable descriptions of one transparent motive.

Section 89.14

The Dutch East India Company, the Crisis of 1643, and the Battle of 1644

The VOC had been interested in Cambodia since the early seventeenth century, attracted by hides, rice, and other goods and by the possibility of a base on the mainland coast. It maintained agents at the Khmer court and sought privileges in competition with Chinese, Japanese, Malay, and Portuguese traders. Alfons van der Kraan's archival study of the episode is the principal research basis for what follows, and it draws chiefly on Dutch company records, so the Dutch perspective is far better documented than the Khmer one.

Relations deteriorated in 1643. Dutch records describe disputes about trading privileges, about a vessel and its cargo, and about treatment of company personnel, in a setting where Malay and Portuguese rivals of the Dutch had influence at court. In 1643 Dutch personnel at the Cambodian court were attacked and killed. Dutch accounts attribute responsibility to the king and to hostile advisers, while the Khmer side is poorly recorded, so students should regard the motives and responsibility as matters of reconstruction from partisan sources.

The VOC responded with a punitive naval expedition. In June 1644 a Dutch force ascended the Mekong toward Phnom Penh, and fighting occurred near the city. Cambodian forces, supported by Malay and other allies, resisted, and the Dutch failed to obtain the settlement they sought, suffering losses and withdrawing. The episode illustrates two points: the company could project force up a mainland river, but it could not easily compel a mainland king in his own waters, and a court with allies and local knowledge could resist. It did not end the Dutch interest in Cambodian trade, but it ended hopes of a privileged position there for a time.

The participants should not be treated as one bloc. Malay, Portuguese, Japanese, and Chinese communities had different interests: the Portuguese and Malays often opposed Dutch competition, while some Chinese and Japanese traders were more concerned with maintaining access than with taking sides. The Khmer court itself was divided, which helps explain why the crisis occurred and why it did not settle the political questions facing the kingdom.

Cause and Effect

The Dutch-Cambodian Conflict of 1643–1644

Long-term causes

Competition among Asian and European merchants for Cambodian goods; the VOC's pursuit of privileged access; the court's dependence on commercial revenue and on multiple trading communities.

Short-term causes

A new king, Ramadhipati I, whose supporters included Malay and Muslim Chinese merchants hostile to Dutch competition; disputes over privileges, a vessel and cargo, and treatment of company agents.

Immediate trigger

The attack on Dutch personnel at the court in 1643, followed by the company's decision to send a naval force.

Immediate effects

Dutch loss of personnel and the failure of the punitive expedition near Phnom Penh in June 1644; demonstration of the limits of company power on the mainland.

Longer-term consequences: Reduced Dutch influence at the Khmer court in the short run, continued Malay and Muslim commercial prominence, and an enhanced reputation for Ramadhipati, though his domestic position remained contested.

Historical complexity: Commercial rivalry, court politics, religious alignment, and misunderstanding all contributed. No single cause explains the violence, and the surviving evidence is overwhelmingly Dutch.

The conflict can be reconstructed in greater detail than many Cambodian wars because Dutch company correspondence survives, although it records the company's interests and grievances. The VOC established a factory near the royal center in 1637 and sought Cambodia's forest products for its East Asian commerce. Relations deteriorated after Ramadhipati's accession in 1642 and amid disputes concerning Portuguese rivals, captured shipping, and the conduct of Dutch representatives. According to van der Kraan's reconstruction, an embassy led by Pieter van Regemortes was attacked in November 1643. The VOC governor-general in Batavia, Anthony van Diemen, authorized a punitive expedition that reached the Cambodian river system in 1644.

The decisive naval encounter took place near Phnom Penh on 12 June 1644. Cambodian forces used prepared obstacles and local knowledge of the waterways to frustrate a Dutch flotilla; the expedition failed to compel the concessions Batavia demanded. The outcome must not be made into a simple story of European defeat by an unchanging traditional army. It involved access to firearms, river engineering, coordination, and the tactical constraints of ships in a confined channel. Nor did the defeat end all Dutch–Cambodian exchange. Endo shows that commercial contact was subsequently revived with Malay mediation because the court and the VOC still needed goods each other could supply. The relationship therefore combined violence, bargaining, and renewed pragmatism.

Section 89.15

Religious Pluralism and Political Authority in Seventeenth-Century Cambodia

Seventeenth-century Cambodia was religiously plural. Theravada Buddhism was the religion of most Khmer people and the monarchy's traditional ideology. Muslim communities, including Malays, Cham, and Chinese, lived in river ports and at court. Catholic missionaries, mostly Portuguese and Spanish, worked among foreign and some local communities. Chinese and Japanese merchants brought their own religious practices. This diversity reflected the commercial openness of Cambodian ports, and it meant that political authority had to be negotiated among communities with different loyalties.

Ramadhipati's conversion put that negotiation under strain. It affected royal legitimacy by challenging the Buddhist basis of kingship, it influenced factions by encouraging some officials and merchants and alienating others, and it shaped foreign relations by strengthening ties to Malay and Muslim networks. The Sangha, which depended on royal and popular patronage, remained a source of authority that no king could ignore, and many Buddhist officials looked to rival claimants.

It would be a mistake to portray Cambodia as becoming an Islamic kingdom comparable to Aceh or Johor (Chapters 80 and 81). The ruler's affiliation, the composition of his coalition, and the beliefs of the wider population are different matters. There is no evidence that the majority of Khmer villagers abandoned Buddhism, and the king's Muslim supporters were concentrated in particular communities. The limits of the evidence matter here: sources describe courts and merchants, not village religious experience, and later chronicles are hostile to the conversion. What can be said is that for a period the throne was associated with Islam and with Muslim commercial networks, and that Buddhist institutions endured and later regained royal patronage.

The most useful analytical distinction is between a ruler's religious policy and the religious life of his subjects. Ramadhipati's conversion affected court alliances and the allocation of patronage, but an account of Cambodia's Buddhist institutions cannot be replaced by a biography of the king. Monastic centers maintained their own networks of learning and donation, and many villagers continued to organize important events around wats. At the same time, Malay and Cham Muslims brought experience of trade, seafaring, and religious scholarship to river settlements. Catholic missionaries and European merchants interacted with these communities through competing commercial and political interests. Their presence does not imply that religious practice was unregulated or that all groups had equal standing.

Evidence of Buddhist patronage during the reign also complicates the picture. Ian Harris discusses a Cambodian inscription associated with Ramadhipati which appears to record donations to a Buddhist establishment despite his conversion. Such evidence should be read cautiously, since inscriptional identification and chronology require specialist interpretation, but it challenges a totalizing narrative of religious replacement. The historical question is how rulers managed multiple religious constituencies and how court crises changed which networks enjoyed access or protection. Acknowledging pluralism does not require denying episodes of violence, hostility, or restriction; it means analyzing them alongside coexistence and practical cooperation.

Section 89.16

The Fall of Ramadhipati I and the Intervention of 1658

By the 1650s Ramadhipati's position had weakened. Buddhist officials and members of the royal family who opposed him, as well as Khmer princes who had been excluded from power, formed a hostile coalition. Chronicle accounts attribute his troubles to his religious policy and to conflict with royal relatives, though these accounts come from the winning side, and the underlying dynamic was the familiar one of rival claimants, with divided officials and foreign allies at their disposal.

Dissident princes turned to the Nguyễn. According to the accounts, they appealed to the Nguyễn lord for help, and in 1658 a Nguyễn military force entered Cambodia, captured Ramadhipati, and removed him from the throne. He was taken to Vietnam, and the chronicles report his death there shortly after. The Nguyễn intervention was therefore not simply an invasion detached from domestic politics: Cambodian claimants helped create the circumstances in which foreign forces became involved, and the Nguyễn acted partly on their invitation and partly in their own interests.

The consequences were significant. The new ruler owed his position to Nguyễn support, and the Khmer court acknowledged Nguyễn influence, which brought obligations such as respect for Vietnamese trading and settlement interests. The position of the Muslim communities, closely tied to the fallen king, became more precarious, though many remained in Cambodia and the trade connections continued. Perhaps most important, the episode established a precedent: a Vietnamese army could determine a Cambodian succession, and a Cambodian claimant could secure the throne by seeking that army's aid.

The mid-seventeenth century therefore marked a change in Cambodia's ability to manage competing outside powers. Earlier kings had balanced Ayutthaya against the Nguyễn, and commercial partners against each other. After 1658, the choice was increasingly between patrons, each of whom had armies, claimants, and interests of their own, and the court's freedom to act without them narrowed. This was not inevitable, and Cambodian rulers continued to maneuver, but the conditions in which they acted had changed.

The 1658 intervention makes the reciprocal risks of foreign alliance visible. Rival Cambodian princes sought help against Ramadhipati, and Nguyễn forces became involved in the struggle. The intervention removed the king from power, but contemporary and later accounts differ about subsequent negotiations, military withdrawal, and the disposition of the captive ruler. Mak Phoeun and Po Dharma's documentary study of the 1658–1659 campaign follows Cambodian, Vietnamese, and other traditions and shows why a simple statement that the Nguyễn invaded and installed a permanent puppet is misleading. Cambodian chronicles place Ramadhipati's death after his release in 1659, while other records transmit conflicting details.

A victory in one succession contest did not resolve the kingdom's underlying political difficulties. The claimants who benefited from foreign troops had to contend with their own rivals and with the reputational costs of calling in an outside army. Nguyễn commanders, for their part, had other military commitments and could not freely occupy every Cambodian district. Local officials and communities might shift allegiance as forces moved; the court's legitimacy depended on more than the outcome of one battle. This episode is best read as a precedent for recurring intervention, not as the moment Cambodia permanently lost all independent authority. It helps explain how later claimants learned that outside aid was available, even as the price and risks of that aid grew.

Division V — Dynastic Competition, External Pressure, and the Eighteenth-Century Kingdom (c. 1660–1750)
Section 89.17

Rival Claimants and the Increasing Role of Siamese and Nguyễn Armies

The second half of the seventeenth century saw repeated succession conflicts. Rather than cataloguing unfamiliar names, it is more useful to identify the mechanisms that repeatedly produced intervention. First, the royal house contained multiple lineages with claims to the throne. Second, ministers and provincial leaders controlled the resources needed to rule, and could shift their support. Third, claimants who lost at court had the option of seeking allies abroad, and both Ayutthaya and the Nguyễn had reasons to accept. Fourth, once a foreign army entered, it influenced who held office, which revenues went where, and who controlled river routes.

By the 1670s the chronicles describe situations in which rival kings or claimants were associated with different external patrons: one based at the traditional capital region and receiving Siamese attention, another connected to the Prey Nokor region under Nguyễn protection. The details of reign dates and alliances vary among sources, so this chapter does not attempt a definitive list. What the pattern shows is that, by this period, the court could be divided along lines of external alliance, and that outside forces could maintain a claimant against his rivals for years.

The consequences for Cambodian governance were practical. Court appointments might reflect an ally's preferences, revenues were diverted to support foreign troops or pay obligations, local autonomy increased when no king commanded general loyalty, and control of important river routes shifted with each campaign. Foreign armies could also devastate agricultural areas and carry off captives, burdening villages that had little voice in the dispute. These costs fell most heavily on those least able to influence the outcome.

At the same time, Khmer agency continued. Kings and claimants negotiated with outside powers, sometimes playing them against each other, and officials and provincial leaders decided whom to support. Foreign rulers, for their part, had limited resources and competing concerns, and their interventions were often short and inconclusive. The system was one of mutual manipulation in which the balance of advantage shifted gradually, not suddenly, away from the Khmer court.

A sequence of royal names alone does little to explain why competition persisted. The relevant political units included extended households, provincial followings, court offices, and military networks that could survive a monarch's death. A succession settlement might redistribute titles without eliminating the defeated line's relatives or supporters. When another dispute arose, these networks could be activated again. The circulation of princes between Cambodia and Ayutthaya likewise created a pool of alternative rulers who possessed some legitimacy but needed military backing to assert it. This situation made temporary victory easier than durable settlement.

The historian's task is to recover the changing coalitions behind each claim rather than assign a permanent national label to the factions. A prince who at one moment sought Nguyễn aid might later negotiate with Siamese officials, while a provincial leader could change sides to protect access to land and revenue. Court chronicles frequently explain such changes through loyalty and betrayal, moral categories that were important to the authors but do not exhaust the political explanation. Asking which appointments, resources, routes, and family ties were at stake reveals why the same kingdom could be contested repeatedly. Yet it also reveals resilience: the contenders fought to control Khmer kingship and its institutions, not to abolish them.

Section 89.18

The Lower Mekong, Commercial Access, and the Constraints on Cambodian Sovereignty

The most consequential long-term change in this period occurred in the lower Mekong and Đồng Nai regions. Chapter 88 examines Nguyễn settlement and administration in detail. For the Cambodian court, the importance lay in what that process did to access, revenue, and authority. As Vietnamese settlers and officials expanded in the delta, the ports and river mouths through which Cambodia reached the sea came increasingly under Nguyễn influence, and the court's ability to collect customs and to receive foreign ships there declined.

Chinese settlers and merchants played a major role. In the late seventeenth century, groups of Chinese migrants, including followers of the fallen Ming cause who arrived around 1679 with Nguyễn permission, settled in places such as Biên Hòa and Mỹ Tho, and Chinese-led communities developed commercial centers on the coast. The Mạc family at Hà Tiên, on the Gulf of Siam, built a Chinese-led port polity that moved between Cambodian and Nguyễn spheres, and is traditionally described as acknowledging the Nguyễn in 1708. These communities were commercial intermediaries with their own interests, and they affected the flow of goods, taxes, and people that had formerly passed through Khmer hands.

In 1698 the Nguyễn sent an official, traditionally named Nguyễn Hữu Cảnh, to organize administrative districts around Gia Định. This mission represented a formal assertion of Nguyễn administration in an area where Khmer rulers and communities had previously exercised influence. It is important to distinguish the effective administration of particular settlements from complete sovereignty over the entire delta: Khmer populations remained in many places, and political control was layered and contested. Yet the long-term direction was clear, and for the Cambodian court it could mean diminished influence over particular commercial outlets and revenues, without ending all delta exchange.

The fiscal effect should not be underestimated. A court whose influence over particular delta customs revenues was reduced could have fewer resources to reward officials and soldiers, which weakened its capacity to resist both internal rivals and external pressure. Combined with the dynastic conflicts of Section 89.17, this change helps explain why the Khmer court became more dependent on foreign patrons. The loss was structural and gradual, and it was not predetermined, but it constrained every later ruler.

The lower Mekong was economically important because control over a particular landing place or customs point could affect revenue even when no government monopolized the whole river system. Officials at the Cambodian court relied on commercial income alongside agrarian resources, but much trade moved through intermediaries and channels difficult to supervise directly. Nguyễn settlement and the creation of administrative offices near Gia Định altered that balance in selected areas. Khmer-speaking communities remained, and river trade continued under more than one jurisdiction. It is more accurate to describe a loss of leverage over some routes and customs stations than a categorical loss of Cambodia's access to the sea.

The consequences were not solely fiscal. Communities faced competing claims over land, tolls, and legal disputes, while merchants might find advantages in one jurisdiction and costs in another. Local Cambodian rulers could seek accommodation, oppose particular appointments, or continue trading across political boundaries. Such responses complicate the familiar picture of a clean boundary moving westward with each Nguyễn decision. The weakening of control over strategic outlets mattered because a court with fewer reliable resources could struggle to reward allies or support military operations; it did not mean that every Cambodian community became economically dependent in the same way. The argument must remain proportional to the surviving evidence and should not treat a modern map as a contemporary administrative survey.

Section 89.19

Cambodian Politics, Buddhist Patronage, and Dynastic Conflict, c. 1700–1750

The early eighteenth century continued these patterns. The chronicles record competing reigns associated with figures such as Ang Em, Satha II, and Thammaracha (Thommo Reachea) III and IV, with kings deposed, restored, and challenged by relatives and by armies from Siam and from the Nguyễn domain. The 1730s and 1740s were marked by especially severe struggles. Rather than exhaustively reconstructing disputed reign dates, this section emphasizes what the period shows: the continued importance of royal marriages in creating alliances, the continuing influence of Siamese and Nguyễn courts, and the persistence of Khmer officials and Buddhist patronage as stabilizing elements.

Buddhist patronage remained a central instrument of royal and elite legitimacy. Kings and officials sponsored monasteries, repaired temples, and made offerings, and religious activity at Angkor Wat continued. These actions linked political figures to the Sangha and to the memory of Angkor, which retained symbolic power. Dated Khmer inscriptions from this period preserve evidence of such activity from the perspective of those who carried it out, which makes them valuable correctives to the retrospective royal chronicles.

Longvek remained part of this political geography. The site was therefore a possible instrument of renewed political authority, not simply a deserted memorial. Such conflicts show how claims to the throne reached beyond palaces into district security, movement of goods, and the mobilization of dependants. Women figure prominently in the same evidence. The official's aunt and female kin help him during a period of hardship, his wife receives an honorific title, and several women participate in a religious ceremony at Angkor Wat. At Angkor Wat in December 1747, the household assembled monks, offered silver and textiles, and commemorated relatives through ritual acts. Such evidence reveals the persistence of a sacred center used for elite patronage even when political capitals shifted. It also shows that Buddhist religious life, court titles, and the struggle over royal legitimacy intersected in concrete personal careers. The inscription cannot tell us the experiences of ordinary cultivators, but it enables historians to ask questions that royal reign lists cannot answer.

Section 89.20

Cambodia between Siam and Vietnam: Historical Significance and Interpretation

By about 1750 Cambodia's political position had become more constrained than it had been two centuries earlier. The reasons were cumulative. Dynastic conflict gave outside powers repeated openings. Foreign assistance created obligations. Vietnamese and Chinese settlement in the lower Mekong reduced the court's access to ports and revenue. And the very commercial success that had enriched Longvek and Oudong exposed the court to rivals and to disruptions it could not control. None of these processes alone determined the outcome, and each depended on choices by Cambodian rulers, officials, and claimants as well as by Siamese and Vietnamese ones.

Yet the period cannot be reduced to constraint. Khmer institutions endured: kingship, the Sangha, village agriculture and fisheries, and the language and ritual traditions that connected the court to Angkorian memory. Rulers like Chey Chettha II and Ramadhipati I pursued independent diplomacy and commercial policy, and the Khmer court resisted the Dutch in 1644. Officials, monks, merchants, and provincial leaders sustained society across dynastic change. The kingdom's difficulties in the eighteenth century were the outcome of particular pressures, and later developments, which belong to Volume VI and beyond, were not foreordained.

Historical Debate

Was Post-Angkorian Cambodia a Declining Kingdom or a Transforming Early Modern State?

The question: Should the history of Cambodia from the fifteenth to the mid-eighteenth century be understood primarily as decline from an Angkorian peak, or as transformation and adaptation within a changed regional environment?

Interpretation A — decline: Older scholarship, drawing heavily on Cambodian chronicles and on the later history of Siamese and Vietnamese pressure, emphasized the loss of Angkor's scale and power, the repeated succession crises, and the shrinking of Khmer control in the delta. On this view, the later difficulties reflect a long-term weakening of the monarchy.
Interpretation B — transformation: More recent work, including archaeological research at Longvek and studies of seventeenth-century commerce, stresses the vitality of Khmer capitals, the court's integration in Asian trade, the continuity of Buddhist institutions, and the capacity of rulers to adapt. On this view, the kingdom changed its form and orientation rather than simply declining, and its difficulties arose from specific political and economic pressures.

Evidence: Both interpretations use the same body of evidence, including chronicles, foreign accounts, inscriptions, ceramics, and excavation data, but weigh them differently. Chronicles emphasize royal conflict, while archaeology and foreign commercial records emphasize urban and commercial activity.

Points of disagreement: Disagreement concerns interpretation and the weight of evidence more than basic facts. Scholars debate how to measure the scale of the kingdom against Angkor, how far the end of Longvek's royal role altered the court's strength, and how much the loss of delta access mattered in the long run.

Why it matters: If the kingdom is understood as simply declining, its later history looks inevitable; if understood as transforming, the role of contingent decisions, factional rivalry, and external pressure becomes visible, and Cambodians appear as historical actors.

Student task: Using the evidence in this chapter, identify which kinds of source support each interpretation. What additional evidence would help decide between them? Consider whether the two interpretations are fully incompatible.

This chapter's conclusion is therefore double. Cambodia after Angkor was a functioning early modern kingdom with significant commercial and religious life, and its autonomy was gradually constrained by the interaction of internal dynastic competition and the pressures of Ayutthaya and the Nguyễn. Chapter 90 turns to the Lao kingdoms, Chapter 92 synthesizes the commercial world of which Cambodia was part, and Chapter 93 opens the eighteenth-century transformations that followed.

The language of “decline” has remained influential partly because subsequent Cambodian history included real episodes of diminished royal independence and territorial loss. Yet applying the outcome of later centuries to the preceding two hundred years conceals changing possibilities. The fortified development of Longvek, the establishment of Oudong, the commercial policies of Ramadhipati I, and the organized river defenses of 1644 each demonstrate capacities that must be accounted for. A political system can be commercially dynamic and vulnerable to succession conflict at the same time. It can preserve Buddhist and administrative institutions while changing its capital, patronage networks, and relations with neighboring states.

Two comparisons help clarify the conclusion. First, the Trịnh and Nguyễn courts discussed in Chapters 87–88 also depended on manpower, political brokerage, and commercial resources; Cambodian challenges were not evidence of a uniquely Khmer failure to create modern institutions. Second, the royal courts of Ayutthaya and Cambodia alike employed foreign merchants and negotiated changing forms of allegiance. The critical difference lay in the resources and coalitions each court could mobilize in particular confrontations. To understand Cambodia's position by 1750 is to explain how those differences developed, not to assign a civilizational rank to the countries. The surviving record permits a careful account of contingent pressure and negotiated autonomy while leaving many questions—especially about non-elite experience—open to further research.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the political and commercial history of Cambodia from the fifteenth century to about 1750, showing that the period after Angkor was one of reorganization rather than simple collapse. Khmer courts moved southward to the Tonle Sap-Mekong corridor, to Srei Santhor, the Phnom Penh area, Longvek, and later Oudong, while Angkor remained a religious center. Continuity in language, kingship, Buddhism, and agriculture accompanied change in the location and orientation of power.

Longvek, associated with Ang Chan I, emerged as a fortified royal and commercial center. Chronicles place its foundation in the 1520s, but archaeology suggests earlier occupation and shows a city integrated in Asian trade. Its geography on the river corridor offered access to rice, fisheries, and markets, and also exposed it to attack. The conventional date of its fall to Naresuan, 1594, ended its long sixteenth-century predominance as the main royal residence, but neither its occupation nor every later episode of royal use.

Longvek also returned briefly to royal use in 1732 under Satha II. The court recovered. After a period of contested succession and temporary residences, Chey Chettha II established Oudong around 1620, resisted Siamese pressure, and is associated in Cambodian chronicle traditions with a disputed marriage alliance involving the Nguyễn ruling family. Kingship rested on descent, Buddhist patronage, and relationships with officials such as the oknha and with provincial notables, and it operated through bargaining rather than uniform administration. Monasteries and villages sustained everyday life across dynastic change, though the evidence for ordinary people is limited and the unfree formed a significant part of society.

Cambodian commerce linked the kingdom to Japanese, Chinese, Malay, Portuguese, and Dutch merchants. Deer hides, forest products, and rice were exported, and ceramics and other goods came in. Trade supported kings and also tied their fortunes to distant markets, as the end of Japanese red-seal voyages demonstrated. Merchants were political actors as well, advising rulers and taking sides in disputes.

The mid-seventeenth century was a turning point. Ramadhipati I came to the throne in 1642, converted to Islam, and relied on Malay and Muslim Chinese networks. His reign included the violent conflict with the VOC in 1643-1644, in which the Dutch failed to compel a settlement. The episode showed the limits of European power on the mainland and the capacity of a Khmer court with allies to resist.

In 1658 the Nguyễn, invited by rival Cambodian claimants, removed Ramadhipati. The intervention illustrated the logic of political dependence through intervention: help given to one claimant could become the basis for later demands. After 1658 the court increasingly faced a choice between patrons, and the freedom to balance rivals narrowed. Rival claimants in the later seventeenth century repeatedly sought Siamese or Nguyễn armies, with costs for court appointments, revenue, local autonomy, and control of river routes.

Meanwhile, Vietnamese and Chinese settlement in the lower Mekong, the Mạc port polity at Hà Tiên, and the Nguyễn administrative mission of 1698 reduced the Khmer court's access to ports and customs revenue. This structural change, combined with dynastic conflict, left Cambodian rulers more dependent on foreign patrons. The early eighteenth century continued the pattern of contested reigns, yet Buddhist patronage and Khmer officials remained stabilizing forces, as the 1747 inscription at Angkor Wat illustrates.

The chapter's historical argument is that Cambodia's constrained position by 1750 resulted from the interaction of internal and external factors, none of which was inevitable. The Historical Debate showed that interpretations of decline and of transformation draw on different kinds of evidence, and the chapter has favored a view that recognizes both the real constraints and the continued agency of Khmer rulers, officials, monks, merchants, and communities. The sources, chronicles, inscriptions, foreign records, and archaeology, must be read in combination, with attention to whose perspective each represents.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it corrects a common image of Cambodia as a kingdom that passively declined between larger neighbors. The evidence of Longvek's archaeology, Cambodian commerce, Buddhist institutions, and diplomatic choices shows a society actively adapting to a changed landscape, with rulers, officials, monks, merchants, and villagers shaping events. At the same time, the chapter takes seriously the real constraints that accumulated, and shows how they arose from specific mechanisms rather than from fate.

The chapter also reveals larger regional processes. Cambodia's experience illustrates how mainland courts engaged in the early modern commercial world examined in Chapter 92, how succession politics and external intervention could interact in Chapters 84 through 90 as well, and how the expansion of the Nguyễn frontier in Chapter 88 reshaped neighboring states. The Dutch conflict of 1643-1644 connects Cambodia to the VOC's wider history in Chapter 77, while Ramadhipati's reign connects it to the Muslim networks of Chapters 62 through 66.

Several continuities carried beyond 1750: the importance of the Sangha, the centrality of floodplain agriculture and fisheries, the memory of Angkor, and the habit of seeking balance among powerful neighbors. So did vulnerabilities, including factional rivalry and exposure along the Mekong. How these played out in the later eighteenth century belongs to Volume VI, beginning with Chapter 93, and the chapter has deliberately stopped before narrating that history.

Finally, the chapter models a method. Chronicles, inscriptions, foreign records, and archaeology each tell part of the story, and none can be accepted without asking who produced it and why. Students who learn to separate event, tradition, and inference will be better equipped to read the many contested histories that follow in this textbook.
