Brunei, Sulu, Mindanao, and the Northern Maritime Networks

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Between the northern coast of Borneo and the coasts of Mindanao lies a sea of islands, channels, and river mouths in which fourteenth- and fifteenth-century rulers, seafarers, merchants, and religious teachers built new political relationships. Their Muslim sultanates were not one empire spreading outward from a single center, but several connected histories that unfolded at different speeds, on different evidence, and within societies that kept a great deal of local autonomy.

In the autumn of 1408, a ruler whom Ming Chinese records call the king of Boni died in the imperial capital at Nanjing. He had crossed the South China Sea with a large company that included his family and officials, and the Yongle emperor's court responded with ceremony, a funeral, and the formal recognition of his heir. Nine years later, in 1417, three rulers of a place the Ming court called Sulu came to China together with a very large retinue. One of them, who is recorded as the eastern king, died at Dezhou in Shandong on the journey home and was buried there with imperial honors. A tomb associated with him can still be visited. These events are among the best-dated happenings in the early history of the northern maritime world, and they are recorded in the archives of a distant empire rather than by the communities involved.

That imbalance is the central evidentiary problem of this chapter. Brunei on the northwest coast of Borneo, the Sulu Archipelago stretching between Borneo and Mindanao, and the coastal and river societies of Mindanao were not marginal places in the fifteenth century. They produced commodities that were valued in China and in the wider Muslim trading world, and their rulers were capable of dispatching embassies across the sea. Yet the sources that tell their stories in detail were mostly written down much later, as genealogies, royal chronicles, and colonial-era compilations. This chapter therefore asks two questions together: what happened, and how do historians know?

The chapter's governing question concerns political creativity. How did local rulers, maritime communities, merchants, and religious specialists across northern Borneo, the Sulu Archipelago, and Mindanao create new political and commercial relationships during the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, and how did those relationships contribute to the emergence of Muslim sultanates? The answer requires three distinct accounts. Brunei emerged as an important Bornean maritime kingdom around a river-mouth capital. Sulu developed from several coastal and island political centers into an increasingly connected Muslim sultanate. The maritime and riverine societies of Mindanao followed a partly different chronology, with their best-known sultanates taking shape later. These were connected developments, not the simultaneous expansion of a single Islamic empire.

Students should expect a chapter that is deliberately cautious about dates. Many of the names most familiar from later tradition, including Awang Alak Betatar, Sharif Abu Bakr, and Sharif Kabungsuwan, come to us through genealogies compiled long after the events they describe, and some of the claims in those traditions contain internal chronological difficulties. Others, such as the Chinese embassies, are contemporary but externally recorded. Part of the chapter's purpose is to show how historians weigh these kinds of evidence against one another without discarding either.

The chapter also depends on its neighbors. Chapter 62 explained how Islam was carried through the Indian Ocean trading world, Chapter 63 examined the early Muslim states of Sumatra, Chapter 64 treated the sultanate of Melaka, and Chapter 65 examined religious change across the Malay-Indonesian world. Chapter 66 turns to the northern arc of the maritime world and asks how political institutions took shape there. The sixteenth-century consequences, including Portuguese and Spanish intervention, belong to Volume V, particularly Chapters 69, 72, 73, and 76. They appear here only as a limited horizon.

Finally, the chapter insists on the agency of the societies it studies. It would be a mistake to describe Brunei, Sulu, and Mindanao as peripheral recipients of Islam and kingship from Melaka or other established centers. Teachers, merchants, and royal families certainly moved through these networks, and traditions often attribute authority to outside arrivals. But those traditions describe arrivals being absorbed into existing systems of kinship, leadership, and commerce, and the documentary record shows local rulers conducting their own diplomacy. The northern maritime order was assembled from local materials.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Identify the kinds of evidence, Chinese records, archaeology, genealogies, and later accounts, that allow historians to reconstruct Brunei, Sulu, and Mindanao before 1500, and explain why each region is documented unevenly.
  2. Describe the geography of Brunei Bay, the Sulu Sea, and Mindanao's coasts and river systems, and explain how winds, islands, and anchorages shaped exchange.
  3. Distinguish documented events, such as the Ming embassies of 1408, 1417, and 1420, from traditional dynastic narratives recorded much later.
  4. Explain why Chinese references to Boni cannot be automatically equated with a later royal genealogy of Brunei.
  5. Trace the emergence of Brunei as a maritime kingdom and analyze the institutions through which royal authority was constructed.
  6. Analyze traditions of royal conversion and religious teaching in Brunei and Sulu as sources for political memory, not as secure chronology.
  7. Interpret the 1417 embassy as evidence for political plurality in the Sulu Archipelago before the conventional sultanate chronology.
  8. Compare Mindanao's coastal and riverine political communities with Brunei and Sulu, and explain why its sultanate formation followed a partly different chronology.
  9. Evaluate competing historical explanations for the emergence of Muslim maritime states, without imposing one explanation on all three regions.
  10. Distinguish the adoption of Islam by rulers and elites from the conversion of whole populations, and direct rule from influence, tribute, alliance, and autonomy.
  11. Assess why the northern maritime world around 1500 should not be described as already fully Islamized or politically consolidated.

Historical Context

The societies of this chapter belong to the Austronesian-speaking maritime world traced in Chapter 14. Over many centuries, island and coastal communities from Borneo to the southern Philippines developed skills in sailing, fishing, shell and pearl gathering, forest collection, and short- and long-distance exchange. By the early second millennium CE, these communities were already linked to the wider commercial systems described in Chapters 18 and 28, in which Chinese ceramics, textiles, and metalware moved south while forest and sea products moved north and west. Camphor, aromatic woods, beeswax, pearls, tortoiseshell, and other goods from the northern Bornean and Philippine zones were part of that flow.

The fourteenth and fifteenth centuries were a period of reconfiguration across the maritime world. Chapter 50 examined Majapahit's claims in the archipelago. Chapter 62 traced the Muslim merchants and scholars who linked Southeast Asian ports to the Indian Ocean, and Chapters 63 and 64 treated Pasai and Melaka. The Ming dynasty, founded in 1368, pursued an active diplomatic policy in the southern seas, sending envoys and receiving embassies, and the great expeditions of the early fifteenth century reinforced a system in which recognition by the Ming court could be useful to maritime rulers. This increased the volume of surviving written evidence about places such as Boni and Sulu at precisely the moment when their political institutions were changing.

The chapter's geographic scope is the northern arc of this world: the northwest coast of Borneo around Brunei Bay, the island chain from Palawan through the Sulu Archipelago, and the coasts and river systems of western and southern Mindanao. Modern borders cut across this arc. Brunei is now a separate sovereign state, northern Borneo is divided among Malaysia and Brunei, and the Sulu Archipelago and Mindanao belong to the Philippines. None of these borders existed in the period studied. Terms such as "Moro," which Spanish writers later applied to Muslim populations of the southern Philippines, are also anachronistic for the fifteenth century and are used here only when discussing later sources, as Chapter 76 explains.

Division I · Section 66.1

Reconstructing the Northern Maritime Networks

Historians reconstructing Brunei, Sulu, and Mindanao before 1500 work with four broad kinds of evidence, each with characteristic strengths and distortions. The first is the Chinese documentary record. Dynastic histories, the compilations known as the Ming shilu (veritable records), and geographical works such as Zhao Rukuo's Zhufanzhi of about 1225 and Wang Dayuan's Daoyi zhilüe of the mid-fourteenth century describe places, products, embassies, and rulers. These sources are often close in time to the events they record, and they offer dates that local traditions seldom provide. But they were composed by officials who were interested in tribute, ceremony, and commodities. Their transcriptions of names and places are variable, and their geography can be vague.

The second kind of evidence is archaeological. Excavated settlements, burials, imported ceramics, beads, and metalwork reveal patterns of trade and habitation that no text describes. The third is the corpus of local genealogies and chronicles, including Brunei's royal genealogies and the Sulu and Maguindanao genealogies known by the term tarsila or salasilah. These express how ruling families and their communities remembered their origins. The fourth is later European and colonial writing, beginning with Antonio Pigafetta's account of Brunei in 1521 and continuing with Spanish, British, and American compilations. Such writing can preserve valuable information, but it reflects the observers' political aims and often arrives after the period in question.

The unevenness of these sources produces uneven histories. For Brunei, Chinese records are relatively frequent and a royal genealogical tradition was elaborated and eventually inscribed, notably on a genealogical stone of 1807 and in textual versions. For Sulu, Ming records document an embassy in 1417 in detail, while the tradition of the sultanate's founding was written down centuries later. For Mindanao, Song and Ming records mention polities such as Butuan and Kumalalang, but the genealogical traditions of the Maguindanao were published in a form recognizable to modern readers only in 1905. A historian cannot simply splice these different kinds of material into one smooth narrative.

Three principles guide the chapter. First, a documented event is not necessarily a more important one than an undocumented event. It is simply better dated. Second, traditions must be interrogated rather than either believed or dismissed. A genealogy that places a figure at an impossible date may still preserve a real memory of alliance, descent, or a legitimating link with a distant center of learning. Third, absence of evidence from a given period does not demonstrate absence of political life. The northern maritime world had rulers, trade, and religious practice long before the sources that describe them in detail.

Source criticism is therefore not a preliminary step to be completed before the history begins. It is part of the history itself, because the way traditions were assembled and transmitted tells us what later generations needed from their past. In Brunei, it shaped a succession that legitimized the later sultanate. In Sulu, it connected Tausug and other communities with religious teachers and Sumatran or Arab lineages. In Mindanao, it defined kinship between Muslim rulers and neighboring peoples. The chapter returns to each case in turn.

Historical Source and Method Study

Four Kinds of Evidence, Four Kinds of Question

Consider how each source type answers different questions. A Ming shilu entry can tell a historian that a ruler was received, what he presented, and how the court responded, but not how his subjects understood his authority. A scatter of ceramics can show that Chinese goods reached a settlement over several centuries, but not who controlled the port. A genealogy can show how a family explained its authority, but not necessarily when the events it describes occurred. A sixteenth-century European description can offer a rich eyewitness portrait of a port, but cannot confirm conditions in 1400.

The method used in this chapter is triangulation: compare sources of different kinds, note where they agree, and refuse to treat agreement among later retellings as independent confirmation. Two genealogies that copy a shared earlier text do not provide two witnesses.

Questions for students:

  1. Which kinds of evidence in this section are contemporary with the events they describe, and which are retrospective?
  2. Why might a genealogy be "reliable" about kinship politics but "unreliable" about dates?
  3. What question could archaeology answer that a Chinese record could not?
Division I · Section 66.2

Borneo, the Sulu Sea, and the Geography of Northern Maritime Exchange

The northern maritime world is organized around a sequence of seas, channels, and coasts. Brunei Bay lies on the northwest coast of Borneo, where a sheltered bay receives several rivers and offers anchorages protected from open-sea swells. The Brunei River, which flows into the bay, provided access to inland communities and to the products of Borneo's forests. To the northeast, the coast continues toward the northern tip of Borneo and the islands that lead into the Sulu Sea. To the east lies Palawan, a long island whose southern end approaches the Balabac Strait, one of the channels connecting Borneo and the Philippine archipelago.

From there the chain of the Sulu Archipelago extends toward Mindanao. Tawi-Tawi, near Borneo's northeastern approaches, is followed by Jolo and its neighboring islands, then Basilan, which lies close to the Zamboanga peninsula of western Mindanao. The Sulu Sea lies to the north of this chain, the Celebes Sea to the south. These are not barriers. For sailing communities, the many islands function as stepping stones, providing anchorage, water, shelter, and markets. Mindanao is not only an island coast but a river world. The Pulangi, a major river of central Mindanao and a principal feeder of the system that reaches the sea near Cotabato, and its tributary valleys connect the interior with coastal landing places on Illana Bay and the Moro Gulf.

Wind and season structured movement. The northeast monsoon, blowing roughly from late autumn into early spring, and the southwest monsoon, blowing in the middle of the year, shape the timing of voyages across the South China Sea and through the Sulu zone, as Chapter 4 explained. Ships bound for China could take advantage of seasonal winds, and ports where merchants waited for a change of wind could become sites of long residence, repair, and marriage. Local conditions, including tidal currents in channels, reefs, and the rhythm of fishing seasons, further shaped routes. Historians should avoid claiming specific travel times, since conditions varied by season, vessel, and cargo.

Geography created opportunities, but it did not determine outcomes. A sheltered river mouth could become a capital if a ruler gathered followers, secured access to forest products, and attracted visiting merchants, and the same landscape could support many small settlements without a dominant center. The coastal waters of the Sulu zone, rich in fish, shells, pearls, and sea products, supported mobile communities that lived substantially on and from the sea, as well as land-based agricultural communities on larger islands and river valleys. These ecological differences help explain why political forms varied across the region. They do not by themselves explain why particular ones emerged.

Division I · Section 66.3

Boni, Brunei, and the Chinese Diplomatic Record

Chinese sources use several names that scholars have connected, with varying confidence, to the northwest coast of Borneo. The names Poli, Poni, and Boni appear in texts from the tenth century onward. A Song-dynasty or earlier reference to a place with a similar name does not automatically describe the same polity as the one that later received Ming envoys. Johannes L. Kurz's study of Chinese references to Boni demonstrates that identifying every early reference with modern Brunei is historically problematic. Chinese writers used names for several places, copied one another's descriptions, and relocated polities in their geographical imagination.

By the early Ming period the evidence is firmer. Ming records report that in 1370 the court sent an envoy to Boni, and in 1371 a Boni ruler sent tribute. Contact continued, and in 1408 the ruler recorded as Manarejiananai traveled to China with his wife, siblings, children, officials, and a large retinue. He was received at Nanjing, and Ming sources report that he fell ill and died there in the autumn of that year. The court arranged funeral rites and burial, and the Ming records state that his heir was recognized as king and sent home with Chinese support. A tomb in Nanjing, with a stone-lined approach of Chinese style, is traditionally identified as his and is today treated as a heritage site.

What does this evidence show? It documents that a polity called Boni, located on the northwest coast of Borneo and probably centered on or near Brunei Bay, was able to send a ruler across the sea, that the Ming court treated it as a kingdom, and that Chinese recognition shaped succession in 1408. It also reveals scale: the ruler traveled with a large company, which implies the resources to mount an expedition and a degree of political organization. These are not trivial inferences.

But the evidence does not show everything that later tradition might want. It does not establish that Manarejiananai occupied a particular place in the Brunei royal genealogy. Later Brunei tradition lists rulers under Malay and Muslim names and dates, and attempts to match the Chinese ruler with a figure on that list rest on inference. Some scholars have noticed phonetic parallels between the name of the Boni ruler of 1371 and the name Muhammad Shah, but a Chinese transcription cannot by itself show conversion to Islam. The Chinese-style tomb in Nanjing cannot show a ruler's religion either, since funeral arrangements were made by his hosts. The 1408 ruler should therefore not be inserted into the later genealogy as an independently verified sultan.

Ming recognition itself requires careful interpretation. Chinese investiture conferred prestige and sometimes trade advantages, and a ruler who obtained it gained a weapon in local competition. It did not make the recipient a Chinese subject in the modern sense, and it does not demonstrate control of the territory the Ming court imagined. Boni's embassy is best read as an act of diplomacy by a local ruler pursuing his own interests within a recognized ritual framework.

Primary Source Window I

The Ming Record of the Boni Embassy, 1408

Creator: Ming court historians and compilers of the Ming shilu, working from palace archives · Date and provenance: record of events of 1408, compiled and edited in later reigns; modern translations include selections by Geoff Wade in Southeast Asia in the Ming Shi-lu · Language: Classical Chinese · Type: Official dynastic diary-style record

Context: The Yongle emperor (r. 1402–1424) pursued an active diplomacy of recognition in the southern seas. Embassies were recorded as tribute missions, and the court noted the arrival, gifts, titles, and (when relevant) the funerals of visiting rulers.

Editorial paraphrase (not a verbatim excerpt):

The record states that the king of Boni, accompanied by his family and officials, came to the court, was received with honors, and presented tribute. During his stay he fell ill and died. The emperor ordered mourning rites, a burial, and a funerary memorial, and confirmed the king's son as his successor.

Interpretation: The entry shows that Boni could organize a large overseas embassy and that Chinese recognition of a successor was part of the ritual of the occasion. It is also evidence for the Ming court's interest in presenting itself as the center of a hierarchy of kings.

Limitations: The text does not say how Boni's own subjects understood the ruler's authority, whether he was Muslim, or where in the Brunei genealogy he belongs. Its name forms are Chinese transcriptions of a name whose original form is uncertain.

Perspective: The source is an imperial court's record. The viewpoints of Boni's courtiers, merchants, and communities are missing.

  3. What purposes might the Ming court have had in recording such events?
  4. What can historians learn about Boni's political capacity from the record?

Division I · Section 66.4

Archaeology, Early Settlements, and Material Evidence of Maritime Exchange

Archaeology offers a counterweight to texts, because it records the traces of settlement and trade independently of rulers' ceremonies. On the northwest coast of Borneo, Santubong, near the mouth of the Sarawak River, preserves early trading and industrial remains. Recent excavations at Sungai Jaong identify iron-smelting furnaces dating approximately from the mid-eleventh through the late twelfth or thirteenth century; associated metallurgical activity extends from about the late tenth into the thirteenth century. Imported ceramics also attest exchange. These discoveries establish coastal production and trade before the Ming period, not a direct political succession from Santubong to the later Brunei sultanate.

Within Brunei itself, Kota Batu, a site on the Brunei River, has been central to the archaeological record. Excavations beginning in the mid-twentieth century recovered large quantities of Chinese ceramics, from the Song and Yuan periods onward, together with glass beads, other imported items, and local pottery. A concentration of burials, imported goods, and settlement debris suggests a place of sustained habitation and trade. The ceramics are especially useful, because kiln origins and styles can be roughly dated and compared across sites. A long sequence of imported ceramics indicates that goods were arriving over many generations.

What archaeological distributions can and cannot reveal needs to be stated carefully. Imported ceramics demonstrate that exchange occurred, but they do not identify who controlled it. A scatter of Chinese bowls might reflect elite consumption, ordinary household use, burial offerings, or later reuse. Likewise, the presence of an object associated with a religion does not establish the religion of every person in the settlement. Dating by style is imprecise, and many early excavations used methods that modern archaeologists would regard as coarse. Political boundaries cannot be read directly from a map of finds.

The 1264 tombstone associated with Master Pu illustrates the problem. A Chinese inscription records the death of a man bearing the surname Pu, and the stone is now associated with Brunei. The surname Pu has been linked with Muslim merchant families in southern China, especially at Quanzhou, and the stone has sometimes been used to argue for an early Muslim presence in Brunei. But a surname does not prove religion, and scholars have noted that the stone's discovery does not securely demonstrate that its occupant was Muslim or that the stone was originally erected in Brunei, as opposed to having been carried there at some later point. It is therefore best treated as a source-criticism exercise, not as proof of a Chinese Muslim resident of Brunei in 1264.

Taken together, the archaeological evidence from Santubong, Kota Batu, and related sites suggests that northwest Borneo was integrated into maritime trade by the early second millennium and that Brunei Bay was a significant site of habitation and exchange before the Ming embassies. Evidence for the Sulu Archipelago and Mindanao is thinner in published form, though finds such as the Butuan boats and gold objects show that coastal Mindanao also participated in earlier networks. These patterns provide the settings in which political institutions later developed, without by themselves narrating them.

Reading a Ceramic Scatter and a Tombstone

Imagine two finds from a Brunei Bay settlement: a layer of Song-period trade ceramics, and a Chinese-inscribed tombstone dated 1264. What can each support?

The ceramics support a claim about trade contact over a span of time. They do not name the merchants, the rulers, or the religion of the buyers. The tombstone supports a claim that a Chinese-literate community or individual was connected with the place, or that the stone moved there. Whether the occupant was Muslim, a resident of Brunei, or a temporary visitor cannot be established from the stone alone.

Division II · Section 66.5

The Emergence of Brunei, c. 1200–1500

The history of Brunei between about 1200 and 1500 is the history of a river-mouth settlement becoming a political center. The archaeological record suggests habitation and trade around Brunei Bay by the early second millennium. Chinese references to Boni from the Ming period onward show that by 1370 a ruler there could be treated as a king. Between these markers lies a period for which sources are sparse, and the chronology of settlement and political consolidation must be inferred.

The most plausible reconstruction emphasizes gradual concentration. Coastal and riverine communities with fishing, sago, rice, and forest-collecting economies were already linked to one another by water. A settlement positioned where a major river met a sheltered bay could act as a point of exchange between upriver producers of forest products, such as camphor and aromatic woods, and coastal and overseas merchants. Chinese ceramics and other imported goods entered the same networks. A ruler who controlled access to the river mouth and offered security to traders could build revenue and followers.

External relationships changed over time. The Javanese poem Nagarakretagama, composed in 1365 under Majapahit, names Buruneng among places connected with the Javanese court. Such a list is a literary claim about the extent of Majapahit's renown and influence, and it does not demonstrate that Majapahit administered Brunei. It does show that Brunei was known to Javanese poets and, presumably, to Javanese traders by the mid-fourteenth century. Chinese diplomatic recognition after 1370 offered an additional resource. Later tradition also refers to relationships with other Malay-world centers, as discussed in Section 66.6.

The development of royal institutions is the hardest part of the story to document. Brunei's later court had titled officials, an organized royal household, and ritual specialists, and the sixteenth-century descriptions by European observers and the later tradition describe a hierarchical society. The antiquity of these institutions before 1500 is uncertain. The most defensible statement is that the ruler recognized by Ming China in 1408 presided over a polity capable of organizing overseas missions, and that over the fifteenth century this polity grew in commercial prominence. Whether its rulers were Muslim by 1400 is a separate question, treated next.

Brunei's emergence should not be written as the story of a single heroic founder. Its rise depended on many actors: coastal fishing and boat-building communities, upriver peoples who supplied forest goods, visiting merchants from China, Java, and the Malay world, and the families who competed to dominate the river mouth. The kingdom was a negotiated arrangement among these groups. A reconstruction that begins with a founder tends to hide the people whose labor and trade made the founder's authority possible.

Division II · Section 66.6

Awang Alak Betatar, Muhammad Shah, and the Problem of Early Conversion

Brunei's royal traditions give the first Muslim ruler's name as Awang Alak Betatar, who on conversion took the name Sultan Muhammad Shah. In the best-known version of the story, Awang Alak Betatar was one of a group of brothers who held authority in Brunei, and he converted to Islam on marrying a princess of Johor. Conventional chronologies place his reign in the later fourteenth century, with the dates 1363 to 1402 often cited. These are traditional dates, not dates verified by contemporary documents.

The story has chronological difficulties that Stephen Druce and Nani Suryani Abu Bakar have explained. The Johor sultanate as a political entity belongs to the sixteenth century, after the fall of Melaka in 1511, as Chapter 81 describes. A royal marriage with a princess of Johor in the late fourteenth century is therefore anachronistic in the form in which it is told. Retrospective interpretations have proposed that the place was really Singapura or Melaka itself. None of these suggestions resolves the problem conclusively. The tradition may preserve a genuine memory of Malay-world connections while attaching them to the wrong name or time.

Marriage nonetheless matters as a historical mechanism. In many Southeast Asian societies, royal marriage served to create alliances, to import prestige, and to link a local ruling family with a distant center. A tradition that says a ruler converted through marriage is making a claim about how Islam entered political life: through kinship and alliance, rather than through conquest or mass preaching. That claim fits the evidence from other regions. It also accounts for the fact that the first adopters of Islam in many ports were ruling families, not whole populations.

The tradition also describes a succession that includes Sultan Ahmad, Sharif Ali (often called Sultan Berkat), Sultan Sulaiman, and then Sultan Bolkiah. Sharif Ali, in the tradition an Arab-descended sharif who married into the ruling family, is portrayed as bringing religious learning and organizing the court in an Islamic manner. This too is tradition. The dynastic order in the genealogies may reflect later editing, and the dates assigned to the successive reigns are unsure. Where independent documentation exists, such as the Ming records, it does not map cleanly onto the traditional list.

Students should therefore distinguish three levels. At the first level, it is reasonably secure that Brunei became a Muslim-ruled kingdom, since later and sixteenth-century evidence shows a Muslim court. At the second, it is probable that adoption by the ruling family occurred through relations with Malay-world Muslim courts and merchants, and plausibly in the fifteenth century or earlier. At the third level, the specific names, marriages, and dates of the conventional account remain traditional and disputed. The adoption of Islam by a ruler also did not mean that the whole population converted. Coastal Malay-speaking communities, upriver groups, and visiting merchants stood in different relations to the new court religion.

Historical complexity: The sequence cannot be reduced to one marriage or one ruler. The traditional story compresses a process that probably involved many encounters, and its chronology cannot be taken at face value.

Division II · Section 66.7

Brunei's Rulers, Political Institutions, and Maritime Economy

How was royal authority constructed in Brunei? The ruler stood at the apex of a society organized by rank, kinship, and service. Brunei tradition and later descriptions identify a titled nobility, often called pengiran in later Brunei usage, along with officials with defined responsibilities and a court that managed ceremony, trade, and law. The extent to which this hierarchy existed in 1400 is uncertain, but the later system probably built on earlier forms. A ruler's authority depended on relationships with great families, who commanded followers and resources, and on his ability to distribute rank and wealth.

The ruler's relationship with established coastal populations was fundamental. Brunei's capital, as described by sixteenth-century visitors, was a large settlement with many houses built on piles over the water, a feature associated with the later water village known as Kampong Ayer. Such a settlement depended on boat-building, fishing, and carrying trade. Many of its inhabitants were maritime workers who owed service to rulers and nobles. Upriver communities, including groups later described as Kedayan, Dusun, Murut, and others, supplied forest products and sometimes rice. Their relationship with the court was probably a mixture of exchange, obligation, and negotiation. Later categories should not be read back as fixed identities.

Revenue came from trade. A port ruler could collect fees from visiting ships, provide warehousing and brokerage, and monopolize or tax certain commodities. Brunei's regional products included camphor, a highly valued aromatic resin from the Borneo interior, as well as beeswax, aromatic woods, and other forest items. Chinese ceramics, textiles, and metalwork were among the goods imported. The ruler's ability to offer security and fair dealing was essential to attracting merchants. Merchant settlement, including Chinese, Malay, Javanese, and others, could also contribute to the court's resources and diplomatic reach.

Political marriage tied Brunei's rulers to other courts and families. Alliances with Malay-world dynasties, as described in tradition, offered prestige, and alliances with local families helped bind the elite. The royal court was thus a node in several kinds of network. It was a Bornean river-mouth polity, a Malay-speaking Muslim court (in later centuries), and an outpost of Chinese tributary diplomacy. These identities overlapped.

The relationship between river systems and maritime power deserves emphasis. Brunei's strength lay in connecting inland and overseas exchange. A ruler who commanded the river mouth stood between producers and consumers, and a capital located there could attract specialized labor. The institution of kingship did not require control of vast territory. It required access to the movement of goods and people. This pattern differs from the large-scale rice-based states of the mainland, though Brunei also drew on agricultural production. Students should be wary of equating a port polity with a territorial empire.

Dynastic and Institutional Evidence Study

What Counts as Evidence for Royal Institutions?

Evidence for Brunei's early institutions comes from several kinds of source. Ming records show that a ruler could mount an embassy and that succession was publicly recognized. Royal genealogies, including the genealogical stone of 1807 and textual versions, show how the court later represented its succession. Sixteenth-century European accounts describe court ceremony and a hierarchy of officials. Later Malay-language texts preserve ideas about kingship and title.

Each source type is separated from 1400 by a different gap in time. The institutions described in late sources cannot be assumed to be unchanged from the fifteenth century. The challenge is to distinguish what probably persisted from what was later elaborated.

Division II · Section 66.8

Brunei and the Fifteenth-Century Regional Political Order

In the fifteenth century Brunei operated within a regional order in which Melaka, Java, China, and numerous Bornean and Philippine communities were interconnected. Its relationship with each was different, and historians need to separate several forms of connection: political influence, trade partnership, tribute, and actual territorial control. These are not synonyms.

With Ming China, Brunei's relationship was diplomatic and commercial. The embassies of 1370 to 1408 show that Boni sought and received recognition. After the early fifteenth century, the intensity of Ming diplomacy in the southern seas diminished, and the frequency of recorded Boni missions did not remain at its earlier level. Brunei continued, however, to participate in trade involving Chinese goods, and ceramics continued to arrive.

With Melaka, Brunei's connection was commercial and, in tradition, dynastic. Melaka, as Chapter 64 showed, was the leading entrepôt of the fifteenth century and a center for Muslim merchants and for the diffusion of Malay court culture. Merchants moved between Melaka and northern Borneo, and Brunei's rulers adopted forms of Malay-Muslim kingship that were prestigious in the Melaka-centered world. But Brunei was not a province of Melaka, and the extent of any political subordination is not documented. Relations with Java, by contrast, are visible in the Javanese literary claim of 1365 and in the continuing presence of Javanese traders, but they do not demonstrate durable rule.

Brunei's relationships with other Bornean communities and with the Sulu region were similarly varied. Traditions record marriages and alliances between Brunei's ruling families and those of the Sulu Archipelago, and later accounts treat Brunei as a senior court in some respects. These claims, however, derive from sources written after Brunei's period of expanded sixteenth-century influence. It would be a mistake to project that later sphere of authority backward onto 1400. The late fifteenth- and early sixteenth-century reign of Sultan Bolkiah is a period in which Brunei appears to have enlarged its maritime influence, and European visitors in 1521 found a large and wealthy court. The reign is better understood as a transition toward greater influence than as the culmination of a long-established empire.

A useful way to describe Brunei around 1450 is as a respected river-mouth kingdom with trade links across the South China Sea and the Malay world, a court that cultivated Chinese recognition and adopted Muslim forms of kingship, and a sphere of influence that probably included nearby coasts but was neither fixed nor administratively organized. Such a description avoids both the older tendency to treat Brunei as a minor satellite of larger powers and the later nationalist tendency to describe it as a fifteenth-century empire.

Division III · Section 66.9

The Sulu Archipelago before the Sultanate

The Sulu Archipelago is a chain of several hundred islands, large and small, with Jolo, Tawi-Tawi, and Basilan among the better-known groups. Long before any sultanate, its islands were inhabited by communities that fished, gathered shells and pearls, farmed on the larger islands, traded, and moved between islands for marriage, exchange, and seasonal resources. Later names for these communities, notably Tausug, Sama, Bajau, Yakan, and others, point to real distinctions of language and way of life, but they should not be treated as fixed ethnic categories extending unchanged into the early second millennium.

The Tausug, whose name is usually glossed as "people of the current," are associated with Jolo and nearby islands and later became the dominant group of the Sulu sultanate. The Sama-speaking peoples are spread across a very wide area, from Tawi-Tawi through the Sulu Archipelago and beyond. Some Sama communities lived on land, others in boats and stilt houses by the sea, and the term Bajau is applied in various places to maritime Sama-speaking groups. The Yakan of Basilan and other communities occupied additional niches. Distinctions among these groups were porous. People intermarried, shifted occupations, and adopted one another's practices.

Leadership in this world was local and based on kinship and followers. Later sources use the term datu for a leader who held authority through descent, personal ability, and the support of a following. A datu's power depended on his ability to attract and hold people, including by distributing goods and providing protection. Several datus might coexist in one island or sea zone, sometimes in alliance and sometimes in competition. This helps explain the plural rulership recorded in 1417. The social world was not an empty space waiting for a state. It had rules for leadership and exchange.

Economic life tied the islands to the broader trade. Pearls and mother-of-pearl, tortoiseshell, and sea products were sought by Chinese and other merchants, and the Sulu pearl fishery became famous. Island communities also exchanged fish, salt, and forest goods with larger islands and with Borneo and Mindanao. Chinese ceramics reached the archipelago, and the Chinese record mentions Sulu in sources of the Yuan and early Ming periods. Wang Dayuan's fourteenth-century account is generally read by scholars as including a place called Sulu, though some details are obscure.

Religion before Islam was varied. Evidence for earlier religious practice is thin, and later sources are unreliable as guides to it. Local spirit beliefs, ancestor veneration, and practices connected to the sea and the community's well-being are likely, and Indic influences are visible in parts of the southern Philippines through Butuan and other sites and through loanwords in local languages. The arrival of Islam did not occur in a religious vacuum, and it interacted with existing practices in ways considered in Section 66.10.

Historical Vocabulary
Sama, Bajau, and Tausug

Definition: Sama is the name of a family of related languages and the peoples who speak them across the Sulu and Celebes Seas. Bajau is a term applied, with varying meaning, to maritime Sama-speaking communities and to people elsewhere in the region. Tausug names the people of Jolo and nearby islands and their language.

Historical context: The names come from later sources, including Spanish and British accounts and modern scholarship. The communities concerned changed over time through migration, intermarriage, trade, and political affiliation.

Potential misunderstanding: It is a mistake to equate "Sama" or "Bajau" with "sea nomad" in every case, or to treat Tausug identity as a settled ethnic fact before the sultanate period.

Division III · Section 66.10

Religious Teachers, Royal Marriages, and the Emergence of Muslim Authority

Sulu's later genealogical tradition, known as the tarsila, tells of a sequence of arrivals that brought Islam and Muslim authority. Cesar Adib Majul's analysis of the "Genealogy of Sulu" is a principal study of this material. The tradition is not a single text, and its versions differ. It nonetheless offers a recurring structure: religious teachers arrive first, followed by a royal figure who marries into a local family, and then a sharif who establishes the sultanate.

The first figure is usually Tuan Masha'ika, associated with early Muslim presence. The second is Karim ul-Makhdum, a religious teacher who is said to have arrived around 1380 and to have taught Islam in the Sulu region. A mosque on Simunul island in Tawi-Tawi is traditionally associated with him. Historians should not claim that a mosque erected around 1380 survives unchanged, or that its traditional founding date has been independently established. The Simunul mosque has been rebuilt and renovated, and the connection with Karim ul-Makhdum rests on tradition. The tradition may nonetheless reflect a real memory of early religious teaching in the southern archipelago, since the region's position on trade routes would have exposed it to Muslim merchants and learned men.

The third figure, Raja Baguinda, is described as a prince of Minangkabau or Sumatran origin who arrived with followers and settled at Buansa on Jolo. His arrival is associated with the formation of a local polity. The name Baguinda is related to a Malay-world title for a prince. The fourth figure, Sharif Abu Bakr, is said to have come from a line of sharifs, descendants of the Prophet, and later genealogies connect him with Arab origins and Malay-world places such as Palembang and Johor. He is said to have married Raja Baguinda's daughter, Paramisuli, and to have established a sultanate. The conventional date is around 1450, and it rests substantially on later genealogical tradition.

How should these accounts be read? A first observation is that they emphasize marriage and kinship. In each case, outsiders become legitimate by marrying into local lines, and the offspring combine local and external authority. This is a narrative of integration rather than conquest. A second observation is that the traditions locate Muslim authority in the combination of three things: religious learning, descent from a prestigious lineage, and acceptance by local datus. A sharif's descent gave religious prestige. A local alliance gave political foundation. The structure of these narratives helped later rulers justify their position in a society that valued descent and consensus.

A third observation concerns historical process. The traditions imply that Islam spread among the island communities through teachers and trade over some decades, and that its political consequences followed. It would be a mistake to treat these accounts as a literal chronology of four consecutive events. They may compress several generations of contact into a few named figures, and different communities may have encountered Islam at different times and through different intermediaries, including Brunei, the Malay world, and Mindanao. Conversion of ruling families did not equal conversion of all islanders, and practices of pre-Islamic origin continued within Muslim communities.

Everyday Life and Community Study

How Did Islam Reach a Fishing Community?

Imagine the paths by which Islam could have entered an island community. A merchant from a Malay-world port might have married into a local family and kept his religious practice. A religious teacher might have settled near a market to teach recitation and rules of conduct. A community might have adopted practices connected with burial, food rules, or oaths because they were useful in dealing with Muslim traders. Few such individuals appear in surviving sources, which privilege royal and learned figures.

The tradition's emphasis on teachers like Karim ul-Makhdum suggests that religious learning carried status. But everyday religion likely mixed new and older practices, and the pace of change varied by island and family.

Division III · Section 66.11

The Sulu Rulers' Embassy to Ming China, 1417

In 1417, Ming records report, three rulers of Sulu traveled to China together. The Ming sources record Paduka Batara as an eastern ruler, Maharaja Kolamating as a western ruler, and a third royal figure called Paduka Prabhu (also rendered Prabu). The Chinese wording for the third figure has been variously interpreted, including the misleading label “cave king”; Scott argues that neither the early reading as a ruler’s wife nor a fixed inland jurisdiction is secure. These names are transcriptions, and their spellings differ among scholars. The embassy included a very large company of family members, officials, and attendants, reported in the record to number in the hundreds.

The three rulers were received by the court and given titles, gifts, and, according to the Ming record, recognition as kings. This detail is significant. The court’s reception of several royal figures demonstrates plural political leadership, although Ming classifications cannot prove that the archipelago had three neatly bounded kingdoms or that no senior ruler existed. Eastern and western labels reflect the record’s distinctions; the third figure’s status and territorial base remain uncertain. The joint embassy shows cooperation, but the participants’ precise hierarchy, kinship, and local jurisdictions cannot be reconstructed from it alone.

On the return journey, Paduka Batara fell ill and died at Dezhou in Shandong. The Ming court ordered his burial with honors, including a tomb and a commemorative stele. A tomb at Dezhou is associated with him and remains a site of historical interest, and later Chinese tradition holds that members of his family remained in China as caretakers. The record states that his eldest son returned to Sulu to succeed him. This detail, if accurate, indicates that succession in the eastern kingdom was understood to pass within the ruler's family and that Chinese recognition attended it.

What can the embassy tell historians? First, it demonstrates that Sulu rulers had the means and motive to conduct long-distance diplomacy. The expedition required ships, provisions, and organized crews. Second, it shows that the political landscape was plural. A model in which a single indigenous ruler gave way to a single foreign missionary and then to a centralized state does not fit this evidence. Third, it indicates that diplomatic recognition was valued. The rulers evidently expected to benefit from Chinese recognition, whether in prestige, trade access, or competition with rivals.

Fourth, the embassy does not directly address religion. The record does not say whether any of the three rulers was Muslim, though some of the names contain Indic and Malay-world titles. The tomb at Dezhou is a Chinese-style tomb, though this reflects the host court's arrangements. Scholars have also noted that Chinese investiture should not be read as a reliable description of how Sulu's inhabitants themselves viewed the political structure. The Ming court sorted rulers into categories familiar to its own system. The 1417 embassy is therefore best treated as evidence of political plurality and maritime capacity, alongside a reminder that the conventional mid-fifteenth-century sultanate chronology rests on different, later evidence.

Division III · Section 66.12

The Formation of the Sulu Sultanate and Its Maritime Foundations

The sultanate of Sulu emerged from the political world described in the preceding sections. The conventional account places the foundation by Sharif Abu Bakr around the middle of the fifteenth century, after the 1417 embassy. Since that chronology rests on later genealogical tradition, a cautious reconstruction describes a process of consolidation extending through the fifteenth century, in which local authority was reorganized through marriage alliances, religious patronage, commerce, and dynastic relationships. The details of when particular steps occurred cannot be fixed.

The geography of the archipelago shaped the form that consolidation took. Jolo, with its sheltered harbors and fertile land, became a main center, and its position facing both Borneo and Mindanao made it a convenient meeting place for ships. Tawi-Tawi, closer to Borneo, was important in the traditions of early Islamic teaching. Control of such places did not mean control of every island. Authority extended through personal bonds with datus on other islands and with maritime communities whose boats and skills were essential to trade and defense. A sultan was a leader among leaders.

The datu system was the framework in which the sultanate developed. In the tradition, the sultan's authority was recognized by datus who retained local followings. Islam added a legitimating language and institutions, including a title of sultan, religious officials, and, in later centuries, a council that included both datus and religious figures. The extent of such institutions around 1450 is unsure. The reasonable reading is that new religious authority was grafted onto existing leadership patterns rather than replacing them. A sharif could be accepted as a ruler because he combined descent, learning, and marriage into a local line, and because datus saw advantage in an arrangement that reinforced their own standing and facilitated trade.

Commerce remained central. Pearls, sea products, and other goods drew visiting merchants, and a ruler able to protect traders and offer a marketplace gained revenue and prestige. Relations with Brunei, the Malay world, and Mindanao brought merchants, religious teachers, and potential marriage partners. Chinese demand for the archipelago's products continued, and, although the Ming tribute system declined in intensity after the early fifteenth century, trade did not end. Over time, the sultanate's identity as a Muslim polity became more pronounced and was reflected in the consolidation of a ruling line.

It would be a mistake to present Sulu's early history as a simple succession from one indigenous ruler to one foreign missionary and then to a fully centralized state. The evidence points to multiple rulers, multiple routes by which Islam arrived, and a gradual accumulation of authority. The sultanate that later resisted European powers and developed a long-distance trade in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, discussed in later volumes, had institutions that were largely products of subsequent centuries. The fifteenth-century sultanate should be understood as a developing political order with an Islamic identity and a still-negotiated relationship with local datus, rather than a mature centralized state.

Division IV · Section 66.13

Mapping the Northern Maritime Networks

The three regions studied in this chapter were joined by a network of routes, but the nature of the links varied. A map that simply draws lines among Brunei Bay, northern Borneo, Palawan, the Sulu Archipelago, Basilan, and Mindanao's coasts risks erasing the difference between a trade route, a cultural relationship, and a political connection. Historians should ask, for each line, what traveled along it and who controlled it.

Trade routes carried goods. Forest products from Borneo, pearls and sea products from the Sulu zone, Chinese ceramics and textiles, and other items moved along coastlines and across the channels. These routes were often composed of short legs, with goods changing hands several times. A merchant carrying Chinese goods might sail to Brunei, where he exchanged them for local products, and a local trader might carry those products onward to Sulu or Mindanao. No central authority directed this flow, though rulers sought to attract and tax it.

Cultural connections were conveyed by the movement of people. Intermarriage among coastal families, the spread of languages related to Sama and Malay, shared boat-building and fishing technologies, and the movement of religious teachers established affinities across the region. Islam moved along these paths, but so did older practices. The result was not uniformity. Brunei Malay, Tausug, Sama, and the languages of Mindanao each developed distinctively, and communities adopted Muslim practices at different times and in different forms.

Political connections were more limited and more variable. Marriages among ruling families, as recorded in the traditions, formed alliances. Rulers sought recognition from one another and from China. But the political arrangements did not form a single hierarchy. A Brunei ruler might claim precedence over a Sulu ruler in tradition, while Sulu's rulers regarded themselves as independent in practice. Mindanao's river polities had their own relationships with their upriver neighbors and with Sulu. Where sources record claims of subordination, historians must ask whether they reflect actual control, ceremonial precedence, or later political aims.

The northern network also connected to wider systems. Via Brunei, it linked to the Chinese coast and to Melaka and Java, and via Sulu and Mindanao it extended toward the Visayas, Maluku, and the eastern archipelago. Ternate and Tidore, treated in Chapter 71, lay at a considerable distance, but the movement of spice-related goods and people tied the northern region to the east. In this sense the northern maritime networks were a connective zone rather than a periphery, and their participants shaped the flows of people and goods that crossed them.

Division IV · Section 66.14

The Political Communities of Mindanao before 1500

Mindanao, the second-largest island of the Philippines, was home to a variety of communities before 1500. Its long coastline, large river systems, interior valleys, and highlands supported coastal trading settlements, riverine agricultural communities, and upland societies with different languages and political forms. The conventional modern picture of Mindanao as the home of Muslim sultanates is incomplete. It describes certain coastal and river regions in later centuries, not the whole island or the whole period.

Among the earliest documented polities is Butuan, in the northeast, which appears in Song Chinese records, with a mission recorded in 1001. Archaeological finds, including remarkable plank-built boats and gold objects, show that Butuan had wealth and long-distance contacts and was influenced by Indic religious and artistic forms. Butuan's records predate the period of Muslim sultanate formation by centuries and illustrate that Mindanao's political history cannot be told only through Islam. Chapter 72 treats Butuan and the pre-Spanish Philippines in more detail.

In the west and south, the coastal settlements of the Moro Gulf and Illana Bay and the great river system of the Pulangi supported communities later associated with the Maguindanao and the Iranun. The Buayan region, in the upper Pulangi valley, was another center, and Buayan and Maguindanao later became distinct and sometimes rival sultanates. Their precise organization before 1500 is unknown. Local authority was exercised by datus with followings, in a manner broadly comparable to Sulu. Rivers served as roads connecting the interior with coastal markets, and control of river mouths and landing places gave coastal leaders leverage.

Mindanao also appears in the Ming record. William Henry Scott identifies Kumalalang as a polity, probably associated with the Dumanguilas Bay area, and names its ruler as Kanlai Ipentun. He appeared at the Ming court in November 1420, requested formal recognition, and died in Fujian in May 1421; his son La-pi was acknowledged as successor. These episodes document a Mindanao ruler engaged in the same diplomatic arena as Boni and Sulu. The precise location and boundaries of Kumalalang remain debated, and this evidence does not establish its relationship to the later Maguindanao or Buayan sultanates, or prove that its ruler was Muslim.

Upland and interior communities, including peoples later called Manobo and others, were part of the same landscape. They traded with coastal and riverine communities, supplied forest and agricultural goods, and in some traditions are described as kin of the coastal rulers. The history of western and central Mindanao should be distinguished from the later spread of Islamic institutions into other regions, including the Lake Lanao area, where the Maranao developed a distinctive society in subsequent centuries.

It is also worth noting how little can be said about ordinary households. Farmers working river-valley fields, fishers on Illana Bay, forest collectors in the uplands, and artisans who made pottery, boats, and metal goods left no written accounts. Archaeology of settlements and burials, together with later ethnographic descriptions used cautiously, gives glimpses of their lives. The political histories of datus and visiting embassies rested on this labor, and a history that mentions only rulers and envoys is necessarily partial.

Division IV · Section 66.15

The Maguindanao Genealogies and the Tradition of Sharif Kabungsuwan

The founding tradition of the Maguindanao sultanate centers on Sharif Kabungsuwan. In the genealogical accounts, Kabungsuwan came from Johor, accompanied by followers, and reached Mindanao, where he married a local woman of high rank and established Islamic authority. Related traditions describe two brothers, Tabunaway and Mamalu, as early inhabitants. In some versions, Tabunaway accepted Islam and became associated with the Muslim ruling line, while Mamalu remained outside and became the ancestor of non-Muslim neighbors. The story thus explains both political authority and kinship between Muslim and non-Muslim communities.

The commonly cited date for Kabungsuwan's arrival is near 1515, although some chronologies place the relevant developments earlier. The precise dating remains uncertain. Because 1515 lies at or beyond the main endpoint of this chapter, students should not infer that the fully established Maguindanao sultanate unquestionably existed in 1450. As in the Brunei case, the reference to Johor is chronologically awkward, since Johor as a polity dates from after 1511, and the tradition may be using a later name for a Malay-world origin.

The genealogies were recorded long after the events they describe. Najeeb M. Saleeby published versions of Maguindanao genealogical manuscripts in 1905, in the context of American colonial rule in the Philippines. The manuscripts he used were copies, and the underlying traditions were older than the copies, but the exact relationship between oral tradition, earlier writings, and the copies cannot be reconstructed. The publication also reflects colonial interests. Colonial officials sought information about local rulers and law, and the framing of Saleeby's collection was shaped by that setting.

What can a genealogy teach? It is poor evidence for exact dates. It is, however, good evidence for what a ruling family wished to be remembered. The Maguindanao accounts emphasize descent from a sharif, kinship with the older inhabitants, and the incorporation of outsiders through marriage. These themes are significant for understanding how authority was legitimized: it combined Islamic descent with local rootedness. The accounts also show an awareness of connections with the Malay world, which Mindanao shared with Sulu and Brunei.

A genealogy is not a neutral chronological record. Its construction involved selection, omission, and adaptation to current political needs. Lines of descent might be adjusted to justify a ruler's claims or reflect alliances. Names of founders might attract several attributions. Students should treat the Kabungsuwan narrative as a valuable record of dynastic memory, and they should not use it as a date-fixed account of events in the early sixteenth century, still less in the fifteenth.

The comparison with Brunei and Sulu is instructive. In all three regions, later genealogies attach the arrival of Muslim authority to a figure of distant and prestigious origin, often with a place name that raises chronological questions. This pattern may reflect a shared literary and political convention in the Malay-Muslim world of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, in which a ruling family secured its status by linking itself to a recognized center of learning or descent. Recognizing the convention does not discredit the traditions. It helps historians read them as evidence for the period in which they were shaped as well as for the period they describe.

Division IV · Section 66.16

Muslim Networks and the Transition toward Mindanao's Sultanates

How did Islam reach Mindanao and become a basis for sultanate formation? The evidence is thin, and the answer must be a probabilistic reconstruction. Muslim merchants and teachers, traveling along the networks described in Section 66.13, reached the coasts of western and southern Mindanao, probably in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, through Brunei, Sulu, and the Malay world. They settled, married, and established religious practice. Some coastal leaders adopted Islam. The first documents that treat Mindanao's rulers as Muslim date from the sixteenth century and later, and the traditions that describe earlier events are retrospective.

Maritime mobility was a central condition. The coast of Mindanao is open to Sulu and Borneo, and travel between them was routine. People of Sulu and Mindanao intermarried, and groups such as the Iranun, later renowned as seafarers, moved across these waters. Religious patronage by coastal leaders could attract teachers and strengthen alliances, as in Sulu. The processes of royal marriage, religious teaching, and local leadership that the traditions describe for Sulu and Brunei were plausibly at work in Mindanao too, but with local variation.

The chronology of Mindanao differs from that of Sulu and Brunei. Whereas Brunei and Sulu appear in Ming records as significant political players in the early fifteenth century, with Muslim sultanate institutions often placed in the second half of the century, the best-known Maguindanao sultanate traditions are associated with the early sixteenth century and later. The later Maguindanao and Buayan sultanates, which developed their organized structures in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, should not be projected back into the fifteenth. Their offices, councils, and legal institutions were the products of subsequent generations, shaped by encounters with the Spanish, Ternate, and other powers, as later chapters describe.

The coastal and riverine histories of western and central Mindanao also need to be distinguished from the spread of Islamic institutions elsewhere. The Lake Lanao region and its Maranao society, the northern and eastern coasts, and the interior highlands followed different timetables and in some cases remained outside Muslim polities until much later or entirely. Even within the Maguindanao zone, populations differed in religion and in their relations with the ruling line. Islam's influence arrived through a mosaic of relationships.

By 1500, then, Mindanao's coastal communities of the west and south had Muslim contacts, and some of their leaders were probably Muslim or in the process of adopting the religion, but the organized sultanates of later histories were still emerging. A fair summary is that Mindanao's political development was independent, rooted in datu leadership and river and coastal exchange, connected to Brunei and Sulu without being derivative of them, and moving toward sultanate forms on a partly different timetable. It also had earlier chapters, including the Indic-influenced polity of Butuan, which remind us that Islam was one of several religious currents in the island's history.

Division V · Section 66.17

Continuity and Change in Northern Maritime Society

One of the risks in studying the emergence of Muslim states is to describe it as a rupture, as if a society of local leaders and seafarers was replaced by a society of sultans and Islamic law. The evidence suggests more continuity than that. Kinship structures, local leadership by datus and other leaders, seafaring livelihoods, and patterns of exchange all persisted through the period of political and religious change. What changed was the language and institutions in which authority was expressed.

Kinship remained the basic organizing principle. Lineage and marriage determined access to leadership, and political alliances were made through marriage. The genealogies that later recorded sultanates are, in this sense, continuations of an older concern with descent, now expressed in Islamic terms by the addition of sharifian ancestry. Leadership also continued to depend on followers. The ruler's resources came from loyalty and exchange rather than from a bureaucratic machinery. These continuities help explain why the early sultanates were often loosely integrated.

Seafaring communities continued to follow their own livelihoods. Sama-speaking fishers, pearl gatherers, and boat-dwellers, Brunei's boatbuilders and carriers, and the Iranun sailors of Mindanao supplied the labor on which maritime trade depended. Rulers needed them, and this gave them bargaining power. Their religious practices may have changed more slowly than those of courts, and older beliefs about the sea, ancestors, and spirits did not disappear because a ruler took a Muslim title. Historians should be wary of assuming that court religion described everyone.

Change appeared in several areas. Islamic titles, such as sultan and sharif, entered political language. Mosques and religious teachers established new institutions. Legal and ritual practices connected to Islam, including marriage, burial, and oath-taking, became part of the toolkit of rulers and communities. Participation in Muslim commercial and diplomatic networks widened, as ports of the northern region became connected to Melaka, Java, and the Indian Ocean. Over generations, the new religion became a marker of identity for many communities, especially in contrast with later European Christian expansion, as Chapters 73 and 76 show.

Continuity and change coexisted because the new institutions were embedded in existing ones. A sultan was often a datu with a higher title. A sharif married into local lineages. Religious teachers settled in coastal communities and learned local languages. The result was a mixed order in which older and newer elements reinforced one another. This helps explain the resilience of local autonomy even under sultans, and it frames the comparison in the next sections.

The comparison among the three regions sharpens the point. In Brunei, continuity is visible in the river-mouth economy and in the dependence of the court on boat-building and carrying communities. In Sulu, it appears in the persistence of datu leadership beneath sultanate titles. In Mindanao, it appears in the river-and-coast exchange that linked interior and shore regardless of the religion of the ruler. Change, by contrast, tended to be most visible where a court and a port were closest together, and least visible in dispersed island and upland settings, where evidence of any kind is scarce.

Division V · Section 66.18

Why Did Muslim Maritime States Emerge? Competing Historical Explanations

Historians have proposed several explanations for the emergence of Muslim states in northern maritime Southeast Asia. None is sufficient alone, and their weight varies among Brunei, Sulu, and Mindanao. This section sets out the main arguments and asks how they apply to each case.

The commercial explanation holds that trade created opportunities for ports that welcomed Muslim merchants and joined Muslim networks. A ruler who adopted Islam gained access to a shared legal and commercial framework, trust among merchants, and links to powerful ports such as Melaka. This explanation fits Brunei well, given its port character and the importance of trade. It also helps explain Sulu, whose pearls and sea products drew merchants. It is less obviously suited to the Mindanao river societies, where agricultural and riverine exchange mattered as well.

The diplomatic-opportunity explanation emphasizes the Ming court's recognition and the wider competition among rulers. Chinese investiture gave Boni and Sulu rulers prestige and leverage. This explains the embassies of 1408 and 1417 but does not by itself explain Islam, because the Ming court did not require rulers to be Muslim. It does show that local rulers pursued external recognition from multiple sources, and Islamic legitimacy could have been another resource.

Marriage and kinship, as the traditions emphasize, provided mechanisms. Alliances with Muslim families gave local rulers prestige and connections, and sharifian descent offered a powerful claim. Related to this is the local political consolidation explanation: ambitious leaders used new religious authority to elevate themselves above rival datus, and Islamic titles and institutions supplied tools for consolidation. In Sulu, the plural rulership of 1417 followed by a later sultanate suggests such a process. Religious authority, including the role of teachers and sharifs, is a further factor, as is the role of seafaring communities whose mobility carried people, ideas, and goods.

Differences among the three cases are instructive. Brunei's ruling family adopted Muslim kingship in the context of a port polity closely tied to Melaka and the Malay world. Sulu's consolidation involved a series of arrivals and marriages that reconfigured a plural island polity. Mindanao's developments, tied to river and coastal communities, occurred on a later timetable and produced sultanates with distinctive structures. A single explanation imposed on all three would obscure these differences. Historians generally prefer to combine factors: commerce, marriage, local ambition, religious learning, geography, and maritime mobility each played roles that varied by place.

Each explanation also carries a risk of overstatement. Emphasis on commerce can imply that rulers adopted Islam purely for profit, which ignores the religious learning, personal conviction, and community obligations that probably accompanied the change. Emphasis on local consolidation can understate how much the arrival of teachers, books, and legal ideas actually altered practice. Emphasis on genealogical sources can reproduce the viewpoint of the families who commissioned them. A sound explanation holds these cautions in tension, and it accepts that the motives of fourteenth- and fifteenth-century actors can usually be inferred only indirectly.

Division V · Section 66.19

Brunei, Sulu, and Mindanao around 1500

Around 1500, the three regions occupied different positions. Brunei was an established Muslim court, with a ruler recognized in tradition as sultan, a capital known to European observers a few years later as large and prosperous, and diplomatic and commercial ties across the South China Sea and the Malay world. Its institutions were developed, though their exact fifteenth-century form is uncertain. Sultan Bolkiah's reign marks a period of increasing maritime influence. That influence belongs mostly to the sixteenth century and should not be projected back.

Sulu was a developing sultanate. By this time, the tradition holds, a sharifian ruling line had been established, probably combining with local datu leadership. Jolo was a center, and relations with Brunei, Mindanao, and the Malay world were active. But Sulu's institutions remained relatively loose, with authority exercised through alliances with datus, and the later centralized features of the sultanate lay in the future. Chinese recognition of the early fifteenth century had faded, but Sulu's trade continued.

Mindanao presents the most varied picture. Some coastal communities of the west and south were in contact with Muslim networks and had Muslim leaders or were moving toward them, and the traditions of the Maguindanao place Kabungsuwan's arrival around 1515. Elsewhere, including parts of the northern and eastern coasts and the interior, communities had different religious and political arrangements. The later Maguindanao and Buayan sultanates were not yet fully formed. It would be misleading to describe Mindanao around 1500 as a Muslim island.

Across the region, the distinction between rulers' adoption of Islam and the conversion of whole populations remained important. Courts and commercial communities were the primary Muslim spaces. Many upriver, upland, and island communities followed other practices, and Muslim practice among the coastal majority was itself mixed with older customs. Similarly, the distinction among direct rule, maritime influence, tribute, alliance, and local autonomy continued to matter. None of the three polities governed a bounded territory in the modern sense.

For some societies, the religion and political status around 1500 remain poorly documented. Palawan, parts of northern Borneo beyond Brunei's immediate sphere, and the eastern and interior regions of Mindanao fall into this category. The honest summary is that the northern maritime world was a patchwork, in which some communities had Muslim courts with recognized rulers, others had developing institutions, and others remained outside such structures. The arrival of Portuguese and Spanish power in the early sixteenth century, to be examined in Volume V, entered this patchwork rather than a uniform Muslim region.

Division V · Section 66.20

Brunei, Sulu, Mindanao, and the Northern Maritime Networks: A Historical Synthesis

The preceding sections allow a synthesis that combines geography, commerce, political change, diplomacy, Islam, and local agency. The northern maritime world was an arc of connected coasts and islands in which exchange was shaped by winds, channels, and rivers. Its communities supplied products desired in China and the Muslim world and mobilized labor, ships, and diplomatic skill to participate in trade. They were not recipients of civilization from larger neighbors. They were actors who selected, combined, and transformed outside elements.

Their political institutions emerged by different routes. Brunei developed around a river-mouth center, supported by maritime labor and forest exchange, obtained Chinese recognition, and later became a Muslim-ruled kingdom within Malay-world networks. Sulu developed from plural island polities, as shown by the 1417 embassy, into a sultanate through marriage, religious teaching, and datu alliances. Mindanao's riverine and coastal communities followed a partly different chronology, with the documented 1420 embassy of Kanlai Ipentun from the polity of Kumalalang preceding the later Kabungsuwan traditions and sultanates. The Muslim institutions of each region were rooted in local society.

The evidence is unequal and demands caution. Ming records provide firm dates but limited insight. Archaeology demonstrates trade without naming rulers. Genealogies convey political memory but not reliable chronology. Combining them yields a picture that is persuasive in outline and uncertain in detail. The most important methodological lesson is that traditional succession lists should not be mistaken for verified history, and that later sultanate structures should not be written back into the fifteenth century.

The chapter's significance lies in what it adds to the wider fifteenth-century order. The northern maritime networks connected the South China Sea with the Sulu and Celebes Seas and with the eastern archipelago, and they placed Muslim courts and plural rulers in a region sometimes overlooked in accounts focused on Melaka and Java. They also prepared conditions for later developments, including the sixteenth-century expansion of Brunei's influence, the consolidation of Sulu, the formation of the Maguindanao and Buayan sultanates, and the encounters with Spanish power treated in Chapters 73 and 76.

Local agency runs through every part of this synthesis. Rulers in Boni and Sulu decided to cross the sea and seek Ming recognition, and the Ming court's records preserve those decisions even though they do not preserve the reasons. Datus decided whether to accept a sharif, a teacher, or a neighboring sultan as a patron, and in doing so shaped the terms on which Muslim authority took root. Seafaring communities decided which ports to visit and which rulers to serve. The picture that results is of negotiated political order, in which outsiders and insiders both mattered and neither simply imposed a model on the other.

A final caution concerns scale. The polities in this chapter were small by the standards of Majapahit, Ayutthaya, or the Ming empire, and their rulers governed through personal ties as much as through offices. Their importance does not lie in territorial size. It lies in their position within a maritime system in which ports, straits, and river mouths carried disproportionate weight, and in the durability of the institutions and memories they produced. Students who keep both points in view, small scale and large significance, will read the later history of the northern maritime world more accurately.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined how Brunei, the Sulu Archipelago, and Mindanao developed interconnected but distinct maritime political societies in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. It argued that the northern maritime world was a historical region in its own right, shaped by local rulers, seafaring populations, trading relationships, religious specialists, and diplomatic opportunities, and not the periphery of Melaka or other established centers.

The chapter began with evidence and geography. Chinese records, archaeology, genealogies, and later accounts differ in date, perspective, and reliability, producing uneven histories for the three regions. Brunei Bay, the Sulu chain, and the Pulangi and Illana Bay coast formed a connected maritime landscape in which monsoon winds, islands, channels, and rivers shaped exchange without determining politics.

Chinese diplomatic records show Boni sending a ruler to the Ming court in 1408 and receiving recognition for his heir. The chapter stressed that identifying every Chinese reference to Boni with modern Brunei is problematic and that the 1408 ruler should not be inserted into Brunei's later genealogy as a verified sultan. Archaeological sites such as Santubong and Kota Batu document trade and settlement, and the Master Pu tombstone illustrates the limits of material evidence.

Brunei emerged as a river-mouth maritime kingdom, supported by boat-building and fishing communities, upriver suppliers of forest goods, and visiting merchants. Traditions about Awang Alak Betatar and Sultan Muhammad Shah describe conversion through marriage, but they contain chronological difficulties, including the anachronistic reference to Johor. The chapter distinguished the reasonably secure fact of Brunei's later Muslim kingship from the disputed names and dates of the traditional account, and it treated Sultan Bolkiah's reign as a transition toward greater influence rather than evidence of a fifteenth-century empire.

Sulu developed from island communities led by datus. The Ming record of 1417, naming three rulers and recording Paduka Batara's death at Dezhou, shows plural rulership before the conventional sultanate chronology. The traditions of Tuan Masha'ika, Karim ul-Makhdum, Raja Baguinda, and Sharif Abu Bakr describe a sequence of religious teaching, royal marriage, and sharifian authority, but their dates, including the mid-fifteenth-century foundation, rest on later genealogy.

Mindanao's coastal and riverine societies followed a partly different chronology. Butuan appeared in Song records in 1001, and Kumalalang appeared in Ming documentation in 1420. The Maguindanao genealogies, published in 1905, describe Sharif Kabungsuwan, Tabunaway, and Mamalu, with a traditional date near 1515 that lies at or beyond the chapter's main endpoint. The later Maguindanao and Buayan sultanates should not be projected unchanged into the fifteenth century.

Continuity and change coexisted. Kinship, datu leadership, seafaring livelihoods, and local exchange persisted, while Islamic titles, teachers, and networks entered political life. Historians explain the emergence of Muslim states through combinations of commerce, diplomacy, marriage, local consolidation, religious authority, geography, and maritime mobility, with different weight in each region.

Around 1500 the three regions occupied different positions: Brunei an established Muslim court, Sulu a developing sultanate, and Mindanao a mixed region whose sultanates were still forming. Rulers' adoption of Islam must be distinguished from conversion of whole populations, and direct rule from influence, tribute, alliance, and autonomy. The northern maritime world entered the sixteenth century as a patchwork, not a unified Muslim region.

The chapter's methodological lesson is that source criticism is part of history. Contemporary but external Chinese records, silent archaeology, and retrospective genealogies must be combined without confusing them. Its historical lesson is that Brunei, Sulu, and Mindanao were active participants in the fifteenth-century order. Volume V will examine how that order met Portuguese and Spanish power.

A polity ruled by a sultan, whose institutions and territorial reach varied greatly by place and period.
Sharif
A person claimed to descend from the Prophet Muhammad, whose lineage gave religious prestige.
Raja
A Malay-world title for a prince or ruler, drawing on Indic political vocabulary.
Tarsila (salasilah)
A genealogical record of ruling families, used to express descent and legitimacy and compiled over time.
Genealogy
An account of descent; evidence for political memory but not necessarily for accurate dates.
Source criticism
The historian's practice of evaluating a source's origin, purpose, and reliability.
Triangulation
Comparing independent sources of different kinds to test an interpretation.
Kota Batu
An archaeological site near the Brunei River with imported ceramics and other evidence of trade and settlement.
Master Pu tombstone
A Chinese inscribed stone dated 1264, associated with Brunei, whose occupant's religion and original location are uncertain.
Entrepôt
A port whose prosperity rests on exchange rather than extensive agricultural territory.
Anchorage
A sheltered place where ships can lie safely, important to maritime settlement and trade.
Monsoon
Seasonal wind systems that structured sailing schedules across the region.
Sama-Bajau
A cluster of related maritime-oriented peoples and languages across the Sulu and Celebes Seas; internal variation and historical change should be recognized.
Tausug
The people and language of Jolo and nearby islands, later dominant in the Sulu sultanate.
Iranun
A people of Mindanao's southwestern coasts and Illana Bay area, later renowned as seafarers.
Maguindanao
A people and later sultanate of the Pulangi river region and Cotabato coast, with genealogical traditions of founding.
Buayan
A region of the upper Pulangi valley that later became a distinct Muslim polity.
Islamization
A gradual, uneven process in which Islam spread among rulers and communities; adoption by elites differs from conversion of whole populations.
Riverine polity
A political society organized around a river system linking interior producers with coastal exchange.
Moro
A term applied by Spanish and later writers to Muslims of the southern Philippines; anachronistic and undifferentiating for the fifteenth century.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it moves the story of Islam and maritime states beyond the great ports of Sumatra, the Malay Peninsula, and Java. Brunei, Sulu, and Mindanao show that political creativity occurred across the whole maritime world, and that the northern arc was neither empty nor derivative. Its rulers sent embassies, its merchants moved goods across the seas, and its communities built institutions suited to their own circumstances.

The chapter also shows how historians work when evidence is uneven. A reader who remembers only the traditional names and dates has learned less than one who understands why the dates are uncertain, what the Ming records can and cannot show, and how genealogies function as political memory. These lessons apply throughout the textbook, from Chapter 1's warnings about anachronism to later chapters on colonial sources and nationalist narratives.

Several continuities survive beyond 1500. The datu-based leadership, kinship politics, and seafaring livelihoods of this period shaped the sultanates described in Volumes V and VI, and the northern maritime networks remained important for trade and political relations. The regions' different chronologies also foreshadow their distinct experiences of Spanish expansion, treated in Chapters 73 and 76, and, much later, their different relationships with the modern states of Brunei, Malaysia, and the Philippines, as Chapters 109, 160, 191, and 216 show.

Finally, the chapter contributes to the textbook's central argument. Southeast Asian societies were not passive crossroads. They were participants in an interconnected world who selected, adapted, and transformed religion, kingship, and commerce. The northern maritime order that entered the sixteenth century was complex, plural, and locally rooted, and understanding it requires the careful, evidence-conscious attention that this book asks of every chapter.
