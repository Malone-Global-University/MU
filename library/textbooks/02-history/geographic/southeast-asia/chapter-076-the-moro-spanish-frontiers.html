The Moro-Spanish Frontiers

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Spanish expansion made Manila a colonial capital within a few years, but across roughly two centuries it never established uniform sovereignty over Mindanao and Sulu. This chapter explains why, and what the long contest involved.

A Spanish commander could occupy a southern fort, but that achievement was not the same as governing the surrounding communities. In 1635 Spanish soldiers and their Filipino allies began a new position at Zamboanga. It brought artillery and a garrison close to important maritime routes, yet its usefulness depended on supplies, alliances, and decisions made far away in Manila. Abandoned as a military strongpoint during the crisis of 1663 and later fortified anew, the site demonstrates both the reach and the limits of an empire that claimed territories it could not always administer.

The people whom Spanish writings classified as Moros were not subjects of one kingdom. Sulu's maritime sultanate, political centers along the Pulangi River, the influential court of Buayan, and communities around Lake Lanao possessed their own histories, alliances, and political arrangements. Tausug, Maguindanaon, Maranao, Sama, and other groups differed in language, settlement, livelihood, and relationship to Muslim rulers. Religious affiliation mattered, but it did not abolish competition among rulers or create a permanent unified command. To interpret the frontier, the historian must recover these distinctions rather than simply repeat colonial labels.

Warfare was frequent but intermittent. Spanish fleets reached Brunei and Sulu in the 1570s, an ambitious expedition failed to occupy the Pulangi basin in 1596, and major campaigns struck Mindanao and Jolo in 1637–1638. Southern fleets attacked settlements in the Visayas, taking captives and damaging livelihoods. Some Spanish officers and indigenous allies in turn seized Muslim prisoners and sold people into slavery. Treaties in 1645 and 1646 reveal that the same antagonists who used military force could also negotiate. The social history of captivity, survival, and forced labor is as important to the chapter as the movement of soldiers.

Commerce made a complete separation of the parties impossible. River products, food, maritime goods, textiles, metals, and weapons moved among island and mainland networks connected to Brunei, Borneo, the Moluccas, and Manila. Merchants, sea captains, royal agents, and local datus had reasons to keep some routes open even in periods of hostility. Ports and waterways mattered because they linked people and resources; a fleet that controlled one harbor did not necessarily control the hinterland, neighboring channels, or the decisions of every trading community.

Islam and Christianity shaped institutions, political claims, and the language of historical records. Spanish officials often depicted southern campaigns as defense of Catholic subjects and efforts to extend conversion. Muslim rulers defended their position through alliances and through religious and political legitimacy of their own. Yet neither a theological explanation nor a purely economic one can account for every decision. Distinct interests in tribute, maritime security, territorial influence, trade, and prisoners changed over time. The central task is to identify how these pressures operated in particular episodes and why particular agreements became possible.

The available evidence is abundant in some respects and starkly unequal in others. Antonio de Morga's 1609 history, Jesuit correspondence and missionary narratives, royal instructions, military reports, and accounts of treaties provide important details, but they were primarily produced by representatives of colonial institutions. Sultanate genealogies and oral histories can preserve different perspectives, though their recording and transmission may be later than the events discussed. People who hauled boats, planted fields, negotiated ransoms, or endured enslavement often appear anonymously. This chapter distinguishes what sources state from the interpretations historians construct and from questions the record cannot answer.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the geography of Mindanao, Sulu, and the surrounding seas and explain how it limited both Spanish and Muslim control.
  2. Distinguish the political institutions of Sulu, Maguindanao, and Buayan, and the Maranao interior.
  3. Explain how the term “Moro” originated and why it obscured real differences.
  4. Trace the principal Spanish campaigns of 1578, 1596, 1635–1638, and the peace of 1645.
  5. Analyze why Spanish military victories often failed to produce durable sovereignty.
  6. Assess the roles of trade, ships, and fortifications in frontier power.
  7. Evaluate captivity and enslavement carried out by several parties, including Spanish allies.
  8. Interpret colonial sources critically, identifying perspective and omission.
  9. Explain the withdrawal from and return to Zamboanga in relation to wider imperial pressures.
  10. Evaluate competing interpretations of the conflicts as religious, political, or economic.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did Spain establish a capital at Manila quickly but fail to subdue Mindanao and Sulu?
  2. What did a sultan’s or datu’s authority consist of, and how did it differ from territorial sovereignty?
  3. How did the label “Moro” create an apparent common enemy?
  4. Why could trade continue during war?
  5. Who profited from, and who suffered in, the capture of people?
  6. Was diplomacy a sign of submission or of survival?
  7. How did inland geography, especially Lake Lanao, limit conquest?
  8. Whose experiences are least visible in the surviving sources?

Historical Context

Spanish forces arriving in the archipelago encountered a complex network of communities, not an empty territory awaiting political formation. Islam had already become influential in Sulu and parts of Mindanao through contacts with merchants and religious figures of the Malay world. Courts in Brunei, riverine centers in Maguindanao, and the maritime societies of the Sulu Archipelago possessed networks of authority and exchange whose histories were not dependent on Spanish arrival.

The establishment of Spanish Manila in 1571 enabled governors to direct maritime campaigns toward southern regions, but resources were never unlimited. The colony required food and labor from indigenous communities and relied on New Spain for essential imperial finance. Efforts to defend the capital, maintain the galleon route, and address European rivals competed with the expense of southern fortifications and military expeditions. Royal claims of control were often broader than the military and administrative means available to enforce them.

The boundaries separating Christianized and Muslim communities were porous. Coastal trade, kin ties, local rivalries, and sometimes coercive capture crossed political divisions. Communities classified as non-Muslim in Mindanao had distinctive leaders and often negotiated independently with both Muslim courts and Spanish agents. The Spanish category Moro obscured this diversity, while Christian was also an imperfect guide to a settlement's choices about alliance or armed service.

Earlier centuries of warfare and exchange in Iberia influenced Spanish official language about Muslims, but Philippine circumstances differed sharply. Sultanates operated through relationships among datus, followers, merchants, and religious figures, rather than through uniformly surveyed territories. A peace agreement with one ruler could provide a diplomatic opening without bringing all linked communities under Spanish government. The distinction between a military victory and durable sovereignty is therefore central to the narrative.

The chapter relies particularly on the contemporary history of Antonio de Morga (1609), the posthumously published work of Jesuit Francisco Combés (1667), and surviving treaty material discussed by modern historians. These sources make certain expeditions and negotiations recoverable while leaving unresolved questions about the experiences of non-elite residents. Historians also draw on local histories, genealogies, archaeology, and scholarship on military labor, demography, maritime trade, and slavery.

The chronological limit of roughly 1750 is important. Later eighteenth- and nineteenth-century trade expansion and large-scale Sulu raiding receive substantial attention in James F. Warren's studies, but their characteristic patterns must not be applied uncritically to the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Likewise, modern Bangsamoro politics cannot be used as a ready-made explanation for early modern alliances. The chapter studies political identities and practices as they developed in their own setting.

Division I — The Southern Maritime World and Muslim Political Society
Section 76.1

Mindanao, Sulu, and the Geography of the Southern Frontier

Mindanao and the Sulu Archipelago formed neither a single isolated island world nor a continuous political frontier. Mindanao's extensive coast, mountain interiors, river basins, and plains created multiple avenues of exchange and military movement. The Pulangi, also called the Rio Grande de Mindanao, joined interior settlements to its delta and the coastal trading centers of Maguindanao. Lake Lanao, high in the interior, supported communities with their own political institutions, cultivation, fishing, and routes toward northern and western coasts. The Agusan basin opened a different corridor toward northeastern Mindanao. These zones belonged to connected but not identical histories.

The Sulu island chain extended southwest from the Zamboanga Peninsula through Basilan, Jolo, and Tawi-Tawi toward northern Borneo. Its islands occupied a strategic position between the Sulu Sea and the Celebes or Sulawesi Sea. Maritime channels linked them to Brunei, eastern Borneo, the Moluccas, and the Visayan islands farther north. Jolo's importance arose from its political and commercial connections, not merely its location on a modern map. Sama seafaring populations, Tausug communities, inland producers, and merchants used different waters and harbors for different purposes; the sultan's influence over them varied.

The same environmental setting could favor several political strategies. A lowland ruler might seek access to river mouths, imported metals, and outward trade; upland leaders might defend passes or cultivate relations with multiple coastal patrons. Island communities could relocate boats or settlements when faced with an expedition, while rulers with fixed buildings had different vulnerabilities. None of these options was unlimited. Weather, food supplies, ship repair, and cooperation from people ashore shaped the practical reach of any fleet. A passage navigable to small local craft could obstruct a larger European vessel.

Seasonality mattered. Winds, rain, currents, and monsoon variations affected sailing opportunities, food production, fishing, and the timing of attacks. Yet it would be mistaken to claim that geography made military resistance inevitable. Alliances, intelligence, local rivalries, technical experience, and the willingness of rulers to negotiate were equally important. Spanish commanders sometimes moved inland with indigenous allies and specialized boats; southern fleets likewise reached Visayan coasts and navigated around defensive positions. Geography supplied opportunities and constraints that politically organized people exploited in different ways.

The Spanish word frontier can mislead if imagined as a straight boundary on a map. The colonial state held Manila, a network of towns in many northern and central islands, and intermittent southern forts; these possessions were not all surrounded by territory governed in the same manner. At a particular coast an indigenous Christian community might recognize Spanish officials while trading with Muslim mariners; a settlement farther upriver might acknowledge several rival rulers or resist all external demands. A treaty could regulate passage and tribute without changing who exercised day-to-day authority in inland villages.

A useful comparative exercise is to consider how authority operated at three locations: the Pulangi river mouth, Jolo harbor, and Lake Lanao. The first permitted leverage over routes linking inland production with external trade; the second supported maritime exchange and alliance-building across islands; the third relied on lake-based communities and upland routes. None can be reduced to a stronger or weaker version of the same polity. Their distinctive environments required different strategies for mobilization, negotiating cooperation, and resisting an expedition. A map should therefore show routes and political relationships, not only modern provincial names.

Modern state borders divide this maritime system between the Philippines, Malaysia, Indonesia, and Brunei. Those borders cannot be imposed on the sixteenth or seventeenth centuries. The historical actors thought in terms of routes, ports, kinship, rights, prestige, alliances, and zones of influence. Maps drawn for colonial governments often simplified those relationships into colored spheres of claimed ownership. Historians must compare maps with accounts of actual tribute collection, military presence, settlement patterns, and negotiated permissions.

For this chapter, the southern frontier is therefore a changing geography of contacts. It includes coastal raids and fortified posts, but also river trade, religious scholarship, agricultural production, refuge, seasonal travel, and the movement of captives. The strategic importance of Zamboanga only becomes intelligible when placed alongside Jolo, the Pulangi valley, Lake Lanao, and the routes toward Borneo. Thinking spatially helps explain both the repeated achievements of military expeditions and their frequent inability to create lasting political control.

Section 76.2

The Sultanates of Sulu and Maguindanao

The Sultanate of Sulu and the political centers of Maguindanao were established before the major Spanish military campaigns in the south. They were not creations of European intervention. Their rulers governed through kinship, religious legitimacy, alliances, control of commercial gateways, and the relationships binding prominent families to their followers. Islamic titles and court practices helped define authority, but a title alone did not create a centralized territorial bureaucracy. Sultanates were institutions that operated through negotiation as well as command. The practical support a ruler could mobilize depended on the interests and capacities of local datus.

In Sulu, Jolo served as a principal political and commercial center, although the sultanate's networks reached beyond one island. Tausug elites, Sama maritime communities, and other populations participated unequally in its affairs. Commercial ties to Borneo, especially Brunei, supported political marriages, diplomacy, and access to valuable goods. Relationships among these participants varied with circumstance. Some were close allies of the court; others preserved local independence, entered temporary coalitions, or moved across waterways beyond the immediate reach of court officers.

In the Pulangi basin, the distinction between coastal Maguindanao and upriver Buayan is indispensable. Both were significant centers, and their relative influence changed. The authority of a Maguindanao ruler near the lower river did not automatically extend to every settlement upstream. Buayan's leaders could maintain their own followers, control important approaches, and make regional alliances. Spanish writers sometimes used place names and the name of Mindanao loosely; their terminology should not be mistaken for a precise depiction of competing indigenous jurisdictions.

A datu was not simply the equivalent of a Spanish provincial governor. His position could depend on ancestry, recognized leadership, command of fighting men, commercial clients, marriages, ritual connections, the ability to distribute goods, and the protection he offered. A sultan might have broader religious and diplomatic prestige while remaining dependent on agreements with powerful datus. Succession was often contested within ruling lineages, and rival claimants could seek allies beyond their immediate region. Such contests were intrinsic to local political life rather than symptoms of inevitable political weakness.

The language of tribute needs similar care. Payments might indicate subordination, acknowledgment, alliance, or an arrangement securing protection and access to a market. A ruler who accepted gifts or tribute did not necessarily command the payer in all circumstances. Conversely, a leader who claimed a coastal settlement might lack power to appoint its officials or collect regular revenue. To reconstruct sovereignty, historians ask who settled disputes, provided protection, organized armed expeditions, regulated commerce, and obtained cooperation when interests diverged.

The structure of political authority also helps explain the uneven quality of surviving evidence. Spanish envoys might identify one man as king because his household received them or his title resembled a European monarch's. But his ability to compel another datu remained a separate question. Indigenous genealogies preserved claims to prestigious ancestry and succession, while courtly traditions could later be reorganized to legitimate new dynasties. These sources are indispensable, yet they must be compared with observed diplomatic acts, military coalitions, and trade relationships. A political title is evidence of claimed rank, not by itself proof of the extent of obedience.

Spanish military policy frequently assumed that capturing a capital or negotiating with one ruler could settle the status of an entire region. But the social networks that made a polity effective survived the loss of a fort or residence. Followers might withdraw with their leaders or attach themselves temporarily to another court. Negotiation with the Spanish could be useful to one faction while threatening another; the results rarely had the finality claimed by victory proclamations. This difference between court authority and territorial occupation explains much of the frontier's durability.

Neither Sulu nor Maguindanao should be romanticized as uniformly united communities defending an already formed nation. Their rulers made strategic calculations, sometimes fought rivals, and could impose taxes, service, and captivity on people with less power. Yet their political institutions were resilient and capable of coordinated response. Understanding them on their own terms is the necessary starting point for interpreting both the failures of Spanish conquest and the choices that shaped Muslim societies during prolonged conflict.

Section 76.3

Islam, Religious Institutions, and Southern Philippine Society

Islam was integral to the political and social worlds of several southern Philippine polities, but it was neither a uniform code imposed everywhere nor the sole source of political authority. Muslim communities had grown through the movement of merchants, teachers, marriage partners, and religious specialists across the Malay maritime world. Sulu and Maguindanao rulers drew on Islamic language to express legitimacy, join regional diplomatic networks, and connect their courts to histories broader than their immediate islands or river valleys. The establishment of a sultanate did not mean that all communities under its influence practiced religion in the same way.

Islamic teachers, mosque communities, and learned figures helped transmit devotional practices and concepts of law, kinship, and proper conduct. Arabic-script learning, Malay connections, and the circulation of texts gave some courtly and religious circles access to wider intellectual traditions. Historical evidence for the precise distribution of institutions remains uneven. Missionary opponents emphasized selected religious features, and later genealogies can reorganize earlier events around the concerns of subsequent generations. The historian must distinguish contemporary observation from retrospective claims about uninterrupted dynastic or religious continuity.

Marriage, inheritance, conflict settlement, and family status were affected by Islamic norms and regional customary practices. Neither category should be treated as fixed or mutually exclusive. Some disputes were settled by local leaders whose judgments combined their own authority, inherited custom, and religious arguments. Coastal merchants might possess connections and knowledge unavailable in upland villages. Political conversion at a court could precede the much slower spread of particular observances through its dependent communities.

The language of holy war appears in some sources and should be taken seriously when contemporaries used it. Spanish officials and friars presented campaigns as defense or advancement of Christianity, while Muslim rulers could invoke faith to justify resistance and organize followers. Yet religious rhetoric did not eliminate the importance of tribute, commerce, kinship, or captives. Muslim and Christian groups sometimes traded, negotiated, or accepted truces despite religious difference. Similarly, rival Muslim rulers sometimes fought one another despite sharing religious affiliation.

Brunei and the Moluccas formed significant points of reference. These societies had their own political conflicts, commercial markets, and relationships with European arrivals. Muslim rulers in Mindanao and Sulu could use wider connections to obtain arms, political allies, prestige goods, or diplomatic information. Religious solidarity might help an alliance, but it did not guarantee one; access to resources and rivalries between courts often determined whether a coalition endured. These external links formed part of the southern polities' capacities, not a foreign force that replaced indigenous initiative.

Another problem concerns the relationship between religion and social mobility. Access to teachers, sacred learning, and networks connected to Muslim courts could enhance prestige for some households. Other communities preserved locally established ritual systems alongside religious practices attributed to Islamic institutions. An official report describing a place as Muslim does not specify how every resident prayed, married, or resolved disputes. Social differentiation matters: rulers, merchants, religious scholars, and agricultural communities had unequal access to knowledge and resources. Reconstructing these variations prevents a court-centered history of Islam from being mistaken for the religious experience of an entire region.

The effects of conflict on religious life were often local and material. A ruined settlement could lose teachers or access to religious spaces. Captivity might separate families and expose people to pressure to convert. Missionary stations in contested regions required protection and local intermediaries, and their presence could be perceived as a political intrusion when joined to Spanish tribute or garrison demands. Muslim communities likewise maintained religious institutions through arrangements with their own rulers and teachers. The development of religious identity cannot be understood apart from these changing conditions.

This section is not a complete account of Islamization, which the textbook has already placed in Chapters 65 and 66. Its task is narrower: to show why faith mattered within the frontier's politics and social institutions. The conflict did not divide a homogeneous Christian north from a homogeneous Muslim south. Diverse non-Muslim indigenous communities inhabited Mindanao and neighboring islands, and even baptized settlements had varied political loyalties. Religious identities were important, but they were woven into a more complex landscape of power and belonging.

Section 76.4

The Historical Meaning and Development of the Term “Moro”

The term Moro entered the Philippines through Spanish imperial language. In Iberian usage, related forms had long referred to Muslims, especially those associated with North Africa and the history of Muslim rule in parts of the Iberian Peninsula. When Spanish conquerors encountered Muslim rulers and communities in Southeast Asia, they applied a familiar category to people with histories and political affiliations quite different from those of the populations previously described in Iberian sources. A name formed in one historical setting was thus repurposed in another.

The label was convenient for officials who wanted to describe an enemy, justify campaigns, or classify subjects by religion. In administrative and missionary writing, Moro could encompass Tausug, Maguindanaon, Maranao, Sama, and other Muslim populations. Yet these communities did not share one language, government, or set of economic interests. Rulers of Sulu and Buayan could be rivals, while coastal families connected by marriage might cooperate across political divisions. Calling them all one people concealed the local relationships that determined actual events.

Nor should the label be treated as proof that all the communities classified by Spaniards used it for themselves in the period under study. People identified themselves through kin, locality, ruling house, religious commitments, and other relationships. Some later adopted Moro as a shared political name, particularly in movements responding to successive colonial and national states. That later history must be recognized but not projected backward as if sixteenth-century leaders already possessed a single modern national program.

Colonial classifications had practical effects. A group named as a religious enemy might be treated differently in law, war, or captivity. Spanish-language accounts could describe campaigns as pacification and captives as prizes while obscuring the political claims of those they attacked. The same writer might use Moro for armed warriors, foreign merchants, unarmed inhabitants, and people enslaved by Spanish allies. A reader who does not distinguish these situations risks treating civilians and soldiers as interchangeable.

The related term piracy presents another problem. Colonial governments commonly called unauthorized attacks piracy, while treating similar actions conducted by commissioned or allied forces as lawful war or privateering. Historians should not erase violence or present all raiding as legitimate. Instead they should identify the behavior—seizure of vessels, burning of settlements, capture of people—and examine who authorized it, who benefited, and which legal vocabulary different participants used. The word used by a source is evidence of a perspective, not automatically a neutral description.

Students can test the power of naming by examining three formulations of the same event: a Spanish report of a Moro raid, a description of a Maguindanao-led expedition, and a social history of captives taken from a Visayan town. Each selects a different subject and scale. The first emphasizes a broad enemy category, the second identifies political organizers, and the third foregrounds people affected by violence. None is necessarily sufficient alone. Asking why particular authors chose particular labels exposes assumptions about political unity, legitimacy, and whose experience counts as the main historical event.

Religious difference could intensify military rhetoric while coexisting with negotiation. Spanish officials exchanged envoys with Muslim rulers, recorded treaties, and permitted trade in circumstances when their own texts also described these rulers as adversaries. This apparent contradiction becomes intelligible once Moro is understood as a flexible colonial category rather than a complete description of actual relationships. War propaganda and diplomatic practice could operate simultaneously.

For the historian, terminology therefore belongs in the analysis, not merely in a glossary. The chapter uses Moro-Spanish because it is a recognizable historical designation for the frontier and its extensive historiography. Where specific actors can be identified, however, it uses names such as Sulu, Maguindanao, Buayan, Tausug, or Maranao. This precision prevents a long and variable history from being rewritten as a timeless clash between two imagined homogeneous blocs.

Historical Vocabulary

Moro: A Colonial Category with a Later Political History

Definition: A term used by Spaniards for Muslims, extended in the Philippines to diverse southern societies.

Historical context: Iberian religious and political language was transferred to an archipelago of independent rulers and varied linguistic communities.

Why it matters: The classification influenced descriptions of enemies, justification for war, and distinctions between legitimate defense and supposed piracy.

Potential misunderstanding: It did not designate a single sixteenth-century state or a uniform self-conscious people. Later adoption of Moro as a collective political identity belongs to a different historical process.

Division II — The First Spanish-Muslim Confrontations
Section 76.5

Brunei, Sulu, and the Spanish Campaigns of 1578

The Spanish expedition against Brunei in 1578 grew from Manila's attempts to assert strategic authority in an already connected maritime world. Brunei was an important Muslim court and trading center with historical relationships to northern Borneo, Sulu, and parts of the Philippine archipelago. From Manila, Spanish governors regarded these connections as obstacles to consolidating their own position and as possible channels through which military or religious influence might challenge Spanish claims. Commercial ambitions and an inherited language of religious rivalry reinforced one another, but neither alone explains the expedition.

Governor Francisco de Sande led a campaign that briefly occupied Brunei's capital before Spanish forces withdrew. The movement of soldiers and ships demonstrated the ability to project force from Manila, not an ability to govern Brunei. Conditions ashore, disease, supply problems, and the resistance or maneuvering of regional actors complicated any plan for sustained occupation. Brunei's political institutions and commerce did not cease to exist because a Spanish commander reported victory. A raid on a center of power had different consequences from a durable annexation.

Spanish efforts to influence Sulu followed within the same phase of expansion. Envoys and soldiers sought acknowledgments of Spanish claims or changes in political relationships, yet brief visits and negotiated undertakings could not create an administrative structure throughout the island chain. Rulers could concede an agreement under immediate pressure and later act independently after the expedition sailed away. The distance between a diplomatic document and everyday obedience is one of the period's recurring historical problems.

The Spanish state was itself dependent on Filipino auxiliaries, Asian maritime labor, provisions, and information. Commanders could not simply transfer the structures of Spanish local government to distant coastal communities. A fort required regular pay and food; control of tributaries required officials and cooperating local leaders; enforcement at sea required vessels suited to the region. These costs mattered because Manila also faced demands from European rivals, transpacific commerce, and defense of its northern possessions.

Sulu and Brunei had their own interests in the results. Their leaders could defend commercial links, adjust diplomatic language, strengthen kin alliances, or cultivate rivals of Spain. The political value of remaining connected to wider Malay networks could outweigh the benefits of a lasting accommodation with a distant colonial capital. None of these choices requires assuming that rulers acted with a complete common strategy. Their responses were situated in local contests for followers and regional influence.

The Brunei expedition also illuminates the difference between maritime diplomacy and land-based colonial rule. Ships could compel temporary negotiation in a harbor without knowing how distant villages supplied a court or how its ruler sustained alliances. When the fleet departed, local institutions continued to mediate trade and political obligations. Subsequent Spanish campaigns are evidence of this gap; authorities returned because they had not obtained durable enforcement. A careful chronology should include the separate 1579 pressure on Maguindanao as part of the same strategic phase, while avoiding the inaccurate assumption that Spanish intentions produced continuous Spanish administration in either Brunei or Sulu.

Spanish records present the expedition within a narrative of imperial initiative. They provide useful evidence for the sequence of movements, the objectives expressed by officials, and claims made afterward. But they are less reliable as direct measures of how much authority was exercised once fleets departed. Independent evidence of continuing local rulers, later Spanish campaigns, and persistent commercial movement makes it clear that possession remained fragmentary. A declaration of submission cannot substitute for evidence that taxes were collected or courts obeyed.

The 1578 campaign therefore marks a beginning of repeated Spanish military confrontation with southern Muslim centers, not the conquest of a ready-made region. The encounter reconfigured relationships among Manila, Brunei, Sulu, and Mindanao without abolishing the agency of their rulers. Its historical significance lies in revealing, early in the colonial era, how difficult it would be to convert temporary maritime superiority into stable dominion across a politically dispersed island world.

Section 76.6

The Spanish Expedition against Mindanao in 1596

Esteban Rodríguez de Figueroa's campaign of 1596 was one of the most ambitious early Spanish attempts to establish a durable southern foothold. He operated under an arrangement with the Crown that linked the prospect of conquest to rights and responsibilities assumed by the expedition's leader. Such contracts reflected the limits of royal resources: the empire could authorize expansion more easily than it could supply the ships, soldiers, food, and continuous administration required to sustain it. Figueroa assembled a force including Spanish soldiers and local allies, hoping to penetrate the Pulangi basin and weaken its ruling centers.

The expedition entered a political landscape dominated by well-established leaders, especially in the Buayan region. Commanders relied on interpreters, pilots, local knowledge, and arrangements with communities whose interests did not necessarily match their own. The rivers and marshes that gave access to inland markets also complicated movement of unfamiliar armed forces. Each crossing or landing required secure supplies, and each delay provided opponents opportunities to reorganize. The campaign's failures cannot be explained by terrain alone; the defending rulers possessed their own military capacity and could draw on networks of followers.

Figueroa was killed during the campaign, a major blow to an expedition built around his personal authority and contractual expectations. Subsequent Spanish forces tried to recover their position, but the attempt did not produce stable occupation of the region. Historians should be cautious with narratives of a single decisive battle: the collapse of a military undertaking usually reflected casualties, supply conditions, leadership disputes, and the ability of local opponents to deny access to important political centers. A leader's death was significant precisely because the expedition's organization gave that leader an unusually central role.

Spanish forces later held or attempted positions such as Tampakan and La Caldera, but these were precarious. A garrison might control the ground on which it stood while having little leverage over river settlements or coastal traffic beyond its immediate reach. Defenders could avoid a strongpoint and continue to move people or goods elsewhere. Maintaining a post imposed an ongoing claim on the colonial treasury and on Philippine communities required to provide provisions and transport. The need to withdraw should therefore be understood as an institutional problem as well as a military setback.

The campaign also affected indigenous politics. Rival leaders could exploit Spanish pressure to strengthen their standing, while alliances against Spain offered occasions for cooperation among groups otherwise divided by competition. A Spanish promise of friendship or tribute recognition might appeal to one court and alarm another. The emergence of such tactical relationships did not make the whole region pro-Spanish or anti-Spanish. For a period, negotiating with colonial officers became one additional way for indigenous leaders to pursue longstanding interests.

The Figueroa episode raises a larger question about the limits of privately organized imperial conquest. The prospect of reward encouraged risk-taking and military investment, but it could also create an expedition vulnerable to leadership loss and uneven financing. A strong local rival might survive by denying supplies or waiting until Spanish troops faced difficult choices about provisioning. The problem was not unique to Mindanao, but here the combination of river geography, skilled resistance, and competing colonial obligations made it especially visible. This institutional explanation is more useful than a morality tale about the courage or incompetence of individual commanders.

The sources through which the expedition is known are heavily weighted toward Spanish eyewitnesses, officials, and subsequent narrators. Their descriptions of defeat often privilege individual heroism, treachery, or the bravery of defenders rather than the full social and logistical structure of the campaign. The surviving record tells relatively little about ordinary workers who transported supplies, farmers whose crops were taken, or families displaced by armed movements. Such silences should prevent confident reconstruction of total casualties and the experience of the surrounding communities.

The key outcome of 1596 was not a permanent closure of the frontier. Instead it established a pattern of intermittent expeditions, contested garrisons, withdrawal, and renewed negotiation. The lesson was apparent from different political perspectives: Manila could deliver an army, but an army's arrival did not dissolve upriver alliances or supply a functioning civil government. Southern rulers could resist and recover, but they also faced the costs of war and changing opportunities for trade. Both dimensions shaped the frontier that developed in subsequent decades.

Section 76.7

Maguindanao, Buayan, and the Consolidation of Regional Power

The political history of the Pulangi basin cannot be reduced to a succession of Spanish attacks. Maguindanao and Buayan competed over influence, access to routes, military followings, and the recognition of other regional leaders. Buayan lay upriver in a position that could connect interior agriculture and movement to downstream commerce. Coastal Maguindanao, with access to river mouths and maritime routes, operated within a different but overlapping sphere. The two centers could cooperate against outsiders while maintaining rival claims over the same networks of datus and settlements.

Rajah Sirongan, also rendered Sirungan in Spanish sources, was a powerful leader associated with Buayan around the turn of the seventeenth century. Buisan, sometimes known as Kapitan Laut or Rajah Buisan, was associated with the Maguindanao leadership rather than simply being a ruler of Buayan. Scholarship distinguishes these figures and also explains that Buisan could at times operate within political arrangements in which Sirongan enjoyed superior influence. Conflating their affiliations obscures the negotiations that made joint campaigns possible.

From the late 1590s through the first years of the seventeenth century, southern forces undertook maritime expeditions against Spanish-controlled settlements. Alliances linked leaders in the Pulangi region with partners from Basilan, Ternate, and other maritime communities. The participants differed in political allegiance, skill, and immediate goal, yet fleet organization allowed them to coordinate attacks over considerable distances. These coalitions demonstrate significant military and diplomatic capacity; they were not evidence of a single permanently unified state.

A telling episode occurred after the 1603 attack on Dulag in Leyte, where the Jesuit Melchor Hurtado was taken captive. Later accounts indicate that Buisan brought the valuable captive into a political environment in which Sirongan and other leading figures had their own bargaining interests. Hurtado's treatment also differed from the fate of many less prestigious captives, underlining how rank and prospective ransom influenced decisions. The incident illustrates negotiations operating through captivity, kin networks, and courtly prestige, not a simple opposition between warfare and diplomacy.

Spanish and indigenous participants subsequently exchanged envoys. Agreements associated with 1605 and 1609 show that truces and negotiations occurred well before the better-known 1645 treaty. Claims that a treaty created final or universal submission should be tested against the actions of different rulers afterward. A leader might accept peace to recover captives, protect commerce, or gain time while remaining unwilling to surrender control of his communities. Likewise, Spanish officers could seek security for exposed settlements without possessing the means to enforce every term of an agreement.

A richer reconstruction must also separate events from retrospective claims about succession. Buisan's position and Sirongan's apparent predominance in some episodes can be understood through the distribution of military resources and diplomatic influence, rather than a simple succession list of rulers. At some moments an admiral or war leader could possess great prestige without commanding every inland household. At others the ruler of an upriver center might dominate negotiations while relying on coastal allies for naval action. Such relationships changed with the scale of a coalition and help explain why Spanish chroniclers sometimes gave apparently conflicting descriptions of the same political network.

Rivalries also created political opportunities. A Maguindanao leader could increase his stature by leading successful expeditions; Buayan rulers could reinforce an upriver power base by controlling access to followers and information; maritime partners could exchange assistance for cargo and influence. Alliances were maintained through material exchange and reputations for protection. When those conditions shifted, cooperation might weaken. This dynamism is why the word alliance should not be taken to mean a fixed organization with a single chain of command.

The emergence of Sultan Kudarat in the following generation must be placed against this prior history rather than portrayed as the spontaneous creation of a southern resistance movement. His political world inherited rival centers, negotiated hierarchies, military methods, religious institutions, and external connections. Appreciating the period of Sirongan and Buisan restores agency to the southern courts and prepares students to understand why subsequent Spanish victories could disturb but not erase the underlying political system.[2]

Section 76.8

Maritime Raiding and Warfare in the Visayas

Maritime raiding became one of the most destructive and politically consequential features of the early modern Philippine frontier. Fleets traveled from southern ports and allied communities toward towns in the Visayas, seeking captives, goods, leverage, or revenge for earlier attacks. Some expeditions had recognized leaders and diplomatic objectives; others drew on smaller coalitions and opportunities created by weak defenses. It is necessary to describe the violence precisely without treating every maritime voyage by a Muslim crew as a raid. Fishermen, merchants, messengers, soldiers, and raiders sometimes used similar waterways and vessels.

Attacks on Panay, Negros, Cebu, Leyte, and neighboring coasts exposed the limits of Spanish claims to protect communities incorporated into the colonial state. A fortified town might receive warning or support while a small coastal village remained vulnerable. Information from traders and local contacts could determine where a fleet landed. Sudden raids targeted boats, food stores, homes, and people; some villages faced repeated insecurity. Yet the chronology was intermittent, shaped by rulers' priorities, truces, other wars, and opportunities rather than a single uninterrupted campaign across the entire period.

The attack on Dulag in 1603 illustrates these dynamics, though surviving narratives emphasize Spanish and missionary experiences. Several residents were taken captive, including Jesuit Melchor Hurtado, whose fate became unusually visible because of his clerical status and the possibility of ransom. Most local captives did not leave comparably detailed records. Their labor, separation from kin, and potential transfer between households were no less historically important. The different quantity of documentation should not be mistaken for a different value attached to their suffering by the historian.

Spanish authorities responded with watch posts, garrisons, naval expeditions, requests for labor, and reliance on armed indigenous auxiliaries. Defenses were uneven and expensive. A stone church or coastal fort could shelter some residents, but it might also concentrate the population, attract attacks, or require substantial work from the very communities it purported to protect. Public funds, tribute, and coerced labor supported shipbuilding and defense. The military costs of raiding therefore extended far beyond the losses suffered during individual attacks.

Violence was not confined to southern fleets. Spanish-led campaigns and their indigenous allies seized vessels, attacked settlements, and captured people who could be sold or transported. Spanish legal and religious justifications sometimes represented their own actions as authorized warfare while describing comparable enemy actions as piracy. This difference in language cannot be accepted without scrutiny. Both the immediate coercion of capture and the wider institutions that made captives valuable require attention, regardless of which side carried out the seizure.

Coastal defense was more than a technological response. A warning post worked only if residents watched it, shared information, and had somewhere safe to go; a fort needed men, supplies, and institutions willing to maintain it. Moving a settlement inland might reduce exposure to raiders while lengthening travel to fishing grounds or markets. A decision that improved security could make daily subsistence harder. These trade-offs were political choices affecting different households unequally. Local initiatives and colonial orders must therefore be distinguished instead of treating all defensive changes as evidence of successful royal policy.

The military burden also changed the relations between Christianized indigenous settlements and colonial authorities. Some people collaborated actively in defense because their communities were threatened; others resented tribute, provisioning, and conscription. An auxiliary force might join a Spanish expedition while maintaining its own rivalry with neighboring communities. Treating all Visayans as passive victims would erase military and political decisions by local leaders. Treating their participation as universal loyalty to Spain would be equally misleading.

The result was a frontier society accustomed to negotiation under pressure. Coastal residents might seek better warning, move fields, construct shelters, negotiate ransoms, or maintain commercial ties across boundaries despite official hostility. A war fleet was part of a wider economy of vessels, supplies, labor, and information. This section therefore places the experience of ordinary people alongside the strategic decisions of rulers. The island frontier was not only where fleets fought; it was where households repeatedly confronted uncertainty about safety, livelihood, and belonging.

Section 76.9

Trade, Political Economy, and Maritime Exchange

Commerce remained essential to Mindanao and Sulu throughout the centuries of intermittent war with the Spanish colonial state. River valleys, coastal ports, and inland producing communities exchanged agricultural commodities, forest goods, marine resources, and imported manufactures. Goods such as wax, resins, foodstuffs, pearls, and other sea products could pass through intermediaries before reaching external buyers. Textiles, metal implements, weapons, and prestige objects arrived through regional circuits linking Borneo, the Moluccas, Manila, and Chinese-facing markets. The precise importance of each commodity changed with place and period; a single catalogue cannot describe every southern economy.

Commercial exchange was also political. Rulers sought to protect harbor access, attract merchants, regulate passage, and demonstrate their capacity to redistribute goods. A datu who could furnish food and protection might gain followers; a sultan who guaranteed trading privileges could strengthen an alliance. Revenue did not depend solely on regular formal taxes. Gifts, duties, transport services, and control of specific exchanges could all matter. These arrangements connected local power to the everyday labor of farming, gathering forest products, fishing, and boatbuilding.

The Pulangi basin provides an example of how inland and coastal interests could overlap. Upriver producers relied on routes passing through settlements with their own leaders. Downriver centers had advantages in negotiating with visiting merchants, but could not automatically compel every upstream community. Control over a river mouth offered bargaining power, not total command. As political circumstances changed, different ports and intermediaries could gain importance. This flexibility helped southern commerce survive military setbacks and occasional disruption of important routes.

Sulu's position was different. The chain of islands offered multiple harbors and connections to northern Borneo. Maritime specialists possessed knowledge of currents, reefs, seasonal passages, and markets that inland rulers might lack. Tausug elites, Sama communities, and other participants were not assigned one fixed occupation by ethnicity; their livelihoods were varied. Nonetheless, seafaring skills allowed goods and people to circulate across a broad zone that Spanish colonial officials could neither fully observe nor easily blockade.

Trade during war was not an anomaly. Rulers might authorize commerce with an adversary at the same time that forces associated with them attacked another settlement. Merchants could obtain protection by gifts or agreements, while governments overlooked trade they could not suppress or needed for supplies. Hostilities sometimes interrupted markets or damaged ports, but political interests also encouraged negotiation. A treaty might make the movement of particular goods safer without extinguishing broader rivalries.

Commerce also provides evidence of the region's integration into wider Asia without suggesting that outside markets dictated every local choice. An imported textile might be redistributed through a court as a symbol of favor; an iron tool might support agricultural production; a coastal product might pass through several hands before reaching a distant merchant. Each transaction involved relationships among producers, transporters, and brokers. A broad description such as China trade does not reveal these intermediate stages. Following commodities from origin to exchange helps reveal the agency of local households and the political benefits rulers obtained from protecting commercial movement.

It would be historically misleading to apply the fully developed late eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Sulu commercial and slave-raiding system wholesale to the period before 1750. James F. Warren's influential reconstruction of the later Sulu Zone concerns a subsequent era of intensified global exchange. Earlier southern markets certainly involved captives and commercial labor, but their scale and mechanisms must be established from evidence appropriate to the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. The distinction prevents an attractive long-run model from replacing chronological analysis.

Trade is central to the chapter because it helps explain both conflict and cooperation. Maritime routes supplied revenue, alliances, weapons, and food; restricting them was a political objective, and protecting them could motivate peace. The same vessels could carry merchants one season and armed men another, but these functions must be distinguished analytically. Southern polities were neither economies organized only for piracy nor passive markets controlled from Manila. They were participants in Southeast Asia's regional political economy, making decisions under changing pressures.

Section 76.10

Ships, Fortifications, Weapons, and Maritime Military Power

Maritime warfare depended on vessels adapted to different conditions. Southeast Asian fleets employed long, relatively shallow-draft craft capable of rowing, sailing, and navigating intricate coastal passages. Terms such as karakoa appear in early Philippine descriptions, but naming conventions varied between languages and sources. Ships described under the same word might differ in size or fittings, while similar craft could bear different names. The historian should reconstruct a vessel's capabilities from contemporary descriptions instead of assuming that later museum illustrations or ethnographic classifications map perfectly onto seventeenth-century practice.

Large war vessels transported fighters and sometimes captives, while smaller boats performed reconnaissance, landing, provisioning, or communication. Rowing allowed maneuver when wind was unfavorable; sails enabled longer movements with less continuous human effort. Operating such fleets required timber, craftsmen, rope, provisions, pilots, and crews accustomed to the sea. The presence of sophisticated local ships demonstrates technological knowledge distributed across specialist communities. Spanish artillery did not make these skills obsolete, especially in shallow channels or during quick raids against dispersed settlements.

Fortifications formed another part of this military environment. A kota could defend a political or residential center with earth, timber, stone, or combinations suited to the place. Its strength depended not only on walls but on its location, defenders, food, and supporting settlements. Spanish forts used cannon and permanent garrisons, but the expenditure required to supply them was considerable. A strongly armed post might dominate its harbor while having limited capacity to pursue opponents who moved through forests, islands, or inland waters.

European vessels likewise had advantages and limitations. Larger sailing ships could carry heavy guns and supplies, but deep draft, dependence on favorable anchorages, and the need for navigational guidance constrained their use in some coastal waters. Spanish commanders therefore relied on locally built craft, indigenous pilots, laborers, and auxiliaries. The military exchange was not between an entirely European technology and an entirely indigenous one. Armaments, shipbuilding methods, and practical knowledge circulated through trade, capture, imitation, and service on mixed crews.

The term lanong is commonly associated with formidable later southern raiding fleets. Some relevant forms and traditions have longer histories, but the most detailed evidence for the large, highly organized lanong-centered raiding system belongs to later centuries. Chapter 76 should not claim that the fleet composition of 1800 was identical to that of 1600. Where earlier texts do not identify a vessel precisely, the safer formulation is oared warship, large canoe, or locally described vessel rather than anachronistic certainty.

Descriptions of naval encounters are especially vulnerable to exaggeration. Chroniclers might report impressive vessel numbers or enormous enemy forces because such figures magnified the glory of a victory or explained an unexpected defeat. A student should ask whether a number came from direct counting, an estimate made at a distance, or later narration. Even an accurate count of hulls would not establish how many fighters, oarsmen, or captives each carried. Quantification is useful only when the evidence and units are specified; vague claims about technologically superior fleets should not replace analysis of crews, supplies, and navigational conditions.

Campaigns involving fortified locations illustrate the interaction of technology and political organization. Capturing a kota with cannon or infantry might create a celebrated victory, yet preventing defenders from retreating or reorganizing elsewhere could be more difficult. Firearms and artillery were valuable but did not settle whether a local population would provide tribute, food, or obedience in later years. Conversely, a ruler's access to fast ships did not guarantee success if provisions failed, allies deserted, or warning systems deprived raiders of surprise.

Military technology therefore belongs within a wider account of labor and logistics. Crews had to be fed, ships repaired, gunpowder supplied, and captured provisions transported. Indigenous craftspeople and seafarers were decisive contributors on several sides of the frontier. Explaining their roles gives students a more accurate account of power than a comparison of weapon names alone and shows why recurrent warfare could burden ordinary communities even when no major battle was being fought.

Kota and Datu

Definitions: Kota refers to a fortification or defended place; datu names a leader exercising authority through kin, followers, alliances, and local resources.

Section 76.11

Captivity, Slavery, Ransom, and Forced Migration

Captivity was among the most consequential experiences produced by the frontier wars. Armed forces seized people in coastal attacks, inland campaigns, and encounters at sea. Captives could be ransomed, held for domestic or agricultural work, assigned to labor in a ruler's household, incorporated into a dependent community, or transferred through markets. Their outcomes depended on age, skill, social standing, captor, and the willingness or ability of relatives or institutions to negotiate release. These practices were not one uniform institution, and no single legal category captures every form of bondage.

Raiding could benefit powerful figures by bringing labor, wealth, and negotiating leverage, but it imposed devastating costs on families whose members disappeared. A captive who knew a skilled trade might be treated differently from a farmer, and a priest could command a ransom that an ordinary villager could not. The surviving record is often unusually detailed for valuable European captives while scarcely recording the names of Filipino women, children, and laborers. The contrast reflects documentary privilege, not necessarily the proportions of those captured.

Southern maritime forces seized people from Spanish-controlled or allied settlements, including in the Visayas. Some captives were used locally and some moved into wider regional circuits. The transfer of people could create new social ties under coercive conditions, but integration into a household did not erase the violence of seizure. A person might acquire dependents, marry, or participate in a new community while remaining unable to choose freely where to live. The historian must distinguish the social transformation of captivity from consent to that condition.

Spanish soldiers and allied indigenous fighters also seized Muslim and other southern captives. In some circumstances Spanish authorities justified enslavement by treating enemies as rebels, pirates, or participants in supposedly lawful wars. Imperial arguments about faith and law interacted with the material demand for servants and workers. Captives could be transported to Manila, where commercial and colonial institutions provided routes to other parts of the Spanish empire. Histories that mention only southern raids conceal the participation of Spanish and allied forces in comparable systems of coercion.

Tatiana Seijas's research on Asian slavery in colonial Mexico demonstrates the global reach of these systems. Some enslaved people moved from the Philippines and other Asian societies across the Pacific on the Manila galleons, entering a colonial world that classified many different Asian origins under broad categories such as chino. That movement does not mean that every person enslaved in the Philippine frontier reached Mexico or that all Asian captives had a southern Philippine origin. It does show that regional military violence and transpacific markets could be linked through institutions that converted human seizure into profit.

The terms slavery, debt bondage, hostage, and war captive overlap in some sources but should not be treated as interchangeable. A person held for ransom could be subject to coercion without necessarily occupying the same status as someone sold as perpetual property. Captivity might be transferred through kin networks, political courts, or markets, and status could change. The historian's responsibility is to describe what happened to people and identify their legal and social position as precisely as evidence allows. Labels drawn from Spanish law may not capture local relationships, while romantic terminology must not conceal forced labor or the loss of freedom.

Ransom and diplomatic negotiation were part of the same political economy. Envoys might discuss the exchange of prisoners alongside tribute, commercial access, or ceasefires. When a high-status captive survived, the fact could enable communication between political enemies, as in the Melchor Hurtado episode. Yet transactions that restored one person's freedom could leave other prisoners in bondage. A treaty's provisions regarding release therefore need careful evaluation: who counted as a protected subject, who was included, and who remained outside the agreement?

The social costs extended beyond captives themselves. Relatives sought information and funds; settlements adjusted defenses or moved; rulers demanded vessels and fighting men; coastal trade became uncertain; and people conscripted for military service faced the risk of capture. The issue is not simply whether raiding was profitable but how systems of coercion redistributed life chances across the frontier. Captivity belongs at the center of this chapter because it links maritime warfare to social hierarchy, state formation, household labor, imperial finance, and the lived consequences of contested sovereignty.[4]

Section 76.12

Frontier Communities, Local Alliances, and Civilian Survival

For coastal and riverine communities, the frontier was not an abstract contest between sultans and governors. It was a set of obligations and risks that shaped cultivation, fishing, marriage, trade, and the timing of travel. Families needed access to food and water, kin networks, religious spaces, and safe routes. A campaign could interrupt the planting season or make a familiar coast dangerous. A fort could offer refuge while demanding labor to construct it. The social history of the frontier emerges from the interaction of these competing needs.

Different communities faced different choices. Christianized Visayan towns might be required to furnish sailors, rowers, provisions, or soldiers for Spanish operations. Some residents welcomed military protection after attacks; others viewed new obligations as another burden imposed by colonial officers. Muslim coastal settlements could support rulers' fleets or seek to avoid their demands. Non-Muslim indigenous communities in Mindanao were not simply bystanders: they negotiated trading access, protection, tribute, and alliance in ways shaped by their own leaders and local circumstances.

The category of ally also requires scrutiny. A community that supplied fighters for a Spanish campaign might still resist some forms of tribute or refuse to abandon its independent trade. Rulers could expect loyalty without possessing constant enforcement power. A settlement might cooperate with one invading fleet to counter another threat or provide intelligence in return for guarantees. Such arrangements were contingent rather than permanent statements of ethnic or religious identity. Political survival often encouraged strategies that official narratives later described as inconsistent loyalty.

Civilians experienced raids in unequal ways. Wealthier residents sometimes possessed better access to protection or ransom; poorer households could be exposed when defensible places were limited. Women, children, and elderly people might remain in a settlement when mobile fighters left, though sources seldom describe their decisions in detail. The loss of boats, seed, animals, or fishing equipment could damage livelihoods for seasons after a raid. Reconstructing these effects requires asking what economic resources were actually destroyed or transferred, not simply counting battles.

The colonial state also generated threats through its own preparations. Building fleets and garrisons required timber, food, water, and labor. Some labor was paid, while other service could be demanded under coercive arrangements. Military provisioning diverted resources from ordinary production and could intensify disputes with local leaders. The cost of warfare thus included burdens imposed by the state that claimed to protect its subjects. The same ambiguity applied to southern rulers whose demands for service or tribute could protect followers while constraining them.

Consider a community that traded with one political center but was expected to supply soldiers to another. If conflict intensified, its leaders might face demands that could not both be satisfied. To understand the resulting decisions, historians should reconstruct relationships before an attack: kin marriages, market dependence, tribute obligations, proximity to a fort, and prior encounters with armed groups. This approach avoids reading every change of allegiance as betrayal. It also demonstrates why an agreement signed far away could fail locally even if both principal rulers had sincere reasons to seek peace.

The everyday-life record is fragmentary. Spanish clerical registers, campaign reports, and accounts of forced migration do not reveal a continuous diary of an ordinary frontier household. Instead, historians combine evidence about settlement location, taxation, food production, refuge, religious routines, and recovery after violence. Where a particular reconstruction is plausible but not directly documented, it must be marked as an inference rather than narrated as an eyewitness scene. This method safeguards historical precision while still allowing attention to people otherwise absent from elite records.

Understanding civilians changes the interpretation of diplomacy. A truce mattered not only because a ruler signed it but because it might reopen a market, permit travel, release some captives, or reduce the risk of sudden attacks. Its failure might have immediate effects on vulnerable communities long before a major army moved. The frontier's history is therefore a history of lived uncertainty and negotiation as much as a succession of military campaigns. It should be studied through both the language of sovereignty and the material conditions of survival.

Section 76.13

The Spanish Fortification of Zamboanga in 1635

The construction of a permanent Spanish military foothold at Zamboanga in 1635 marked an important change in the frontier strategy. The location near the western end of Mindanao offered access toward Basilan and the Sulu islands while providing a base from which vessels could patrol surrounding waters. Spanish officials hoped that a fortified position would protect settlements farther north, complicate the movement of raiding fleets, and support missions and expeditions. These objectives were connected but not identical: preventing attacks, projecting imperial authority, and spreading Christianity required different resources and forms of local cooperation.

The chronology of the fort's construction deserves precision. Work began in June 1635 under the interim governorship of Juan Cerezo de Salamanca, before Sebastián Hurtado de Corcuera formally assumed office that month. Captain Juan de Chaves and the Jesuit engineer Melchor de Vera were associated with the undertaking. The National Museum of the Philippines identifies June 23, 1635, as the start of construction and notes the participation of Spaniards and Visayan auxiliaries. Later military administration and construction were closely associated with Corcuera's governorship, but attributing the original foundation solely to him obscures the sequence.

The site was first a military and logistical undertaking rather than an extensive territorial government. Its defenders needed regular food, ammunition, repairs, and communication with colonial authorities. Ships bringing supplies were vulnerable to weather and attack. A garrison could operate effectively only while supported by labor and by nearby communities willing or compelled to provide resources. Political relations with local non-Muslim populations and with coastal trading groups could therefore be as important as the strength of the walls themselves.

Jesuit participation added a religious and diplomatic dimension. Missionaries could provide interpreters, intelligence, regional contacts, and proposals for settlement or conversion. At the same time, the presence of armed missionaries or mission stations associated with a fort could prompt resistance from rulers who saw religion and imperial claims as intertwined. The frontier produced differing priorities among soldiers, priests, provincial officials, and the households affected by military labor demands. It would be inaccurate to assume a fully coordinated colonial plan carried out without disagreement.

For Muslim rulers the new position threatened particular routes and offered new bargaining opportunities. A war fleet might avoid a patrol area, attack an isolated settlement, or negotiate safe commerce. Inland leaders were less directly vulnerable to a coastal cannon position, but could still be affected when ships carrying goods or messengers encountered Spanish surveillance. The influence of the fort thus depended on networks extending beyond its immediate physical range. Its strategic importance was real without being unlimited.

Historical heritage should be distinguished from historical chronology. Modern Fort Pilar is a prominent surviving landmark, but its walls and names reflect construction, alteration, abandonment, and rebuilding over a long period. A present-day visitor should not assume every visible feature dates from 1635. Architectural history, archaeological work, and documentary records can identify successive phases. This matters because the changing fort materializes the argument of Chapter 76: a southern imperial foothold was repeatedly reconstructed as financial circumstances, military threats, and the expectations of local communities changed.

The fortress also became a symbol in later colonial accounts, sometimes represented as a decisive forward line separating Christianity from Islam. Such imagery simplifies the historical situation. Spanish missions and allies existed beyond particular walls, while Muslim merchants and travelers continued to move across the surrounding region. Zamboanga's political and commercial life involved communities with various languages and loyalties. The fort could anchor a Spanish presence but could not make the region homogeneous or end the independent operation of southern sultanates.

The history after 1635 confirms these limitations. Spanish troops expanded operations from the base, confronted southern rulers, negotiated peace, and eventually withdrew under pressure from other imperial priorities. Its establishment should therefore be treated as a significant but reversible strategic investment. It changed what Spanish commanders could attempt; it did not guarantee that they could sustain those attempts. The chronology and practical functioning of the fort help explain why the subsequent campaigns of the 1630s were dramatic yet did not settle the question of southern sovereignty.[6]

Section 76.14

Sebastián Hurtado de Corcuera and the Campaigns of 1637–1638

Sebastián Hurtado de Corcuera's campaigns in 1637 and 1638 were among the best known Spanish offensives against southern Muslim polities in the seventeenth century. They followed the creation of the Zamboanga position and reflected an attempt to move from defense and intermittent punitive expeditions toward more ambitious military pressure. Colonial accounts presented victories as evidence that royal power could overcome southern resistance. Yet the objectives, course, and consequences of the campaigns need to be separated if the historian is to evaluate what the Spanish actually achieved.

The 1637 campaign targeted positions associated with Sultan Kudarat in Mindanao. Spanish forces used artillery, soldiers, and indigenous auxiliaries to attack fortified sites and disrupt the ruler's immediate military position. Kudarat survived and later rebuilt his influence. A victory report recorded the success of an attacking army at a particular site; it did not establish the dissolution of a political system sustained by followers, alternative routes, and allied leaders. Rulers able to retreat and reorganize retained options unavailable to a garrison that depended on fixed supply lines.

In 1638 the Spanish attacked Jolo, an important Sulu political center. The operation was substantial and costly, and contemporary accounts celebrated Spanish achievement. But Sulu's maritime and political networks extended beyond the specific positions occupied. An enemy could abandon a strongpoint while maintaining commercial and family ties across islands and toward Borneo. Governing those networks would have required more than possessing one harbor; it would have required accepted officers, continuing military reach, and compliance among communities outside the immediate garrison.

Both campaigns depended heavily on non-Spanish resources. Indigenous auxiliaries supplied fighting capacity, local navigation, transport, and provisions. Some were motivated by defense of communities previously exposed to raiding; others participated under colonial pressure or sought advantages in local rivalries. The crews that carried armies through coastal passages possessed knowledge not supplied by European officers alone. The campaigns were therefore joint operations within an unequal colonial structure, not isolated accomplishments of a small European force.

The human costs were considerable but often imprecisely documented. Spanish chroniclers could amplify enemy losses, emphasize heroic episodes, or underreport damage caused by their own troops. Accounts of captured people and destroyed settlements need the same scrutiny as reports of battlefield deaths. Retreating residents, families separated during campaigns, and laborers compelled to supply fleets rarely receive sustained treatment. A responsible reconstruction should identify clearly documented movements and outcomes while avoiding unsupported numerical certainty.

A comparison of the 1637 Mindanao and 1638 Jolo campaigns should ask not merely which was more successful but how their different targets changed the possibilities for occupation. Attacking a river-centered political network was not identical to attacking an island port with wide maritime connections. Both allowed defenders to regroup beyond the area occupied by Spanish soldiers. Distinct strategic environments therefore produced a common outcome for reasons that must still be explained separately. This comparative method avoids treating the two years as a single victory and preserves the individuality of Maguindanao and Sulu political history.

The immediate gains also generated new demands. Forts and military positions required occupation, while further campaigns needed resources that Manila might not provide indefinitely. European competition in the region, especially with the Dutch, continued to absorb colonial attention. A governor who could organize an expedition in one year might find that political priorities or available ships changed the next. The military chronology therefore cannot be understood apart from imperial finance and wider Asian maritime competition.

Corcuera's campaigns altered diplomatic calculations without producing permanent subjugation. Subsequent negotiations with Kudarat and Sulu rulers demonstrate that Spanish officials still had to deal with these leaders as political actors. This is not proof that the campaigns were insignificant. They changed the terms of conflict, affected settlements, and encouraged diplomacy. Their lasting lesson is narrower than the triumphal language of some sources: tactical success and occupation of selected positions did not equal comprehensive colonial sovereignty.

Section 76.15

Sultan Kudarat, Political Recovery, and the Peace of 1645

Sultan Kudarat, a major seventeenth-century Maguindanao ruler, confronted Spanish campaigns while navigating the complicated political world of the Pulangi basin. His authority depended on alliances, relationships with datus, access to trade, and the ability to secure followers. It was not a modern territorial state whose institutions could be captured by taking one palace. Following the attacks of the late 1630s, Kudarat and his associates continued political activity and sought arrangements that would protect their standing. His subsequent diplomatic negotiations demonstrate the survival of meaningful independent authority.

Religious and political considerations interacted. Missionaries represented the Spanish demand for Christian expansion, while Muslim rulers had reasons to resist institutions that might encourage subjects to recognize competing sources of loyalty. Yet the question was also practical. Safe movement, access to markets, the status of captives, and the security of settlements mattered to both sides. A ruler could negotiate with Jesuits without accepting Spanish sovereignty, and an imperial governor could tolerate a Muslim sultan's continued power if that provided security for trade or defense.

A peace agreement dated June 24, 1645, was negotiated with Kudarat under Governor Diego Fajardo's administration. The Spanish negotiating party included Francisco de Atienza Ibáñez, governor of Zamboanga, and the Jesuit Alejandro López. Scholarly work identifies a Spanish-language documentary record of the capitulations in a Jesuit archive, providing a stronger basis than unattributed treaty summaries. Such a document records claims and undertakings in the vocabulary of its producers; it cannot by itself establish how all parties interpreted those commitments or whether every provision was later enforced.

Published scholarship describes provisions involving missionary residence or access, political relationships, commerce, and security. One concern was the protection of Spanish maritime movement against hostile attacks while European naval rivals threatened communications with New Spain. Spanish governors valued the possibility of safer coastal routes; missionaries valued permission to establish or maintain religious institutions. Kudarat could seek relief from immediate pressure and recognition of arrangements compatible with his continuing rule. These interests overlapped sufficiently for a treaty even when basic religious and political differences remained.

Language about friendship, tribute, boundaries, or permissions requires close reading. A Spanish official might understand consent to missionary activity as movement toward imperial integration. A Muslim ruler might treat it as a negotiated allowance with limits, subject to local relationships and changing circumstances. An agreement made between prominent negotiators did not automatically bind every local leader. Distinguishing the text of a treaty from later reports of its effectiveness is essential for evaluating how diplomacy operated in a polity with distributed political authority.

The 1645 treaty offers a model for differentiating archival evidence from interpretation. Its Spanish-language record can establish what negotiators said they agreed to, but historians must still ask whether the provisions were implemented and how parties with different political traditions understood terms such as friendship or jurisdiction. Missionary chronicles written afterward may celebrate openings for evangelization; a Maguindanao-centered narrative may emphasize the ruler's continued independence. The coexistence of those interpretations is not necessarily evidence that the document was fraudulent. It may reflect an agreement made possible because each party could present its outcome differently.

The treaty also challenges a narrative built only around armed victories. The colonial state negotiated because military conquest had not eliminated the southern ruler's capacity to make war, disrupt travel, or influence regional alliances. Kudarat negotiated because prolonged conflict carried costs and offered few guarantees of absolute security. Neither decision should be reduced to surrender. Diplomacy could be a means of preserving resources, containing enemies, and reorganizing political options after a costly period of confrontation.

The 1645 settlement belongs alongside earlier agreements involving southern rulers and the separate Sulu peace of 1646. Together they reveal a frontier where treaties were recurrent political instruments. Some terms were temporary, others reshaped relationships for longer periods, and each depended on people and institutions that could change. Kudarat's historical importance lies not only in military resistance but in his capacity to build authority and negotiate within an international and inter-island political environment without disappearing into Spanish colonial government.[3]

Sulu, Rajah Bungsu, and Diplomacy with the Dutch

The Sultanate of Sulu responded to Spanish military pressure through a combination of defensive maneuver, commercial calculation, and diplomacy with several external powers. Rajah Bungsu, also known in some sources as Muwallil Wasit, led a polity whose influence depended on Jolo and wider maritime networks. The attack on Jolo in 1638 did not eliminate his political relevance or prevent subsequent negotiation. Sulu's rulers had commercial relationships in Borneo and elsewhere and could adjust their position within an expanding field of Spanish and Dutch rivalry.

The Dutch presence in Asian waters complicated Spanish strategy. Operating through the VOC and regional alliances, Dutch forces challenged Iberian positions and shipping. The southern Philippines were not simply a stage on which European powers fought: Sulu leaders assessed whether cooperation with the Dutch might improve bargaining power or help resist Spanish occupation. Such arrangements could be temporary and subject to disagreement among elite factions. The existence of an external ally did not transfer Sulu's political objectives to Batavia.

Scholarship on mid-seventeenth-century Jesuit records describes contacts between Sulu's leadership and Dutch officers during the crisis of the 1640s. The presence of Dutch ships near Jolo in 1645 and the movement of Sulu representatives toward Dutch allies illustrate the multiple diplomatic directions open to the court. Spanish commanders could not assume that negotiations with Sulu occurred in isolation. Commercial rivalries at Manila, the Moluccas, and Batavia helped shape the military resources and threats that each party could mobilize.

On April 14, 1646, a peace and friendship agreement was concluded at Lipir with Rajah Bungsu. A modern study identifies the date, the parties, and Spanish descriptions of payments or deliveries associated with the arrangement. Published excerpts refer to vessels or cargoes of rice described in language combining gratitude, brotherhood, and obligations. The precise meaning of such terms requires reading them in their documentary setting. They should not be simplified into proof that Sulu had become effectively governed by Spanish officials.

Spanish forces withdrew from their position at Jolo in the mid-1640s, and the settlement was compatible with the continued operation of the Sulu court. Withdrawal could reflect military expense, the danger of naval rivalry, and the political value of a negotiated relationship. Sulu leaders likewise had reasons to resume commerce and limit immediate attack. The decision was strategic, not necessarily an admission that earlier confrontations had been meaningless. A fort's evacuation changed the military map but did not erase the experiences of communities affected by its occupation.

The 1646 settlement should be taught beside the Maguindanao treaty rather than inserted as an isolated date. Both agreements came after major military campaigns, both addressed security or movement, and both occurred amid competing imperial demands. Yet their parties and provisions were not interchangeable. Sulu's court had different connections to Borneo and maritime trade, and Dutch involvement was more immediately visible in some accounts. Comparisons must therefore identify the common logic of negotiation while preserving the distinctive bargaining resources of each ruler. The goal is not to rank treaties as Spanish victory or defeat but to evaluate how they altered practical relations.

Trade had a central place in diplomacy. If merchants and vessels could move with lower risk, rulers might gain revenue and prestige, and Spanish officials might obtain supplies or reduce pressure on northern settlements. The boundaries between gift, tribute, debt, and commercial payment were not always clear in multilingual negotiations. A Spanish transcript might preserve the wording favored by imperial officials while omitting the cultural or political understandings of Sulu's representatives. Historians should therefore distinguish agreement on an immediate exchange from agreement on ultimate sovereignty.

The Sulu case shows the limits of a purely Spanish-centered chronology. A series of Spanish victories and retreats would be incomplete without the sultanate's own choices to cultivate multiple connections. Diplomacy with Dutch or Spanish officials was one instrument in a broader political strategy shaped by maritime commerce, elite relationships, and the welfare or exploitation of dependent communities. By the middle of the seventeenth century, Sulu was neither securely annexed nor untouched by colonial pressure. It remained a consequential political actor within an increasingly crowded maritime world.[3]

Lake Lanao, Maranao Political Society, and the Limits of Conquest

Lake Lanao and the surrounding highlands reveal why the southern frontier cannot be understood only through ports and coastal fleets. Maranao communities inhabited a region whose lake, upland routes, agricultural lands, and local political networks supported a history distinct from that of Jolo or the Pulangi delta. Authority was distributed among many leaders and lineages rather than concentrated under one ruler whom an invading force could remove. The political landscape was also connected to lowland societies through exchange, marriage, and competition; upland did not mean isolated.

From a coastal base, Spanish soldiers could move toward Iligan and attempt to penetrate the interior, but the undertaking demanded knowledge of trails, waterways, and the locations of cooperative and hostile communities. A military party might negotiate with one datu without thereby acquiring the allegiance of other leaders. The lake itself offered transportation and defense to people who knew it, while presenting a formidable logistical problem for newcomers who lacked boats or access to local supplies.

Campaigns in 1639 and 1640 demonstrate these difficulties. Accounts describe an expedition involving Francisco de Atienza and the Augustinian Recollect Agustín de San Pedro, followed by efforts to establish a more permanent presence. An often repeated narrative describes boats transported in components overland and assembled on the lake. Details such as the size of forces and the experience of local inhabitants vary among sources and should be checked rather than repeated as unquestioned numerical facts. What is clear is that Spanish forces tried to convert exploratory movement into political control and met determined limitations.

Maranao leaders could negotiate, resist, retreat, or attempt to use rivalries among outsiders. Defensive works known as kota exploited strategic locations and local materials. A strongpoint was not necessarily the center of an empire; it could protect a particular community or lineage. Spanish troops holding one route or settlement could not assume free movement through the surrounding territory. Local autonomy therefore rested on more than the military advantage of elevation. It involved social organization that allowed communities to maintain or rebuild authority independently of outside commands.

The experience of the interior also complicates the image of a common Muslim military front. Maranao leaders had relationships with Maguindanao and other Islamic centers, but they did not automatically accept every decision made by a coastal sultan. Religious learning and shared practices could coexist with competition over influence. Political agreements reached at a distant court did not necessarily settle access to upland villages. This is one reason colonial records that described a treaty with a major ruler cannot be used to infer submission across Mindanao as a whole.

The Lake Lanao case also reveals why source terminology requires attention. Spanish writers could describe upland communities as independent, hostile, or subdued depending on the stage of a campaign and on their own diplomatic objectives. Local leadership systems, however, cannot be reconstructed solely from military labels. Evidence of alliances, fortifications, settlements, and later tradition helps restore a more complex picture. The difference between passing through a territory, obtaining temporary cooperation from some datus, and maintaining a functioning administration is especially clear in the interior. These are distinct historical outcomes and must not be conflated.

The human costs of an expedition extended to the surrounding area. Guides and porters might be recruited or compelled to provide service; crops and dwellings could be damaged; and communities negotiating with armed strangers risked hostility from neighboring leaders. The surviving texts often concentrate on European officers, clerics, and military movements, leaving the actions of ordinary Maranao families difficult to reconstruct. Historians can identify the structural pressures without inventing individualized scenes unsupported by the archive.

Lake Lanao illustrates the intersection of environment and political institutions. The highland setting obstructed some external operations, but it was community organization, local alliances, intelligence, and resistance that turned geographical obstacles into a sustained political constraint. The results of 1639–1640 did not create permanent Spanish governance of the lake region. A history centered only on Zamboanga or the coast would therefore miss one of the clearest examples of the limits of colonial expansion within Mindanao itself.[7]

Section 76.18

Withdrawal, Reoccupation, and the Changing Zamboanga Frontier

The history of the Spanish position at Zamboanga after the great campaigns reveals the contingent character of imperial power. Fortification in 1635 strengthened Manila's ability to intervene in the south, yet maintaining the position required resources that could be redirected elsewhere. A garrison was never self-sufficient. It depended on pay, provisions, ships, labor, and cooperation from neighboring settlements. When threats emerged closer to the imperial capital, those needs became politically difficult to defend against other priorities.

The crisis associated with Zheng Chenggong, known in many sources as Koxinga, brought such a reordering. In the early 1660s, Spanish leaders feared a possible attack on Manila. Forces and resources were concentrated for the defense of the capital, and the withdrawal from Zamboanga is normally dated to 1663. The decision did not mean that Muslim polities had suddenly defeated the garrison in a decisive local engagement. It reflected the colonial administration's judgment that the danger to Manila outweighed the value of maintaining its southern forward position.

The chronology requires nuance. Some heritage accounts record Jesuit activity or rebuilding after the evacuation, including an episode in 1666, while the major renewed fortification and sustained military re-establishment are associated with 1718–1719. These events should not be collapsed into a simple statement that the site was wholly unoccupied between 1663 and 1718. A mission presence, a repaired structure, and a fully financed presidio were different kinds of occupation. The key historical question is the extent and function of actual Spanish authority at each stage.

For neighboring rulers and communities, the withdrawal changed the practical balance of power. Routes that had been monitored by a garrison could become easier to use, and local leaders could reassess alliances or trading arrangements. People who had supplied the fort might lose an important customer or an oppressive demand. Others who had experienced raids from the sea might lose a perceived line of defense. The consequences depended on the community's own position; a single narrative of liberation or abandonment would not capture them all.

The renewed construction of a substantial Spanish position in the early eighteenth century reflected another shift in imperial priorities. Officials sought again to protect routes, support missions, and influence relations with surrounding polities. A rebuilt fort could improve the capacity to patrol some waterways, but Spain still lacked the resources to police every island and river basin. Reoccupation did not reverse decades of independent political development or make treaties unnecessary. Renewed presence was a strategic adaptation within a continuing contested region.

A chronology of Zamboanga is particularly suitable for studying continuity and change because the institution could survive in several forms. The Spanish government might withdraw soldiers while missionaries retained interest; a fortification might be repaired without the return of a large garrison; a later construction campaign could reuse an older site while changing its purpose. Students should locate the evidence for each phase and avoid equating stone walls with permanent rule. This distinction makes the changing southern frontier a case study in the relationship between military infrastructure, administrative continuity, and local acceptance.

Changes at Zamboanga were tied to events far away. European rivalries, naval defense of Manila, the movement of galleons, and the financial health of the colony all shaped decisions about distant garrisons. A governor in Manila had to weigh expensive opportunities against the danger of leaving the capital insufficiently protected. Southern rulers made analogous decisions about where to direct fleets or court alliances, though their administrative and financial systems differed. The frontier was therefore connected to broader imperial and regional calculations on both sides.

Taken together, the sequence of 1635, 1663, and 1718–1719 rejects a linear history of unbroken Spanish advance. Power expanded, contracted, and was renegotiated. The continuity was not permanent military control but persistent political competition, commerce, and the survival of locally rooted institutions. Examining these phases helps students understand a crucial historical distinction: a state can claim sovereignty for centuries while the territory to which that claim refers remains subject to changing and incomplete forms of effective power.

Section 76.19

Frontier Identities, Religious Representations, and Historical Memory

The long frontier struggle left records shaped by the language and priorities of its participants. Spanish officials needed to justify campaigns, expenses, and appointments; missionaries needed to explain religious work and threats to congregations. Their texts therefore often portray armed conflict within a moral frame contrasting Christian subjects with Muslim adversaries. Such descriptions are historically significant because they influenced policy and memory, but they cannot be treated as transparent representations of the people described. A victory report or sermon speaks from an institution with interests of its own.

Antonio de Morga's early seventeenth-century narrative and Francisco Combés's later history differ in authorial position and experience, yet both belong to the written world of Spanish colonial power. Morga was a lay official whose history appeared in Mexico in 1609. Combés was a Jesuit who served in the southern mission environment; his detailed history of Mindanao and Jolo was published posthumously in Madrid in 1667, after his death in 1665. The timing and character of these publications should inform their use as evidence.

Combés's work brings together geographical description, missionary history, military action, and assessments of communities encountered by the Jesuits. It can preserve valuable information not easily recovered elsewhere. But its very subtitle, referring to progress of religion and Catholic arms, shows that the author was not a neutral modern ethnographer. Statements about Muslim rulers or religious customs must be examined alongside the purposes of a mission-based narrative and, wherever possible, compared with other records. Its detail makes critical reading more necessary, not less.

Later Spanish and Philippine cultural traditions continued to represent conflict between Christians and Moros, sometimes through dramatic performances or simplified stories of raid and defense. The chronology of those particular forms of representation matters. A nineteenth- or twentieth-century festival cannot automatically be used as evidence of how seventeenth-century islanders imagined themselves. The history of memory involves later reinterpretation, selective emphasis, and changing political uses of the past. The same event may acquire new meaning when invoked under another colonial government or modern nation-state.

Muslim genealogies, oral traditions, regional chronicles, and surviving material culture provide different ways of recovering the southern perspective. They are indispensable but require their own source criticism. A genealogy written or edited long after an event may preserve important political memory while reorganizing succession to legitimize later rulers. Oral traditions can retain local names and relationships absent from Spanish archives, but they cannot always supply precise chronology without corroboration. Contrasting source traditions is a method of inquiry, not a choice between assuming colonial texts are true and assuming all later testimony is equally contemporary.

An ethical approach to source criticism resists replacing colonial prejudice with an equally uncritical heroic narrative. Muslim rulers were capable political actors who could defend followers and negotiate, but their courts could also demand service and benefit from captivity. Spanish officials used religious justification and committed violence, but Christianized indigenous villagers might have concrete reasons to request protection. Source comparison should reconstruct the motives and constraints of multiple groups without flattening them into categories of villain and victim. The strongest historical account identifies the power relations in each episode and remains attentive to people for whom all major rulers were distant authorities.

The greatest archival silences concern people with little institutional power. Captives, women, ordinary fighters, boatbuilders, farm laborers, and inland households appear most often when officials needed to count their labor or losses. A captured priest might leave a long account while dozens of other captives remain unnamed. The challenge is to expose these inequalities of documentation without inventing testimony. Careful language—identifying what a source explicitly records, what may be inferred, and what remains unknown—is essential to responsible social history.

The term Moro itself belongs to the history of representation. It condensed multiple communities into a colonial religious category, but later generations could reclaim or contest that category in different political circumstances. Readers should therefore avoid two errors: treating the name as a timeless ethnic unity and assuming that colonial vocabulary determined every local person's identity. Chapter 76 ends with the early modern frontier; later political histories must explain on their own evidence how new forms of collective identity emerged.[1]

Section 76.20

The Moro-Spanish Frontiers in Early Modern Southeast Asian History

The history of the Moro-Spanish frontiers from the late sixteenth century to approximately 1750 is a history of competing institutions rather than the march of one empire toward inevitable control. Spanish authorities expanded from Manila into southern waters, sent armies, occupied selected positions, and attempted to protect Christianized settlements. Muslim rulers in Sulu, Maguindanao, Buayan, and other centers organized military defenses, traded across the seas, cultivated alliances, and negotiated treaties. Neither side commanded every community it claimed to represent. Political relationships remained layered, local, and changeable.

Repeated Spanish victories did not necessarily produce lasting governance. The campaigns of 1578, 1596, and 1637–1638 demonstrate different combinations of naval reach, tactical success, logistical failure, withdrawal, and negotiated outcome. The Zamboanga fort improved Spanish military capacity but required external resources and was abandoned in 1663 amid wider imperial threats. Its later rebuilding revealed renewed ambition without demonstrating comprehensive sovereignty. The persistence of independent sultanates and upland political societies followed from institutions and strategies as well as geography.

Conflict was not simply a series of battles between faiths. Islam and Christianity shaped political language, communal institutions, and moral arguments; rulers and missionaries invoked them in concrete situations. At the same time, control over ports, followers, river routes, commercial exchanges, and captives provided practical motives for confrontation. Religion could strengthen alliances or intensify hostility without eliminating the possibility of truces, exchanges, and diplomacy. Historical explanation must treat these causes together and ask when each mattered most.

Captivity and slavery are particularly important for evaluating the frontier beyond the perspective of rulers. Southern raiders seized people from exposed settlements, and Spanish and indigenous allied forces enslaved captives of their own. Some people were ransomed or incorporated into households; some were transferred through long-distance commercial systems that linked Asian ports to Spanish America. These experiences reveal how imperial conflict affected labor, family, mobility, and power. The violence endured by ordinary people cannot be assessed solely by counting forts taken or treaties signed.

Diplomacy was neither a sign of friendship without conflict nor proof of submission. The 1645 settlement involving Sultan Kudarat and the 1646 agreement with Sulu's Rajah Bungsu grew from a political environment in which multiple actors still possessed bargaining power. Treaties could create conditions for safer movement or missionary activity while leaving fundamental questions of sovereignty unresolved. They show that frontier politics involved selective cooperation as well as military opposition, and that agreements had to be interpreted within the political institutions of each side.

The frontier also raises a question relevant to wider comparative history: what makes a political claim effective? A ruler may issue titles, demand tribute, send an army, or obtain a treaty. These are evidence of power, but not identical proof of comprehensive government. Effective authority requires recurring relationships through which commands are enforced, disputes resolved, and resources collected. In Mindanao and Sulu those relationships remained plural and uneven. The lesson extends beyond Southeast Asia, but its explanation here must remain grounded in particular ports, lake communities, military decisions, commercial routes, and documented agreements of the early modern period.

Source criticism changes the chapter's conclusions. Spanish governors and Jesuit writers provide much of the surviving detailed chronology, but their categories and claims cannot automatically substitute for the intentions of Maguindanao, Sulu, or Maranao leaders. Later oral and genealogical traditions expand the field of inquiry while presenting different methodological problems. A defensible synthesis must recognize both the possibilities and the boundaries of the evidence. It should preserve names, dates, and causal distinctions where verifiable, and acknowledge uncertainty where the record is thin.

By 1750 the southern archipelago had not been absorbed into a uniform Spanish colonial system. Spanish-held footholds existed and mattered, but significant Muslim polities and many inland communities maintained political structures outside effective Spanish rule. Their relationships with Brunei, Borneo, the Moluccas, and neighboring seas demonstrate that the region continued to participate actively in Southeast Asian history. The conclusion is not that contact had no effect; colonial campaigns, migration, captivity, missionary activity, and commerce had transformed many lives. It is that those transformations occurred without a single victorious political settlement, leaving a history of negotiation and uneven authority for later chapters to investigate.

Chapter Summary

The Moro-Spanish frontier was a changing political and maritime contact zone rather than a stable boundary dividing two homogeneous religious communities. By the time Spanish forces reached the southern Philippines, Sulu, Maguindanao, Buayan, and other centers possessed their own institutions, alliances, and trading networks. The geography of islands, rivers, and uplands influenced the reach of their rulers, but political decisions and social relationships mattered just as much as terrain. Spanish efforts to impose imperial authority encountered this preexisting complexity rather than a political vacuum.

The Sultanate of Sulu and the political world of the Pulangi basin did not share one centralized government. Sultans and datus exercised authority through kinship, followers, protection, commercial resources, and religious legitimacy. Relations between coastal Maguindanao and upriver Buayan shifted over time. Rajah Sirongan and Kapitan Laut Buisan illustrate how military leadership and courtly influence might be distributed across allied but distinct political centers. Defeating one leader or taking one fort therefore did not necessarily dissolve broader networks of political action.

The term Moro was introduced by Spanish officials from Iberian religious vocabulary and applied to multiple Muslim populations. Its apparent unity disguised differences among Tausug, Maguindanaon, Maranao, Sama, and other communities. Religion nevertheless mattered deeply: Islam shaped southern political legitimacy and social institutions, while Catholicism shaped the Spanish monarchy's claims and missionary ambitions. Neither faith, however, dictated every alliance, treaty, or commercial decision. Colonial terminology must be analyzed as evidence of political classification rather than accepted as neutral social description.

Spanish expeditions against Brunei and Sulu in 1578 and the attempt to occupy Mindanao in 1596 revealed the distance between maritime military power and territorial control. Later campaigns under Corcuera in 1637 and 1638 achieved important tactical results but did not eliminate southern political structures. Spanish successes depended on Filipino allies, ships, supplies, and fixed garrisons, all of which imposed costs. Southern rulers could defend particular centers, withdraw to other positions, reconstitute alliances, and sustain commerce beyond the range of colonial forts.

Raiding and captive-taking shaped frontier life on multiple sides. Southern fleets attacked towns in the Visayas, but Spanish troops and indigenous auxiliaries also seized and enslaved prisoners. Captives might be sold, held for labor, incorporated into households, or ransomed; these outcomes differed according to circumstances and social status. Research on transpacific slavery shows that the consequences could extend from Asian ports into colonial Mexico. The social and economic damage to families and communities must be included alongside military narratives of victories and defeats.

Trade and diplomacy continued amid warfare. Muslim southern centers exchanged food, marine products, forest commodities, and imported goods through networks linked to Borneo, the Moluccas, Manila, and other Asian ports. Rulers sought maritime security and political prestige as well as material gains. Agreements associated with earlier seventeenth-century rulers and the better documented 1645 treaty with Sultan Kudarat reveal how negotiation could serve several parties without ending fundamental competition. The 1646 agreement with Rajah Bungsu similarly shows Sulu's capacity for independent diplomacy under Spanish and Dutch pressure.

The Spanish fort at Zamboanga, begun in June 1635, illustrates changing imperial priorities. It offered a significant forward position but required continuing financial and logistical support. Spanish military forces withdrew in the crisis of 1663 when Manila faced wider danger; later Jesuit-related activity and the substantial refortification of 1718–1719 show that occupation and construction were not simple uninterrupted processes. The fort's history thus makes the difference between an imperial claim, a military presence, and comprehensive local governance especially clear.

The upland political world of Lake Lanao reveals a further dimension of resistance to outside rule. Maranao communities possessed distributed authority and defensive resources in a region difficult for coastal expeditions to occupy. Spanish movements in 1639–1640 did not create enduring administration. The experience cautions against using the interests or agreements of one sultan to infer the subjection of all Muslim communities. It also demonstrates why a historical map must show multiple political and environmental zones rather than a single linear frontier.

The history is reconstructed from unequal sources. Morga's 1609 narrative, Combés's posthumous 1667 history, treaty records, royal correspondence, and later traditions illuminate important episodes, yet they privilege the perspectives of officials, missionaries, and ruling families. Historians must also ask what can be learned about captives, laborers, women, and non-elite coastal and inland households. By approximately 1750 Spanish footholds remained consequential, but substantial southern political centers operated outside effective Spanish sovereignty. Mindanao and Sulu continued as active participants in the wider Southeast Asian maritime world.

Chapter Reflection

The frontier makes an unusually clear distinction between claiming a territory and exercising authority over its people. Spain's rulers could authorize campaigns and declare success, yet they depended on local alliances, mobile labor, limited garrisons, and negotiations with leaders they could not eliminate. Understanding that distinction enables students to assess imperial history without assuming that the actions of a capital determined everything happening at distant ports, rivers, and settlements.

The chapter also demonstrates how religious categories shape political narration. Spanish writers adapted an inherited word, Moro, to classify diverse communities, while rulers in the south organized relations through their own political and religious institutions. The task of historical scholarship is not to deny religion's role but to identify when it interacted with trade, captivity, local rivalry, and strategic diplomacy. Evidence from treaties and everyday economic life often complicates the absolute categories of military propaganda.

The history of captivity gives the subject an indispensable human scale. Treaties may name rulers while omitting the enslaved and displaced; chronicles celebrate victory while leaving households without voices. Recovering those experiences requires patient comparison of administrative records, demographic scholarship, commercial evidence, and traditions of remembrance. Where direct evidence is missing, historical integrity requires acknowledgment rather than an invented narrative.

As this volume turns to Dutch commercial and military competition elsewhere in Southeast Asia, the Philippine frontier remains part of the same maritime world. Sulu, Maguindanao, and their neighbors traded, negotiated, and fought within connections that reached Brunei, the Moluccas, Manila, and beyond. Their histories cannot be reduced to resistance to Spain alone. They remained historical actors whose own institutions and choices shaped the outcomes of early modern imperial rivalry.
