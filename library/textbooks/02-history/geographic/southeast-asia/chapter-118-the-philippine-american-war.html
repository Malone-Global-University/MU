The Philippine-American War

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

In 1898 Filipino revolutionaries believed they were winning independence from Spain. Within weeks of the Spanish defeat, they found themselves facing a second imperial power.

The Philippine-American War began on the night of February 4, 1899, on the outskirts of Manila, and the United States officially declared the insurrection over on July 4, 1902. Between those dates, an army that had fought Spain became a conventional force, was defeated in set-piece fighting, and reorganized as a dispersed guerrilla movement whose authority rested on villages, kin networks, and local officials.

The war is sometimes called the "Philippine Insurrection" in older American writing, a name that presumes the legitimacy of American sovereignty. Filipino historians have often called it a war of resistance to conquest, or a continuation of the Revolution. This textbook uses "Philippine-American War" and treats the naming itself as a historical question.

The chapter follows five questions: why a war over sovereignty arose; how American forces defeated the Republican army without ending resistance; how the struggle became one for local control; what repression, atrocity, and disease did to civilians; and how military victory established colonial power while leaving nationalist aspirations unresolved. Evidence includes official correspondence, field orders, congressional testimony, soldiers' letters, Filipino proclamations and captured documents, and later memory. Most surviving records come from the American military and the Filipino literate elite, so the experience of peasants and women is often reconstructed indirectly.

The war did not merely replace one imperial administration with another. It tested whether a revolutionary republic, formed through armed resistance to Spain, could maintain its institutions in the face of an overseas power claiming authority through a treaty negotiated without Filipino representatives. The outcome was shaped by armies, diplomatic claims, local political networks and the ability of civilians to withstand prolonged disruption. The question of who possessed sovereignty therefore remained present even in the smallest decisions concerning roads, towns and taxes.

Different Philippine regions experienced different wars. Central Luzon supplied the most visible campaigns of 1899, but Cebu, Panay, Marinduque, Samar, Batangas, Mindanao and Sulu reveal different political histories and military conditions. The distinction between the war against the Philippine Republic and other American colonial campaigns must be maintained throughout. Calling the entire archipelago a single military theater can conceal both Filipino agency and the diversity of local responses.

Military evidence requires special scrutiny. Official reports commonly counted towns occupied, arms captured and enemy casualties; commanders often presented their decisions favorably. Filipino correspondence could likewise magnify victories, defend contested actions or represent disputed political claims as unanimous. The chapter compares these records with historical scholarship and makes clear where allegations, estimates, and later commemorations are not equivalent to established fact.

The chapter ends in 1902 because that was the American government’s official declaration of the war’s end, not because every armed opponent surrendered on that date. The resulting colonial state and the continuing nationalist argument belong to the transition into Chapter 119. This chapter’s responsibility is to explain the warfare and social crisis that made the new political order possible, rather than to narrate the entire history of American government in the islands.

Historical Context

Chapter 117 traced the Katipunan, the 1896 revolution, the Pact of Biak-na-Bato, and the declaration of independence at Kawit on June 12, 1898. When the United States went to war with Spain in April 1898, Emilio Aguinaldo returned to the Philippines on American ships and revolutionary forces besieged Manila. American forces under Commodore George Dewey had destroyed the Spanish fleet on May 1, and in August 1898 a staged Spanish surrender of Manila excluded Filipino troops from the city. This chapter begins from that unresolved situation: two armies facing one another around a captured city, each with a different understanding of what the war against Spain had been for. The history of civilian colonial government belongs to Chapter 119.

The Malolos Republic was inaugurated on January 23, 1899, after the declaration of independence of June 12, 1898 and the retreat of Spanish authority. Its government attempted to operate through a constitution, provincial administration, military command and taxation. American soldiers meanwhile occupied Manila following the August 1898 surrender of Spanish forces, and a treaty concluded in Paris assigned the Philippines to the United States. These overlapping developments brought two rival political projects into contact.

President McKinley announced a policy of benevolent assimilation in December 1898. Filipino leaders rejected the idea that their country could be transferred between foreign governments without consultation. Military positions tightened around Manila, and clashes of authority developed over the nearby settlements and communications. The opening battle of February 1899 occurred within this unresolved diplomatic and constitutional dispute.

The analysis below separates three phases: the initial American conventional campaigns, the Philippine shift to irregular warfare, and the prolonged counterinsurgency and colonial consolidation of 1900–1902. These phases overlap in particular provinces, and local histories do not follow one identical chronology. The account also treats racial ideology, opposition to empire, mortality and legal controversy as integral parts of war rather than as optional aftermaths.

Division I — From the Philippine Revolution to War against the United States

Section 118.1

The Treaty of Paris, Philippine Sovereignty, and the Crisis of 1898–1899

On December 10, 1898, Spain and the United States signed the Treaty of Paris, in which Spain ceded the Philippines to the United States in return for a payment of $20 million. No Filipino representative took part. In the American view, sovereignty passed by treaty from Spain. In the Filipino view, Spanish sovereignty had already been broken by revolution, and Spain had nothing it could lawfully transfer. On January 23, 1899, the Malolos Congress inaugurated the First Philippine Republic with Aguinaldo as president. The republic had a constitution, an army, provincial administration, and taxation across much of Luzon, though no foreign government recognized it. Diplomatic recognition and effective local authority are different things, and both are part of the story.

The treaty was a settlement between the United States and Spain, not an agreement with the Philippine revolutionary government. In its Article III, Spain ceded the archipelago and the United States agreed to pay twenty million dollars. Diplomats could recognize the transaction within the system of relations among states, but the treaty did not resolve what authority the new Philippine Republic actually exercised. By late 1898 revolutionary officials collected contributions, issued instructions to provinces, maintained troops and tried to establish courts and civil offices. The practical reach of those institutions was uneven, especially outside Luzon, yet they were neither imaginary nor simply extensions of American administration. The central disagreement was whether a defeated imperial state could dispose of people who had already asserted their own independence. Philippine representatives, most notably Felipe Agoncillo, attempted to press the republic's claim abroad but were excluded from the Paris negotiations.

The preceding revolution gave this dispute urgency. Aguinaldo's forces had besieged Spanish Manila, while the United States possessed a fleet, reinforcement routes and the captured city after August 1898. Filipino leaders had reason to believe that the struggle against Spain had created a political nation capable of determining its future. American officials, however, treated Spanish cession and military occupation as foundations for sovereignty, and often refused to recognize republican officials as representatives of an independent state. The resulting dispute cannot be reduced to miscommunication between commanders. It was a conflict between incompatible constitutional and political claims: the republic understood independence as the reward and outcome of revolution, while Washington intended to govern an overseas territory acquired through its victory over Spain.

The Malolos constitutional process supplied a visible alternative to American descriptions of Filipino political incapacity. Delegates met in 1898, debated the relationship of legislature and executive, and produced a constitution associated with a republican form of government. Aguinaldo's inauguration in January 1899 symbolized the effort to institutionalize revolution rather than prolong military rule indefinitely. The practical independence of particular localities should not be overstated; communications could be slow and provincial loyalties diverse. Nevertheless, the existence of elected and appointed officials, fiscal measures, formal decrees and an army complicates later portrayals of the republic as nothing more than a wandering insurgent headquarters.

The American position itself was not settled instantly. Some policymakers expected that naval stations or a limited territorial arrangement might secure commercial interests in Asia. Others argued that control of the entire archipelago was required to prevent European intervention or renewed disorder. The question was argued in the United States in the language of treaty power, strategic responsibility, racial hierarchy, commerce and republican ideals. The Senate's narrow ratification of the treaty on February 6, 1899, after hostilities had begun, demonstrated the close contest over empire even within the American political system. Ratification strengthened Washington's formal claim; it did not confer consent from the Philippine population or end the armed confrontation.

On the ground, the immediate problem was that American troops held Manila and its outskirts while republican forces occupied surrounding areas. Each commander tried to avoid conceding a political principle through an apparently minor military adjustment. Controlling a bridge, waterworks, road or suburb could establish a claim to jurisdiction as well as offer a tactical advantage. The United States could insist on protective lines around its forces; Filipinos could see such lines as encroachment upon their capital and surrounding territory. Maps of military dispositions therefore document not only troop positions but the spatial expression of rival sovereignties. The encounter at the edge of Manila in February was the result of this unresolved situation, not an inexplicable quarrel of two sentries.

Historical Vocabulary I

Sovereignty is supreme authority over a territory; annexation is its formal incorporation into another state; occupation is military control that need not confer sovereignty; self-determination is the claim that a people decides its own political status. The American government called its authority sovereignty; Filipino leaders called it occupation. Potential misunderstanding: "colonial rule" was not an official American term in 1899.

Section 118.2

The Outbreak of War: Manila, February 4–5, 1899

By early 1899 American and Filipino lines stood close together around Manila, and incidents multiplied. On the evening of February 4, a sentry of the Nebraska volunteers, Private William Grayson, challenged and fired on Filipino soldiers near Santa Mesa; general fighting followed along the line. Accounts differ on who fired first and who intended escalation. American commanders interpreted the fighting as an attack and directed a broad offensive. Filipino sources record that Aguinaldo sought to halt the fighting and negotiate; American commanders refused to stop once hostilities had begun. Older commemoration placed the first shot at the San Juan bridge; in 2003 the National Historical Institute relocated the marker to the Santa Mesa area, an example of how memory sites are revised by research.

The area east and northeast of Manila was crowded with political significance and military danger. American forces occupied the former Spanish capital; Filipino formations held positions close enough for sentries to hear or challenge one another, while each side watched roads, water infrastructure and river crossings. The physical proximity magnified small incidents. A patrol could be interpreted as reconnaissance; an attempt to establish a new post could appear to violate a boundary that had never been accepted by both sides. Soldiers had different instructions, imperfect maps and uncertain information. Official correspondence had already become increasingly accusatory before the first shots were fired. It is misleading to imagine that relations had remained peaceful and orderly until a single isolated moment caused an otherwise avoidable war.

Accounts of the evening of February 4 identify Private William W. Grayson of the First Nebraska Volunteers as having fired at Filipinos whom he challenged near the American line. His own retrospective description, American reports and Filipino statements differ in detail and in their assumptions about responsibility. A historical inquiry must separate the narrow question of who discharged a weapon from the broader questions of why armed formations were confronting one another and why commanders allowed the incident to escalate. Evidence of Grayson's action does not by itself demonstrate that one government intended the ensuing full-scale offensive, just as proclamations issued after fighting began cannot automatically establish who had previously planned aggression.

The geographical identification of the first-shot site has itself become a subject of source criticism. An older marker associated the incident with San Juan Bridge. The National Historical Institute's Board Resolution 07 of 2003 moved the official historical designation to the Silencio and Sociego streets area of Santa Mesa, drawing upon examination of testimonies and contemporary descriptions. The correction matters because students may encounter both locations in older maps, textbooks and memorial practice. Historical commemoration is evidence for the history of memory, not necessarily conclusive proof of the original military event. When documents disagree, the location and sequence should be reconstructed from their contemporaneous spatial references.

As exchanges of fire spread on February 4–5, local clashes became major combat. Artillery and naval gunfire supported American advances against Filipino trenches and defensive positions around Manila. The American command chose to expand its military response rather than accept an immediate ceasefire on terms advanced by Filipino representatives. Republican officials portrayed the conflict as an invasion of national territory; American military reports characterized opponents as insurgents resisting lawful authority. Both terms carried an implicit answer to the sovereignty question. How the violence was named affected the military rules officers believed applicable to prisoners, civilian supporters and irregular fighters.

The news traveled through telegraphs, military dispatches, newspapers and word of mouth. Rumors of battlefield success and catastrophe could precede verified reports. The U.S. Senate was simultaneously considering ratification of the treaty with Spain, so the fighting was quickly incorporated into American arguments about whether the archipelago had to be held. In the Philippines, the beginning of war tested the ability of the Malolos Republic to coordinate distant provinces and to sustain the loyalty of people with different local interests. A metropolitan-centered narrative alone cannot show how quickly these events affected countryside enlistment, taxation and provisioning.

Section 118.3

American Expansion, Benevolent Assimilation, and the Debate over Empire

President William McKinley's December 21, 1898 proclamation announced "benevolent assimilation": American authority would replace Spanish rule and aim at the welfare of the inhabitants. Justifications mixed strategy (the China trade, naval bases), commerce, claims of duty toward people said to be unprepared for self-government, and fear that other powers would seize the islands if the United States withdrew. Most of these arguments assumed Filipino incapacity, which Filipino leaders, many of them educated and propertied, rejected. The American Anti-Imperialist League, organized in 1898, argued that governing a people without consent contradicted American republican principles. The Senate ratified the treaty on February 6, 1899, two days after the fighting began, by a margin of one vote over the required two-thirds.

President McKinley's proclamation of December 21, 1898, set out the language of benevolent assimilation, describing an extension of American authority as compatible with the welfare of Philippine inhabitants. Its phrasing presented occupation as protection of lives, property and religious rights rather than conquest for private gain. The gap between this assertion and Filipino resistance is a central object of historical analysis. An occupying power could claim to be creating the conditions for future self-government; the people claiming independence could understand the same policy as coercion supported by superior military resources. Neither representation can be accepted without examining who made decisions, who exercised authority and what happened when local communities resisted.

American supporters of expansion did not speak with one voice. Naval strategists valued a presence across the Pacific; commercial advocates anticipated access to Asian markets; political leaders feared that relinquishing the islands would invite European powers or renewed Spanish influence. Some formulated a tutelary idea of colonial government: Filipinos would learn the practices of self-government under American supervision. That assumption placed the United States in the position of determining when an entire people was ready to exercise rights it already claimed. Religious language of duty and uplift sometimes mixed with racial classifications and openly hierarchical ideas of civilization. It is insufficient to describe imperialism as simply economic or simply humanitarian; the arguments were often combined and mutually reinforcing.

American anti-imperialists exposed inconsistencies in these claims. The Anti-Imperialist League attracted politicians, writers, reformers and business figures whose reasons for opposing annexation varied considerably. Some argued from the Declaration of Independence and the principle of government by consent. Others feared costly permanent deployments, corruption or foreign entanglements. Still others voiced racially exclusionary anxieties about incorporating nonwhite populations into the American polity. Thus opposition to empire did not always imply racial equality or unambiguous solidarity with Philippine independence. Mark Twain's criticism, Andrew Carnegie's proposals and political objections by different factions should be read as distinct positions rather than grouped into a single emancipatory movement.

The issue divided Congress and the electorate. The Senate ratified the Paris treaty by a narrow margin on February 6, while fighting around Manila provided both supporters and critics with fresh arguments. Supporters insisted that armed resistance made occupation necessary to restore security. Critics replied that American insistence on annexation had provoked the resistance. In the presidential contest of 1900, empire became part of a broader contest over national priorities, economic policy and leadership. The eventual defeat of anti-imperial candidates did not erase substantial domestic opposition; it reinforced the executive's ability to continue the campaign. Reports of atrocities later reopened debate even among some who had initially supported expansion.

Filipino political voices were systematically underrepresented in American legislative debate. Their petitions and diplomatic initiatives were filtered through colonial officials, journalists and hostile witnesses. The description of the revolution as the work of a small educated minority ignored its local roots, even if elite leadership was itself real and contested. The question of popular consent cannot be resolved by pointing either to provincial collaboration or to the existence of an independence army. Local populations differed in their allegiances and opportunities, and coercion could be exercised by competing authorities. A rigorous account asks how political preferences were expressed under conditions of war, restricted mobility and unequal power.

Section 118.4

The Battle of Manila and the Opening Military Campaign

In the first days of fighting, American forces under General Elwell Otis, supported by naval gunfire and artillery, drove Filipino troops from positions around the city, with heavy Filipino losses. The U.S. force enjoyed major advantages in modern rifles, artillery, naval support, coordination and sea-based supply, although relative numbers varied by place and date. Filipino forces had a large number of men but uneven arms, limited ammunition, and a patchwork of local commands. Manila mattered because it was the colony's port, financial center, and railway terminus: the Manila–Dagupan line ran north through the Central Luzon plain, shaping American operations.

In February 1899 the central military theater was not simply Manila's walls but a ring of rivers, suburbs, roads, forts and railway approaches that controlled access to the city. The Pasig River connected Manila Bay with the lake district to the east. To the north the Manila–Dagupan railway crossed the Central Luzon plain, linking commercial towns and offering a corridor for troops, supplies and telegraph communications. American control of the port permitted reinforcement and replenishment by sea, while Philippine Republican forces depended on a much more fragile network of locally acquired ammunition, carts and provisioning. The map of these communications helps explain why tactical successes near railway junctions could yield consequences beyond the immediate battlefield.

American infantry initially met Filipino defensive earthworks at close range around the city's eastern and northern approaches. Artillery and naval fire, modern shoulder arms and relatively coordinated command gave the Americans substantial advantages, but the terrain still demanded difficult assaults and unit-level decisions. A trench line that blocked a main road could be bypassed only after reconnaissance or an attack across exposed ground. American soldiers also faced heat, disease, unfamiliar vegetation and the difficulty of distinguishing roads from paths in fields and settlements. Accounts that portray the opening campaign as effortlessly decided by superior technology overlook the costs and operational uncertainty experienced by participants on both sides.

Filipino forces had not been prepared as a single standardized field army. Units inherited revolutionary organizational practices and often reflected province, town and personal command networks. Some possessed rifles and had experience in the struggle against Spain; others used older weapons or improvised means, and ammunition supplies were inadequate for sustained exchanges with American firepower. Commanders defended positions partly because the republic needed to show that it could contest American movements. Yet concentrating troops in fixed trenches made them vulnerable to the weapons and fire control of an enemy who could bring artillery forward. This was the first of several mismatches between the political need to defend territory and the operational logic of surviving the war.

On February 5 American forces attacked and extended their perimeter, and the movement continued toward Caloocan and adjoining towns in the following days. The occupation of the northward routes weakened Philippine positions near Manila and prepared the subsequent advance into Bulacan. The fighting did not occur in empty ground: waterways served as transport and obstacles, agricultural districts provided food and labor, and settlements offered shelter, guides and intelligence. When armies occupied a town they acquired responsibilities for civilians as well as tactical advantages. Local inhabitants could become laborers, informants, refugees or hostages to conflicting obligations, even before guerrilla warfare became the dominant mode of combat.

The distinction between winning a battle and controlling the surrounding countryside appeared almost immediately. An American column could take a road and raise a flag in a municipal building, yet the legitimacy of its officers might remain disputed and armed republican units might reappear beyond the perimeter. American commanders faced a choice between concentrating forces for major offensives and dispersing them into garrisons to hold roads, bridges and towns. Republican commanders conversely had to choose whether to defend every locality or preserve armed formations for another campaign. These dilemmas foreshadowed the transition from conventional war to the contest over local networks that defined 1900–1902.

Division II — Conventional Warfare and the Defeat of the Philippine Republican Army

Section 118.5

American Offensives in Central Luzon and the Fall of Malolos, 1899

After Manila, American columns under Arthur MacArthur Jr. and others advanced north along the railway and river lines, taking Caloocan and pushing toward Malolos, which fell on March 31, 1899. Filipino defenders had trenches and some prepared positions but could not withstand American artillery, naval support, and rapid maneuver, and they lacked reserves. The government withdrew northward. Yet American commanders had occupied territory without eliminating the army, and in the rainy season the advance stalled as supply lines lengthened, disease struck, and many volunteer regiments reached the end of their enlistments.

The advance northward in 1899 involved a succession of engagements rather than a single march upon the revolutionary capital. American commanders sought to defeat organized Philippine forces, secure the railway and prevent the republic from using the Central Luzon plain to assemble another army. Caloocan, Malabon and towns farther north linked the Manila perimeter with the approaches to Bulacan. Railway bridges, roads and river crossings were operational objectives because they moved ammunition and food and allowed commanders to communicate with detachments. Destruction of a bridge could impose delays on an advancing army, but it could also harm surrounding communities that depended on the same route for trade and movement.

The Philippine Republican Army built trenches and attempted to defend towns and natural obstacles with limited firepower. The central problem was not lack of determination; it was the difficulty of sustaining an extended defensive system against better supplied columns operating with artillery and frequent reinforcement. American attacks could threaten the flank of a prepared position or use waterways to approach from an unexpected direction. Filipino commanders were often forced to withdraw to avoid destruction, which surrendered ground but preserved soldiers for later fighting. The same withdrawal that appeared to American commanders as evidence of collapse could be described by the Philippine side as a necessary choice between territorial possession and the survival of national resistance.

Malolos fell to American troops on March 31, 1899. The government had withdrawn, so the event had enormous symbolic value without delivering the person of Aguinaldo or the entire army into American hands. The republic's leadership attempted to continue functioning while moving to more defensible locations. The fall of the capital was therefore a test of whether a political movement depended on a permanent administrative seat. In practice, revolutionary sovereignty was maintained through networks of officials, communications and regional commanders whose strength varied from place to place. American possession of municipal centers did not automatically transfer allegiance in villages or agricultural districts beyond them.

Operations continued at Calumpit, along the rail line and in other areas of the plain. The landscape changed markedly between dry and rainy seasons. Roads became difficult, disease reduced the effective strength of armies, and river crossings acquired new importance as water levels rose. American volunteer regiments originally mobilized for the war against Spain were approaching the limits of their enlistments. Replacement troops and a more durable occupation force had to be organized. Philippine commanders, however, faced their own exhaustion: casualties, shortages of rifles and cartridges, internal disagreements and the burdens placed on villages supplying soldiers weakened sustained resistance in open battle.

The United States increasingly learned that the occupation of towns required substantial garrisons, while mobile columns were needed to pursue formations that withdrew from them. A garrison could protect a telegraph station, courthouse or market, but soldiers often knew little about neighboring barrio networks. Municipal officials might comply publicly with American demands while covertly supplying republican fighters. American patrols collected some information from local auxiliaries, but such intelligence could reflect longstanding rivalries as much as military fact. The growing tension between formal occupation and effective control is one of the chapter's major analytical themes.

Brian McAllister Linn's military history is valuable here because it reconstructs operations across commanders and provinces without assuming that the capture of a capital meant decisive victory. The campaign's outcome was neither an uninterrupted American advance nor a conventional military stalemate. American forces won the principal encounters and secured strategic communication lines, while republican formations reorganized or withdrew and the political contest remained unresolved. In this respect the spring campaign created the conditions for later guerrilla warfare while also demonstrating the limitations of military operations judged solely by the number of towns taken.

Cause and Effect

Why military superiority did not produce political control. Causes: long supply lines, thin garrisons, an armed population, and a nationalist movement that existed beyond its capital. Effect: the occupation of towns left the surrounding countryside and local officials largely outside American authority. Complexity: Filipino elites differed on whether to fight on, so American success also depended on political divisions.

Section 118.6

The Philippine Republican Army: Leadership, Soldiers, and Internal Conflicts

The army was formed from revolutionary units raised against Spain, with officers drawn from local elites and veterans. Antonio Luna, a pharmacist trained in Europe, became chief of operations and attempted to impose discipline, military schooling, and unified command, sometimes harshly, offending officers loyal to regional leaders. Tensions with Aguinaldo's circle were severe. On June 5, 1899, Luna was killed at Cabanatuan by soldiers of the presidential guard. Historians agree on the killing and on the climate of rivalry; who ordered it remains disputed. The army lost its most capable organizer, and many soldiers' loyalties were local rather than national. This chapter concerns the army as an institution; the earlier leadership rivalries are covered in Chapter 117.

The Philippine Republican Army emerged from a revolution that had depended upon local networks and charismatic political leadership. A national government wanted a force able to obey orders across provinces, but soldiers were recruited through towns, provincial relationships and existing revolutionary commands. Officers did not always agree about the authority of a chief of staff or how to balance military necessities against the claims of provincial political leaders. A company might be loyal to a commander who supplied it with food and rifles more readily than to a distant headquarters. Such relationships were not peculiar to the Philippines, but the speed with which the republic had to organize for war made the problem unusually severe.

Antonio Luna is central to this history because he sought to convert disparate revolutionary formations into a disciplined army. His training, organizational ability and insistence on centralized orders made him an important figure during the early campaign. He proposed stronger defensive arrangements, improved training and clearer command relationships, and he frequently clashed with officers whom he considered careless or insubordinate. Accounts of Luna often swing between idealizing him as the republic's only competent general and condemning his harsh manner. Neither simplification explains why professionalization was so difficult. Military competence, political trust and local authority were different resources, and the republic lacked an institutional mechanism strong enough to reconcile them consistently.

The material limitations were formidable. Uniforms and standardized equipment were uneven; weapons and ammunition varied considerably; transport relied on carts, animals, waterways and local provisioning. Soldiers needed food during planting and harvesting seasons, and their families needed labor at home. Political loyalties could coexist with reluctance to leave one's village for a prolonged campaign. For revolutionary officials, extracting resources to sustain the army could weaken the very support on which independence depended. American accounts sometimes interpreted these difficulties as evidence that the Philippine government lacked legitimacy. A more careful view recognizes the obstacles confronting any emerging state fighting an industrially supplied opponent, while still acknowledging the republic's real internal weaknesses.

Luna's assassination on June 5, 1899, at Cabanatuan exposed the breakdown of trust within the revolutionary high command. The National Historical Commission of the Philippines identifies members of Aguinaldo's presidential guard as his killers, while the question of responsibility for authorizing or failing to prevent the attack remains disputed in historical interpretation. His aide Francisco Román was also killed. It would be irresponsible to identify a single person as the proven author of a conspiracy without adequate documentary evidence. More important for this chapter, the incident demonstrated how political factionalism, military rivalry and personal security could interact during wartime, undermining the credibility of central command.

Luna's death did not by itself cause American victory, a claim that treats a complex military process as if it depended on one individual. Even with a more coherent command, the Philippine army would have faced American superiority in arms, naval logistics and the ability to reinforce its expeditionary forces. Yet the loss of an assertive organizer damaged efforts to impose centralized discipline and contributed to a wider crisis of confidence. Other officers and regional commanders still fought; different parts of the archipelago experienced different military situations. The history of the republican military establishment must therefore be told as a history of institutions and relationships, not merely as biographies of Aguinaldo, Luna and Gregorio del Pilar.

At the level of ordinary soldiers, the war was a struggle over endurance. Repeated withdrawals required units to improvise supply, maintain communication with retreating political authorities and care for wounded companions. American commanders did not always appreciate these constraints, while Filipino memoirists sometimes elevated extraordinary sacrifice over routine administrative problems. A well-grounded account reads orders, correspondence and local testimony alongside campaign histories. It examines how a soldier reached the front, how ammunition arrived, what happened to injured men, and how a local commander obtained food. These ordinary questions explain both the army's temporary cohesion and the conditions under which decentralized guerrilla warfare eventually became more viable than set-piece defense.

Republican officers sought a regular army under central command. Ordinary soldiers often fought with obsolete arms and returned home for harvests. Local officials balanced demands for men and rice against community survival. American commanders read Filipino divisions as evidence the republic lacked popular unity. These viewpoints are reconstructed syntheses, not quotations.

Section 118.7

The Northern Luzon Campaign and the Geography of Retreat

Through the second half of 1899, American forces pushed north in the dry-season campaign beginning in the autumn, aiming to trap Aguinaldo, who moved from town to town toward the Cordillera. Rivers, flooded rice land, poor roads, and bridge destruction slowed both sides. On December 2, 1899, a small Filipino force under General Gregorio del Pilar held Tirad Pass against American troops to cover Aguinaldo's retreat; del Pilar was killed. American accounts and Filipino commemoration differ in emphasis, and the heroic portrait of del Pilar is later patriotic memory laid over a modest documentary record.

The geography of northern Luzon made the pursuit of the republican leadership different from the fighting outside Manila. The central plain gave way to foothills, deeper valleys and ridges where roads were narrow, bridges vulnerable and transport uncertain. Philippine commanders hoped that retreat could preserve the leadership and armed cadres beyond the reach of American columns. American forces sought to strike quickly enough to prevent the revolutionaries from regrouping. Both armies depended upon information from local inhabitants, guides and scouts. A mountain route could protect a small party and impose enormous difficulties on a column carrying artillery and supplies; the same route could isolate the fugitives from the agricultural communities that previously sustained them.

In the autumn of 1899 American columns pressed through the railway towns and interior routes, with major operations in Tarlac, Pangasinan, Nueva Ecija and adjacent provinces. The revolutionary administration shifted its headquarters, and documents, personnel and military stores were moved under pressure. The occupation of another provincial town could disrupt local collection systems, but it also forced American commanders to leave garrisons behind. The military outcome of a campaign depended not simply on the location of the pursuing column but on the number of detachments available to protect its communications. A force scattered along the railway might hold important junctions while leaving nearby villages open to insurgent influence.

The retreat brought into sharper focus the relationship between a political government's survival and military mobility. Aguinaldo's continued freedom represented a powerful symbol, yet moving a president and his attendants across difficult terrain was not the same as preserving a functioning nationwide administration. Communications to distant commanders were sporadic; reports of victories or defeats arrived late; individual officers had increasingly wide discretion. The decision to disperse forces and operate locally was therefore both an intentional strategic transformation and a response to lost central coordination. The line between organized withdrawal, fragmentation and planned guerrilla resistance cannot be reduced to a single order issued on one day.

The rear-guard action at Tirad Pass on December 2, 1899, illustrates these problems. Gregorio del Pilar's small command attempted to delay American pursuers moving through a constricted mountain approach while Aguinaldo escaped. Del Pilar was killed, and the encounter became a central episode in the later patriotic memory of the war. In military terms the engagement was limited in scale compared with the battles around Manila and the railway plain. Its significance lay in the time and space a rear guard could provide, and in its postwar symbolic life. Heroic representations should be compared with surviving American accounts, Filipino recollections and the terrain itself rather than accepted without qualification.

Maps are particularly useful but can mislead if the reader treats arrows as complete descriptions of movement. A road on a colonial-era military map may have been little more than a trail, and seasonal rains could transform its utility. Elevation changes are sometimes shown in hachures or contour lines that require careful interpretation. A sequence of dated maps is preferable to a single modern road map because the war's operational geography included destroyed bridges, routes used only seasonally, and communities that have since grown into cities. Students should distinguish geographic location, asserted control and confirmed troop presence.

After the destruction of the last large field formations, American officers could report victory in conventional terms. Yet revolutionary resistance still drew strength from political loyalty and local networks that a retreating army had left behind. The northern pursuit thus points toward the central paradox of the war: American military forces became increasingly able to capture places and break concentrations, while their dispersion into a broad occupation system made them vulnerable to small-unit attacks. The mountains helped preserve leadership for a time, but the more durable source of resistance proved to be organized support in villages, towns and provinces. The northern campaign marks the end of one phase rather than a final resolution.

Section 118.8

From Conventional Defeat to Guerrilla Warfare, Late 1899

In November 1899 Aguinaldo dissolved the main field army and directed local commanders to wage irregular war; the U.S. Army's own history marks this as a turning point. The decision followed from repeated defeats in fixed positions, not from abandonment of independence. Guerrilla war allowed small units to avoid American firepower, use local knowledge, and force the occupier to hold many places at once. It also put the burden of the war on villages.

By November 1899 a decisive change of military method had occurred. The republic could no longer depend on defending capitals, long trench systems or concentrations of troops against American artillery and trained infantry. Aguinaldo and his commanders recognized that a force which could not sustain battle in the open might survive by dispersing, attacking communications and refusing an unfavorable engagement. The decision is commonly associated with Aguinaldo's late-1899 orders to adopt guerrilla warfare. Its implementation, however, differed by province; in some districts irregular tactics had already been used, while others retained elements of conventional organization. This was a process of adaptation under defeat rather than an instantaneous national transformation.

Guerrilla strategy shifted the definition of useful military success. Rather than occupy a major city, fighters might cut a telegraph line, ambush a patrol, collect arms, intercept a courier or attack an isolated outpost. Small detachments could use farms, ravines, forests and hills that denied a pursuing column the chance to employ its full strength. Local intelligence helped commanders identify weak points and avoid concentration. A successful raid could produce weapons and reinforce confidence, while a poorly judged attack might provoke reprisals that punished the same civilians on whom fighters relied. Tactical initiative was real, but its political costs had to be calculated carefully.

The new method required a hidden administrative structure. Fighters needed food, medical aid, safe houses, informants, correspondence routes and funds. These resources could be supplied voluntarily by families and provincial sympathizers, but they could also be demanded through pressure or threats. The republic's claim to be a national government persisted in proclamations and organizational language, yet its authority increasingly depended on local commanders and civilian networks. In certain towns officials played double roles, appearing to cooperate with American garrisons while assisting guerrillas clandestinely. Other communities favored accommodation because warfare endangered crops, trade or personal security. No blanket statement about uniform Filipino allegiance is historically responsible.

The American army confronted a different problem from that of February 1899. A field army could be pursued, surrounded or forced to retreat; a clandestine organization could disappear when large columns approached and reappear when they moved elsewhere. American commanders therefore had to hold towns and protect supply lines while maintaining mobile detachments capable of responding to raids. This required more posts, more information and greater involvement in civilian administration. As garrisons multiplied, the need to distinguish supporters from enemies increased, but the evidence available to local officers was often ambiguous and vulnerable to manipulation by local political rivalries.

The transformation also changed the language of the conflict. American officials increasingly described the opposition as banditry or insurgency; Filipino revolutionaries continued to present their struggle as a national war for independence. The classification mattered because officials used it to justify different policies toward fighters without uniforms, political committees and suspected supporters. The Lieber Code contained categories for irregular fighters alongside limits on violence toward civilians and prisoners; arguments about which provisions applied would become acute during the later campaigns. Political and legal claims therefore entered into everyday decisions about detention, search, property and punishment.

Finally, a guerrilla movement could endure without being able to compel an enemy to relinquish its political objectives. American resources permitted continued reinforcement, and the revolution's regional commanders could disagree about priorities or become isolated from one another. The lack of secure external supplies limited prospects of a decisive military reversal. Historians should distinguish continued resistance, local legitimacy and strategic capacity. The shift to irregular war kept the independence cause militarily alive and increased the cost of occupation, but it also moved the conflict into inhabited communities and made the civilian population central to American methods of suppression.

Continuity and Change I

Changed: organization (national army to local commands), tactics (set battles to ambushes), and the main arena (towns to countryside). Continued: the political goal of independence, many officers, and reliance on community support. Why it matters: the war became harder to end by capturing leaders or places.

Section 118.9

Guerrilla Organization, Intelligence, and Local Revolutionary Government

Guerrilla resistance relied on regional commands, small detachments, underground civilian committees, couriers, and contributions of food and money. In many towns, the same men served as nominal officials under the American garrison while supporting the guerrillas, and American officers learned that a pacified town on paper could conceal an organized shadow government. Intelligence, concealment, and terrain were decisive.

Decentralized guerrilla warfare did not mean the disappearance of political organization. Commanders relied on couriers, trusted officials, secret committees, revenue collectors and local intermediaries. A military detachment hidden outside a town could receive information about a garrison's strength, its patrol schedule and the arrival of supplies from people who moved in and out of the occupied center. Correspondence sometimes passed through relatives, clergy, merchants or public officials whose ordinary activities offered cover. In other places a commander depended on an isolated network of personal supporters. The extent of organization varied, and the survival of a national title did not guarantee that orders could be carried out everywhere.

Revolutionary legitimacy could function without formal possession of the municipal hall. A town president appointed or recognized by American authorities might face expectations to cooperate with those authorities while simultaneously being approached by supporters of the republic. The resulting dual administration made it difficult to determine whose laws were effective. Taxes, contributions and food requisitions became tests of authority: accepting or rejecting them could have material consequences and political meanings. Soldiers and officials on both sides often labeled opponents' financial demands as extortion, while defending their own as necessary taxation or procurement. The historian should investigate who ordered a contribution, whether a household could refuse, and how the money or food was used.

Intelligence was crucial because a guerrilla force rarely controlled the battlefield long enough to conduct sustained conventional reconnaissance. Farmers and travelers could provide warnings about patrols, telegraph workers could observe communications, and municipal offices could reveal who had declared allegiance to the occupier. Yet such knowledge was easily contaminated by private animosity, disputes over land or older factional divisions. American officers sometimes recruited guides who had powerful reasons to denounce local rivals. Revolutionary commanders also punished people suspected of informing. These conditions produced a politics of secrecy in which the identity of a trustworthy neighbor could become a matter of survival.

Guerrilla organization depended on the environment. In rugged regions, small detachments could move along paths difficult for American units to follow; in agricultural plains, concealment among settlements could be more important than forest cover. Coastal islands presented opportunities for movement by boat but also exposed guerrillas to American naval patrols. Seasonal rains interrupted roads and streams. A district with active merchant networks might supply arms and information, whereas a food-scarce area could support only limited numbers of fighters. The diversity of these conditions complicates generalizations about a single universally successful Philippine guerrilla system.

The political relationship between guerrillas and civilians was neither purely voluntary nor uniformly coercive. Many families saw the fighters as defenders of independence and accepted serious risks to help them. Others were divided by kinship, class, property and religious loyalties. A revolutionary officer might rely on relatives to obtain supplies, while another attempted to compel wealthy landholders to contribute. American authorities also offered protection, local office and economic opportunities to communities that accepted their rule. The choices facing ordinary people changed as control shifted and the probability of retaliation increased. A family's outward compliance with one side need not reveal its inner convictions.

Military accounts often celebrated intelligence successes after the fact, turning the capture of a courier or a guerrilla base into evidence that a region had been pacified. Yet the destruction of one network could be followed by the appearance of another. Evaluating effectiveness requires more than counting captured arms: it asks whether roads remained open, whether crops were harvested, whether a community could make decisions without coercion, and whether new fighting followed an announced surrender. This broader measure of control explains why the guerrilla phase persisted after the fall of the republic's original field army and why political accommodation became as important to American victory as raids or patrols.

Historical Vocabulary II

Guerrilla warfare: irregular warfare by small mobile units. Counterinsurgency: measures to defeat it, including by separating fighters from civilian support. Collaboration is a loaded term; this book uses it descriptively, since people cooperated for many reasons. Pacification: the American term for imposing control.

Historical inquiry: Define intelligence as information collected and evaluated about an opponent. Distinguish reconnaissance from informant testimony and distinguish an armed guerrilla from a civilian supporter; these classifications were frequently contested.

Section 118.10

Everyday Life under Occupation and Guerrilla Warfare

Civilians faced demands from both armies: requisitions of food and labor, contributions to guerrillas, billeting of soldiers, and suspicion of informing. Farmers risked losing crops and carabao; merchants risked accusations; priests, teachers, and women who kept households and supplied information carried out essential but dangerous work. Communities were not uniformly loyal to either side, and many chose according to local alliances, safety, or property. The disruption of planting and the loss of draft animals, treated further in 118.17, undermined subsistence.

The ordinary Philippine household encountered the war through demands that were often more immediate than the slogans of national leaders. A farmer needed seed, draft animals and access to fields; a trader needed safe roads and predictable market days; a family needed able workers at home. American garrisons might demand labor, transport or information, while guerrilla detachments sought food, money and shelter. Communities might support the independence struggle yet fear that any visible act of support would invite searches, imprisonment or destruction of property. The boundaries between voluntary contribution and forced requisition could blur as shortages increased. Historians can best reconstruct this world by combining local records, military correspondence and the social history of particular provinces.

Farming communities experienced military operations in seasonal cycles. Rice planting and harvest did not pause because a commander issued an order. A guerrilla raid during the harvest could invite a punitive patrol when food was most urgently needed; a blockade of roads might prevent rice from reaching a nearby town. Loss of carabao through requisition, disease or slaughter reduced the ability to cultivate land, potentially extending suffering beyond the fighting. Poor households were particularly vulnerable because they could not easily store food or finance flight. Wealthier families had greater resources but could become targets for contributions, extortion or suspicion because they possessed property and local influence.

Women performed essential labor in disrupted households, shops and markets, and also participated in political and military networks as couriers, suppliers, nurses and organizers. The surviving documents, however, disproportionately name male commanders and municipal officials. The historian should not invent heroic biographies to fill that absence. Instead, household accounts, court cases, petitions, memoirs and local histories can reveal the forms of participation that a campaign report ignored. Women might find ways to move messages through gendered assumptions about civilian status, but those assumptions also exposed them to harassment or the risks of being treated as collaborators. Gender shaped both opportunity and vulnerability under occupation.

Clergy and teachers occupied equally complicated positions. Priests could be approached for influence or intelligence, while parish buildings and schools might provide shelter, meeting places or administrative offices. American authorities frequently sought the assistance of local intermediaries to normalize town government. Revolutionary supporters could see cooperation as betrayal; others regarded it as the only means of obtaining medicine, maintaining order or preventing a punitive expedition. Religious and local institutional identities did not map neatly onto national political allegiance. In many cases the official labels loyalist and insurgent tell less about a person's motives than about the government that produced the record.

Displacement altered the texture of social life. Families moved to relatives in other barrios, fled toward towns protected by garrisons or hid in rural areas when reprisals were anticipated. Children could lose schooling, markets could close and routine journeys became hazardous. Families separated during military campaigns struggled to locate relatives or recover property. Public health risks increased when people congregated without reliable water, sanitation and food. These pressures were particularly severe in districts subjected to reconcentration policies, but even areas without formal relocation orders could experience repeated flight and insecurity.

Because records of civilians are incomplete, students should resist treating a composite everyday-life scenario as the history of an identified town. A reconstructed farmer, shopkeeper or priest is useful for exploring constrained choices, not for proving that every such person behaved similarly. The strongest local histories identify people in specific sources and explain who left records and who did not. This perspective returns the chapter's attention to the central fact that warfare was conducted not only against armed formations but within an inhabited society whose livelihoods, rights and relationships were transformed by the contest for political authority.

Section 118.11

American Counterinsurgency: Small Units, Scouts, Intelligence, and Military Administration

The American army dispersed into hundreds of small garrisons, used mobile patrols, recruited Filipino scouts and guides, collected intelligence, and combined inducements (local government, public works, amnesty) with coercion. Policy varied by commander and moment: some emphasized conciliation, others increasingly harsh measures, especially after 1900 as frustration grew. Filipino auxiliaries, including Macabebe Scouts and members of the Philippine Scouts, fought on the American side, showing that the war was also a conflict among Filipinos with different interests and regional histories.

American counterinsurgency combined military movement with efforts to reshape local government. Early commanders expected that occupation of key towns, public works and the restoration of trade would reduce support for independence forces. General Elwell Otis emphasized military victory and the projection of a benevolent political program; subsequent commanders faced a more persistent insurgency. Regular units and volunteers occupied municipal centers, guarded telegraph stations, patrolled roads and assembled information about local power holders. A network of small posts offered greater reach than a few large bases, but it dispersed manpower and exposed isolated garrisons to attack. Military success increasingly depended on the quality of relationships with nearby communities.

Local Filipino scouts and auxiliaries were indispensable. Some came from groups with longstanding rivalries with the Tagalog-centered revolutionary leadership; others enlisted for pay, protection, political calculation or personal advantage. The Macabebe Scouts became especially visible, but treating every Filipino serving the United States as part of a single loyalist population obscures important differences. Guides interpreted terrain and language, identified suspected guerrillas and helped patrols locate hidden camps. Their information could be accurate and tactically useful while still reflecting local disputes. American officers operating through intermediaries needed to understand the social relationships behind an accusation; many did not have the linguistic or historical knowledge to do so.

American policy could appear conciliatory in one district and coercive in another. Officials organized municipal elections or appointments, permitted some local officeholders to retain authority, encouraged public markets and offered amnesty to those who surrendered. The so-called policy of attraction sought to demonstrate that cooperation produced order, security and material benefits. Yet a road repair project, school opening or public-health effort under military occupation could also serve the occupier's logistical interests. The coexistence of civic programs and coercive patrols was not a contradiction that can be explained away by declaring one of them unreal. It was part of how a territorial military government attempted to gain compliance.

Where attacks continued, commanders used arrests, curfews, searches, fines, property destruction and movement restrictions to weaken insurgent organization. The threshold for treating a suspected supporter as a combatant was often contested. The 1863 Lieber Code, promulgated as General Orders No. 100, distinguished different irregular forces and allowed certain measures considered militarily necessary; its Article 16 nonetheless declared that military necessity did not permit torture, cruelty for its own sake or wanton devastation. A responsible chapter cannot cite only provisions permitting severe treatment while neglecting provisions designed to restrain abuse. Nor does the existence of a written rule prove that troops complied with it.

The military administration generated extensive paperwork: reports of patrols, captures, quantities of arms seized, civilian petitions, tax records and intelligence summaries. These documents reveal operational priorities but can create an illusion of comprehensiveness. A report describing a pacified district might omit continuing clandestine contributions, while an officer seeking additional troops might exaggerate the danger. Local people also had motives to misrepresent allegiances. Later historians therefore compare official statistics with local histories, captured revolutionary correspondence, congressional testimony and the timing of renewed violence. The gap between apparent and actual political control is one of the conflict's most important methodological lessons.

The long-term result was a system in which American victory emerged through both force and collaboration. Guerrilla detachments could be disrupted by raids, yet they often became vulnerable when civilian intermediaries withdrew aid, surrendered or revealed their networks. Such changes could arise from genuine political preference, exhaustion, economic pressure, fear or calculation about the likely outcome. Counterinsurgency history must examine those causes rather than simply award all success to improved military technique. The transition to colonial government depended on the gradual creation of local relationships of power, which Chapter 119 will investigate after this chapter explains their military origins.

Section 118.12

Resistance throughout the Archipelago: The Visayas, Marinduque, Samar, and Regional Variation

The war varied by island. In Panay, Negros, and Cebu, local elites, existing rivalries, and different revolutionary histories shaped events; Resil Mojares's study of Cebu shows resistance and collaboration as contingent local choices, not fixed loyalties. Marinduque and Samar saw intense guerrilla fighting. Campaigns involving Muslim communities in Mindanao and Sulu followed separate histories, with the Bates Agreement of 1899 with the Sultan of Sulu and later fighting in the early 1900s; they are distinct from the war against the Republic.

A national history told entirely through the northern Luzon campaign would miss much of the Philippine-American War. The archipelago comprised islands with different commercial systems, revolutionary histories, linguistic communities and relationships to Spanish rule. The arrival of American troops altered local conflicts that predated 1899, while the authority of the First Philippine Republic varied considerably. Military operations in Cebu, Panay, Negros, Samar, Leyte and Marinduque therefore cannot be treated as identical copies of the fighting around Malolos. The methods of resistance and occupation depended on local political leadership, communication routes, terrain and access to coastal shipping.

In Cebu, armed opposition and accommodation unfolded amid established municipal, commercial and elite networks. Resil B. Mojares's study emphasizes the mixture of resistance, collaboration and shifting local calculation rather than a permanent division into patriots and traitors. A prominent landholder might cooperate to protect trade or property while another funded guerrillas because of nationalist conviction or local rivalry. The presence of a U.S. garrison could create opportunities for certain officeholders and threaten others. Economic class mattered, but so did kinship, provincial competition and the credibility of American assurances. The chapter should use this evidence to expand national history beyond Manila without assuming that one island represents all the Visayas.

Panay and neighboring islands presented related but distinct circumstances. Revolutionary authority had developed in relation to the anti-Spanish struggle, sometimes independently of the Malolos leadership. Maritime movement and coastal towns could facilitate American arrival, yet inland routes and mountainous areas provided shelter for armed resistance. Control of a port did not mean that surrounding barrios accepted the same government. In Negros, local political arrangements and the response of elites to American occupation differed from developments on Panay. Historians must identify the particular governments and local commanders involved rather than generalize that all Visayan elites simply welcomed annexation or all rural populations fought it.

Marinduque illustrated the vulnerability of American detachments moving in difficult territory; the Battle of Pulang Lupa in September 1900, when an American force suffered a serious reverse, became an example of effective local guerrilla action. American response depended on sea transport, intelligence and punitive operations as well as occupation of towns. On Samar, dense vegetation, rivers, mountains and coastal routes shaped a longer contest that culminated in the widely remembered events at Balangiga in 1901. These theaters show that the operational history did not finish with the dispersal of the main republican army. Local formations could pose serious threats after conventional defeat at the national level.

Mindanao and Sulu require careful classification. Muslim polities, communities and leaders had their own political histories and did not uniformly regard themselves as participants in the Malolos Republic. The Bates Agreement with the sultan of Sulu in 1899 and subsequent American military encounters belonged to a partially separate process of extending imperial authority. Collapsing all such conflicts into the Philippine-American War against the First Republic would erase distinctions in sovereignty, religion, local political structures and chronology. At the same time, American annexation ambitions affected the entire archipelago, making the relationship between these theaters historically relevant. The narrative should explain both the connection and the difference.

A comparison among provinces also exposes the limitations of military statistics. A report of ten towns occupied might signify substantial control in an island with concentrated populations and stable coastal roads; in a mountainous province it might show only possession of municipal centers. Civilian collaboration recorded under threat should be interpreted differently from stable support expressed where people had meaningful alternatives. Regional history thus changes the assessment of national victory. The United States eventually consolidated rule through a collection of local arrangements and campaigns, not through a single decisive battlefield event, while Filipino responses formed a spectrum from sustained armed resistance to strategic accommodation.

Multiple Perspectives II

Tagalog revolutionaries saw an extension of their own movement. Cebuano elites weighed property, autonomy, and Manila's leadership. Samareño communities faced a harsh frontier campaign. American officers often misread local politics through the lens of Luzon. Students should ask why a single national narrative fails.

Section 118.13

The Capture of Emilio Aguinaldo, March 23, 1901

Colonel Frederick Funston led a small expedition to Palanan, Isabela, where Aguinaldo had his headquarters. Using captured correspondence, Macabebe Scouts posing as reinforcements, and American officers posing as prisoners, Funston's party seized Aguinaldo on March 23, 1901. Aguinaldo took an oath of allegiance on April 1 and on April 19 urged his compatriots to accept American sovereignty. Many commanders, including Malvar and Lukban, continued to fight, showing that the movement had outgrown any one leader.

The capture of Aguinaldo required more than an American column pursuing him directly. By 1901 he had retreated into northeastern Luzon, where the terrain complicated large-unit operations and reliable communication with distant commanders. American officers exploited captured correspondence and information about his location, seeking a means of approaching his headquarters without alarming defenders. Frederick Funston devised an operation that depended on deception as well as military coordination. Macabebe Scouts, whose language, appearance and local experience were important to the plan, played the principal role in posing as arriving Filipino reinforcements and conducting the party toward Aguinaldo's position at Palanan.

The expedition represented a distinctive kind of counterinsurgency operation. Rather than defeat the Philippine president's troops through superior artillery, the Americans used knowledge of revolutionary communication and a staged appearance of loyalty. American officers presented themselves as captives, helping the disguised force gain entry. Once close to Aguinaldo, the party seized him on March 23, 1901. The episode illustrates both the operational effectiveness of intelligence and the political importance of Filipino participants who chose to serve American objectives. Describing Funston alone as the captor would understate the contribution of the Scouts and the broader network that made the intelligence possible.

Aguinaldo's capture was a propaganda success for the United States. Officials could represent it as evidence that organized resistance was crumbling and that the new colonial regime was becoming irreversible. For Filipino revolutionaries the loss was more complicated. The president was an emblem of the independence cause and the central link among several commands, but decentralized guerrilla organization had already placed substantial discretion in regional leaders. Some interpreted his capture as a reason to surrender; others saw his predicament as confirming the necessity of continued struggle. The political consequences must be evaluated through subsequent actions in different provinces rather than inferred from a single individual.

The documentary sequence after the capture is relatively clear. Aguinaldo took an oath of allegiance to the United States on April 1, 1901. On April 19 an address attributed to him, published through American military authorities, called upon Filipinos to accept the new sovereignty and end hostilities. These should be treated as distinct acts, with their respective dates preserved. A proclamation produced in custody presents additional questions: it can reveal the signer's stated position and the authorities' political aims, but it cannot automatically demonstrate what the signer's supporters believed or whether all statements were freely formed. The source is valuable precisely because students can examine the conditions of its publication.

The capture also raises problems concerning the representation of Filipino agency. Macabebe Scouts were themselves Filipinos, and their choices were embedded in local rivalries and experiences of earlier conflicts. A national narrative that describes all cooperation with Americans as treachery leaves those historical circumstances unexplained. Conversely, imperial accounts presenting the scouts' participation as proof of broad consent to annexation confuse the decisions of particular recruits with the will of an entire population. The appropriate question is how the American force gained access to local networks and how those networks reshaped military power.

The continued fighting under Miguel Malvar in Batangas, Vicente Lukban on Samar and other local commanders demonstrates the limits of a leader-centered account. Aguinaldo's removal accelerated American efforts to persuade or compel surrender, but it did not terminate armed resistance. Indeed some of the most controversial American operations occurred in the following year. The political conclusion is therefore precise: capture diminished the coordination and symbolic authority of the First Philippine Republic while leaving the broader struggle for independence and local control unresolved.

Historical Debate II

The question: Did the capture destroy the revolution's legitimacy or only weaken its central leadership? A: it removed the symbolic head and encouraged surrenders. B: regional commands kept fighting, and the war's most violent campaigns came after the capture. Evidence: timing of surrenders versus campaigns in Samar and Batangas.

Historical inquiry: Evidence evaluation: the capture weakened central command but regional fighting continued. Which measures—incidents, proclamations, surrender decisions, local contributions—would establish when a political movement truly ceased to operate?

Section 118.14

Samar, Balangiga, and the American Campaign of Reprisal, 1901–1902

On September 28, 1901, in Balangiga, Samar, townspeople and guerrillas attacked Company C of the 9th U.S. Infantry during the morning, killing approximately forty-four to forty-eight American soldiers, depending on the casualty accounting, in a severe American defeat. Americans retaliated with a campaign under Brigadier General Jacob H. Smith, who reportedly instructed Marine Major Littleton Waller to make Samar a "howling wilderness" and to treat males over ten as combatants, claims that became central to later controversy. The campaign destroyed villages, food, and boats and killed civilians. Waller was court-martialed and acquitted; Smith was court-martialed in 1902 and admonished and retired. Balangiga's church bells, taken as war trophies, were returned to the Philippines in 2018.

Balangiga lay on the southern coast of Samar, where the American garrison was small and depended upon relations with local inhabitants for information and supplies. The town's inhabitants had experienced the strains of occupation, including demands for labor and restrictions imposed by soldiers. Tension with Company C of the Ninth U.S. Infantry grew amid the wider conflict between American military control and revolutionary resistance associated with Vicente Lukban's regional command. Accounts differ over the extent of prior planning, the roles of particular town officials and the coordination between residents and guerrillas. The attack cannot be explained adequately by a timeless picture of Filipino hostility or by assuming all participants shared one motive.

On September 28, 1901, Filipino attackers struck the American garrison in the morning when many soldiers were unprepared. Bladed weapons and surprise were especially consequential at close range. About forty-four to forty-eight Americans are commonly recorded as killed, depending on how dead and missing personnel are classified; others were wounded. The action was a severe loss for an isolated American unit, and survivors' accounts helped produce a national outcry in the United States. Philippine public memory often emphasizes the townspeople's resistance to occupation. Both perspectives form part of the historical record, but neither resolves questions about individual conduct or subsequent retaliation without additional evidence.

American response quickly expanded beyond an investigation of those who had participated in the attack. Brigadier General Jacob H. Smith was placed in command of the campaign on Samar and sought to break guerrilla support through sweeping punitive operations. In later court-martial testimony the instruction to make the interior a "howling wilderness" and the discussion of killing males over ten years old became notorious. The record requires careful handling: witnesses differed over wording, operational meaning and the relation between particular orders and field conduct. The historian should identify what a military proceeding actually established and resist treating every later retelling as an independent source. The existence of an alarming command statement is clear; its application to every reported death requires separate evidence.

The punitive campaign involved destruction of buildings and boats, seizure or destruction of food, and mobile operations designed to deprive guerrillas of support and movement. These measures could affect entire communities rather than only armed fighters. The result was suffering and dislocation beyond the immediate battlefield, especially where agriculture and coastal transport were already vulnerable. American military records celebrated the disruption of resistance and listed large quantities of property destroyed; such statistics can reveal the scale of operations while telling little about the fate of individual households. Local Filipino accounts are therefore essential for reconstructing civilian consequences and distinguishing armed opponents from noncombatants.

Marine Major Littleton Waller and General Smith faced courts-martial in connection with conduct or orders in the Samar campaign. Waller was acquitted; Smith was convicted in connection with his instructions and received a reprimand before retirement. The proceedings were shaped by military law, public scrutiny and political controversy, and the outcome should not be represented as proof that no wrongdoing occurred or that all alleged abuses had been judicially established. Congressional debate and reporting also reflected disagreements within the United States over the legitimacy of the occupation and its methods. Students can compare witness testimony, command correspondence and contemporary newspaper reactions to ask whose interests the inquiries served.

The bells taken from Balangiga remained powerful objects of memory. Their eventual return from the United States to the Philippines in 2018 reflected a long struggle over the meanings of conquest, sacrifice and national commemoration. That later history does not change the chronology of 1901, but it shows why artifacts can remain politically significant long after the fighting has ended. The chapter should therefore end its Balangiga discussion by distinguishing event, reprisal, legal reckoning and historical memory. Each layer must be based on evidence appropriate to its own question.

Section 118.15

The Batangas Campaign: Miguel Malvar, Reconcentration, and the End of Organized Resistance

After Aguinaldo's capture, Miguel Malvar led resistance in Batangas and neighboring provinces. In December 1901 Brigadier General J. Franklin Bell began a campaign designed to separate guerrillas from supporters: civilians were ordered into protected zones around towns, outside property and food were destroyed or confiscated, and movement was restricted. Glenn Anthony May's study documents the severe hardship this imposed, and its effects on civilian mortality remain debated. Malvar’s surrender is commonly dated April 16, 1902, although an NHCP historical marker states April 18; it marked the collapse of a major organized resistance command in Luzon.

Batangas and neighboring portions of southern Luzon became among the most difficult areas for American pacification after the collapse of conventional republican warfare. Miguel Malvar commanded forces that continued to draw supplies, intelligence and recruits from local networks. The landscape offered both opportunities and constraints: mountains and wooded districts permitted concealment, while agricultural towns and road systems were essential to the survival of civilians and guerrillas alike. American commanders found that repeated patrols and the occupation of town centers did not reliably eliminate support in the countryside. Civilian loyalties were varied and often conditional, but continuing resistance showed the inadequacy of purely territorial measures of victory.

Brigadier General J. Franklin Bell's campaign sought to make such support unsustainable. Orders issued in December 1901 directed local commanders to establish protected zones near towns and required rural residents to move into specified areas by deadlines. Movement outside these limits could be restricted or treated as suspicious. Food and stores left in the countryside were vulnerable to seizure or destruction, reducing guerrillas' ability to live from local resources. American explanations presented the zones as a means to protect peaceful civilians from insurgent coercion; critics noted that enforced relocation exposed inhabitants to disease, overcrowding, deprivation and the loss of crops and animals.

The administrative mechanism mattered as much as the military rationale. Town boundaries had to be marked, registration and surveillance organized, and supplies delivered or controlled. Residents who moved could lose the ability to maintain farms far from the designated zones. Those who remained in the countryside risked being categorized as aiding guerrillas. The burden was not distributed equally: people with money, carts or connections could sometimes secure food and shelter more readily than families whose survival depended on daily agricultural labor. A policy might appear on a map as a neat perimeter while producing complicated patterns of dislocation across surrounding barrios.

Bell's troops also used intelligence operations, arrests and searches to weaken guerrilla political networks. Municipal officials faced pressure to demonstrate loyalty or identify supporters, and local intermediaries were central to American enforcement. As other chapters of this history show, the mere existence of an order does not establish how uniformly it was applied. Some officers interpreted instructions more aggressively than others, while military reports could minimize or rationalize suffering. Glenn Anthony May's provincial history is therefore important for evaluating both the operations and their impact on ordinary Batangueño families. It questions older narratives that assume the rural population formed a simple, undifferentiated base of revolutionary support.

Malvar faced a deteriorating strategic position. Guerrilla operations required resources that became increasingly hard to obtain, while the civilian population faced mounting hardship. Surrender was not simply a measurement of personal courage or failure; it involved the relation between continued resistance and the survival of communities. April 16, 1902, is commonly reported as the date of his surrender, though a National Historical Commission historical marker gives April 18. The documentary record should be used to distinguish the initial surrender, subsequent formalities and later commemoration rather than conceal the discrepancy. The general outcome is clear: the surrender marked the effective end of the largest remaining organized resistance network on Luzon.

Whether Bell's campaign should be celebrated as effective counterinsurgency or condemned as a system of collective coercion is not a choice that can be answered by one military statistic. The campaign disrupted resistance and obtained surrender, but its effects on livelihoods and health must be examined independently. Military success does not erase civilian suffering, just as civilian suffering does not demonstrate that every reported death was caused directly by an individual American order. The analytical task is to connect military decisions, policy implementation and human consequences without collapsing them into either triumphalist or uniformly denunciatory accounts. This inquiry is central to understanding the war's final phase.

Section 118.16

Torture, Reprisals, Prisoners, and the Laws of War

The "water cure," involving the forced administration of water to extract information, was documented in testimony concerning American military interrogations; other abuses included summary executions and village burning. Filipino guerrillas also killed prisoners and assassinated or buried alive suspected collaborators. The scale of power was unequal: the American army conducted campaigns at a scale that produced far greater civilian harm. Senate hearings chaired by Henry Cabot Lodge in 1902 aired testimony of abuses; a few courts-martial followed with mostly light sentences. Defenders cited military necessity; critics cited the army's own rules against cruelty.

The Philippine-American War was fought under legal and moral claims that frequently conflicted with military practice. American commanders described the Filipino army as an insurgent force because Washington claimed sovereignty through the Treaty of Paris. Filipino leaders described their troops as soldiers of an independent republic resisting foreign conquest. The legal classification shaped arguments about prisoners, military jurisdiction and the permissible treatment of irregular fighters. Yet rules governing wartime conduct also imposed restrictions on a state's treatment of enemy soldiers and civilians, regardless of the political terminology chosen. A chapter examining atrocities must distinguish these categories instead of assuming that the word insurrection settled every legal question.

The Lieber Code of 1863 remained influential in American military thinking. Its provisions included harsh categories for armed irregulars not regarded as conventional belligerents, but Article 16 declared that military necessity did not justify cruelty, torture to extort confessions or unnecessary destruction. Other articles addressed protection of property, treatment of prisoners and occupation. In the Philippines officers invoked military necessity to justify coercive interrogation, detention and communal punishments, while critics used established rules of war to condemn abuse. Historical analysis should not infer compliance simply because a code existed, nor should it treat every military operation as a violation. It must ask what was done, by whom, on whose orders and with what consequences.

The so-called water cure forced or introduced large quantities of water into detainees as a method of interrogation or punishment. Testimony during American congressional investigation described its use, and the practice became a powerful symbol in debates about imperial violence. Other abuses included beating, threats, extrajudicial killing, destruction of property and coercive confinement. Some testimony was direct; other statements were hearsay or politically charged. The historian must evaluate individual allegations by comparing witnesses, the dates and locations of incidents, military records and subsequent findings. A blanket dismissal of reports as anti-imperialist propaganda is unjustified, but a list of unexamined accusations is not an adequate scholarly account either.

Violence by Filipino guerrilla forces also formed part of the conflict. Suspected informants and collaborators could face intimidation, killing or severe punishment; captives and civilians were not always protected. Such actions should be documented by local circumstance and command responsibility, rather than casually placed alongside American atrocities as if the opposing forces possessed equivalent coercive capacities. The U.S. Army had superior firepower, extensive transport and the ability to impose broad restrictions across districts, so the scale and mechanisms of its violence differed from those of guerrilla detachments. Acknowledging violence on both sides does not require erasing those differences. Proportional historical evaluation compares evidence, institutional reach and civilian impact.

The hearings and courts-martial of 1902 are essential sources because they record accusations, admissions, defenses and official investigations. Senate Document 422 assembled testimony concerning army conduct, reconcentration, colonial administration and the future of self-government. It was a product of congressional controversy, not a transparent catalogue of all incidents. Witnesses had political stakes, some sought to defend their careers, and coverage was uneven. Still, the existence of contemporaneous testimony prevents later historians from dismissing the issue as an invention of subsequent generations. Proceedings against Smith and Waller also permit examination of the difference between disciplinary decisions and the historical evaluation of military policy.

The moral and evidentiary stakes are most visible when studying civilians who never served in a formal army. Reprisal operations, property seizures and restrictions on movement could impose severe costs on people whose political allegiances were unknowable. Students should ask whether the declared military objective was connected to the actual means used and whether less destructive alternatives were available. The chapter does not need to stage a false equivalence between revolutionary and imperial violence; it needs a consistent evidentiary method applied to all documented conduct. That method is essential to explaining how war-making became a central element in the establishment of American colonial authority.

Historical Vocabulary III

Laws of war: rules limiting conduct in conflict, including the 1899 Hague Conventions and the Lieber Code. Reprisal: retaliation, limited by law. Collective punishment: penalizing a group for acts of individuals. Military necessity: a claim that measures are required for victory, which does not license unlimited force.

Historical inquiry: Historical terms should not replace evidence. A claim of military necessity is an argument to be evaluated against actual orders, available alternatives, the relevant legal rules and consequences for noncombatants.

Division V — The Human Cost, American Victory, and the Meaning of the War

Section 118.17

Disease, Famine, Destruction, and the Human Cost of War

The U.S. State Department's historians cite more than 4,200 American and more than 20,000 Filipino combatant deaths, and as many as 200,000 Filipino civilian deaths, mostly from famine and disease. These are widely cited estimates, not agreed totals: the Philippines lacked reliable vital statistics, and deaths from cholera, malaria, and other diseases, the 1902–1904 cholera epidemic, and the rinderpest epidemic that killed draft animals are hard to separate from the war. Violence, displacement, crop destruction, and concentration all increased vulnerability.

Quantifying the war's human cost is difficult because the available figures were compiled for different purposes and often cover different dates, populations and causes of death. American military records documented their own casualties comparatively well but were less consistent in counting Filipino soldiers and civilians. Revolutionary forces did not maintain comprehensive, surviving registers for all districts. Parish records, population enumerations, provincial reports and medical documents offer other evidence, yet these too are incomplete and affected by displacement. A responsible narrative must distinguish a recorded count from an estimate and must avoid treating one widely repeated total as a final demographic verdict.

The U.S. Department of State's historical summary cites more than 4,200 American military deaths and more than 20,000 Filipino combatant deaths, while estimating as many as 200,000 Filipino civilian deaths, many associated with famine and disease. These figures help establish the scale of loss but should be paired with an explanation of their uncertainty. Some historians derive much higher estimates by comparing populations across intervals that include other crises; others use narrower periods or more restrictive attribution. A population deficit is not the same as a list of persons killed in battle, and mortality after July 1902 cannot simply be assigned to the war without considering wider epidemic and economic conditions.

Disease was both a cause of death and an indicator of social disruption. Cholera, malaria, dysentery and other illnesses affected troops and civilians, while damaged water systems, crowded settlements and inadequate food increased vulnerability. The severe cholera epidemic beginning in 1902 extended beyond the United States' formal declaration that the insurrection had ended. Historians debate how much epidemic mortality should be counted within the war's cost, since combat, displacement and destruction could worsen exposure even when a pathogen was not introduced by the fighting. The correct method is to identify the disease, place and period separately, then assess how military policy altered susceptibility and access to care.

Agricultural devastation compounded the crisis. Rinderpest and other animal diseases reduced the number of carabao available for plowing and transport. Lost draft animals made it difficult to plant fields or move rice, increasing food prices and the risks of hunger. Army requisitions, burned stores, destroyed boats, disrupted irrigation and restrictions on movement could magnify those losses. In some places villagers could rebuild quickly once security improved; elsewhere the cumulative effects of repeated military operations and epidemic disease lasted for years. These regional differences caution against projecting the most heavily devastated provinces onto the entire archipelago or minimizing destruction because some towns recovered.

Forced relocation deserves attention as a distinct social phenomenon. Families moved into designated zones or fled violence without formal orders, sometimes leaving crops, dwellings and livestock behind. Crowding could make disease spread more readily, and loss of access to fields reduced household resilience. Official assertions that protected zones improved civilian safety should be tested against accounts of food supply, sanitation and freedom of movement. A military commander might count residents relocated as removed from guerrilla influence, while the residents themselves experienced confinement and the risk of hunger. Different kinds of data capture different parts of this history.

Section 118.18

American Anti-Imperialism, Race, and the Politics of Conquest

American opponents included Andrew Carnegie, Mark Twain, Carl Schurz, and the League's members, though their reasons varied: some defended republican principle, others feared racial mixing and cheap labor. Imperialists often spoke of Filipinos as unready for self-rule, drawing on racial hierarchy; Paul Kramer's work traces how race shaped the war's conduct and politics. Some African American soldiers and writers criticized the campaign as racial domination, while a few Black soldiers deserted to the Filipino side, the best-known being David Fagen. Among Filipinos, elites debated whether to resist, negotiate, or accommodate, and the Federal Party, founded in 1900, advocated accepting American rule.

The American debate over the Philippines took place within a republic that professed government by consent while maintaining racial segregation and denying full equality to many of its own inhabitants. Supporters of annexation frequently represented Filipinos as incapable of immediate self-rule or as requiring American tutelage. Such claims were not merely descriptions of political institutions; they drew upon contemporary racial classifications and assumptions about civilization. The existence of a Philippine constitution, educated leaders and functioning provincial networks challenged claims of incapacity, but American advocates could still argue that their own government would better secure stability and development. The moral contradiction between asserted benevolence and armed conquest became a major theme of domestic political criticism.

Opponents of empire also differed among themselves. Andrew Carnegie argued against permanent annexation and contemplated financial or diplomatic alternatives; Mark Twain's writing attacked the violence and hypocrisy of imperial policy; politicians such as Carl Schurz framed empire as a danger to constitutional republicanism. Other opponents worried about military expenditures, competition from overseas labor or demographic incorporation of nonwhite subjects. Thus an anti-imperialist position did not necessarily imply an egalitarian view of the Filipino population. The content of arguments matters more than the label attached to their authors, and students should evaluate particular speeches, editorials and pamphlets rather than assume the League possessed a single unified ideology.

African American responses exposed additional contradictions. Black soldiers serving in the U.S. Army could identify with professional military service and loyalty to their units while also encountering racial discrimination in the American armed forces and at home. African American newspapers and political commentators debated what it meant for a nation practicing racial inequality to deny self-government to another nonwhite population. David Fagen, an African American soldier who deserted and joined Filipino resistance, became a particularly prominent figure, but his example should not be treated as representative of all Black soldiers. Many served throughout the campaign, and their experiences and opinions were diverse.

American racial thinking also shaped the language of military command. Some officers referred to Filipinos in derogatory terms, generalized about supposed cultural traits or portrayed warfare as a civilizational contest. These statements influenced perceptions of danger and the treatment of communities, although direct causal links between ideology and a particular atrocity must be supported rather than assumed. Paul A. Kramer's analysis of race and empire is useful because it demonstrates how categories were produced through military encounters, administrative classification and American political debates. Race was not merely a prejudicial attitude held by isolated soldiers; it formed part of the vocabulary through which imperial power was justified and organized.

On the Philippine side, judgments about collaboration were also complicated. Some politicians thought accommodation could preserve property, expand local participation or secure gradual self-government when military victory was no longer realistic. Others saw every concession as a retreat from independence. The Federal Party, founded in 1900, became an important vehicle for support of American authority, though its program and constituency changed over time. Such political decisions occurred amid the destruction of republican military institutions and the material pressures of occupation. Historians should distinguish free preference from constrained choice, without assuming every collaborator acted solely from fear or every guerrilla solely from idealism.

Filipino nationalists: a war for independence. American imperialists: a duty and strategic opportunity. American anti-imperialists: a betrayal of republican principle, sometimes on racial grounds. African American critics: skeptical of fighting a darker-skinned people on behalf of an empire that denied equality at home. Power was unequal among these groups.

Section 118.19

The American Declaration of Victory, July 4, 1902, and Continuing Resistance

On July 4, 1902, President Theodore Roosevelt proclaimed the insurrection ended and announced amnesty, with the proclamation expressly excluding the Moro-inhabited areas. Resistance continued: bands in several provinces, religious and millenarian movements, and Macario Sakay's "Tagalog Republic" fought into 1906; Sakay was captured by a ruse and executed in 1907. Moro resistance continued through the 1910s in a separate conflict. The declaration marks a political and legal endpoint, not the disappearance of armed opposition.

The date July 4, 1902, acquired significance because the American government chose it as the moment to declare that organized insurrection had ended. President Theodore Roosevelt issued a proclamation of peace and amnesty on the United States' Independence Day, a symbolic choice that aligned the consolidation of overseas authority with national celebration at home. Its text asserted that resistance to American sovereignty had been suppressed in most of the archipelago and provided amnesty for many offenses connected with the conflict. The proclamation also contained exceptions and conditions that require careful reading. A proclamation is an act of executive government, not an independent census of armed men or a demonstration that every community accepted the claim it announced.

The language of the document is especially revealing. It uses the term insurrection, presupposing that the United States possessed legitimate sovereignty against which Filipinos had rebelled. It also excludes the territory described as inhabited by the Moro peoples from the peace declaration. That clause exposes the distinction between the conflict against the Philippine Republic and subsequent operations in Mindanao and Sulu. A student comparing the document with Filipino proclamations should ask who possessed the power to name a conflict, to declare its end and to decide which regions belonged within the account. The official American endpoint corresponds to a major transformation in organized warfare, but it cannot by itself establish political consent.

By 1902 a series of significant commanders had surrendered or been captured. Aguinaldo's capture in 1901, Lukban's capture in February 1902 and Malvar's surrender that spring greatly reduced the coordination and scale of resistance associated with the republic. American forces could therefore claim substantial success against its main armed structures. Yet armed groups continued in various provinces for years, including movements that described themselves in nationalist, religious or local terms. The extent to which each was a direct continuation of the wartime republic has to be studied rather than assumed. Different movements inherited different leaders, political vocabularies and local bases of support.

Macario Sakay is a particularly important example of continued resistance associated with the independence cause. His forces operated after the official endpoint, and the history of his Tagalog Republic and subsequent capture in 1906 and execution in 1907 complicates a neat narrative of peace descending in July 1902. The story also illustrates the danger of simply copying American descriptions of later armed opponents as bandits. At the same time, the evidence for particular activities, manifestos and dates needs to be separated from retrospective nationalist commemoration. The continuation of armed resistance does not imply that all such groups operated under an unbroken chain of command from Aguinaldo's government.

The evolving American colonial apparatus benefited from the reduction of large-scale fighting. Roads could be repaired, officials could travel more predictably, and civilian administration could expand beyond garrison towns. Yet these changes were uneven. Military authorities remained influential in areas where security was contested, and an announced civilian government could depend on troops to enforce its decisions. The process of pacification therefore requires several different indicators: the decline of organized attacks, the ability to collect taxes, local elections or appointments, freedom of travel and the willingness of inhabitants to follow civil instructions without coercion. An executive announcement alone cannot demonstrate all of them.

The Establishment of American Colonial Rule and the Historical Legacy of the War

American military government gave way to civilian rule under the Taft Commission in 1901, and the Philippine Organic Act of 1902 formalized it, with cooperating Filipino elites incorporated into local and later national politics. Military victory therefore created the framework for colonial government while leaving the independence question unresolved; Chapter 119 examines how that government worked. Historians still disagree on how to characterize the war.

The establishment of American colonial rule did not wait until every guerrilla had surrendered. Military authorities occupied and administered towns while civilian institutions were being organized in areas deemed sufficiently secure. The Second Philippine Commission, associated with William Howard Taft, expanded American administrative control and offered certain Filipino leaders opportunities to participate in municipal and provincial government. Its activities overlapped with military operations, illustrating how war and civil administration developed together. The claim that the United States governed for the welfare of inhabitants should be examined against the distribution of authority, the absence of sovereign Filipino consent and the continued use of troops to secure compliance.

The Philippine Organic Act of 1902 established an important legal framework for civilian colonial government. It did not grant immediate independence, and the system remained subordinate to American sovereignty. Its provisions concerning government and political participation belonged to a longer project of reorganizing the islands after military conquest. For Chapter 118 the essential fact is chronological and institutional: military victory gave civil administrators a territorial base and the ability to incorporate cooperative local elites. Chapter 119 will study how education, administrative law, taxation, infrastructure and electoral politics operated once this new structure had been established.

Filipino elites entered the colonial order for varied reasons. Some believed continued armed struggle had become impossible and that participation in new institutions offered the best chance to protect communities or advance eventual self-government. Others gained influence and resources through cooperation or accepted American rule as preferable to earlier local opponents. Such choices did not necessarily signify popular approval of annexation. A family could oppose foreign sovereignty in principle and still seek office, schooling or protection under the existing government. The historian should examine the incentives and restrictions that shaped participation rather than collapse every change of allegiance into either opportunism or reconciliation.

The war also shaped American military institutions. Officers learned methods of intelligence gathering, dispersed garrisons and cooperation with local auxiliaries, while public scrutiny of torture and reprisals exposed limits of the military's claims to professional restraint. Retrospective accounts sometimes portray the campaign as a model of successful counterinsurgency; others emphasize its coercion and civilian consequences. Both may contain evidence of real operational processes, but neither should replace direct analysis of the measures taken in particular provinces. A technique could be effective at reducing armed attacks while imposing serious harm on civilians. Operational success and legal or moral assessment must remain analytically distinct.

Philippine national memory subsequently honored leaders and localities that had resisted the United States. Memorialization of the first shots at Santa Mesa, Tirad Pass, Balangiga and the guerrilla campaigns demonstrates that the conflict remained meaningful long after American officials declared it ended. Commemorations, however, are not substitutes for the archival record; they select heroes, landmarks and language to answer the needs of later generations. The historian's task is to place memory alongside contemporary correspondence, Philippine testimonies, demographic studies and military records. This approach respects the independence movement without turning it into a collection of unqualified legends.

Chapter Summary

The Philippine-American War developed from conflicting claims to sovereignty after the defeat of Spain. The Treaty of Paris transferred Spain’s claim to the United States without Philippine participation, while the Malolos Republic asserted that revolution had established a government with its own institutions, army, and authority. This was a collision of political projects, not merely a dispute between ambitious commanders.

Fighting began in the Manila suburbs in February 1899. Historical investigations identify American sentry William Grayson as firing the opening shots, while the circumstances of escalation and responsibility for general hostilities remain contested. The movement of the first-shot historical marker from San Juan Bridge to Santa Mesa illustrates the need to separate contemporary evidence from later commemoration.

American forces possessed important advantages in equipment, logistics, artillery and naval support. The battles around Manila, the Central Luzon campaign and the fall of Malolos demonstrated these strengths. Yet the capture of a capital did not end the revolutionary movement, which possessed political networks and military units outside the territories occupied by American columns.

The republican army faced material shortages, difficult command coordination and disputes among political and military leaders. Antonio Luna’s attempts to professionalize the army and his assassination in June 1899 illuminate the relationship between discipline and legitimacy. The northern retreat and the death of Gregorio del Pilar at Tirad Pass show how the struggle moved away from major fixed positions.

From late 1899 many Filipino commanders relied increasingly on guerrilla methods. Attacks against patrols and communications required local information, food, shelter and networks of civilian assistance. These techniques reduced exposure to superior American firepower but also moved the conflict into agricultural communities, where neither neutrality nor loyalty was always a safe choice.

American counterinsurgency combined mobile detachments and numerous garrisons with Filipino scouts, political inducements and the gradual formation of civilian administration. Different commanders balanced coercion and conciliation in different ways. The local history of Cebu and other provinces shows that resistance and accommodation were shaped by political conditions that a Manila-centered account cannot fully explain.

Aguinaldo’s capture in March 1901 and his subsequent April declarations weakened the central authority of the First Philippine Republic but did not end major regional campaigns. The attack at Balangiga in September 1901 and retaliatory operations on Samar generated enduring controversy about the use of punitive force, command responsibility and the treatment of civilians.

In Batangas, General J. Franklin Bell’s concentration policy sought to deny supplies to guerrillas by restricting civilian movement and access to the countryside. The surrender of Miguel Malvar in April 1902 marked a decisive military change, but the suffering accompanying reconcentration requires assessment independent of battlefield results. The laws of war, including the Lieber Code’s protections against torture and cruelty, offer one framework for investigating these operations.

The cost of the war included military casualties, deaths from disease and hunger, displacement, destroyed livelihoods and the loss of draft animals needed for agriculture. Precise civilian totals remain disputed because data are incomplete and the severe epidemic crises straddled the formal endpoint of the war. Estimates should be identified by period, cause and method rather than combined without explanation.

Roosevelt’s July 4, 1902 proclamation marked the official end of the U.S. war against the Philippine Republic, while armed opposition continued in some areas and separate imperial campaigns unfolded in Mindanao and Sulu. Military victory enabled an American colonial system, but neither political consent nor Filipino nationalist aspirations disappeared. The next chapter examines the institutions and conflicts of the resulting colonial order.

Chapter Reflection

The Philippine-American War demonstrates how a claim of national sovereignty can survive the loss of a capital and the defeat of conventional armies. It also demonstrates the costs of trying to maintain that claim while a stronger power extends military and administrative control. Neither a purely diplomatic account nor a sequence of battlefield victories can explain the conflict without the local political networks on which guerrillas and occupiers relied.

The civilian history is not secondary to the military history. Farmers, merchants, women, clergy and local officials were drawn into the struggle through taxes, labor, intelligence, displacement and fear. Reconcentration, reprisals, disease and loss of agricultural resources make the consequences of war impossible to measure solely by casualties on the battlefield. Historical rigor requires acknowledging these harms while remaining careful about disputed numbers and the attribution of particular acts.

The archival record is uneven. American official papers are abundant, while the views of many Filipinos survive through captured documents, oral histories, regional studies and later memory. Each source has limits. The historian must neither equate American state records with disinterested fact nor treat every patriotic remembrance as a transparent eyewitness account. Comparison is the foundation of a reliable synthesis.

In 1902 an American proclamation declared the conflict ended; the political demand for Philippine independence did not disappear. The resulting government created institutions that would transform the archipelago while remaining dependent on a sovereignty Filipinos had contested with arms. Chapter 119 examines this colonial order, its reforms and contradictions, and the ways national politics continued under American administration.
