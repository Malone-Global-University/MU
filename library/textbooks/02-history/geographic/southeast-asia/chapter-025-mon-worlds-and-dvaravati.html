Mon Worlds and Dvaravati

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

A small silver medallion, struck sometime in the middle centuries of the first millennium CE and later recovered from the soil of Nakhon Pathom, bears a brief Sanskrit inscription that modern scholars read as naming "Dvaravati." The name itself was not a modern discovery: it had already been reconstructed decades earlier from Chinese textual and geographical sources, especially the seventh-century account of the Buddhist pilgrim Xuanzang, which lists a southern polity whose name scholars read as a transcription of Dvaravati. What the medallion and its companions provided was something different but no less consequential: concrete, locally produced archaeological confirmation that a place or polity called Dvaravati had genuinely existed in the Chao Phraya world. That confirmation encouraged generations of historians to imagine a unified Mon kingdom stretching across central Thailand, and continues to raise a question this chapter cannot fully resolve. What, precisely, was Dvaravati: one kingdom, several related polities, or a shared artistic and religious culture that never required political unity at all?

Travel today along the lower and middle reaches of the Chao Phraya–Tha Chin system and the neighboring Mae Klong basin, and the modern landscape gives few outward signs of the world this chapter studies. Rice fields, provincial towns, and modern highways occupy ground that, roughly between the fourth and the eleventh centuries CE, supported a dense scatter of moated and walled settlements. Within and around those settlements, communities that scholars generally associate with Mon-speaking populations built Buddhist monuments, produced a distinctive sculptural tradition, minted silver coinage bearing Sanskrit legends and Brahmanical or Buddhist symbols, and maintained commercial and religious connections stretching from the Bay of Bengal to the Khorat Plateau and the Gulf of Thailand. Archaeologists and art historians call this world "Dvaravati," a name reconstructed decades earlier from a Chinese textual reference and subsequently confirmed archaeologically by inscribed silver medallions found at Nakhon Pathom and elsewhere, but the name has always carried more political weight than the fragmentary evidence can securely bear.

This is not a minor definitional quibble confined to specialists. Older generations of scholarship, working from a relatively small body of evidence concentrated at a few prominent sites, often described "the Dvaravati kingdom" as though it functioned like a single, centrally administered state comparable to Angkor or Pagan, examined later in this textbook. Subsequent decades of archaeological survey and excavation across the Chao Phraya basin and neighboring regions have complicated that picture considerably. Numerous walled and moated settlements, each with Buddhist monuments, inscriptions, coinage, and craft production of broadly similar character, appear scattered across a wide area with no securely documented evidence of a single overarching capital, dynasty, or administrative hierarchy binding them together for the entire five centuries during which Dvaravati-style material culture flourished.

This chapter therefore approaches Dvaravati the way Chapter 24 approached the Pyu city-states of the Irrawaddy basin: as a genuinely important early Southeast Asian world whose political shape must be reconstructed cautiously from archaeology, inscriptions, coinage, and art, rather than assumed in advance from later, retrospective, or overconfident narratives. The chapter's central historical problem, inherited directly from the uncertainty embedded in the evidence itself, can be stated plainly: was "Dvaravati" a single Mon kingdom with a capital and a ruling dynasty, a loosely connected network of related but politically independent centers, or chiefly a shared cultural and artistic horizon that historians have, perhaps too readily, translated into the language of statehood?

Answering that question responsibly requires attention to several distinct centers, each with its own excavation history and its own claim to significance: Nakhon Pathom, long regarded as a leading candidate for a Dvaravati "capital"; U Thong and Khu Bua, whose material culture illustrates the breadth of Dvaravati-style settlement; Lopburi, later remembered as Lavo and destined to play a continuing role in the region's political history; and Si Thep, a site whose artistic and religious connections to the Dvaravati world are unmistakable even as its material culture displays distinctive features that complicate any simple inclusion within a single political unit.

The chapter also insists on a second, closely related distinction. "Mon," "Dvaravati," and "central Thailand" are overlapping historical categories, not interchangeable synonyms. Mon-speaking communities extended well beyond the specific archaeological horizon that scholars call Dvaravati, both chronologically and geographically, and continued to play significant roles in Southeast Asian history for centuries after the Dvaravati horizon itself had transformed into other political configurations. The land later organized as the Kingdom of Thailand did not yet exist in this period in any political or ethnic sense, and Dvaravati should not be read as an early chapter in a specifically Thai national story, a caution that recurs at several points below and connects directly to the treatment of Tai-speaking migration in later chapters of this textbook.

Finally, this chapter takes seriously the kinds of evidence available for reconstructing Dvaravati: excavated settlement remains, Buddhist monuments and votive objects, Old Mon and Sanskrit inscriptions, coinage bearing religious and dynastic symbols, sculpture in stone and stucco, and scattered references in Chinese sources that may, with appropriate caution, correspond to polities in the Chao Phraya region. None of these sources speaks with a single voice, and none by itself demonstrates political centralization. Reading them together, critically and without forcing them into a tidy narrative of kingdom-building, is the central historical task of this chapter.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain what the term "Dvaravati" refers to and distinguish its use as a place-name attested in inscriptions, an art-historical style, an archaeological horizon, and a hypothesized political kingdom.
  2. Describe the physical geography of the Chao Phraya basin and explain how rivers, floodplains, and overland routes shaped settlement and exchange.
  3. Trace the chronological development of Dvaravati-associated material culture from a proto-Dvaravati phase through its later transformation.
  4. Identify major Dvaravati-associated centers, including Nakhon Pathom, U Thong, Khu Bua, Lopburi, and Si Thep, and describe what archaeology reveals about each.
  5. Evaluate competing scholarly interpretations of Dvaravati's political organization, including single-kingdom, multicentered-network, and cultural-horizon models.
  6. Analyze the relationship, and the important differences, among the categories "Mon-speaking," "Dvaravati," and "central Thailand."
  7. Describe Dvaravati settlement patterns, including moated cities, agricultural hinterlands, and their relationship to waterways.
  8. Explain the significance of Dvaravati coinage, craft production, and long-distance trade for understanding the region's economy.
  9. Examine the role of Buddhism, and of continuing Brahmanical worship, in Dvaravati religious life and material culture.
  10. Interpret distinctive Dvaravati artistic forms, including the dharmacakra and characteristic Buddha imagery, as evidence of local religious and artistic innovation.
  11. Apply the concept of selective localization to Dvaravati's relationship with South Asian religious, political, and artistic traditions.
  12. Assess why Dvaravati should be understood as transforming into later political worlds rather than simply disappearing or being replaced.

Historical Context

Chapter 18 examined the expansion of Indian Ocean and South China Sea trade networks that linked South Asia, mainland and island Southeast Asia, and China during the first millennium CE, and Chapter 19 introduced the historiographical debate over "Indianization," arguing that Southeast Asian communities selectively adopted and transformed Sanskritic religious, political, and artistic traditions rather than passively receiving them. Chapter 20 examined Funan and the lower Mekong delta, Chapter 21 traced the political transformations conventionally grouped under the label Chenla, and Chapter 24, immediately preceding this one, examined the Pyu city-states of the Irrawaddy basin as a genuinely important but only partially centralized early urban world. This chapter extends that comparative project eastward across the mainland to the Chao Phraya basin.

By the middle centuries of the first millennium CE, the Chao Phraya basin already supported established agricultural and exchange networks with roots extending back through the Iron Age societies examined in Chapter 16. Bronze and iron metallurgy, expanding wet-rice cultivation, and growing regional exchange in metals, salt, and forest and marine products had, over preceding centuries, supported increasingly complex settlement and social organization across the basin and its surrounding uplands. These developments did not by themselves produce a unified political system, but they created the demographic density, agricultural surplus, and commercial infrastructure within which the more visibly monumental, Buddhist-associated societies of the Dvaravati horizon would later emerge.

The Chao Phraya basin also sat within reach of the same expanding Indian Ocean and Bay of Bengal exchange networks that shaped Funan, the Pyu cities, and other early historic Southeast Asian societies. Maritime and overland routes connected the basin westward toward the Andaman coast and the Bay of Bengal, northward toward the Pyu-associated Irrawaddy world, eastward toward the Khorat Plateau and the emerging Khmer-associated societies of the middle Mekong, and southward toward the Gulf of Thailand and the Malay Peninsula, examined in Chapter 26. This position at the intersection of several regional networks helps explain both the presence of South Asian religious and artistic influence in Dvaravati material culture and the region's own capacity to redistribute goods, ideas, and religious practices onward to neighboring societies.

It is within this context, and not as an isolated or self-contained development, that the settlements and communities conventionally labeled "Dvaravati" should be understood. They were neither an untouched indigenous society discovering Indian religion for the first time nor a colonial outpost of an Indian civilization; they were Southeast Asian communities embedded in regional exchange, applying inherited local traditions of settlement, agriculture, and craft production to new religious, artistic, and commercial opportunities that expanding Indian Ocean and mainland networks made available.

Section 25.1

The Chao Phraya Basin before Dvaravati

Long before any settlement in the Chao Phraya basin produced the moated cities, Buddhist monuments, or inscribed coinage associated with the Dvaravati horizon, communities across the region had already established the agricultural and technological foundations on which later developments depended. Chapter 16 examined the spread of bronze and, later, iron metallurgy across mainland Southeast Asia, along with the expanding agricultural systems, elite display, and regional exchange networks that metallurgy helped support. Sites across the Chao Phraya basin and its surrounding uplands participated in these Iron Age developments, cultivating rice in the basin's fertile alluvial soils, exploiting riverine and marine resources, and exchanging metal goods, salt, and other commodities across increasingly extensive networks.

These Iron Age communities were not simply a passive backdrop awaiting the arrival of Buddhism and Sanskritic political ideas. They had already developed settled village life, social differentiation visible in burial practices, and craft specialization in ceramics, metalwork, and other goods. What changed in the centuries that followed was not the sudden creation of society where none had existed, but the layering of new religious institutions, new artistic vocabularies, new forms of long-distance exchange, and, in some places, new and more elaborate settlement architecture onto societies that had already achieved considerable social and economic complexity.

This chapter does not attempt to retell that earlier Iron Age history in detail, since Chapter 16 examines it directly. It is important, however, for students to recognize that the moated settlements and Buddhist monuments discussed later in this chapter did not spring from an undeveloped landscape. They represent a transformation and elaboration of existing agricultural, technological, and social patterns under the influence of expanding regional and Indian Ocean exchange, rather than the arrival of civilization in a previously empty or undeveloped space.

Section 25.2

What Does "Dvaravati" Mean?

Before examining specific sites or historical developments, students must understand that "Dvaravati" is used in modern scholarship in at least four overlapping but distinct senses, and that failing to distinguish them is the single most common source of confusion in popular accounts of this period.

First, "Dvaravati" is a place-name attested in the historical evidence itself, and its modern scholarly identification actually proceeded in the opposite order from what popular accounts sometimes suggest. Historians first reconstructed the name "Dvaravati" by comparing Chinese textual and geographical sources, especially the seventh-century pilgrim Xuanzang's listing of a southern polity rendered as T'o-lo-po-ti, with the Sanskrit form it most plausibly transcribed. Only later did local archaeological evidence confirm this reconstruction directly: a small number of inscribed silver medallions recovered from ritual or foundation-deposit contexts at Nakhon Pathom and U Thong carry a Sanskrit formula naming "Dvaravati" and its "lord," and a separate Sanskrit stone inscription from Wat Phra Ngam at Nakhon Pathom names the city of Dvaravati explicitly, outside any numismatic context at all. Together, these sources demonstrate that a real place or polity bearing this name existed in the Chao Phraya basin, even though, as later sections of this chapter explain, they cannot by themselves establish how large or how centrally governed it was.

Second, "Dvaravati" functions in modern scholarship as an art-historical style label, describing a recognizable body of sculpture, architecture, and decorative motifs, particularly Buddha images with distinctive facial features and hand gestures, dharmacakra wheels, and stucco relief ornament, that appears at numerous sites across the Chao Phraya basin and beyond.

Third, "Dvaravati" designates an archaeological horizon, meaning a set of material culture, including moated settlement patterns, coinage types, votive tablets, and construction techniques, that appears broadly contemporaneously across many sites, allowing archaeologists to group them together chronologically and culturally regardless of whether those sites were ever politically connected.

Fourth, and most contested, "Dvaravati" has often been used, especially in earlier twentieth-century scholarship, as the name of a hypothesized political kingdom, imagined as centrally governed, possessing a capital, a ruling dynasty, and defined territorial boundaries comparable to better-documented mainland states such as Angkor or Pagan. It is this fourth usage that the evidence supports least securely. The existence of an inscribed place-name, a shared artistic style, and a broadly contemporaneous archaeological horizon does not, by itself, demonstrate that a single government administered the entire area in which Dvaravati-style material culture appears. This chapter uses "Dvaravati" throughout primarily in the second and third senses, as an art-historical and archaeological label, while treating the fourth, political sense as an open historical question addressed directly in Section 25.9.

Historical Vocabulary
Dvaravati

Dvaravati is a Sanskrit name, sometimes translated approximately as "the one having gates" or "the gated city," attested on inscribed silver medallions from several Chao Phraya basin sites, most prominently Nakhon Pathom, and also confirmed directly in a Sanskrit inscription from Wat Phra Ngam at Nakhon Pathom that names the city of Dvaravati explicitly. The name had, in fact, been identified by scholars well before these inscriptions were studied in detail, through comparison with Chinese textual and geographical sources, especially the seventh-century pilgrim Xuanzang's account, which refers to a southern polity rendered as T'o-lo-po-ti. This linguistic and geographic identification of Chinese T'o-lo-po-ti with Dvaravati is conventional and longstanding among specialists; what remains genuinely uncertain is not whether the name corresponds to a real place, but the territorial extent, political structure, and relationship of that polity to the wider archaeological horizon examined in this chapter.

Modern historians and archaeologists have extended the name Dvaravati well beyond its original inscriptional context, applying it to an entire regional style of Buddhist art and architecture and to the broader archaeological horizon within which that style appears. This extension has been useful for organizing an otherwise scattered body of evidence, but it has also encouraged, especially in earlier scholarship, an unwarranted assumption that a single inscribed name necessarily corresponds to a single unified kingdom governing every site where Dvaravati-style material culture has been found.

Students should therefore treat "Dvaravati" as a modern scholarly convenience built around a genuine ancient name, rather than as proof, on its own, of the kind of centralized political structure the word "kingdom" often implies in casual usage.

Historical Vocabulary
Cultural Horizon

A cultural horizon, in archaeological usage, refers to a widespread and broadly contemporaneous pattern of material culture, such as pottery styles, architectural forms, coinage, or artistic motifs, that appears across a geographic area without necessarily indicating political, linguistic, or ethnic unity among the communities that produced it. Archaeologists use the concept to describe connections and shared practices among sites without assuming that those connections took the form of a single government, a single ethnic group, or a single language community.

Applying this concept to Dvaravati allows historians to acknowledge the genuine similarities among Nakhon Pathom, U Thong, Khu Bua, and other sites, similarities in coinage, sculpture, religious architecture, and settlement form, without committing to the stronger and less well-supported claim that these similarities resulted from centralized political control. A cultural horizon may emerge through trade, shared religious institutions, itinerant craftspeople, marriage alliances, or simple imitation of prestigious styles, none of which requires a single ruling authority.

Section 25.3

Geography of the Chao Phraya Basin

The Chao Phraya River, together with the Tha Chin, one of its distributary channels, and the neighboring Mae Klong, a separate river system draining an adjoining basin to the west, together form an interconnected lowland world at the heart of what is today central Thailand. Seasonal flooding deposited nutrient-rich silt across the lower basin, supporting productive wet-rice agriculture capable of sustaining dense populations and, eventually, the labor and surplus required for monumental construction. The basin's relatively flat terrain and interconnected waterways also facilitated movement of people, goods, and ideas among settlements, making river and canal transport an efficient alternative to overland travel across much of the region.

Geographically, the Chao Phraya basin sits at an important crossroads. Routes leading southward reach the Gulf of Thailand and, beyond it, the Malay Peninsula and its own early historic polities, examined in Chapter 26. Routes leading westward cross toward the Tenasserim coast and, ultimately, the Andaman Sea and Bay of Bengal, connecting the basin to the same Indian Ocean networks that shaped the Pyu world examined in Chapter 24. Routes leading northward reach into the upper Chao Phraya tributaries and beyond toward Tai-speaking regions whose political significance would only develop centuries later, discussed in Chapters 54 through 57. Routes leading eastward cross toward the Pa Sak valley, the site of Si Thep, and onward to the Khorat Plateau and the Mekong world associated with early Khmer polities.

This position at the intersection of several regional corridors helps explain both the appearance of South Asian religious and artistic influence at Dvaravati sites and the region's capacity to serve as a node redistributing goods and ideas to neighboring areas. As with the mandala model introduced in Chapter 1 and the geographic patterns discussed for the Pyu world in Chapter 24, however, students should resist the temptation to treat this geography as determining a particular political outcome. Fertile floodplains and convenient waterways created opportunities for settlement, agriculture, and exchange; they did not, by themselves, guarantee that any particular pattern of political organization, whether centralized or fragmented, would result.

Section 25.4

From Proto-Dvaravati to the Dvaravati Horizon

Archaeological evidence suggests that the developments conventionally associated with Dvaravati did not appear suddenly as a complete and fully formed cultural package. Instead, many specialists now describe a gradual proto-Dvaravati phase, roughly datable to the fourth and fifth centuries CE, during which settlements in the Chao Phraya basin began to expand, moated enclosures became more common, and increasing quantities of material bearing South Asian religious and artistic influence appear in the archaeological record. This phase built directly on the Iron Age agricultural and social foundations discussed in Section 25.1, rather than representing a sharp break from them.

The mature Dvaravati horizon, generally dated from approximately the sixth through the ninth centuries CE, is marked by a considerably denser and more widespread body of evidence: substantial Buddhist monuments, characteristic coinage bearing Sanskrit inscriptions and religious symbols, a recognizable sculptural style applied to Buddha images and narrative reliefs, and continued expansion of moated urban settlement. It is from this mature horizon that most of the evidence examined later in this chapter derives.

Students should treat both phases of this chronology as approximate rather than precisely fixed. Few Dvaravati-associated sites have produced securely dated royal inscriptions comparable to those available for Angkor or Champa, and much of the chronological framework rests on stylistic comparison, radiocarbon dating of associated organic material, and comparison with better-documented neighboring societies. Ongoing archaeological research continues to refine, and occasionally revise, the dating of specific sites and developments, and students encountering more specific dates in other sources should recognize that such dates typically represent a current scholarly estimate rather than an unquestionable fact.

Section 25.5

Nakhon Pathom

Nakhon Pathom, in the lower Chao Phraya basin not far from the Gulf of Thailand, has long occupied a central place in Dvaravati studies. The site has produced an unusually large concentration of Buddhist monuments, including the substantial fired-brick Chedi Chula Prathon, decorated with stucco and terracotta reliefs, along with extensive quantities of Dvaravati-style coinage, sculpture, and votive material. Nakhon Pathom also provides the chapter's two strongest textual confirmations of the Dvaravati name itself: the inscribed silver medallions discussed in this chapter's Primary Source Window, recovered from ritual or foundation-deposit contexts, and the Sanskrit stone inscription from Wat Phra Ngam, which names the city of Dvaravati directly. This combination of monumental scale, abundant material culture, and direct textual naming led many earlier scholars to identify Nakhon Pathom as the leading candidate for a Dvaravati "capital," a political and religious center from which a broader kingdom might have been administered.

More recent archaeological assessment treats this identification with greater caution. The presence of extensive Buddhist monuments, abundant material culture, and even direct textual naming of "Dvaravati" demonstrates that Nakhon Pathom was an important religious, artistic, political, and probably commercial center, closely associated with the name Dvaravati itself, and capable of supporting substantial architectural investment and sustained craft production over an extended period. It does not, by itself, demonstrate that Nakhon Pathom governed a defined territory extending across the entire Chao Phraya basin, nor that other prominent centers, such as U Thong or Khu Bua, were subordinate to it in any formal political sense. Prominence in the archaeological and art-historical record, and even direct naming in local inscriptions, is not equivalent to documented evidence of a territorially extensive capital exercising authority over the whole horizon, a distinction this chapter applies consistently to every major Dvaravati site.

What can be said with more confidence is that Nakhon Pathom's monuments and material culture reflect sustained royal, elite, or communal patronage capable of mobilizing considerable labor and resources over a period of centuries, and that its religious architecture, particularly its large stupa-like structures, likely served as a focus of Buddhist pilgrimage and devotion for a wide surrounding population, whether or not that population fell under a single political authority centered there.

Section 25.6

U Thong and Khu Bua

U Thong, situated in the western Chao Phraya basin along an ancient course of the Tha Chin river system, provides important evidence for the breadth of Dvaravati-associated settlement beyond Nakhon Pathom. Excavations at U Thong have revealed a substantial moated and walled settlement with evidence of craft production, including bead-making, metalworking, and pottery, alongside Buddhist religious material and coinage broadly consistent with the wider Dvaravati horizon. The site's position along river and overland routes toward the Gulf of Thailand and toward routes leading westward suggests it functioned as a node in regional and possibly longer-distance exchange networks, discussed further in Section 25.12.

Khu Bua, located near the Mae Klong River in the western basin, is particularly notable for its stucco relief decoration, which survives in unusually good condition and provides valuable evidence for Dvaravati artistic conventions, including depictions of musicians, dancers, and figures in clothing and ornament that may reflect contemporary dress and social practice as much as purely religious iconography. Alongside its artistic significance, Khu Bua's remains include monastic structures and other evidence of established Buddhist institutional life.

Taken together, U Thong and Khu Bua reinforce the picture of a Dvaravati world composed of multiple substantial settlements, each capable of independent religious patronage, craft specialization, and participation in regional exchange, rather than a single dominant center surrounded by dependent satellites. The broad similarity of religious architecture, coinage, and artistic style across these sites reflects genuine cultural connection, most plausibly transmitted through shared religious institutions, itinerant craftspeople and religious specialists, and commercial contact, rather than necessarily through a shared political administration.

Section 25.7

Lopburi and Other Chao Phraya Centers

Lopburi, situated along a tributary system in the central Chao Phraya basin, presents an unusually long and complex historical trajectory. Its Dvaravati-period remains connect it firmly to the broader horizon examined in this chapter, including Buddhist monuments, sculpture, and material culture broadly consistent with sites such as Nakhon Pathom and U Thong. In later centuries, however, Lopburi came to be remembered and, in some traditions, referred to as Lavo, a center whose subsequent history involved substantial Khmer political and artistic influence and, still later, incorporation into the political worlds associated with early Tai-speaking states, developments examined in Chapters 53 through 57 of this textbook.

This chapter deliberately treats only Lopburi's Dvaravati-period evidence in detail, reserving its later Lavo-period and Khmer-influenced history for the chapters specifically assigned to those developments. Students should resist projecting Lopburi's later political importance, whatever form that importance took under later rulers, backward onto its Dvaravati-period status, since the available evidence does not establish that Lopburi held a privileged or dominant position among Dvaravati-associated centers during the period examined here.

Other Chao Phraya basin sites, including smaller moated settlements identified through archaeological survey across the region, further reinforce the impression of a multicentered landscape. Many of these smaller sites have produced Dvaravati-style coinage, votive tablets, and religious architecture on a more modest scale, suggesting that Dvaravati-associated culture was not confined to a handful of major centers but extended, in varying degrees of intensity, across a considerably broader area of settlement.

Section 25.8

Si Thep and the Limits of the Dvaravati Label

Si Thep, located in the upper Pa Sak valley to the east of the main Chao Phraya floodplain, occupies a particularly instructive position in this chapter's argument. The site's monuments, sculpture, and religious material share important features with the broader Dvaravati artistic and religious tradition, including Buddhist imagery, Brahmanical material, and architectural forms recognizable from other Dvaravati-associated centers. At the same time, specialists studying Si Thep's material culture have identified distinctive local characteristics, along with artistic and religious connections extending eastward toward the Khorat Plateau and the early Khmer-associated world, that distinguish its production from what might be called a standard or typical Dvaravati style.

This combination, genuine connection alongside genuine distinctiveness, makes Si Thep an important test case for the limits of the Dvaravati label itself. If Si Thep shared enough artistic and religious vocabulary with Nakhon Pathom, U Thong, and Khu Bua to be meaningfully grouped within the same broad horizon, yet developed its own regional variations and maintained its own distinct external connections, then the underlying reality being described is better understood as a set of related, interacting, but locally distinct communities than as branches of a single unified state radiating outward from one governing center.

Si Thep's position also illustrates a broader methodological lesson relevant well beyond this chapter: shared artistic style, shared religious practice, and even shared material culture do not, on their own, establish political unity. A community may adopt widely admired artistic conventions, participate in the same regional religious networks, and use similar coinage without belonging to the same government as its neighbors. Historians must therefore look for independent evidence, administrative inscriptions, references to rulers and territorial claims, or clear hierarchical relationships among sites, before concluding that shared culture reflects shared political structure.

Section 25.9

Was Dvaravati One Kingdom?

Having examined the major sites individually, this section addresses directly the central historical problem this chapter has been building toward: how should historians characterize the political organization of the Dvaravati world as a whole?

Three broad interpretive positions recur in modern scholarship. The first, associated chiefly with older twentieth-century research, treats Dvaravati as a centralized kingdom, most likely governed from Nakhon Pathom or another leading center, with subordinate territories or dependent settlements extending across the wider Chao Phraya basin. This interpretation drew heavily on the inscribed coinage and inscriptions bearing the Dvaravati name, on Nakhon Pathom's monumental scale, and on the conventional identification of Chinese T'o-lo-po-ti with Dvaravati, treating these threads together as sufficient evidence for a unified state comparable to better-documented mainland polities, even though the Chinese identification itself establishes only that a polity of that name existed, not how large or centralized it was.

The second position, increasingly favored by archaeologists working with expanded survey and excavation data, describes Dvaravati as a network of related but politically independent or loosely connected regional centers, each with its own local elite, religious patronage, and commercial relationships, bound together by shared religious institutions, artistic conventions, and overlapping trade networks rather than by centralized government. Under this model, Nakhon Pathom, U Thong, Khu Bua, Lopburi, and other centers may have interacted, competed, allied, and exchanged with one another in shifting patterns over several centuries without ever forming a single durable state.

The third position treats "Dvaravati" chiefly as a cultural and art-historical horizon, cautioning against political language altogether. Under this view, historians should describe the shared coinage, sculpture, and religious architecture across the Chao Phraya basin as evidence of a connected cultural and religious world, while remaining agnostic, given the current evidence, about whether any political entity beyond the local settlement or its immediate hinterland actually existed.

This textbook does not adopt a single position dogmatically, because the surviving evidence does not permit a confident resolution. The Wat Phra Ngam inscription and the inscribed medallions discussed in this chapter's Primary Source Window, taken together as independent pieces of evidence, make the existence of a real city or polity called Dvaravati considerably more secure than earlier generations of skeptical scholarship sometimes allowed, the medallions through their formula naming a "lord of Dvaravati" and Wat Phra Ngam through its separate explicit mention of the city in a royal religious endowment; this is not, at this point, chiefly a debate about whether the name corresponded to anything real. The unresolved question is narrower and more specific: whether that Dvaravati polity's authority extended, continuously and uniformly, across every site examined in this chapter, or whether it was centered more narrowly on Nakhon Pathom and its immediate region while other centers, sharing the same religious and artistic culture, retained their own local authority. It is entirely possible that political organization varied across the roughly five centuries of the Dvaravati horizon, with periods of greater local independence alternating with periods in which one or another center exercised wider influence, possibly including tributary or ritual relationships that fell short of full territorial administration. What the evidence does support, and what this chapter asks students to accept as the more defensible conclusion, is that no currently available evidence demonstrates that a single, continuously governed Dvaravati state administered the entire Chao Phraya basin for the full duration of the horizon. Historians should therefore describe Dvaravati as a real named polity of confirmed but uncertain extent, embedded within a wider cultural and religious horizon, while treating claims of basin-wide centralized political unity as a hypothesis requiring stronger evidence than currently exists.

Historical Debate

One Dvaravati Kingdom or Multiple Political Centers?

The question: Does the archaeological, epigraphic, numismatic, and textual evidence associated with "Dvaravati" support the existence of a single centralized kingdom governing the Chao Phraya basin, or does it more plausibly describe a network of related but politically distinct centers sharing a common religious and artistic culture?

Interpretation A — Dvaravati as a centralized kingdom: Proponents of this view point to the inscribed Dvaravati medallions and the Wat Phra Ngam inscription as evidence of a recognized state or city name, to Nakhon Pathom's exceptional monumental scale as evidence of a capital capable of mobilizing resources beyond a single locality, and to the conventional identification of Chinese T'o-lo-po-ti with Dvaravati as external corroboration that a tributary polity of this name was known to Chinese observers. They argue that a shared coinage system, in particular, is difficult to explain without some form of centralized or coordinated authority.
Interpretation B — Dvaravati as a multicentered cultural and religious horizon: Proponents of this view emphasize that multiple Dvaravati-associated sites, including U Thong, Khu Bua, Lopburi, and Si Thep, each show independent evidence of substantial religious patronage, craft production, and settlement investment, with no securely documented administrative or dynastic evidence linking them under a single ruler. They argue that shared coinage, artistic style, and religious practice can spread through trade, itinerant religious specialists, and prestige imitation without requiring centralized government, and that the absence of administrative inscriptions naming a continuous ruling dynasty across multiple sites weighs heavily against the kingdom model.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of archaeological, art-historical, numismatic, and textual evidence, but differ in how much political weight to assign to shared material culture versus the absence of direct administrative or dynastic documentation.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns primarily interpretation and evidentiary sufficiency rather than the underlying facts themselves; specialists on both sides generally agree on the archaeological record but differ on what political conclusions that record can support.

Why it matters: The choice between these interpretations affects how historians describe Dvaravati's place in the broader history of early Southeast Asian state formation, and it shapes comparisons with other early societies examined in this volume, including the Pyu city-states of Chapter 24 and the early Khmer polities of Chapters 20 and 21.

Student task: Using the evidence discussed in Sections 25.5 through 25.8, construct an argument for each interpretation, then explain what kind of additional evidence, an administrative inscription naming a ruler with authority over multiple named centers, for example, would most strongly support Interpretation A, and what kind of evidence would most strongly support Interpretation B.

Section 25.10

Mon Languages, Inscriptions, and Identity

The chapter title's reference to "Mon Worlds" reflects an important body of linguistic evidence bearing on the population, or populations, associated with the Dvaravati horizon. A number of inscriptions recovered from Chao Phraya basin sites are written in Old Mon, an early form of the Mon language belonging to the Austroasiatic language family discussed in Chapter 13. These inscriptions, typically religious dedications, donative records, and similar short texts, demonstrate that Mon-speaking communities maintained a literate presence in the region during the Dvaravati period and used their own language, alongside Sanskrit and Pali, for religious and commemorative purposes.

Old Mon inscriptions establish a significant Mon-speaking presence within the Dvaravati world, documenting Mon as a language used for religious dedication and official or commemorative purposes, but the surviving evidence cannot determine whether Mon speakers constituted a demographic majority throughout the archaeological horizon. This linguistic evidence does not, however, license several stronger claims that popular writing sometimes makes without adequate caution. First, the presence of Old Mon inscriptions at particular sites does not demonstrate that every Dvaravati-associated settlement was exclusively or even predominantly Mon-speaking; the Chao Phraya basin, like most regions examined in this textbook, likely supported linguistic diversity, including Austroasiatic-speaking communities beyond Mon specifically and populations whose linguistic affiliation remains undetermined from the available evidence. Second, "Mon" as a linguistic category should not be automatically equated with a single bounded ethnic or political identity in the modern sense; language, as Chapter 13 emphasized for Austroasiatic populations generally, does not correspond neatly to fixed biological or political categories, particularly in a period long before anything resembling a modern national or ethnic consciousness existed.

Third, and most directly relevant to this chapter's central argument, the presence of Mon-speaking communities across the Dvaravati horizon does not by itself establish that those communities formed a single unified "Mon kingdom." Mon-speaking populations, as later chapters of this textbook make clear, continued to exist across mainland Southeast Asia for many centuries after the Dvaravati horizon's transformation, contributing to Buddhist and political developments in Lower Burma, examined in Chapter 60, and elsewhere. Their long historical presence and cultural contributions should not be collapsed into a single political entity simply because they shared a language and, for a time, a broadly common religious and artistic culture in the Chao Phraya basin.

Section 25.11

Cities, Moats, Waterways, and Agricultural Hinterlands

A recurring feature of Dvaravati-associated settlements is the construction of moats and earthen ramparts, often in oval, circular, or irregular configurations adapted to local topography and watercourses, enclosing central areas containing religious monuments, elite residences, and craft production zones. These moated enclosures likely served multiple purposes simultaneously: defense against raiding or warfare, water management for both flood control and dry-season storage, and symbolic demarcation of a sacred or politically significant central space distinct from the surrounding agricultural countryside.

Beyond the moated core, Dvaravati settlements depended on productive agricultural hinterlands, principally wet-rice cultivation in the surrounding floodplain, to sustain urban populations, craft specialists, and religious institutions that did not themselves produce food. The relationship between city and countryside in this period likely resembled patterns documented in comparable early Southeast Asian urban societies, including the Pyu cities examined in Chapter 24: a dense, monument-rich center supported by a dispersed rural population engaged in agriculture, whose labor and surplus, whether extracted through taxation, tribute, religious donation, or some combination of these, financed monumental construction and elite consumption.

Waterways played a double role in this settlement pattern, serving simultaneously as sources of water for agriculture and daily life and as transportation corridors linking individual settlements to one another and to more distant trading partners. This dual function reinforces the broader point made in Section 25.3: the Chao Phraya basin's geography created conditions favorable to the growth of multiple substantial, economically self-sufficient settlement centers rather than favoring the emergence of a single dominant capital controlling all others.

Section 25.12

Trade, Craft Production, and Coinage

Dvaravati-associated sites have produced substantial evidence of craft specialization, including bead-making using glass, carnelian, and other stones, metalworking in bronze, iron, and precious metals, ceramic production, and the manufacture of the region's distinctive silver coinage. This craft production served both local consumption and broader exchange networks, connecting Chao Phraya basin communities to overland and maritime trade extending toward the Bay of Bengal, the Gulf of Thailand, the Malay Peninsula, and other mainland regions.

The coinage itself deserves particular attention as a distinct category of evidence, and students should distinguish two related but different bodies of material. Ordinary Dvaravati-style silver coinage, bearing symbols such as the śrīvatsa auspicious mark, representations of the sun, conch shells, and other motifs drawn from South Asian religious and royal iconography, circulated broadly across Chao Phraya basin sites and most plausibly functioned as currency supporting everyday exchange. A smaller, separate group of inscribed silver medallions, bearing the Sanskrit formula naming "Dvaravati" discussed in this chapter's Primary Source Window, were recovered from what excavators interpret as ritual or foundation-deposit contexts beneath or within religious structures at Nakhon Pathom and U Thong, and are better understood as dedicatory or commemorative objects than as money placed into ordinary circulation. The wide distribution of broadly similar ordinary coin types across multiple Chao Phraya basin sites, and occasionally beyond, has been cited by proponents of the centralized-kingdom interpretation as evidence of a shared monetary and political system. Numismatic specialists studying these coins in relation to comparable issues from the Pyu world and elsewhere have noted that similar coin types and symbols circulated across a wide area of mainland Southeast Asia during this period, suggesting that shared coinage conventions may reflect broad regional commercial and cultural connections extending well beyond any single Dvaravati polity, rather than necessarily indicating a single unified minting authority.

Trade connections extended in multiple directions. Evidence of imported beads, glass, and other materials points toward exchange with South Asia and, potentially, more distant Indian Ocean and even Mediterranean-connected networks discussed in Chapter 18. Overland and riverine connections linked the Chao Phraya basin to the Pyu world to the west, the Khorat Plateau and early Khmer-associated societies to the east, and the Malay Peninsula to the south. This position within multiple overlapping exchange networks helps explain both the wealth visible in Dvaravati religious architecture and sculpture and the region's capacity to absorb, adapt, and retransmit South Asian religious and artistic ideas to neighboring societies.

Section 25.13

Buddhism and the Sacred Landscape

Buddhism occupies a central place in the material culture associated with Dvaravati, evident in the scale and number of stupas, monastic structures, votive tablets, and devotional sculpture recovered from sites across the Chao Phraya basin. Monuments such as Nakhon Pathom's Chedi Chula Prathon and the monastic remains at Khu Bua indicate sustained institutional investment in Buddhist religious life, likely supported by a combination of royal or elite patronage, communal donation, and the economic activity of resident and itinerant monastic communities.

Votive tablets, small clay or terracotta objects impressed with Buddha images and sometimes inscribed with short Buddhist formulas, appear in considerable quantity at Dvaravati sites and provide evidence for popular devotional practice extending beyond elite monumental patronage. Sema stones, boundary markers used to demarcate consecrated monastic ground, likewise appear at several sites and illustrate the institutional organization of Buddhist monastic communities during this period.

The specific Buddhist tradition or traditions practiced within the Dvaravati world remain a subject of ongoing scholarly discussion. Much of the surviving material culture is consistent with what later became known as Theravada Buddhism, though some scholars caution against assuming a fully developed Theravada institutional and doctrinal system identical to later periods, given that Chapter 53 identifies the broader Theravada transformation across the mainland as a longer and more gradual process extending well beyond the Dvaravati period itself. Some evidence, including inscriptions and iconographic elements, may also reflect Mahayana Buddhist influence current in South Asian and Southeast Asian networks during parts of this period. Rather than assigning the Dvaravati world a single fixed sectarian identity, this chapter treats its Buddhist religious life as an evolving and locally adapted set of institutions and practices, connected to but not simply identical with later, better-documented Theravada traditions.

Section 25.14

Wheels of the Dharma and Dvaravati Art

Among the most distinctive artistic productions associated with the Dvaravati horizon are stone dharmacakra, or "wheels of the dharma," often mounted on pillars and sometimes accompanied by sculpted deer, an iconographic reference to the Buddha's first sermon delivered in a deer park. These monuments, found at multiple Dvaravati-associated sites, represent a genuinely distinctive regional artistic and devotional tradition, one without a precise equivalent at this scale and frequency in contemporary South Asian Buddhist art, illustrating the kind of local artistic innovation this textbook emphasizes throughout its treatment of religious and cultural exchange.

Dvaravati sculpture also developed a recognizable regional style for depicting the Buddha, characterized by particular facial features, hairstyles, and, notably, a distinctive double-handed teaching gesture in which both hands are raised in the gesture of turning the wheel of the dharma, a variation on gestures more commonly depicted with a single hand in South Asian prototypes. Stucco relief work, particularly well preserved at Khu Bua, depicts a wide range of figures, including musicians, attendants, and decorative ornament, providing valuable evidence for artistic technique, narrative religious imagery, and possibly contemporary dress and adornment.

Section 25.15

Religious Plurality

Although Buddhism dominates the surviving religious material culture of the Dvaravati horizon, the evidence does not support characterizing the region as exclusively or uniformly Buddhist. Brahmanical religious material, including imagery associated with Vishnu, Surya, and liṅga representations connected to Shaivite worship, appears at several Dvaravati-associated sites, indicating that Hindu religious traditions coexisted alongside, and possibly interacted with, Buddhist institutions during at least part of the period under discussion. The Wat Phra Ngam inscription provides especially important evidence for this religious plurality: although recovered from a site now dominated by Buddhist remains, it records a royal endowment, including gold, land, cattle, and bonded laborers, to Paśupati, a form of Śiva, while also praising the king through imagery comparing him to an incarnation of Vishnu. This evidence demonstrates substantial Brahmanical royal culture, including explicit Śaiva patronage and Vaishnava royal imagery, within the wider Dvaravati world, rather than an exclusively Buddhist religious landscape, and should not be mistaken for a Buddhist dedication merely because it was found near Buddhist monuments.

This coexistence should not be interpreted as evidence of sharp sectarian division or conflict. As Chapter 19 explained regarding the broader process of selective localization across Southeast Asia, elite and popular religious practice in this period frequently combined elements drawn from multiple traditions, and patrons capable of funding a Buddhist stupa might, in the same community or even the same family, also support Brahmanical ritual specialists or temple imagery associated with kingship and cosmic order. The presence of both Buddhist and Brahmanical material at Dvaravati sites likely reflects this kind of religious plurality and complementarity rather than competing, mutually exclusive religious communities.

Students should also bear in mind that indigenous religious practices, ancestor veneration, spirit cults, and other beliefs not directly derived from South Asian traditions almost certainly persisted throughout the Dvaravati period, even though such practices are considerably more difficult to document archaeologically than monumental Buddhist or Brahmanical architecture and sculpture. The religious life of ordinary Dvaravati communities was very likely more religiously diverse and more locally variable than the surviving monumental evidence, weighted heavily toward elite and institutional patronage, can fully reveal.

Section 25.16

South Asian Connections and Localization

The Dvaravati world's religious architecture, sculpture, coinage, and epigraphy all display substantial South Asian influence: Buddhist doctrine and institutional forms, Sanskrit as a language of religious and commemorative inscription, and artistic conventions recognizably related to South Asian sculptural traditions. Applying the framework established in Chapter 19, this chapter treats these connections not as evidence of passive reception or colonization by an external Indian civilization, but as evidence of active selection, adaptation, and transformation by Chao Phraya basin communities themselves.

Several pieces of evidence discussed earlier in this chapter illustrate this process concretely. The distinctive double-handed teaching gesture developed for Dvaravati Buddha images, discussed in Section 25.14, represents a genuine local artistic innovation rather than simple copying of South Asian prototypes. The prominence of dharmacakra-and-deer monuments, likewise, reflects a specific devotional emphasis that, while drawing on South Asian Buddhist narrative tradition, developed into a particularly elaborate and widespread local monumental form not paralleled at comparable scale elsewhere. The use of both Sanskrit and Old Mon in inscriptions, rather than Sanskrit alone, demonstrates that local language and script traditions continued to operate alongside, rather than being replaced by, the prestige language associated with South Asian religious and political models.

This chapter therefore avoids framing Dvaravati's relationship with South Asia in terms of "Indianization" understood as one-directional cultural transfer. Chao Phraya basin communities participated actively in a transregional Buddhist and commercial world, drawing selectively on available religious, artistic, and political vocabulary, including scripts, iconography, and coinage conventions, and reshaping these elements to serve local religious, social, and possibly political purposes that are not always fully recoverable from the surviving evidence.

Section 25.17

Dvaravati in Regional Networks

The Dvaravati world did not develop in isolation from its neighbors. To the west, connections with the Pyu city-states of the Irrawaddy basin, examined in Chapter 24, are suggested by broadly comparable coinage traditions, shared Buddhist institutional patterns, and plausible overland and coastal routes linking the two regions. To the east, growing connections with the Khorat Plateau and the early Khmer-associated societies discussed in Chapters 20 and 21 are visible in the artistic and religious material at Si Thep, discussed in Section 25.8, and in the increasing Khmer influence that becomes more pronounced in the Chao Phraya basin during the later centuries of the horizon, discussed further in Section 25.18. To the south, connections with the early states of the Malay Peninsula, examined in Chapter 26, likely operated through maritime and overland routes reaching toward the Gulf of Thailand and beyond.

These connections reinforce a broader argument developed across this Part of the textbook: the earliest documented states and cultural horizons of mainland and maritime Southeast Asia, whether Funan, the Pyu cities, or Dvaravati, developed through sustained interaction with one another and with wider Indian Ocean and East Asian networks, rather than through isolated, independent invention. Dvaravati's own religious and artistic innovations, in turn, likely influenced neighboring societies, illustrating that cultural transmission across the region flowed in multiple directions rather than moving in a single line from South Asia outward to passive Southeast Asian recipients.

Section 25.18

Transformation rather than Disappearance

The Dvaravati horizon did not end abruptly at a single identifiable moment, and this chapter deliberately avoids describing a sudden "fall" of Dvaravati comparable to older, oversimplified narratives once applied to other early Southeast Asian societies. Instead, archaeological and art-historical evidence points toward a gradual transformation beginning around the ninth century CE and continuing over the following centuries, during which growing Khmer political and artistic influence reached into parts of the Chao Phraya basin and the Khorat Plateau, existing centers such as Lopburi experienced significant cultural and possibly political reorientation, and new patterns of settlement and patronage gradually altered the religious and artistic landscape described earlier in this chapter.

Mon-speaking populations and Buddhist institutions associated with the Dvaravati tradition did not vanish during this transformation. They persisted, in modified form, within the changing political configurations that followed, and Dvaravati artistic and religious conventions continued to influence subsequent developments in the region. The broader Theravada Buddhist transformation examined in Chapter 53, the gradual Tai-speaking migrations and political formation examined in Chapters 54 through 57, and the eventual emergence of states such as Sukhothai and Ayutthaya, examined in Chapters 55 and 57, all occurred within a landscape whose religious, artistic, and demographic foundations had been shaped in important ways by the preceding centuries of Dvaravati-associated development.

This chapter deliberately stops short of narrating those later developments in detail, since they belong to the chapters specifically assigned to them within this textbook's continuous chronological sequence. What this chapter establishes, and what students should carry forward into those later chapters, is that the Chao Phraya basin's later Theravada Buddhist and Tai-associated political history did not begin from an empty or culturally undeveloped landscape. It began from a region already shaped by centuries of Mon-speaking settlement, Buddhist institutional life, distinctive artistic tradition, and sustained participation in regional and Indian Ocean exchange networks, the very developments this chapter has examined under the name Dvaravati.

Historical Vocabulary
Mon

Mon refers both to a language belonging to the Austroasiatic family, discussed in Chapter 13, and, by extension, to communities historically associated with speaking that language across mainland Southeast Asia. Old Mon inscriptions from the Dvaravati period demonstrate a documented Mon-speaking presence in the Chao Phraya basin during the first millennium CE, but Mon-speaking populations were never confined to this period or region alone. Mon-speaking communities continued to play significant historical roles for many centuries afterward, notably in Lower Burma, where the Mon-centered kingdom of Hanthawaddy, examined in Chapter 60, developed a distinct and later political trajectory.

Students should therefore treat "Mon" as a linguistic and cultural category with a long, geographically extensive history of its own, only one part of which intersects with the specific Dvaravati archaeological horizon examined in this chapter. Equating "Mon history" with "Dvaravati history" would understate the much longer and broader historical presence of Mon-speaking communities across the mainland.

Context: Students should first distinguish two categories of evidence often blurred in popular accounts. Ordinary Dvaravati-period silver coinage, bearing symbols such as the śrīvatsa mark, a rising sun, a conch shell, or a stylized vase, circulated for economic purposes, broadly comparable to monetary coinage in neighboring early Southeast Asian societies. Separately, a small number of inscribed silver medallions bearing a specific Sanskrit formula were recovered from what excavators interpret as ritual or foundation-deposit contexts at Nakhon Pathom and U Thong, placed within or beneath religious structures rather than necessarily circulated as currency. These medallions are therefore better understood as dedicatory or ritual objects than as ordinary money. Independently of either kind of coinage, a Sanskrit stone inscription from Wat Phra Ngam at Nakhon Pathom explicitly names the city of Dvaravati, providing confirmation of the name outside any numismatic context at all.

Description of the medallion formula (a scholarly reading, not an unmediated translation, since interpretive nuances remain):

The published medallions carry a Sanskrit formula generally read as śrī dvāravatī īśvara puṇya, broadly rendered as something like "the meritorious act [or religious foundation] of the lord of glorious Dvaravati." The presence of the Dvaravati name in this formula is considered secure by specialists; what remains open to interpretation is the precise grammatical relationship among its terms and, more importantly, exactly what kind of authority "the lord of Dvaravati" denotes, a king ruling a defined territory, a more locally bounded ruler or official, or a title whose political reach cannot be determined from the inscription alone.

Analysis: The two sources should be read as independent lines of evidence rather than a single combined text. The medallions attest a figure styled the "lord of Dvaravati." The Wat Phra Ngam inscription separately names Dvaravati within a damaged text that praises an unnamed king and records his Śaiva religious endowment; its fragmentary condition does not allow historians to confirm that this king is the same figure named "lord of Dvaravati" on the medallions. Together, however, the two sources demonstrate that a place or polity called Dvaravati was known and named by people living in the Chao Phraya basin itself, not only reconstructed by outside observers or modern scholars working from Chinese sources, which considerably strengthens the case that a real entity bearing this name existed, even without establishing the exact relationship between the ruler, the city, and the wider archaeological horizon.

Limitations: Confirming that a "lord of Dvaravati" was named on the medallions, and that a city of that name was separately invoked in a royal endowment at Wat Phra Ngam, does not by itself establish the territorial extent of any ruler's authority, whether it extended uniformly across every site examined in this chapter, or whether "Dvaravati" named a single enduring kingdom rather than a city or a more local polity whose name later came to label a wider archaeological horizon. The ritual deposit context of the medallions also means they cannot be assumed to illustrate how, or whether, similarly inscribed currency circulated in ordinary economic exchange.

Perspective: Both sources reflect the perspective of religious and political patrons wealthy and literate enough to commission inscribed dedications; they do not directly record the perspective of ordinary farmers, laborers, or craftspeople living under whatever authority "the lord of Dvaravati" represented.

Multiple Perspectives

Living in a Dvaravati Center: Reconstructed Perspectives

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives drawn from archaeological and comparative evidence. They are not quotations from any named historical individual, since no personal testimony from Dvaravati-period Chao Phraya basin residents survives.

From the perspective of a Buddhist monastic patron or religious donor: Funding the construction of a stupa or the carving of a dharmacakra monument brought religious merit and public recognition, reinforcing a donor's social standing within a community organized substantially around Buddhist institutional life and periodic religious festival and pilgrimage.
From the perspective of a craftsperson or artisan working in bead-making, metalworking, or sculpture: Skilled production of coinage, religious imagery, or ornament connected an artisan's livelihood to both local religious patronage and wider regional exchange networks, and artistic conventions likely spread as much through the movement of skilled workers and portable models as through any centralized political direction.
From the perspective of a wet-rice farming household in the agricultural hinterland: Daily and seasonal life centered on rice cultivation, water management, and participation in local religious observance, with the great monuments of nearby moated centers representing a visible but not necessarily politically controlling presence in a farming family's economic and social world.
From the perspective of a merchant engaged in regional or long-distance trade: Moving goods along the Chao Phraya's waterways and overland routes toward the Gulf of Thailand, the Bay of Bengal, or the Khorat Plateau required navigating relationships with multiple local centers, each with its own market, patronage networks, and possibly its own coinage practices, rather than dealing with a single unified administration.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Dvaravati to Later Chao Phraya Basin Worlds

What Changed

  • Growing Khmer political and artistic influence reshaped material culture at several Chao Phraya basin centers, particularly Lopburi, from roughly the ninth century onward
  • Distinctive Dvaravati coinage, sculptural conventions, and moated settlement patterns gradually gave way to new artistic, architectural, and political forms over subsequent centuries
  • Tai-speaking populations and, eventually, new Tai-associated political centers such as Sukhothai and Ayutthaya became increasingly significant in the region from the thirteenth century onward, examined in Chapters 54, 55, and 57

What Stayed the Same

  • Buddhist institutional life, monastic patronage, and devotional practice continued in the Chao Phraya basin, even as specific artistic and architectural conventions changed
  • Mon-speaking communities persisted in the region and beyond it, continuing to contribute to religious and cultural life for centuries afterward
  • The basin's fundamental geography, its fertile floodplain, interconnected waterways, and position among multiple regional trade corridors, continued to shape settlement and commerce across every subsequent political configuration

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity allows historians to explain how later Theravada Buddhist and Tai-associated political developments built upon, rather than replaced from nothing, the religious, artistic, and demographic foundations established during the Dvaravati period.

Cause and Effect

Possible Conditions Favoring Multiple Political Centers

Possible long-term factors

The Chao Phraya basin's extensive fertile floodplain and dense network of interconnected waterways may have allowed multiple settlements to develop independent agricultural surpluses and commercial access simultaneously, potentially reducing any single site's structural advantage over its neighbors.

Possible reinforcing factors

Expanding Indian Ocean and regional trade during the mature Dvaravati horizon may have provided multiple centers, not merely one, with the wealth and religious prestige needed to sponsor substantial Buddhist monuments and craft production largely independently of one another.

Observed archaeological pattern

Archaeological evidence shows several major and numerous minor Dvaravati-associated centers sharing broadly similar coinage, religious architecture, and artistic style without securely documented evidence of centralized administrative subordination among them, a pattern consistent with, though not conclusively proven by, the factors above.

This textbook's response

Consistent use of "Dvaravati" primarily as a cultural and archaeological horizon rather than an assumed centralized kingdom, while remaining open to the possibility that political relationships among centers varied over time in ways not yet fully documented by surviving evidence.

Historical complexity: The absence of clear evidence for centralized government is not itself evidence of political chaos or weakness; multicentered regional systems, bound by shared religion, culture, and commerce rather than unified administration, represent a coherent and historically significant form of political organization in their own right, one this textbook takes seriously rather than treating as a lesser or incomplete version of a "proper" centralized state.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary Life within and around Dvaravati Centers

Most surviving evidence for the Dvaravati world derives from religious monuments, elite artistic production, and coinage, all of which reflect the activity and priorities of patrons wealthy enough to commission lasting material culture. Reconstructing the lives of farmers, laborers, artisans, and other non-elite residents of the Chao Phraya basin requires reading this elite-weighted evidence carefully for indirect clues, supplemented by archaeological evidence of household remains, agricultural tools, and settlement patterns beyond the monumental core of major sites.

Households in the agricultural hinterland surrounding moated centers likely organized daily and seasonal life around wet-rice cultivation, supplemented by fishing, gathering, and small-scale craft production. Religious practice for most residents probably combined elements of institutional Buddhist devotion, evident in the mass production and wide distribution of small votive tablets, which suggest Buddhist devotional participation extending beyond the patrons of major monuments even though the social identities of individual tablet users cannot usually be recovered, with continuing local and indigenous religious practices less visible in the monumental archaeological record. Craft specialists, including bead-makers, metalworkers, and sculptors, occupied an important intermediate position in Dvaravati society, connecting agricultural surplus and elite religious patronage to broader networks of regional and long-distance exchange discussed in Section 25.12.

Women's roles in Dvaravati society remain difficult to reconstruct in detail from the available evidence, though stucco relief imagery from sites such as Khu Bua, depicting female musicians and attendants, along with the general patterns documented for comparable early Southeast Asian societies, suggest that women participated in religious, artistic, and economic life in ways that surviving elite-focused inscriptions and monuments do not fully capture.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the archaeological, artistic, epigraphic, and numismatic evidence associated with "Dvaravati," a name confirmed locally by inscribed silver medallions recovered from ritual deposits at Nakhon Pathom and U Thong and by a separate Sanskrit stone inscription from Wat Phra Ngam, and subsequently extended by modern scholarship to describe a broader art-historical style and archaeological horizon spanning roughly the fourth through the eleventh centuries CE. The chapter's central historical problem, following directly from the Authoritative Chapter Outline's instruction to address uncertainty concerning political unity and territorial extent, has been whether "Dvaravati" designates a single centralized Mon kingdom, a network of related but politically independent centers, or chiefly a shared cultural and religious world that historians have sometimes translated too readily into the language of statehood.

The chapter examined the Chao Phraya basin's physical geography, emphasizing how its fertile floodplain and interconnected waterway system created conditions favorable to the growth of multiple substantial settlement centers rather than a single dominant capital. It traced a gradual chronological development from a proto-Dvaravati phase in the fourth and fifth centuries through the mature horizon of the sixth through ninth centuries, cautioning throughout against treating any specific date as more securely established than the underlying archaeological and stylistic evidence actually supports.

Individual site studies of Nakhon Pathom, U Thong, Khu Bua, Lopburi, and Si Thep demonstrated that substantial Buddhist monuments, coinage, and craft production appeared at multiple centers across the basin, each capable of independent religious patronage and commercial activity. Si Thep, in particular, illustrated the limits of the Dvaravati label itself, sharing significant artistic and religious features with the broader horizon while also displaying distinctive local characteristics and eastward connections toward the Khorat Plateau and the early Khmer-associated world.

The chapter's Historical Debate feature presented directly the competing interpretations of Dvaravati's political organization, concluding that the current evidence is compatible with a multicentered political landscape and does not demonstrate continuous, basin-wide centralization, while acknowledging that a named Dvaravati polity, centered most plausibly on Nakhon Pathom, clearly existed, and that political relationships among centers may have varied across the horizon's roughly five-century span in ways not yet fully understood.

The chapter also examined Old Mon inscriptions as evidence for a literate Mon-speaking presence within the Dvaravati horizon, while cautioning against three related errors: equating "Dvaravati" with "every Mon-speaking community," equating Mon-speaking populations with a single unified political kingdom, and equating the Dvaravati horizon with "early Thailand," since the region's later Theravada Buddhist and Tai-associated political history belongs to chapters specifically assigned to those developments.

Sections addressing settlement patterns, trade and coinage, Buddhism, distinctive artistic forms such as the dharmacakra and characteristic Buddha imagery, and continuing Brahmanical worship together demonstrated that Dvaravati communities actively selected and transformed South Asian religious and artistic models into genuinely local forms, consistent with the localization framework established in Chapter 19, rather than passively adopting an imported civilization unchanged.

Finally, the chapter examined Dvaravati's connections to neighboring societies, including the Pyu city-states of Chapter 24, the early Khmer-associated polities of Chapters 20 and 21, and the early states of the Malay Peninsula treated in Chapter 26, and traced the horizon's gradual transformation from the ninth century onward into the political and cultural worlds that later chapters of this textbook examine directly.

Understanding Dvaravati as a genuinely important but only partially centralized early Southeast Asian world, comparable in this respect to the Pyu cities examined immediately before it, equips students to approach the remaining early historic states covered in Part V, and the classical kingdoms covered in Volume III, with appropriate historical caution about the relationship between shared culture and centralized political power.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Dvaravati
A Sanskrit name attested on inscribed coinage from the Chao Phraya basin, extended by modern scholarship to describe an art-historical style and archaeological horizon of roughly the fourth through eleventh centuries CE; its use as the name of a single centralized kingdom remains debated.
Mon
An Austroasiatic language and, by extension, communities historically associated with speaking it, documented in the Chao Phraya basin through Old Mon inscriptions and present across mainland Southeast Asia for many centuries beyond the Dvaravati period.
Chao Phraya basin
The fertile floodplain drained by the Chao Phraya River and its distributary the Tha Chin, together with the neighboring Mae Klong basin to the west, at the heart of present-day central Thailand.
Cultural horizon
A widespread, broadly contemporaneous pattern of shared material culture across a geographic area that does not by itself indicate political, linguistic, or ethnic unity among the communities that produced it.
Proto-Dvaravati
A scholarly designation for the fourth- and fifth-century phase of gradual settlement expansion and South Asian contact preceding the mature Dvaravati horizon.
Moated settlement
A settlement enclosed by an excavated moat and often an earthen rampart, a common form across Dvaravati-associated sites, likely serving defensive, water-management, and symbolic functions.
Dharmacakra
A "wheel of the dharma," a Buddhist symbol representing the Buddha's teaching; Dvaravati communities produced large stone dharmacakra monuments, often accompanied by deer imagery, as a distinctive regional devotional form.
Votive tablet
A small clay or terracotta object impressed with a Buddha image, produced in quantity for popular Buddhist devotional use.
Stucco relief
Decorative sculptural work in plaster applied to brick or masonry structures, well preserved at sites such as Khu Bua and providing evidence of Dvaravati artistic and narrative conventions.
Sema stone
A boundary marker used to demarcate consecrated Buddhist monastic ground, attested at several Dvaravati-associated sites.
Localization
The process by which Southeast Asian societies selected and transformed outside religious, artistic, and political elements to fit local needs, as introduced in Chapter 19 and applied throughout this chapter to Dvaravati's South Asian connections.
Brahmanical
Relating to Hindu religious traditions and ritual specialists, evidenced at several Dvaravati sites alongside the more prominent Buddhist material.
Theravada Buddhism
A major Buddhist tradition that became dominant across much of mainland Southeast Asia over a long and gradual process examined in Chapter 53; Dvaravati material culture shows features consistent with, though not necessarily identical to, later Theravada practice.
Old Mon inscriptions
Inscriptions in an early stage of the Mon language, attested at Dvaravati-associated sites and used for religious dedication and commemorative purposes; Old Mon designates a language stage, not a single distinct script.
Śrīvatsa
An auspicious symbol appearing on Dvaravati coinage and medallions, also found in South Asian and other Southeast Asian iconography.
Entrepôt
A commercial hub whose importance derives chiefly from its role in exchange; several Dvaravati centers likely functioned in this capacity within regional trade networks.
Epigraphy
The study of inscriptions, a central source of evidence for Dvaravati religious dedication, donation records, and the Old Mon and Sanskrit textual material examined in this chapter.
Numismatics
The study of coins and related objects; central to interpreting both ordinary Dvaravati-style silver coinage and the inscribed silver medallions discussed in this chapter's Primary Source Window.
Art-historical style
A recognizable and describable pattern of artistic conventions, such as the distinctive Dvaravati Buddha image, that can be identified and compared across multiple sites and periods.
Anachronism
The error of applying a later concept, identity, or political boundary to an earlier period in which it did not exist or apply, particularly relevant to avoiding the description of Dvaravati as "early Thailand."
Political fragmentation
A condition in which political authority is distributed among multiple independent or loosely connected centers rather than concentrated in a single government; the evidence examined in this chapter is compatible with this model for much of the Dvaravati world and does not demonstrate continuous basin-wide centralization, though it does not rule out variation over time.
Regional exchange network
The interconnected system of trade routes, commodities, and commercial relationships linking Dvaravati-associated centers to neighboring regions and to wider Indian Ocean and mainland Southeast Asian commerce.
Chedi Chula Prathon
A substantial Buddhist monument at Nakhon Pathom, among the most significant surviving architectural remains associated with the Dvaravati horizon.
T'o-lo-po-ti
A name appearing in Chinese textual and geographical sources, especially the seventh-century pilgrim Xuanzang's account, for a southern polity conventionally and longstandingly identified by modern scholars with Dvaravati; the identification of the name is widely accepted, while the polity's territorial extent and political structure remain uncertain.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it applies, in a new geographic and cultural setting, one of this textbook's most important recurring lessons: shared material culture, however impressive or extensive, does not automatically demonstrate centralized political organization. Just as Chapter 24 asked students to distinguish the Pyu archaeological-cultural sphere from an assumed unified Pyu kingdom, this chapter has asked students to distinguish the Dvaravati archaeological and art-historical horizon from an assumed unified Dvaravati kingdom, while still taking seriously the genuine religious, artistic, and commercial coherence that the name "Dvaravati" usefully describes.

The chapter also reveals a larger process at work across early Southeast Asian history: the selective adoption and transformation of South Asian Buddhist, Brahmanical, and artistic traditions into genuinely local forms, visible here in the distinctive Dvaravati Buddha image, the dharmacakra monuments, and the continued use of Old Mon alongside Sanskrit in inscriptions. This localization process, examined in general terms in Chapter 19, receives one of its clearest concrete illustrations in the material examined throughout this chapter.

Later developments in the Chao Phraya basin, the Theravada Buddhist transformation of Chapter 53, the Tai migrations and new political worlds of Chapter 54, and the rise of Sukhothai and Ayutthaya in Chapters 55 and 57, did not begin from an undeveloped landscape. They built upon centuries of Mon-speaking settlement, Buddhist institutional development, and artistic innovation that this chapter has examined under the name Dvaravati, even though the political shape of that earlier world remains, and may always remain, only partially recoverable from the surviving evidence.

Finally, this chapter's insistence on separating "Mon," "Dvaravati," and "central Thailand" as related but distinct categories reinforces one of the textbook's foundational commitments, introduced in Chapter 1: modern national and ethnic identities should not be projected backward onto periods and peoples who would not have recognized them. Understanding Dvaravati honestly, as a genuinely significant but incompletely centralized early Southeast Asian world, prepares students to approach the remaining chapters of Part V, and the classical kingdoms of Volume III, with the same careful attention to evidence and the same resistance to premature political certainty.
