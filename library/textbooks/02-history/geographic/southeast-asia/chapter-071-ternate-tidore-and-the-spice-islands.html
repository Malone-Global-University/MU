Chapter Opening

Two small volcanic islands, separated by a narrow strait off the west coast of Halmahera, sat at the center of the sixteenth-century clove trade. Their rulers were not waiting to be discovered. They commanded fleets, collected tribute, arranged marriages, adopted Islamic titles, and bargained with Javanese, Malay, Chinese, Portuguese, and Spanish visitors from positions of established authority.

A traveler approaching northern Maluku in the early sixteenth century would have seen a chain of steep, forested volcanic cones rising directly from the sea. Ternate and Tidore, the two best known, lie so close together that people on one island can see the shoreline of the other. Neither is large, and neither could have supported a dense population on its own agricultural land. Yet these islands, together with their neighbors Moti, Makian, and Bacan and the great mountainous island of Halmahera behind them, were the home of the clove tree, and the dried flower buds of that tree were sold across much of Asia and, by way of Asian intermediaries, as far as Europe. This chapter asks how small island polities with limited farmland became centers of regional power, and what happened when Portuguese and Spanish ships entered their world.

The standard older story is told from the deck of a European ship: Portuguese captains search for the source of the spices, reach the "Spice Islands" in 1512, build a fortress in 1522, and compete with Spain until the Dutch take over. That story is not false in its dates, but it misplaces the historical center of gravity. Cloves were already moving by Asian ships through Javanese and Malay ports before any European saw a clove tree. The rulers of Ternate and Tidore already had courts, officials, military followers, and long-standing rivalries. When Portuguese and Spanish newcomers arrived, they did not enter an empty space waiting to be organized; they entered an existing political field, and they were quickly absorbed into its quarrels.

The chapter follows a sequence of connected transformations. Maluku before 1512 was commercially integrated into wider Asian trade but politically divided among four principal royal centers: Ternate, Tidore, Jailolo, and Bacan. Between 1512 and 1550 Portuguese and Spanish arrivals offered rival Malukan courts new military allies, new sources of imported goods, and new risks. The Portuguese fortress on Ternate, begun in 1522, turned a local alliance into a permanent and increasingly resented foreign presence. The murder of Sultan Khairun of Ternate by Portuguese officers in 1570 provoked a protracted resistance under his son Babullah that ended in December 1575 with the Portuguese surrender of the fortress. Ternatan power then expanded across eastern Indonesia, while Spanish and Portuguese interests regrouped around Tidore. In 1606 a Spanish expedition from Manila seized Ternate and carried its sultan into captivity, opening a phase in which the Dutch became decisive.

This chapter treats Malukan society and politics as its subject, and Portuguese and Spanish expansion as one force within that subject. That means attention to institutions that students may not have met before, such as the offices of jogugu, hukum, sangaji, and kapita laut; to the Malukan conception of a quadripartite order of four kingdoms; and to the ways in which kinship, marriage, ritual, and religious affiliation shaped alliance. It also means attention to the people whose labor made cloves available: village households that tended and harvested trees, boatbuilders and rowers, fishers, porters, and the women and men whose marriages tied courts to producing districts and sometimes to foreign newcomers.

The evidence demands careful handling. The most detailed early descriptions of Maluku come from Portuguese and Spanish writers, who observed events from the position of outsiders with commercial, military, and religious agendas. Malukan chronicles and royal genealogies survive, but many were compiled or recopied long after the events they describe. Archaeology of the sixteenth-century courts is still limited. Section 71.1 therefore sets out the evidence before the narrative begins, and the three Primary Source Windows in this chapter, drawn from a Portuguese treatise, an Italian chronicler of Magellan's expedition, and Jesuit correspondence, are used as evidence to be questioned rather than as authoritative narration.

The chapter belongs within a larger argument of this textbook. Chapter 69 examined the Portuguese conquest of Melaka in 1511 and Chapter 70 the struggle for the Strait of Malacca; the Portuguese expedition to Maluku was a direct consequence of the first and an extension of the logic of the second. Chapter 72 turns to the Philippine archipelago before Spanish conquest, a world connected to northern Maluku by sea routes and by Spanish ambitions that later ran from Manila. Chapters 77 and 78 take up the Dutch East India Company and its violent monopoly in Banda. The present chapter provides the Malukan foundations on which those later developments rest, and it insists that the foundations were Malukan.

Historical Context

By 1500, Southeast Asia had long been connected to wider commercial systems, as earlier chapters have shown. Chapter 18 traced the early maritime trade linking the region to South Asia and China, and Chapters 50 through 52 examined how Majapahit-era Java and its successor ports organized commerce across the archipelago. Chapters 62 through 66 explained how Muslim merchants, scholars, and rulers spread Islam through the ports of Sumatra, Java, the Malay world, Brunei, and Sulu. Maluku belonged to this world. Javanese and Malay shipping carried cloves westward, Melaka served as a great exchange point for them, and Muslim merchant communities ran along the same routes.

Chapter 67 surveyed the regional balance around 1500, and Chapters 68 through 70 followed Portuguese expansion into Asian waters, the conquest of Melaka in 1511, and the Portuguese struggle for the straits. Portuguese leaders understood that the most valuable spices originated beyond Melaka, and the capture of the great entrepôt was followed within months by an expedition eastward. Chapter 71 begins where the Portuguese eastward voyage ends, but it reaches backward to explain who inhabited the destination.

Two cautions frame the chapter. First, the "Spice Islands" of European usage were not a single place with a single spice. Cloves came from northern Maluku, while nutmeg and mace came from the Banda Islands to the south, which had a different social and political organization and receive their own treatment in Chapter 78. Second, the sixteenth-century names for Malukan rulers, offices, and places appear in many spellings, because European writers transcribed what they heard and Malukan traditions were later written down in several languages. This chapter uses the forms most common in modern scholarship and notes variants where they matter. Dates before the arrival of Europeans, especially for royal succession and religious change, usually rest on later traditions and are treated accordingly.

Historical Vocabulary
Sangaji

Sangaji is a Malukan title for the head of a district or territorial grouping within a sultanate. A sangaji typically combined local leadership with obligations to the sultan, such as supplying tribute, labor, and fighters, and often had authority over subordinate village leaders.

Historical context: The term appears in descriptions of Ternate and other courts, and its application changed over time. It is most fully documented in later sources, so historians use it cautiously for the early sixteenth century.

Potential misunderstanding: A sangaji was not a salaried official of a bureaucratic state. His authority rested on local standing and negotiated loyalty, and it could be withdrawn if the ruler failed to protect or reward his supporters.

Historical Vocabulary
Kapita Laut

Kapita laut, literally "sea captain," refers in Malukan usage to a commander of the sultanate's naval forces and war fleets, a role central to expeditions, raiding, and the projection of influence across the straits and seas.

Historical context: The title combines a word derived from Portuguese influence with a Malay word for "sea." Its documentation is stronger for the later sixteenth and seventeenth centuries than for the period before 1512, so the fully developed office should not be projected backward without evidence.

Potential misunderstanding: The kapita laut was not simply a ship's captain. The office carried political weight, since command of fleets was central to a ruler's power and could make its holder influential at court.

Section 71.1

Reconstructing the History of the Spice Islands

Every statement about sixteenth-century Maluku rests on a particular kind of source, and the kinds are unevenly distributed. The richest narrative material comes from Portuguese and Spanish writers: administrators' reports, letters to the crown, the account of Magellan's expedition, and the narratives of captains and soldiers. These authors saw Maluku from the viewpoint of outsiders seeking trade, military advantage, or converts. They often recorded what mattered to their own purposes, such as the price and quantity of cloves or the conduct of their rivals, and they sometimes misunderstood Malukan titles, kinship, and ritual. They named rulers by whatever approximation reached their ears, called Muslim traders "Moors," and applied European categories such as "king" and "vassal" to relationships that Malukan participants understood differently.

A second body of evidence comes from the Catholic missions. The Jesuit and other correspondence collected in the multivolume Documenta Malucensia covers the 1540s onward and offers detailed, often vivid information about Christian communities, local rulers, and the progress of conflict. Missionary letters were written to superiors and patrons, not as neutral history. They frequently stress conversions, hardships, and the perceived treachery of Muslim rulers, and they say little about how Malukan Christians themselves understood their choices.

Malukan voices survive in more limited and later forms. A small number of sixteenth-century letters from Malukan rulers to Portuguese authorities, written in Malay, are preserved in European archives, and they are precious because they show rulers addressing foreign powers in their own idiom. Genealogies, royal chronicles, and oral traditions recount the origins of the four kingdoms, the conversion of rulers, and the deeds of famous sultans, but many were written down in the seventeenth century or later, sometimes in the context of rivalry with Dutch officials or neighboring courts. Such traditions carry real historical knowledge, yet they also reflect later political needs, including the legitimation of dynasties and the ranking of kingdoms. For this reason the chapter treats traditional dates for founders and conversions as indicative rather than established.

Other kinds of evidence are scarcer for this period. Chinese and Javanese texts mention Maluku, and Majapahit-era literature lists it among the lands in its sphere of interest, as Chapter 50 noted. Archaeological research on early Malukan settlements, fortifications, and trade goods has progressed, but it does not yet give a detailed picture of court politics or village life in 1500. Comparative scholarship, especially the regional synthesis by Leonard Y. Andaya, uses anthropological insight into Malukan ideas of kinship, authority, and exchange to interpret the fragmentary documentary record. Historians should therefore keep three questions in view throughout the chapter: who is speaking, how close were they to the events, and whose experience is absent?

Section 71.2

The Geography of Northern Maluku and Its Maritime Connections

Maluku, a name that appears in many spellings and whose etymology is debated, lies between Sulawesi to the west, New Guinea to the east, and the Philippines to the north. Northern Maluku centers on a small group of islands along the western edge of Halmahera. Ternate and Tidore are the best known, each built around a steep volcanic cone. South of them lie Moti and Makian, also volcanic and compact, and further south is Bacan, a larger island. On the western coast of Halmahera itself stood the royal center of Jailolo. Halmahera, an irregularly shaped island of mountains, forests, and many coastal inlets, formed the hinterland of the kingdoms and held a great variety of communities speaking different languages and following different ways of life.

Volcanic geography shaped both opportunity and risk. The slopes of the islands provided fertile soil for tree crops, and clove trees thrived on them. At the same time, the small islands had limited flat land for large-scale rice cultivation, and their populations relied heavily on imported rice, on sago processed from palms on Halmahera and neighboring islands, and on fish. Volcanic activity, earthquakes, and the steepness of the terrain constrained settlement, and eruptions recorded in later centuries were a permanent possibility. Environmental conditions did not determine political outcomes. They did create dependence on maritime provisioning, which helps explain why access to boats, harbors, and trading partners mattered so much.

The sea was both barrier and highway. Short crossings joined Ternate, Tidore, Moti, and Makian, so that rulers could communicate and, when necessary, attack one another in a few hours by oared vessel. Longer routes connected northern Maluku westward to Sulawesi, Java, and the Strait of Malacca, southward to Ambon, Seram, and Banda, and eastward toward the islands off western New Guinea. The seasonal monsoon rhythms discussed in Chapter 4 governed long-distance voyages, but historians should avoid precise claims about sailing times that the sources do not support. What can be said is that the islands were not remote in the commercial sense. Many shipping routes passed through ports in Java and the Malay world on the way to Maluku, and Malukan sailors themselves traveled widely in light, fast war and trading vessels.

Geographic understanding also corrects a common misconception. The "Spice Islands" of European usage covered two different production zones. Cloves came principally from northern Maluku. Nutmeg and mace came from the Banda Islands, a small southern group with very different political arrangements, in which village communities led by prominent families managed trade without royal houses of the Ternate and Tidore type. The two zones were linked through shared trading networks, but they were not the same society, and the history of Banda, treated in Chapter 78, cannot be read from that of Ternate. The same caution applies to maps. A Dutch map of 1640 may usefully identify the islands, but it reflects seventeenth-century cartographic conventions and a political geography that had changed since 1500.

Section 71.3

Cloves, Trade, and the Commercial Foundations of Malukan Power

The clove is the dried, unopened flower bud of an evergreen tree native to northern Maluku. A tree needs a number of years to begin bearing, lives for a long time, and yields buds in a seasonal cycle that requires careful harvesting by hand before the flowers open. After picking, the buds are dried until they turn dark. Because the tree responds to local soil and climate, production was concentrated on Ternate, Tidore, Moti, Makian, and Bacan during the period of this chapter, with cultivation spreading to Ambon and nearby coasts as demand rose. Clove production was therefore tied to specific landscapes and to the households who held rights to tend and harvest the trees.

Cloves reached consumers through a chain of intermediaries. Villagers and headmen gathered and sold the product to traders, who carried it to larger exchange points. Javanese and Malay merchants played an important role, as did traders from Sulawesi and, later, Makassar. From ports in Java and from Melaka, cloves moved onward to Indian, Chinese, and Middle Eastern markets. Chapter 18 showed how Southeast Asian merchants had long managed such exchanges, and cloves were one of several highly prized commodities that sustained them. Historians generally agree that cloves were exported long before the sixteenth century, but they should avoid claims about precise early quantities or prices that the sources cannot support.

In return, northern Maluku imported goods it could not produce in sufficient quantity. Rice and other foodstuffs were essential. Textiles, especially Indian cotton cloth carried through Melaka and Javanese ports, were valued for clothing and as prestige gifts. Iron and iron tools, ceramics, gongs, beads, and other goods were also in demand. Exchange was not a simple market of equals, for rulers, nobles, and merchants occupied different positions. It is useful to distinguish four kinds of actors: the growers, usually village households; collectors and local leaders who assembled cloves; merchants who carried and sold them; and rulers who claimed tribute, dues, or preferential access to trade. Royal authority drew strength from the ability to supply visitors with cloves and to provision the people of the islands with imported food.

This commercial position explains how small islands could exert large influence. Control over producing districts, harbors, and fleets gave rulers revenue and followers. Imported cloth and other goods circulated through marriage and alliance, binding courts to leaders of subordinate communities. A ruler who could offer gifts, protection, and trade attracted adherents. The commercial foundation also created vulnerability. Rulers depended on external merchants for food and manufactured goods, and a blockade or a loss of trading partners could threaten the entire system. These conditions shaped how Malukan rulers received Portuguese and Spanish newcomers, as potential trading partners and as possible military allies, and why the newcomers' demand for exclusive access later provoked resistance.

Section 71.4

The Political World of Maluku before 1512

Malukan political thought organized the northern islands around four royal centers: Ternate, Tidore, Jailolo, and Bacan. Traditions refer to this grouping as Moloku Kie Raha, often rendered "the four mountains of Maluku," a phrase that ties political order to sacred landscape. The four kingdoms were conceived as a set, each with its own ruler, sacred associations, and territory, and traditions describe them as related through descent from common ancestors and linked by marriage. The concept did not eliminate rivalry. It provided a vocabulary in which rivalry could be understood, and it gave each ruler a place in an order larger than his own island.

Traditional accounts trace the origins of these kingdoms to founding ancestors and describe a gathering of the four rulers, sometimes associated with the island of Moti, that established principles of precedence and cooperation. Such accounts attach specific early dates to rulers and events, but those dates derive from later compilation and should not be treated as secure. Likewise, Malukan tradition often gives the earliest ruling title as kolano, and it describes a later transition to the title of sultan as rulers adopted Islam. Historians generally accept that this transition occurred gradually and that its exact timing varied among the courts.

Ternate and Tidore emerged in these traditions as the rival poles of a wider system of alliances. Later accounts describe two groupings of allied communities: one associated with Ternate and often called Uli Lima, and one associated with Tidore and called Uli Siwa. The labels refer to numerical groupings of allied territories, and their organization and membership shifted over time. What matters for this chapter is the principle that the Malukan world was a field of overlapping alliances in which each ruler sought to attract followers through kinship, gift exchange, military leadership, and control of trade.

By the time the Portuguese reached Ternate in 1512, the political map was therefore plural. Ternate's ruler, whom Portuguese sources associate with the name Bayanullah, had substantial authority over producing districts and possessed war fleets. Tidore's rulers asserted equal standing. Jailolo, with its mainland base on Halmahera, held a prestige that Malukan tradition sometimes ranks high, and Bacan held its own territory to the south. Marriage ties connected these houses, and so did conflicts over precedence and access to clove-bearing lands. This was not a stable federation or a collection of ready-made targets for conquest. It was a competitive political order in which rulers had years of experience dealing with outside merchants and who saw in new foreign arrivals one more opportunity to strengthen their position against neighbors.

Section 71.5

Ternate: Kingship, Cloves, and Maritime Expansion

Ternate's strength rested on a combination of a productive clove-growing base, a strategic harbor, and a ruling house skilled at building alliances. The ruler stood at the center of a court that included relatives, nobles, religious specialists, and officials. Portuguese observers described him as a king, and Malukan sources describe a title that shifted from kolano to sultan as Islamic practice spread. The ruler's authority had a ritual and genealogical dimension as well as a military one: he embodied the descent of the royal house, presided over ceremonies, and was expected to protect the community and distribute rewards to followers. Authority was personal and relational rather than bureaucratic in the modern sense.

Surrounding the ruler were officials whose roles are described in later sources and in Portuguese accounts. The jogugu is usually described as a chief minister or principal executive officer who administered affairs and could act in the ruler's name, which gave the office special weight during regencies. The hukum was associated with law and, in the Islamic context, with religious judgment and customary adjudication. The sangaji were heads of districts or territorial groupings, often with a local base and followers, and the kapita laut was a commander of naval forces. These offices should be understood with caution. Some details come from sources later than 1500, and the precise duties and titles varied over time and between courts, so historians use them as indicators of an administrative vocabulary rather than as a fixed constitution.

Ternate's power extended through alliances with leaders of producing islands and districts, through marriage connections, and through the expectation that subordinate communities would furnish tribute, labor, and fighting men. Fleets of large, multi-oared vessels known as kora-kora carried warriors and goods, and the ability to mobilize such fleets was central to a ruler's reputation. Makian and Moti, with their dense clove plantations, were of particular importance to Ternate's revenues, and Ternatan influence reached toward the Halmahera coast and across to Sulawesi coastal communities. Each extension of influence was negotiated rather than simply imposed, and the loyalty of local leaders had to be renewed through rewards and protection.

This pattern of authority explains why a Portuguese partner was attractive. A foreign ally with firearms and ships could strengthen Ternate against Tidore and Jailolo. At the same time, the ruler's authority could be undermined if foreigners interfered in succession or in the allocation of trade. Succession in Malukan courts involved competition among eligible princes and their supporters, and regencies could magnify the influence of officials. When Portuguese captains later intervened in succession, they stepped into a process already full of tensions and competing candidates, a decision that carried risks for both sides.

Section 71.6

Tidore: Rival Kingship and Eastern Maritime Networks

Tidore stood as an independent center of sovereignty, not a satellite of Ternate. Its rulers belonged to a royal house with its own traditions of descent and its own place in the Moloku Kie Raha. Tidore's court maintained officials, fleets, and followers, and it asserted equal standing in ceremonial precedence. The rivalry with Ternate was both political and familial, for marriages repeatedly joined the two houses even as their rulers competed for followers and clove-producing territory. Ternate and Tidore were separated by a narrow strait, which made contact constant and conflict never far from the surface.

Tidore's networks reached in directions that complemented those of Ternate. Its influence extended across parts of Halmahera and toward the islands west of New Guinea, where its rulers claimed relationships with local leaders and received products drawn from the eastern seas. These eastern connections gave Tidore access to trade goods and to followers beyond those commanded by its rival. They also meant that Tidore's strategic horizon differed from Ternate's, which faced west toward Java and Melaka and south toward Ambon and Banda. A ruler with ties to a different set of routes had reason to cultivate different outside partners.

That logic helps explain Tidore's reception of the Spanish in 1521. The Portuguese had already established a relationship with Ternate, and a rival ruler who feared the growth of Ternatan power had every reason to welcome another European partner. The decision was a deliberate political choice, made in the context of a long rivalry. It did not mean that Tidore's rulers misunderstood the newcomers or were manipulated by them. They had dealt with foreign merchants for generations and judged that the Spanish could supply military support, access to new goods, and prestige. The wider consequence, treated in Sections 71.10 through 71.12, was that Iberian rivalry became intertwined with an existing Malukan rivalry.

Tidore's later history shows persistent strategic flexibility. Its rulers sometimes allied with Spanish and Portuguese authorities, sometimes resisted them, and consistently sought to protect their own sovereignty and their standing relative to Ternate. As in Ternate, authority depended on personal relationships, kinship, and the ruler's ability to deliver protection and reward. The historian's task is to read Tidore as an actor with its own goals rather than merely as a Spanish proxy.

The sources allow only a partial reconstruction of Tidore's eastern connections. Later Malukan and Dutch-era traditions describe Tidorese claims over communities in the islands west of New Guinea and on parts of the New Guinea coast, and these claims were expressed through tribute relationships, marriage, and the recognition of local leaders rather than through garrisons. Products from the eastern seas reached Tidore through these ties and entered the wider trade. Because most evidence for the details is later than the sixteenth century, historians use it to understand the character of Tidorese influence and not to assign exact limits to it in 1500.

Section 71.7

Jailolo, Bacan, Makian, Moti, and the Wider Malukan Political Order

A history that stops with Ternate and Tidore would misrepresent the Malukan world. Jailolo, on the western coast of Halmahera, held a prestige that some Malukan traditions rank highly, and its rulers could draw on the large resources of the Halmahera hinterland. Its position on the larger island gave it access to communities and products that the smaller islands lacked, and its relationship with Ternate and Tidore shifted between alliance and rivalry. Portuguese and Spanish sources of the early sixteenth century mention Jailolo as a significant power. Its fate shows how foreign intervention could interact with local politics: Ternatan forces, with Portuguese assistance, overthrew the ruling house at Jailolo around 1551, although details of the event are contested in the sources.

Bacan, to the south, was the fourth royal center. Its ruling family had its own traditions and, like the others, maintained marriage ties with neighboring houses. Bacan controlled clove-producing land and had access to the southern routes toward Ambon, Seram, and beyond. Portuguese and Jesuit sources report Christian conversions among members of the Bacan elite in the mid-sixteenth century, a development discussed in Section 71.14, although the circumstances of those conversions and their political meaning remain subjects of interpretation. Bacan's example warns against assuming that its ruling family acted merely as Portuguese clients.

Makian and Moti were smaller but economically significant. Makian, with its dense clove cultivation, was valuable to those who controlled its harvest, and traditions associate Moti with the gathering of the four rulers that, according to later accounts, defined the Malukan order. Both islands supported communities with their own leaders and loyalties, and rulers of Ternate and Tidore competed to secure their allegiance. The islands appear in the sources as prizes, as sources of revenue and manpower, and as political actors in their own right.

Beyond these centers lay a wide spectrum of communities on Halmahera and neighboring islands. Coastal populations along the Moro coast and in the north, upland groups in the interior, and seafaring people maintained relationships of varying closeness with the courts. Some acknowledged a ruler's supremacy and paid tribute, others served as allies, and others maintained considerable independence. The Malukan order was therefore a layered system of alliances and obligations rather than a set of territorial states with fixed borders. Treating it as such would impose modern assumptions, a risk this textbook has highlighted since Chapter 1.

The case of Jailolo also shows why the Malukan order cannot be treated as a simple duopoly. A kingdom with a land base on Halmahera could draw on interior communities and mainland resources, and its rulers could contest precedence with the island courts. Its overthrow did not make the Halmahera communities disappear. Rather, many came into the orbit of Ternate, which acquired followers, access to producing lands, and new obligations to defend them, an example of how conquest extended Ternate's network without creating a uniformly administered territory.

Long-term causes

Island geography divided the clove lands into separate harbors and districts; relational, kin-based authority favored multiple centers of rank; and the Malukan concept of four kingdoms legitimized plurality.

Short-term causes

Rivalry over precedence, marriage alliances, and succession disputes continually redistributed followers among Ternate, Tidore, Jailolo, and Bacan.

Effect

No single ruler monopolized clove production or the allegiance of all communities, which allowed external merchants to deal with multiple courts and later gave Iberian newcomers openings for alliance.

Plurality was not a failure of state formation. It reflected an established political logic, and rulers like Khairun and Babullah later extended Ternate's reach without abolishing it.
Section 71.8

Islam, Indigenous Religious Traditions, and Social Organization

Islam reached northern Maluku through the same merchant networks that carried cloves. Javanese, Malay, and other Muslim traders and teachers brought religious practice and learning along the routes described in Chapters 62 and 65. By the time Tomé Pires wrote his account of Asia around 1512 to 1515, he reported that rulers of Maluku followed Islam and that the religion had been present for some decades. Later Malukan traditions describe specific rulers who adopted Islam and, in some versions, studied with teachers in Java, and they name particular figures and dates. Historians generally treat these accounts as valuable traditions but caution against assigning precise dates to royal conversions.

Islamization is best understood as a gradual and uneven process. Rulers adopted Muslim titles and names, introduced Islamic legal and ritual forms at court, and appointed religious officials, while villages and outlying communities followed their own timetable. Elite adoption reflected political advantages: alliance with the Muslim merchants who dominated trade, access to the prestige of wider Muslim courts, and new vocabulary for kingship. The shift from kolano to sultan was a visible marker of this process, but the change should not be treated as a clean break. Older beliefs about ancestors, sacred mountains, and spirits persisted and interacted with Islamic practice, producing forms of religious life that historians often call syncretic.

Malukan society was organized around kinship units, village leadership, and ranked relationships. Descent groups and their leaders anchored local communities, and marriage connected families in alliances that crossed island and kingdom boundaries. Bridewealth and exchange of goods, including imported cloth, expressed and reinforced rank. Village authorities adjudicated disputes and organized communal labor and ritual, while the courts relied on them for tribute and military service. Slavery and dependent labor existed, as in many Southeast Asian societies, and captives and debtors could be incorporated into households. Evidence for the details of these arrangements is thin and mostly comes from later sources, so generalizations should be modest.

Two interpretive points govern the rest of the chapter. First, Malukan communities were religiously mixed. Muslim courts coexisted with communities that retained non-Muslim practices, and, after Portuguese arrival, with Christian communities as well. Second, religious affiliation did not automatically determine political allegiance. Muslim rulers formed alliances with Christian Europeans when it served their interests, and Christian communities could resist Portuguese demands or align with Muslim neighbors. As Leonard Andaya has argued, understanding Malukan politics requires attention to kinship, rivalry, and exchange as much as to religion. Later sections apply this principle to the missions and to the conflict of 1570.

Islam also entered the courts through law and ceremony. Rulers took part in Friday prayers and festivals, religious officials attended the court, and Islamic names and titles appeared alongside older ones. Still, the evidence for the depth of religious instruction in villages is limited. Historians generally think that Islamic learning and observance were most developed in court and trading communities, while many rural communities adopted practices more slowly. That uneven pattern is the background against which Christian missions later found some communities receptive, some indifferent, and some firmly opposed.

Section 71.9

António de Abreu, Francisco Serrão, and the Portuguese Arrival of 1512

The Portuguese expedition to Maluku followed directly from the conquest of Melaka in 1511. Portuguese leaders had learned in Melaka that the most valuable spices came from islands to the east, and within months a small squadron under António de Abreu was dispatched to locate them. Francisco Serrão commanded one of the vessels. Malay and Javanese pilots and local intermediaries, not Portuguese navigators alone, made such a voyage possible. The route ran along the northern coast of Java and eastward through the Lesser Sunda Islands toward Banda, using sailing knowledge that long predated the Portuguese. This is the sense in which the arrival of 1512 is a European "reaching" of Maluku: it was Portuguese contact with a destination that Asian mariners had visited for generations.

Abreu reached the Banda Islands, where the squadron obtained nutmeg and mace, and then returned toward Melaka. Serrão's ship was wrecked in the eastern seas, and Portuguese sources report that he and his companions were rescued and carried to the region of Ambon and then to Ternate. Details of the voyage, including the sequence of landfalls and the circumstances of the wreck, vary among accounts and cannot all be reconstructed with confidence. What the sources agree on is that Serrão arrived in Ternate in 1512, that the ruler received him, and that he remained there for the rest of his life. He became an adviser to the Ternatan court and, according to Portuguese reports, aided Ternate in its conflicts with Tidore.

Why did Ternate welcome him? The reasons lie in the political situation described in Section 71.4. A ruler in rivalry with Tidore could use skilled foreign fighters and the prospect of a Portuguese connection to Melaka, a source of imported goods. Serrão's presence also gave Ternate access to firearms and to information about the wider maritime world. The relationship was reciprocal. The Portuguese gained cloves and a foothold, while the Ternatan court gained a military and commercial resource at a moment of competition. Serrão himself appears in the sources as embedded in Ternatan society: he married locally and took part in court affairs, a pattern that shows how individual Europeans could be absorbed into Malukan networks rather than directing them.

The expedition of 1512 did not establish Portuguese rule. It created a channel of communication and a small, informal Portuguese presence under Ternatan protection. Permanent fortification came later, in 1522, and the distinction between a welcomed individual ally and a fortified garrison is central to the chapter's argument. In the years following 1512, Portuguese vessels continued to visit Maluku from Melaka, and Serrão's letters and reports encouraged further interest. Portuguese accounts also link his correspondence to the thinking of Ferdinand Magellan, a connection that, in the sources, connects the Melaka-based expedition to the later Spanish voyage described in the next section.

Section 71.10

The Spanish Arrival at Tidore and the Rivalry of Iberian Crowns

The Spanish arrival followed a separate Iberian initiative. Ferdinand Magellan, a Portuguese navigator in Spanish service, had sailed in 1519 to reach the Spice Islands by a western route. He died in April 1521 in the Philippines, in the fighting on Mactan, long before any of his ships reached Maluku. Under other officers, the two surviving vessels, the Trinidad and the Victoria, continued south and east and arrived at Tidore on 8 November 1521. The Spanish ships had crossed the Pacific and the Philippine and Moluccan seas with the help of local pilots and information, and their arrival at Tidore rather than Ternate was in part a consequence of political circumstances: Ternate was already in relationship with the Portuguese.

At Tidore, the expedition was received by the ruler known in European sources as al-Mansur. The Spaniards traded for cloves and held discussions with the court. Spanish accounts, particularly that of Antonio Pigafetta discussed in Section 71.11, describe a ruler who welcomed the visitors and offered to supply cloves. The reception makes sense in terms of Tidore's position. A ruler rivaling Ternate had every incentive to obtain a European partner, and the arrival of a second Iberian power offered a counterweight to the Portuguese alliance on Ternate. The Spanish presence also promised access to imported goods and to the prestige of a connection with a distant monarch.

The expedition's stay was short. After loading cloves, the Victoria departed in December 1521 under Juan Sebastián Elcano and eventually returned to Spain in 1522, completing the first circumnavigation of the globe by a European-crewed ship. The Trinidad was leaking and remained for repairs. Its crew's later fate, entangled with Portuguese pressure from Ternate, belongs to the story of competing Iberian claims. The first circumnavigation was a European achievement in the narrow sense, but it relied heavily on Southeast Asian guidance, and the cargo that made the voyage financially successful came from Tidore's clove trade.

For Tidore, Spanish partnership was an opportunity rather than a submission. Later Spanish expeditions from Spain and from New Spain, in 1526 and 1527 to 1528, reached Tidore and extended the alliance. Each of these visits entangled the court in conflict with the Portuguese on Ternate, and Spanish forces were often weak and poorly supplied. The Tidorese court therefore received intermittent support at the price of repeated Portuguese hostility. The Iberian rivalry did not replace the Ternate–Tidore rivalry. It reinforced and expanded it, a pattern that recurs throughout the sixteenth century.

Section 71.11

Portuguese Fortification, Spanish Competition, and the Treaty of Zaragoza

The Portuguese decision to build a fortress on Ternate marked a decisive change. António de Brito arrived with a force from Melaka and began construction of the fortress of São João Baptista in June 1522. Until then the Portuguese presence had been informal and dependent on Ternatan hospitality. A fortress was a permanent installation that housed a garrison, stored goods, and symbolized a Portuguese claim to a share of authority. It also offered a base for the crown's commercial ambitions, since a stronghold allowed captains to regulate trade and protect cargoes.

The Malukan response was mixed. Some Ternatan leaders saw advantages in a closer connection with a European partner who could defend the court against Tidore and Jailolo. Others recognized that a permanent foreign garrison would alter the local balance of power. Ternate was then passing through a period of instability following the death of its ruler, with regency arrangements that magnified the influence of court officials. Portuguese officers inserted themselves into this setting, and from the beginning the fortress became a factor in succession politics. The history of the fortress is therefore inseparable from the history of Ternatan court rivalry.

Meanwhile, the Spanish continued to seek a position at Tidore. The rival presences generated armed conflict between Portuguese and Spanish forces in the 1520s, with local allies on both sides. Behind these clashes lay an unresolved dispute between the two Iberian crowns. The Treaty of Tordesillas of 1494 had divided the world beyond Europe by a line of demarcation, but the line could not be fixed on the far side of the globe, and each crown claimed Maluku as lying in its zone. The Treaty of Zaragoza of 1529 addressed this dispute. By its terms, Charles V of Spain, in need of funds, granted Portugal his claims to the Moluccas in return for a large payment, while retaining a right to repurchase and defining a new line of demarcation east of the islands.

The treaty should be read for what it was. It was an agreement between two European crowns about their own competing claims. Neither the sultans of Ternate and Tidore nor their subjects were party to it, and it did not transfer sovereignty over Malukan territory. Spanish interests did not disappear permanently: Spanish ambitions persisted, as the expeditions from the Philippines later in the century and the seizure of Ternate in 1606 show. For the rulers of Maluku, the treaty meant a reduction in the Spanish presence at Tidore, but not the end of European competition or of Malukan agency.

Section 71.12

Fortresses, Captains, Trade Regulation, and the Limits of Foreign Authority

The Portuguese presence in Maluku was organized as a captaincy within the Estado da Índia, the network of Portuguese bases and officials in Asia. A captain, appointed through the Portuguese administrative system, commanded the fortress and represented the crown. The crown aspired to control the clove trade and to channel it through royal agents, but its ambitions rarely matched its capacity. Captains and soldiers engaged in private trade, officials were frequently in conflict with one another, and the distance from Melaka and Goa made supervision weak. Historians therefore distinguish crown policy from the conduct of individual captains, merchants, and soldiers, whose actions often determined local relations.

Dependence on Malukan suppliers and allies shaped daily realities. The garrison needed food, labor, and protection, which Ternatan communities supplied, and its trade depended on Malukan collectors and rulers. The Portuguese had firearms and ships, but their numbers were small and their reach limited to the vicinity of the fortress and the sea lanes they could patrol. Asian merchants continued to trade cloves outside Portuguese channels, and Malukan rulers sold to whoever offered favorable terms. The ambition to monopolize the trade encountered a practical obstacle: no foreign garrison could govern the archipelago or compel the allegiance of all producing communities.

Tension arose most acutely over succession and commerce. Portuguese captains interfered in the selection of rulers, supporting favored candidates and, in some cases, deposing and removing sultans from Ternate. Portuguese sources report the removal of Sultan Tabarija to Goa in the 1530s, and Khairun's accession around 1535 occurred in this context, though the sequence and dates in the sources are not entirely consistent. Such interventions alienated Ternatan nobles and officials, who regarded succession as a matter for the royal house and its supporters. Disputes over the price and allocation of cloves added further grievances, since captains pressed for advantageous terms while rulers sought to maintain their revenue and access to other buyers.

The result was a relationship marked by interdependence and friction. The fortress gave Ternate's rulers a powerful ally and made them dependent on Portuguese help against rivals, but it also exposed them to foreign interference. For the Portuguese, the fortress provided a base but also a liability: it required supplies, men, and an effective relationship with a host who could withdraw cooperation. Foreign authority in Maluku was thus never simply imposed. It was negotiated, resisted, and bounded, and the events of 1570 to 1575 demonstrated how quickly those boundaries could be tested.

The fortress community itself was not uniform. Alongside officers and soldiers lived clergy, merchants, slaves, local wives and their kin, and converts who depended on the garrison for protection. Their interests did not always coincide with those of the captain. Some Portuguese sought quick private profit, while others settled in Maluku for long periods and built local ties. Historians reading Portuguese complaints about corruption and quarrels among officials should remember that the same sources reveal how thinly the crown's authority extended and how heavily the garrison relied on negotiation with Malukan hosts.

Section 71.13

Clove Production, Labor, Households, and Everyday Life

Behind the diplomacy of courts and captains lay the daily work of village households. Clove cultivation was a seasonal and skilled activity. Households tended trees, harvested buds by hand before the flowers opened, and dried the product for sale. Because the trees grew on slopes and in groves on small islands, harvesting involved climbing and careful handling, and the work drew on family labor and local cooperation. Rights to trees and groves were held through households and descent groups, and the distribution of the harvest passed through headmen, local leaders, and ultimately the court. Details of tenure and labor organization are poorly documented for 1500 and are inferred from later evidence, so general statements should be taken as probable rather than certain.

Food provisioning was a central feature of everyday life. Rice was imported, and sago from Halmahera and nearby islands supplemented local production, along with fish, vegetables, and tree crops. Imported cloth served as clothing and as a marker of status, and ceramics, iron tools, and other goods circulated through exchange. Fishing was a daily occupation on the coast, and boatbuilding and maritime labor were essential to communications between islands. The kora-kora and other vessels demanded skilled builders and large crews of rowers, whose labor the courts could call upon. Rowing for a war fleet or a trading expedition was a significant obligation that took men away from fields and groves.

Women's roles appear mainly in connection with marriage and household management. Marriage alliances tied families, villages, and courts together, and the movement of brides and goods through bridewealth exchange reinforced rank. Portuguese sources record marriages between Portuguese men and local women, as in Serrão's own case, and such unions created households that linked the fortress community to the surrounding society. These marriages often gave newcomers local kin and obligations and could draw them into Malukan quarrels. Evidence for women's own perspectives is scarce, and the sources speak of them mostly through the eyes of men.

Social inequality ran through these arrangements. Nobles and officials held rank and claimed tribute, commoners worked the groves and the sea, and enslaved and dependent people performed labor in households and on vessels. Commercial and military pressure fell unevenly. Demands for tribute, rowers, and fighting men increased during periods of conflict, and blockades and raiding threatened food supplies. In the 1570s, as later sections show, the siege of the Portuguese fortress and the diversion of resources to war placed heavy demands on ordinary communities. Historians should avoid assuming that the profits of the clove trade were shared evenly, or that growers benefited in proportion to the value of what they produced.

Section 71.14

Christianity, Francis Xavier, and Indigenous Religious Agency

Christianity arrived in northern Maluku with the Portuguese. Priests and friars accompanied the fortress community from the 1520s onward, and baptisms of members of the Ternatan and other elites are reported in Portuguese sources. The Jesuit Francis Xavier traveled to the region in 1546 and 1547, visiting Ambon, Ternate, and the Moro coast of northern Halmahera, and the letters of Xavier and his companions describe Christian communities and the difficulties of mission work. Xavier's visit has often been treated as the center of a missionary narrative. In fact, Christian communities already existed, and the Jesuit presence was one episode within a longer and more complex process.

Missionary reports speak of conversions in terms of baptisms and numbers, and they portray Christian expansion as the result of missionary effort. Recent scholarship, notably the research of Brett Charles Baker, has challenged this picture by showing that indigenous leaders and households played active roles. Communities and leaders had their own reasons for accepting baptism. Some sought protection from more powerful neighbors, access to Portuguese trade, or alliance with a distant but potent partner. Others responded to new ritual and spiritual possibilities that complemented existing beliefs. Baptism could be a strategic decision, a family or village choice, or an expression of religious conviction, and the sources rarely allow these motives to be separated.

Christian communities appeared in northern Halmahera and Morotai, which Portuguese sources call the Moro coast, in Ternate itself, in Ambon, in Bacan, and in neighboring islands. "Moro" here is a Malukan-region usage and should not be confused with the term applied to Muslims of the southern Philippines discussed in Chapter 76. The communities' relations with Muslim rulers varied. Some Christian villages were subject to pressure or attack from Muslim neighbors and courts, while others coexisted peacefully with them. Portuguese support for Christian communities could provoke resentment among rulers who saw it as interference in their own sphere of authority, and this resentment contributed to the breakdown of the Portuguese–Ternatan relationship.

The distinction between conversion and political submission is vital. A community that accepted baptism did not necessarily accept Portuguese sovereignty, and a community that refused it was not necessarily hostile. Religious change interacted with kinship and alliance, and it could divide families and villages. The Malukan religious landscape in the mid-sixteenth century was therefore plural, with Muslim, Christian, and non-Muslim non-Christian communities in contact, tension, and sometimes cooperation. Treating the story as a simple confrontation between Islam and Christianity would erase this complexity.

Section 71.15

Sultan Khairun, Portuguese Intervention, and the Crisis of 1570

Sultan Khairun, who came to power around 1535 and ruled until 1570, presided over a period of expansion and growing tension. He consolidated Ternatan authority over producing districts and neighboring communities, and in about 1551 he participated, with Portuguese assistance, in the overthrow of the ruling house at Jailolo. His reign shows the advantages Ternate drew from the Portuguese connection. It also shows the costs. The fortress, the captains, and the missionaries all asserted claims that overlapped with royal authority, and the Portuguese presence extended to areas, such as Ambon and the Moro coast, that Ternate regarded as within its sphere.

Disputes arose over royal authority, trade revenue, and interference. Captains sought advantageous terms for cloves and sometimes acted to promote private gain. Portuguese protection of Christian communities limited Ternate's ability to exercise authority over them. Rumors, rivalry among Portuguese officials, and mutual suspicion grew. Historians point to the cumulative effect of these grievances rather than to a single cause, and they stress that the sources, mainly Portuguese and missionary, present the conflict from the standpoint of those who were themselves participants. By the late 1560s the relationship between the sultan and the captain at the fortress had deteriorated badly.

In 1570 Portuguese officers murdered Khairun. Sources agree that he was killed in the context of a meeting at or near the fortress and that the act was carried out by Portuguese officers under the captain. Accounts differ in their details and in their attributions of blame, and the chapter does not reconstruct private motives that the evidence does not support. What matters historically is the effect. The killing of a ruler under the pretense of negotiation violated the norms of hospitality and alliance that had underpinned the relationship, and it provoked a general mobilization of Ternatan nobles and communities. Khairun's son Babullah was proclaimed ruler and led the resistance.

The crisis of 1570 was therefore not simply a Portuguese–Ternatan quarrel. It drew on grievances held by many groups, including nobles who resented interference in succession, officials who saw their authority undermined, and communities that experienced the demands of the fortress. It also had a religious dimension, though one that should be read carefully. Muslim leaders spoke the language of resistance to foreign infidels, but the conflict also involved Christian communities whose position was complicated by their ties to both sides. The violence that followed affected Malukan Christians and Muslims alike.

Khairun's own position deserves emphasis. He was a Muslim ruler who dealt with Portuguese captains over several decades and who, by Portuguese accounts, maintained a long working relationship with the fortress community. He sought to preserve Ternatan control over the clove districts and over Ambon and the Moro coast, regions where Portuguese protection of Christian communities cut across his claims. His murder therefore removed a ruler who had managed the partnership for decades, and the Ternatan nobility's reaction reflected a sense that the terms of that partnership had been broken by the Portuguese side.

One surviving missionary letter is especially valuable for understanding conditions just before the rupture, although it was written on Ambon rather than in Ternate. On 3 August 1569 Pero Mascarenhas told fellow Jesuits about local requests for baptism, the labor contributed to building a fort, and fears arising from regional conflict. His concerns reveal the connection between religious protection and political security before Khairun's murder, but they do not document the later murder or Babullah's blockade firsthand. Such a distinction matters because historians must not fill gaps in the record of the early 1570s with invented letters or projected eyewitness testimony.

Section 71.16

Sultan Babullah and the Expulsion of the Portuguese from Ternate, 1570–1575

Babullah's resistance was a political campaign as well as a military one. He mobilized the Ternatan nobility and the communities within Ternate's alliance, and he secured the support of Muslim leaders and other communities in the region. Portuguese sources and later accounts describe a sustained effort to isolate the fortress. A garrison that depended on local food and labor was vulnerable to withdrawal of both. Ternatan forces cut off supplies, restricted access to the fortress, and harassed communications, and the besieged community faced shortages. Blockade and food supply were as important as any assault, a point that reflects the garrison's dependence on the very society it sought to dominate.

Babullah's alliances extended the pressure. Communities that resented Portuguese demands or feared Portuguese power joined or supported Ternate, and his diplomacy with neighboring rulers increased his freedom of action. Tidore, by contrast, remained associated with Portuguese and Spanish interests, which illustrates how the Ternate–Tidore rivalry continued to shape alignments. The Portuguese sought reinforcements from Melaka and Goa, but distance and competing demands limited what could arrive. Portuguese strength elsewhere in Maluku, including a fortress on Ambon, offered a refuge but did not relieve the besieged garrison on Ternate.

The siege lasted for years. In December 1575, the Portuguese commander surrendered the fortress of São João Baptista, and the garrison and many Portuguese residents left Ternate. The terms and the details of the surrender are reported in Portuguese sources, which are not neutral. The loss of the fortress was a significant defeat, but it did not mean the complete disappearance of Portuguese activity elsewhere in Maluku. Portuguese forces and traders continued to operate in Ambon and elsewhere, and, as the next division shows, they soon established a new base on Tidore.

The expulsion displaced both Portuguese and local Christian populations connected with the fortress. Families formed through marriage, converts, and dependents faced the choice of leaving or remaining under Ternatan rule. Some left with the Portuguese, and others stayed or dispersed. The violence of the conflict, including the experiences of Christians in Ternate and on the Moro coast, belongs to the history of the period. Care is needed to distinguish political resistance to Portuguese authority from the history of religious affiliation: not every Christian was a Portuguese ally, and not every Muslim leader acted in the name of religion alone. What the events of 1570 to 1575 showed was that a European fortress with naval and gunpowder support could not extinguish indigenous sovereignty when the host society withdrew cooperation and organized collective resistance.

The Expansion of Ternatan Power under Sultan Babullah

After 1575, Ternate stood at the height of its regional influence. Babullah, now unchallenged at home, extended Ternatan claims over a wide area of eastern Indonesia, including coastal communities of northern and eastern Sulawesi, the Sula Islands, Ambon and neighboring islands, and parts of Halmahera and its surroundings. Later Malukan tradition credits him with supremacy over a very large number of islands. Such figures express a claim to prestige and should not be read as a census of administered territory. The key analytical task is to distinguish royal claims of supremacy from direct administration.

Ternatan supremacy operated through personal and political ties. Local leaders acknowledged the sultan's precedence, provided tribute or labor in varying degrees, and received recognition and protection. Ternate seldom stationed permanent officials over such distant areas. Instead, it sent fleets under commanders such as the kapita laut, installed or confirmed local leaders, and relied on marriage and alliance. The degree of control varied from place to place and over time, and subordinate communities could shift their allegiance when Ternatan power weakened or when a rival offered better terms. This is the mandala-like logic of overlapping and negotiated authority that Chapter 1 introduced, applied to a maritime setting.

The importance of clove commerce continued. Ternate controlled much of the production and trade in cloves and used the revenue to maintain its fleets and followers. Indigenous maritime capabilities, including the ability to construct and man war fleets and to travel long distances, underlay the expansion. Babullah also engaged in diplomacy with outside powers on his own terms. In 1579 the English captain Francis Drake, sailing around the world, called at Ternate, where he was received by the sultan and loaded cloves. The visit shows a ruler dealing with a foreign visitor as an independent sovereign who could choose among partners and who took care not to place himself under any of them. Drake's own narrative and associated English accounts reflect this independence, though they too are the writings of an outsider.

Babullah died around 1583, and Ternate's later fortunes were more uncertain. The very extent of the Ternatan network, based on personal ties and fleet mobilization, made it difficult to sustain after the death of a forceful ruler. Succession disputes, resistance from subordinate communities, and renewed external pressure all affected Ternate's position in the following decades. Yet the expansion under Babullah had established Ternate as the preeminent power in northern Maluku and as an actor with influence beyond it, and it demonstrated that the expulsion of the Portuguese had strengthened Ternate rather than leaving it isolated.

Section 71.18

Tidore, Iberian Alliances, and Competing Interpretations of Malukan Power

Ternate's rise intensified Tidore's dependence on outside partners. After the loss of the Ternate fortress, the Portuguese relocated their principal Maluku base to Tidore, establishing a fortress there around 1578, and the Tidorese court welcomed the connection as a counterweight to Ternatan power. The decision repeated a familiar pattern: an Iberian presence served a Malukan court's purposes while exposing it to foreign claims. In 1580, the union of the Spanish and Portuguese crowns under Philip II placed both Iberian presences under a single monarch, though the practical administration of Portuguese and Spanish possessions remained distinct, and the new situation did not end local rivalries.

Spanish power in the region grew after the founding of Manila in 1571, a development treated in Chapters 73 and 74. From the Philippines, Spanish governors sought to project power into Maluku. In 1593 an expedition against Ternate under the Spanish governor Gómez Pérez Dasmariñas ended in failure after the governor was killed in a mutiny among his Chinese rowers. The episode shows both Spanish ambition and the logistical limits of operating from Manila. By the end of the century, the balance in northern Maluku was shifting toward renewed Iberian activity and, from 1599, toward the arrival of Dutch ships, which sought cloves and alliances in their own right.

These developments underlie a historiographical question: did foreign intervention primarily fragment Malukan power or reinforce it? Different answers depend on emphasis and evidence. The Portuguese and Spanish presences certainly changed the local balance. They armed particular courts, supported certain candidates for rule, and contributed to the overthrow of Jailolo. They also generated new conflicts and divisions between Ternate and Tidore. At the same time, Malukan rulers used European partners for their own purposes and, in the case of Babullah, defeated a European garrison.

Tidore's alliance with the Portuguese after 1578 carried costs. The presence of a foreign garrison on Tidore exposed its rulers to the same pressures that Ternate had experienced, including claims over trade and interference in court affairs. Tidorese rulers nevertheless retained their own political agenda, and they continued to compete with Ternate for allies among the producing islands and the communities of Halmahera. The relationship between the two courts remained both antagonistic and entangled, joined by kinship and separated by rivalry, and neither Iberian partnership altered that basic structure.

The end of the century also saw changes in the commercial landscape. Clove cultivation had spread beyond its original islands, particularly in the Ambon region, which altered the distribution of production and gave new leaders a stake in the trade. Asian merchants continued to buy cloves despite Iberian efforts at control, and the arrival of the Dutch added another competitor for supplies. The courts of northern Maluku therefore faced a market in which their original advantage as sole producers was eroding, even as their political weight remained considerable. This shift helps explain why later contests over the trade became so intense.

Section 71.19

The Spanish Seizure of Ternate in 1606 and the Coming Dutch Challenge

Dutch ships reaching Ternate in 1599 introduced a new European interlocutor into a political field already shaped by Malukan rivalries and Iberian interventions. Sultan Saïd and Ternatan leaders sought commercial and military cooperation with these visitors, while the Dutch pursued cloves and challenged Portuguese positions. The sequence matters: a Dutch bombardment of the Portuguese position on Tidore in 1601 failed, but a Dutch–Ternatan force captured the Portuguese fort on Tidore in May 1605, after the fort's gunpowder magazine exploded during the attack. These were early-seventeenth-century operations, not a mid-seventeenth-century event, and the fort in 1601 should not be called a Spanish garrison. Subsequent Spanish fortification and military action belonged to the changing circumstances of 1606 and after. These Dutch interventions are introduced only to establish the transition; the VOC's wider corporate system and monopoly strategies belong to Chapters 77 and 78.

In 1606 the Spanish governor of the Philippines, Pedro Bravo de Acuña, led a large expedition from Manila against Ternate. The force included Spanish soldiers and Filipino and other auxiliaries, and it seized the fortified center of Ternate. Sultan Saïd of Ternate was captured and carried to Manila, and a Spanish garrison was installed. The expedition was a significant demonstration of Spanish capability and a striking reversal for Ternate, whose sultan was removed from his own kingdom and held in captivity.

The seizure should be understood with precision. It was a Spanish capture of Ternate's principal stronghold and of its ruler, not Spanish control of the whole archipelago. Ternatan nobles, communities, and exiles remained, and other courts, including Tidore, followed their own strategies. Many Ternatans did not accept Spanish rule, and Dutch forces soon returned to ally with Ternatan leaders against the Spanish. The Spanish presence in northern Maluku lasted for decades, but it coexisted with persistent local resistance and with the growing power of the Dutch, a story that belongs to later chapters.

As an endpoint, 1606 marks the beginning of a new phase and not the end of Malukan agency. The islands had become an international arena in which Spanish, Portuguese, and Dutch forces contended, and Malukan courts had to navigate a more dangerous environment. Yet the political institutions, kinship networks, and commercial ties described in this chapter continued to shape events, and Malukan rulers and communities remained participants in the decisions that determined the region's future.

For Ternatan leaders, the situation after 1606 required choices among bad options. Some accepted a degree of accommodation with the Spanish, others withdrew to other parts of the island group, and many sought Dutch help. The Dutch, for their part, were pursuing their own commercial interests and saw an alliance with Ternatan leaders as a means to displace the Iberians. The agreements that followed, and the colonial consequences of Dutch intervention, belong to later chapters. What belongs here is the recognition that Malukan leaders weighed the alternatives and acted within a changing field of power, and did not wait for outsiders to decide the region's future.

Section 71.20

Ternate, Tidore, and the Spice Islands: A Regional Historical Synthesis

The history of northern Maluku in the long sixteenth century shows a region in which commerce, kinship, religion, and warfare formed an interconnected system. Cloves gave small islands extraordinary commercial significance, and rulers translated that significance into power through alliance, marriage, and fleets. The Moloku Kie Raha provided a conceptual framework for a plural political order in which Ternate, Tidore, Jailolo, and Bacan competed within a shared understanding of rank and descent. This political order predated European arrival, and its logic shaped what Portuguese and Spanish newcomers could do.

Religious change followed the same pattern. Islam spread gradually through merchant networks and court adoption, and Christianity entered through the Portuguese presence and local decisions. Neither process was uniform, and neither determined political allegiance. Muslim rulers allied with Christian Europeans when it served them, and Christian communities made choices that cannot be reduced to Portuguese military control. Understanding Maluku requires attention to the agency of rulers, households, and communities rather than to confessional labels alone.

European intervention was real and consequential. The Portuguese fortress changed local politics, the murder of Khairun triggered a crisis, and the arrival of Spanish and later Dutch forces reshaped the strategic situation. But the history of the period is not well described as a European conquest. Portuguese and Spanish actors entered Malukan rivalries and were constrained by dependence on local supplies, allies, and goodwill. Babullah's expulsion of the Portuguese from Ternate, and the continued importance of Ternate and Tidore after it, demonstrate that fortifications and naval weapons could not extinguish indigenous sovereignty. That recognition does not minimize the violence and disruption the period brought to Malukan communities, including the Christian and Muslim households caught in the conflicts of the 1570s.

The wider lesson belongs to the larger argument of this textbook. Southeast Asian societies were historical actors that selected, transformed, resisted, and used outside influences. Narratives centered on European discovery reduce the Spice Islands to a prize. A Malukan-centered history shows them as the home of political institutions, commercial networks, and religious communities with their own histories. The Dutch monopoly and the violence of Banda, examined in the chapters that follow, would change the region profoundly, but they would do so in a world already shaped by the events described here.

Finally, the history of Maluku offers a comparison with other regions studied in this textbook. As in the Strait of Malacca after 1511, a European power gained a base by working through existing networks, and as there, its position depended on the cooperation of local rulers and on Asian merchants who did not disappear. Yet northern Maluku differed from Melaka and from the Philippines in the strength of its indigenous courts and in the lack of a single dominant state. The Malukan case therefore shows that European expansion in maritime Southeast Asia took different forms depending on the political structures it encountered.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the history of Ternate, Tidore, and the Spice Islands in the sixteenth century is best understood as a history of Malukan power and society into which Portuguese and Spanish expansion was integrated. The clove-producing islands of northern Maluku were commercially connected to Asian trade long before 1512, and their rulers possessed established institutions, fleets, and regional ambitions.

The evidence is uneven. Portuguese, Spanish, and missionary sources provide the richest narratives but reflect outsider perspectives, while Malukan chronicles are often later and shaped by dynastic interests. Historians therefore reconstruct Malukan history by reading these sources critically and by drawing on comparative and anthropological scholarship, especially the work of Leonard Andaya.

Geography and commerce shaped political life. Ternate, Tidore, Moti, Makian, Bacan, and Halmahera formed a connected maritime world with limited farmland and heavy dependence on imported food. Cloves, distinct from the nutmeg and mace of Banda, gave rulers revenue and followers, and the structure of the trade, from growers to collectors, merchants, and rulers, explains how small islands exercised wide influence.

Politically, Maluku was plural. The Moloku Kie Raha framed an order of four kingdoms, Ternate, Tidore, Jailolo, and Bacan, linked by kinship and rivalry. Offices such as the jogugu, hukum, sangaji, and kapita laut supported courts whose authority was personal and negotiated. Islam spread gradually among elites and did not determine political allegiance.

The Portuguese arrived in 1512, welcomed by Ternate as a potential ally, and the surviving ships of Magellan's expedition reached Tidore in 1521, where al-Mansur received them. Portuguese construction of a fortress on Ternate in 1522 transformed an informal alliance into a permanent foreign presence. The Treaty of Zaragoza of 1529 settled a dispute between European crowns but did not transfer Malukan sovereignty.

The following decades combined cooperation and friction. Portuguese captains depended on Malukan supplies and allies but interfered in succession and trade, and Christian communities grew through a mixture of missionary effort and local initiative. The murder of Sultan Khairun in 1570 provoked a mobilization under Babullah, whose blockade and alliances forced the Portuguese surrender of the Ternate fortress in December 1575.

After 1575, Ternate expanded its influence across much of eastern Indonesia through supremacy rather than administration, while the Portuguese relocated to Tidore and Spanish and later Dutch interests complicated the picture. Historians debate whether intervention fragmented or reinforced Malukan power, and the evidence suggests it did both, depending on the court and the moment.

The Spanish seizure of Ternate in 1606 opened a new phase but did not erase Malukan agency. The chapter's central conclusion is that Malukan rulers and communities shaped the terms of foreign intervention, and that European fortifications and naval weapons could not extinguish indigenous sovereignty. This foundation is essential for understanding the Dutch monopoly examined in Chapters 77 and 78.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a region often described as a prize of empire was in fact a society with its own political logic. Ternate and Tidore were not points on a European map waiting to be claimed. They were courts with rivals, allies, officials, and fleets, and their choices shaped how Portuguese and Spanish newcomers could act.

The chapter also illustrates a broader pattern in the textbook: outside powers entered Southeast Asian worlds through existing networks and were constrained by them. As in Melaka's aftermath in Chapters 69 and 70, Portuguese power in Maluku depended on Asian knowledge, supplies, and alliances, and its limits were exposed when local cooperation was withdrawn.

Later developments built on these foundations. The Dutch arrived in a region in which Ternate and Tidore were established actors, Islam and Christianity coexisted, and clove production had spread beyond its original islands. The violent monopoly examined in Chapter 78 and the colonial order of Chapter 79 would profoundly alter Malukan society, but they did so in a world whose institutions and memories came from the period studied here.

For students, the chapter offers a method as well as a story: ask who is speaking, how they know, and whose voice is missing. A history of the Spice Islands told through Malukan institutions and choices is not less rigorous than one told from European decks. It is more complete.
