Chapter Opening

Indonesia became a credible political project through institution-building, ideological argument and shared claims to sovereignty across a diverse colonial archipelago. That process involved contested choices and defeats as well as achievements.

In the Netherlands Indies at the beginning of the twentieth century, political life was organized under a colonial state that classified its subjects, divided authority among numerous jurisdictions and denied the population the right to determine its own political future. The territory was linked by commercial, religious and administrative networks, but no single movement possessed the authority to represent all its inhabitants. The rise of Indonesian nationalism was the process by which organizers sought to turn the inhabitants of that territory into a political community with its own claim to sovereignty.

On 20 May 1908, students at the medical school in Batavia founded Budi Utomo, initially concerned with educational and cultural development. Twenty years later, delegates at the Second Youth Congress affirmed a common homeland, a common nation and Indonesian as a language of unity. Between those dates lay the extraordinary growth of Sarekat Islam, socialist and communist organization, disputes over political authority, failed uprisings, and the emergence of associations explicitly committed to independence. The two events mark distinct stages of organizational development; neither can alone account for the formation of the national movement.

The political movements treated in this chapter faced problems that a declaration of national unity could not resolve. How could organizers sustain extensive networks while the colonial government restricted associations and communications? How might Muslims, Marxists, regional associations, constitutional reformers and noncooperating nationalists work together without relinquishing their commitments? Who could participate in nationalist politics, and who was excluded by unequal access to education, resources and institutions? These questions concern the actual functioning of movements rather than their stated objectives alone.

The chapter follows an overlapping chronology from the early twentieth century to the Japanese invasion of 1942. It examines the social circumstances of the nationalist organizations, their institutional structures, political ideas and leadership, and the participation of workers, students, women and regional communities. The final division examines the political circumstances created by repression and the alternative strategies pursued during the 1930s. The changing international environment is treated only insofar as it altered the possibilities for Indonesian political activity.

Historical evidence includes association statutes, political publications, congress resolutions, judicial records, colonial intelligence reports, census tables, personal correspondence and subsequent scholarship. The interests of the producers of these records matter. A claim of membership is not necessarily a count of dues-paying members; a police allegation is not proof that the individual accused participated in an uprising; a congress declaration establishes the position of its delegates, not the assent of the entire population. Throughout the chapter, a distinction is made between the historical record of actors' objectives and the historian's interpretation of their consequences.

The achievements of the national movement before 1942 were substantial, but they did not make independence in 1945 inevitable. The Japanese occupation, the revolutionary moment and the transfer of sovereignty belong to later chapters. The immediate responsibility of this chapter is to explain how national independence became a credible political project within the diverse colonial archipelago, why the movements pursuing it differed so much, and which of their organizational and intellectual resources survived the efforts of the Dutch administration to contain them.

Historical Context

The Dutch consolidation of authority beyond Java, discussed in Chapters 103–104, was still under way at the beginning of the twentieth century. Military campaigns and contracts with local rulers altered political jurisdictions, while administrative development and a growing system of transportation increased the importance of offices and ports. The future Indonesian territory thus had a history of regional polities and communities that cannot be reduced to the development of Dutch administration. Nationalists later claimed the colonial territorial framework, but they did not create it or inherit it as a fully integrated national society.

Chapter 121 established the differentiated legal and social arrangements of the colonial plural society. Racial classifications affected education, social standing and legal authority while obscuring substantial differences within classified populations. Chapter 122 analyzed the export economies that reshaped landholding, wage employment, labor migration and commerce. These economic and social relationships provided an important foundation for political grievances, but their effects were mediated by local institutions and conditions. The interests of an urban railway worker were not identical to those of a batik merchant or a rural tenant.

The institutional developments examined in Chapters 124 and 125 supplied political resources. Access to schools, print, correspondence and voluntary association allowed participants to meet, share arguments and maintain organizations. Religious education and reform movements had their own intellectual and organizational networks. Nationalist activity did not replace these environments; it frequently drew upon people and institutions formed within them. The relationship between religious and political affiliation would become a continuing subject of disagreement among activists.

International events influenced the opportunities and ideas available to Indonesian organizers. The First World War intensified economic pressures and political expectations; the Russian Revolution and the Communist International offered new revolutionary examples and networks. Indonesian students in Europe encountered anticolonial movements beyond the Indies. During the 1930s, the worldwide economic crisis and the rise of fascism complicated the choice between immediate confrontation with colonial authorities and conditional political cooperation. These influences were interpreted and adapted by Indonesians. They did not prescribe a single course for the movement.

The constitutional position of the Netherlands Indies remained unequal despite the development of the Volksraad and other reforms. The governor-general and colonial executive possessed authority that representative institutions did not. As organizations sought greater autonomy, full independence or changes in economic and social relations, they encountered different forms of state surveillance and coercion. The history of Indonesian nationalism is consequently a history both of the construction of political capacities and of the efforts to control or disrupt those capacities.

Division I — The Emergence of Indonesian Political Consciousness · Section 126.1

The Netherlands Indies and the Problem of Indonesian Nationhood

At the opening of the twentieth century, the name of the territory governed from Batavia was the Netherlands Indies, not an independent Indonesia. The inhabitants of that enormous colonial possession did not form a single political electorate or a population administered under a uniform system. Some lived in areas directly governed by colonial officials; others lived under rulers whose powers were constrained by contracts, military pressure and Dutch residents. The great extent of the colonial jurisdiction was itself a relatively recent result of conquest. The campaigns in Aceh, the interventions in Bali and the expansion of authority in Sulawesi and elsewhere continued into the twentieth century. The future national territory was therefore neither a natural political unit nor an innocent administrative inheritance. For many communities, the immediate political question was whether local autonomy could survive Dutch expansion at all.

Politically consequential connections nevertheless preceded the nationalist movement. The trade in rice, spices, cloth and forest products had long linked the islands. Malay functioned in many settings as a language of exchange among people who spoke other languages at home. Muslim scholars traveled between coastal settlements and across the Indian Ocean; Christian communities maintained their own networks of education and worship. Families from Sulawesi, Sumatra, Java and other islands lived in ports far from their places of origin. Neither these connections nor earlier empires should be renamed Indonesia retrospectively. They did, however, furnish languages, relationships and institutions through which later political messages could travel. Colonial authorities transformed rather than invented many of the connections that made organizing across great distances possible.

The population numbered approximately 60.7 million in the 1930 census. The census reproduced distinctions between people classified as indigenous, European, Chinese and other non-indigenous Asian groups. Such classifications combined descent with rules of legal status; they do not establish fixed biological communities or uniform political opinions. Legal regimes differed in matters such as marriage, inheritance, commercial transactions and judicial procedure. The status called European was not simply a synonym for people born in Europe. Local rulers and officials within the indigenous population also occupied radically different positions from tenants, plantation laborers and urban casual workers. The political problem of nationhood was thus also a problem of citizenship: who could belong to a future political community, and which inherited distinctions would remain?

Geography affected political opportunity without determining political conviction. The concentration of offices and schools in Java gave Batavia, Bandung, Surakarta, Semarang and Surabaya a disproportionate importance. Yet West Sumatra was a major center of Islamic education, the press and later radical political organizing. The port of Makassar was connected to the eastern archipelago, Medan to the plantation economy of eastern Sumatra, and Manado and Ambon to educational and military employment extending well beyond their local districts. People from these regions might meet at a school in Java and encounter political literature in common, while remaining attached to their own communities. The movement's ability to create a shared vocabulary could not be measured merely by counting its headquarters on Java.

The term Indonesia had nineteenth-century scholarly antecedents, notably in geographical and ethnological writing, and was not invented by the nationalist organizations of the 1920s. In the early twentieth century writers and associations increasingly used it as a self-designation. Political usages were not uniform: an inclusive Indies patriotism, a community of indigenous peoples, and an independent territorial nation were distinct possibilities. Indonesian students overseas, particularly in the Netherlands, helped make the new name an explicit claim to a people capable of ruling itself. The choice mattered because it asserted that the population was more than the object of a European colonial jurisdiction. It also made possible statements addressed to compatriots whom the writer had never met.

Neither territorial consolidation nor a new name guaranteed collective political action. Most inhabitants lived in rural communities whose immediate concerns included access to land, taxes, harvests, religious obligations and family livelihood. A party program published in Batavia did not automatically reach or persuade them. Nationalists had to develop institutions capable of connecting different interests and establish reasons for regarding an injustice in a distant place as relevant to one's own position. The following sections trace how these connections were constructed, where they broke down, and why political identity remained contested even as the language of Indonesia gained wider currency.

Geography Study I

Political Centers and Regional Diversity of the Netherlands Indies

The principal geography of the movement is a geography of institutions, not of uniformly nationalist populations. Batavia contained the colonial secretariat, STOVIA, newspapers and student associations. Buitenzorg (Bogor) was an important administrative center. In the Priangan region, Bandung combined technical education with publishing and the study club that helped create the PNI. The older court cities of Surakarta and Yogyakarta contained schools, workshops and religious networks; Surakarta's batik industry was central to the history of Sarekat Islam. Semarang and Surabaya were ports and railway centers, important sites of labor and political organization. On Sumatra, Padang and Bukittinggi connected reformist education and trading communities, while Medan was strongly linked to plantation enterprise. Makassar, Manado and Ambon remind readers that the eastern archipelago was connected through trade, missionary education, migration and administrative service.

When studying these places, distinguish the colonial territory from the territorial claims of a later independent state. A location of a party office identifies a center of organization, not the residence of everyone who supported that party. A railway corridor in Java is not equivalent to a maritime route between the islands. Connections involved people traveling, correspondence and print; the significance of any particular connection should be confirmed through records of journeys, meetings or distribution. Use colonial-era names alongside modern names where they help locate the places; do not assume the modern municipal boundaries apply in 1930.

The Ethical Policy and the Formation of a New Political Generation

In 1901 the Dutch monarchy announced a new orientation in colonial policy that became known as the Ethical Policy. Its advocates argued that the Netherlands owed obligations to the inhabitants of the territories from whose labor and resources it benefited. C. Th. van Deventer's earlier argument concerning a debt of honor was among the influential expressions of that position. Education, irrigation and migration became familiar components of the new policy. Yet Dutch political authority and the maintenance of the colonial economy remained fundamental assumptions. The policy did not grant political equality, nor did the wider provision of schools amount to a commitment to educate the entire population to a common standard.

The expansion of schooling was especially important to the formation of a new political generation. Medical and administrative schools offered channels of advancement to families that could meet admission and financial requirements. Graduates could work as doctors, teachers, clerks or officials; their qualifications were useful to a state that needed a relatively small, trained indigenous workforce. For students, however, the value of education exceeded preparation for colonial employment. Access to Dutch enabled them to read newspapers, political literature and scholarly works unavailable in their home languages. Students also encountered peers from other districts and took part in debates whose subject matter extended beyond the curriculum of the school. The school became a social connection between people whose families otherwise might never have met.

Access remained sharply differentiated. The educational pathways available to children categorized as European differed from those intended for indigenous villagers. Dutch-language schools and secondary institutions were particularly important gateways to professional advancement, while many rural children received little formal instruction or attended schools run by religious and local authorities. Colonial educational spending was unequal, as Ewout Frankema's study of the Indies emphasizes. Even when children could enter a school, travel costs, linguistic prerequisites and the cost of losing their help in the family economy restricted their opportunities. The concentration of the educated minority in particular occupations and towns shaped the early leadership of associations.

The School tot Opleiding van Inlandsche Artsen (STOVIA), the medical school in Batavia, exemplified both opportunity and restriction. Its students had to master a demanding professional curriculum. As future indigenous physicians they acquired skills that were socially valuable but entered a system marked by disparities between European and indigenous practitioners. A shared experience of study and an understanding that professional competence did not eliminate status distinctions could encourage collective reflection about the rights of educated Indonesians. It did not dictate how those individuals would respond. Some pursued careers within the government; others tried to improve society through educational organizations; still others became critics of the colonial order.

The new generation also included people formed in educational environments outside Dutch institutions. Muslim boarding schools, reformist madrasahs and study abroad produced religious scholars, journalists and merchants who could mobilize resources unavailable to a colonial-trained official. In the 1920s independent schools such as Taman Siswa and the Sarekat Islam-associated educational initiatives gave a political meaning to the ability to teach outside the institutions of the state. Teaching could become a claim to define the needs, history and moral obligations of one's own community. Different curricula and languages reflected different political and religious projects, not simply different degrees of progression toward the same modern education.

A small educated minority could exercise influence beyond its size through institutional positions. A teacher could organize pupils, an editor could connect readers, a clerk could understand administrative procedures, and a doctor could move between official and community circles. Their access to a salary, a place to meet or the ability to correspond could give associations a practical foundation. The same advantages could create distance from farmers and laborers. Scholarship campaigns and public lectures were attempts to bridge that distance, but many educated organizers initially sought improved status and welfare within the existing colonial framework. The clearest historical lesson of the Ethical Policy is not that education inevitably produced independence. It is that a restricted reform supplied resources with which recipients could pursue objectives that its sponsors had not intended, in a system that continued to deny equal political authority.

Cause and Effect

Colonial Education and the Formation of Political Leadership

Long-term conditions included colonial conquest, racial differentiation and a demand for trained subordinate personnel. The Ethical Policy encouraged more educational provision after 1901, although the government limited access and spending. The immediate mechanisms through which education could influence politics were the creation of peer networks, access to print and professional expertise, and opportunities for students to compare their expectations with unequal treatment in the colonial system.

The effects were contingent. Wahidin's scholarship campaign and the subsequent establishment of Budi Utomo demonstrate one route from schooling to organization. A graduate who sought a secure administrative position might choose a different route. A teacher in an independent school could mobilize a constituency without having attended the same kind of Dutch institution as a doctor. Education created differentiated resources, not an automatic progression from literacy to nationalism. The ability to maintain an association required money, recognition, leadership and relationships that a school qualification did not necessarily supply.

To establish a causal argument, compare the backgrounds of particular organizers with those of graduates who remained outside the political movement. School enrollment data give an indication of the potential reach of education, but they do not measure political convictions. Frankema's analysis of unequal colonial educational investment and Nagazumi's study of Budi Utomo enable this distinction to be investigated.

Budi Utomo and the Beginnings of Modern Political Organization

On 20 May 1908, students of STOVIA formed Budi Utomo (often written Boedi Oetomo in the sources). Sutomo, later a prominent organizer and physician, was among them. The initiative was stimulated by the efforts of Wahidin Sudirohusodo, a retired physician who had traveled seeking support for a scholarship fund. He associated educational advancement with the improvement of Javanese society. Questions concerning how more young people could acquire skills and how educated men could serve their communities provided a practical starting point for organization. The students' initiative transformed a campaign for assistance into an association whose activities could extend beyond individual acts of patronage.

The first congress, held at Yogyakarta in October 1908, showed that the student initiative encompassed different interests. Senior indigenous officials, aristocrats and professionals possessed resources and official connections that a student association needed. They also brought a preference for constitutional methods and cultural improvement that did not necessarily coincide with the political ambitions of younger members. The leadership passed into the hands of senior figures, including the regent of Karanganyar, Tirtokoesoemo. The predominance of men belonging to the priyayi, the Javanese bureaucratic and aristocratic strata, affected the association's social language and the range of people it could readily recruit.

The early geographical and cultural orientation was predominantly Java and Madura. Support for education and the improvement of Javanese culture could involve an expansive understanding of social progress without implying the abolition of Dutch sovereignty or the creation of a state including every island. This is why historians differentiate the aims of Budi Utomo at its foundation from the meaning subsequently ascribed to that foundation. Some of its members were interested in political action from an early stage, and the association's policies changed over time; describing it as wholly apolitical throughout its history is as misleading as crediting it with a fully developed nationalist independence program in 1908.

The association provided its members with a means of establishing branches, convening meetings, electing officers and defining a program in writing. These activities might appear routine compared with later revolutionary episodes, but they were consequential. An association could continue after its founders left school, transmit decisions to other localities and represent views to officials in a recognized institutional form. Its existence suggested that educated Indonesians could take collective initiatives in matters that the colonial state habitually treated as matters for official oversight. The problem was how far that initiative might extend and to whom it was accountable. Reliance on prominent officials could allow negotiations with the administration, while also discouraging confrontations that threatened those officials' positions.

The First World War and debates over an indigenous militia, representative institutions and the future of the colony offered opportunities for Budi Utomo to participate in wider politics. It became involved in campaigns for representation and participated in the Volksraad after its inauguration in 1918. Such participation was consistent with a strategy of gradual improvement, rather than evidence that all members supported the same ultimate constitutional settlement. Later, Sutomo helped build the Indonesian Study Club in Surabaya, and organizational developments culminated in the formation of Parindra in 1935 through a merger including Budi Utomo. The history of the group thus connects the early cultural associations to the cooperative nationalism discussed in Section 126.19.

In independent Indonesia, 20 May is commemorated as National Awakening Day. This national memory recognizes the importance of the modern association, but its chronology is an interpretation as well as a date. The name of the holiday does not establish that Indonesians were politically inactive before 1908. Earlier regional resistance, mutual-aid organizations, journalism and associations all formed part of the historical background. Akira Nagazumi's study of Budi Utomo's early years provides a way to examine the organization's actual membership, institutional decisions and changing political position, rather than infer its original aims from later celebrations. A reasonable judgment is that Budi Utomo was a landmark in the emergence of modern organized politics, but the emergence of an archipelago-wide independence movement required developments that it neither anticipated fully nor controlled.

Historical Debate I

Was Budi Utomo the Beginning of Indonesian Nationalism?

The choice of a starting date depends on the concept being studied. If nationalism means the organization of educated local people to improve their society through permanent associations, Budi Utomo can reasonably be seen as a landmark. Its founding by students, branch structure and congresses helped establish a form of collective activity that subsequent organizations could adapt. Nagazumi's research on the early association provides evidence of its development and internal disputes.

If nationalism means an organized demand for independence by a political community identified as Indonesia, the designation is more problematic. The early association's orientation was largely Javanese and Madurese, its priorities were educational and cultural, and its leadership was closely connected to colonial officials and the indigenous elite. The Indische Partij and later PI and PNI more explicitly addressed the legitimacy of Dutch rule. Earlier journalism, religious organization and resistance also complicate the suggestion that collective politics began in May 1908.

A third issue is historical memory. The later national commemoration established a shared beginning that could recognize a modern association without choosing among the rival political parties that subsequently emerged. Commemoration is historically important, but the choice of a commemorative date is not identical to the explanation of the origins of a political movement. Both the organization and its retrospective significance deserve study.

Sarekat Islam and the Emergence of Mass Political Mobilization

The origins of Sarekat Islam (SI) illustrate how an association formed for practical local purposes could become a major political institution. In Surakarta, a center of the batik industry, Haji Samanhudi and associates had organized Rekso Roemekso to provide mutual protection in the commercially active Laweyan neighborhood. Relations with a Chinese association deteriorated and confrontations drew official attention in 1911 and early 1912. As Takashi Shiraishi shows, the question of legal recognition was crucial: an organization without recognized standing could be dissolved by local authorities. Samanhudi turned to the journalist and organizer Tirto Adhi Soerjo for assistance with the institutional arrangements of the Islamic commercial association. The transformation into Sarekat Islam in 1912 therefore involved legal initiative, existing mutual-aid relations and commercial conflict, rather than a single act of foundation whose date can be stated without qualification.

Commercial grievances included competition over access to cloth, credit and markets. Under colonial rule, occupational opportunities and commercial law were differentiated by legal status. Conflict could be expressed through religious and ethnic identities; violence against Chinese residents was a real feature of certain early confrontations. It would be wrong to explain every Chinese trader as a privileged intermediary or every Muslim merchant as economically disadvantaged. They occupied many different positions within the economy. Sarekat Islam's potential to organize Muslims against inequalities was accompanied by the possibility that its language could be directed against other Asian communities. The expansion of the movement therefore involved a problem of defining interests as well as a problem of enlarging membership.

H.O.S. Tjokroaminoto emerged as a nationally prominent organizer as the association expanded. His capacity to address audiences in accessible political language, his standing among Muslim groups and his skill in dealing with officials helped give the dispersed movement a public identity. The government initially recognized local associations rather than granting a single central association legal standing. A central structure received recognition in 1916, and the national congress in Bandung that year was a landmark in efforts to coordinate the movement. Congresses provided occasions for delegates to articulate demands and meet colleagues from elsewhere. They also brought to light differences in what various branches understood the movement to represent.

The appeal to Islamic brotherhood linked men and women who shared neither occupation nor regional origin. Traders might value mutual assistance; workers might seek better conditions; rural participants might understand membership as protection from unjust officials. Leaders also drew upon a moral vocabulary in which economic exploitation could be condemned as an ethical as well as a political wrong. Membership did not imply a common theory of the state or a shared understanding of sovereignty. Local leaders interpreted central decisions in light of their own grievances and resources. In some areas, expectations of liberation drew on religious idioms and older forms of popular hope. Leaders could not simply direct these varied expectations from a central office.

The statistical record reflects the looseness of organization. Fred von der Mehden's bibliographical survey places the many reported estimates of membership in the early 1920s between roughly 350,000 and 800,000, with the movement's own claim reaching two million. These numbers do not represent a sequence of comparable censuses; they should not be used to calculate an annual rate of growth. Branch registers, congress representation, newspaper claims and the size of crowds attached to a speaker measure different phenomena. The movement's scale was nonetheless remarkable in comparison with the smaller educated associations that preceded it. It drew people into meetings and political language who previously had little contact with colonial institutions of representation.

This diversity made the organization an arena for ideological competition. Socialists and communists developed influence in branches such as Semarang; Islamic leaders articulated forms of social reform rooted in religious commitments. The subsequent conflict discussed in Sections 126.6–126.7 arose partly because these constituencies shared organizational spaces while pursuing different long-term programs. Colonial pressure compounded the difficulties of maintaining a central direction. Sarekat Islam's achievement cannot be measured solely by the durability of its unity. It made mass participation a practical problem that every later independence organization had to address, established networks reaching beyond elite schools, and demonstrated that anticolonial grievances could be expressed in an institution of far wider social reach than the earliest associations.

Continuity and Change I

From Commercial Solidarity to Mass Political Organization

Compare the organization at three levels: a local mutual-aid group, a recognized association with branches, and a mass political movement attempting to coordinate widely dispersed constituencies. The continuity lay in interpersonal relations, merchant resources, Muslim solidarity and concern for protection against insecurity. The changes lay in the scale of recruitment, the new meaning of membership and the increasing importance of discussion of political authority and economic inequality.

Self-help did not cease when Sarekat Islam became more politically active. Local leaders still had to maintain relationships among neighbors and traders. But a new branch could recruit laborers and cultivators whose grievances differed from those of the original batik merchants. Congresses and public speeches supplied a wider language of solidarity without eliminating local priorities. The gap between central aspirations and the autonomy of branches became visible when socialists and Islamic leaders disagreed about policy.

Compare Shiraishi's account of Rekso Roemekso and the process of obtaining legal standing with the movement's later institutional histories. A founding narrative may emphasize the earliest group to establish continuity, while an institutional historian may ask when the recognized organization was formed. The Indische Partij and Early Anticolonial Nationalism

The Indische Partij advanced a very different approach to political belonging. Ernest François Eugène Douwes Dekker, an Indo-European journalist later known as Danudirja Setiabudi, called for an Indies political community that would not be defined exclusively by European descent or by indigenous ancestry. Tjipto Mangoenkoesoemo, a physician associated with the earlier Budi Utomo generation, and Suwardi Suryaningrat, a Javanese aristocrat and writer later known as Ki Hajar Dewantara, were prominent collaborators. In 1912 their organization made the political condition of the colony itself an object of criticism. Its inclusive conception of Indies patriotism challenged legal classifications that had granted Europeans a privileged position even though the European category included people born and raised in the archipelago.

An important part of the party's audience consisted of people whose social position was insecure within the racial order. Indo-Europeans could have European legal standing and yet face prejudice and restricted opportunities compared with Europeans who had arrived from the Netherlands. The experience was not identical to that of indigenous subjects, who were subordinated under different laws and economic relations. Douwes Dekker sought to make shared attachment to the land a stronger basis of belonging than such divisions. His political program raised the question of whether the future country should be defined by its residents and their loyalties, rather than by a communal claim of indigenous origin. It did not erase the unequal interests or prejudices that made coalition building difficult.

The party sought a more openly political role than the educational associations of 1908. Its newspapers and public meetings made a claim to political agency that the colonial government regarded as dangerous. The administration refused legal recognition in 1913, illustrating how association law limited the scope of political activity. Lack of recognition did not make every member disappear or erase the party's ideas. It did, however, restrict legal organizational continuity and expose activists to official intervention. Its experience showed that a formally articulated political objective could be grounds for repression even when a group lacked the means to overthrow the colonial state.

The crisis of 1913 arose in connection with the celebration of the centenary of Dutch liberation from Napoleonic rule. Organizers intended to hold festivities in the Indies and seek contributions from the population. Suwardi and Tjipto helped establish the Comite Boemi Poetra, the Committee of Indigenous Sons, to criticize the arrangement. Suwardi's essay Als ik eens Nederlander was used the rhetorical position of an imagined Dutch patriot to expose the contradiction of celebrating national freedom in a territory deprived of equivalent rights. A Dutch text and a Malay translation circulated in July 1913. Its audience therefore extended across a boundary that the colonial system both enforced and depended upon: the unequal access of European and indigenous readers to public political debate.

The response involved more than a prosecution of a newspaper article. The governor-general's extraordinary administrative powers permitted the removal of people treated as threats to order without proving a conventional criminal charge in open court. The three leaders were initially subject to measures intended to remove them from their centers of activity; ultimately they went to the Netherlands. Kenji Tsuchiya's study of the committee and pamphlet situates this affair within the relationship between colonial domination and resistance. Administrative exile could interrupt organizing while limiting the opportunities a defendant might have had to make a legal proceeding into a public platform. It also exposed the gulf between metropolitan claims of liberty and the powers exercised in the colony.

The consequences were not confined to the formal life of the Indische Partij. Its successors and associates continued to pursue various forms of politics, and experience in the Netherlands influenced the exiles' later activities. Suwardi eventually turned much of his attention to independent education, establishing Taman Siswa in 1922. The movement of political activists between journalism, party politics and education was characteristic of this period. The Indische Partij was small and short-lived compared with Sarekat Islam; its importance lay particularly in its explicit challenge to colonial legitimacy and its effort to formulate a cross-racial political community. Later nationalist parties would address the same question of inclusion, sometimes with narrower membership categories than those advocated by this early experiment. The Indische Partij therefore belongs in the history of competing visions of nationhood, not merely a chronological list of parties leading toward Sukarno.

Socialism, Labor Organization, and the ISDV

Socialism entered the public politics of the Netherlands Indies in a setting in which the separation between economic and political grievances was especially difficult to maintain. The colonial economy brought wage workers together in plantations, workshops, ports, railways and government services. Employers could call upon official power in disputes; government departments were themselves important employers. In the railway system, for example, a grievance over pay or dismissal could involve decisions taken by public authorities rather than by an independent private company. The ability to organize workers therefore raised political questions about who could speak for employees and whether they could challenge decisions by officials. The different status and wages of European and Indonesian workers also demonstrated the interaction between racial classification and economic inequality.

The Vereeniging van Spoor- en Tramwegpersoneel, or VSTP (Association of Railway and Tramway Personnel), founded in 1908, provided an important organizational base for later socialist activity. Unions developed among employees with sufficient continuity of employment and access to communication to maintain an organization. Railway workers had opportunities to meet people stationed in different places and circulate news beyond a single workplace. They also confronted difficulties familiar to other workers: the loss of wages during a dispute, the threat of dismissal, and the possibility that employers would replace strikers. The persistence of an association depended on collecting contributions, maintaining membership records and communicating decisions as much as on the eloquence of a leader.

Henk Sneevliet, a Dutch socialist who arrived in the Indies in 1913, became closely involved in the labor movement. The Indische Sociaal-Democratische Vereeniging (ISDV), established in 1914, initially drew much of its leadership and political literature from Dutch socialist circles. It did not remain merely an expatriate association. Activists developed contacts with Indonesian organizations and sought to explain socialism in terms relevant to colonial society. Semarang, an important port and railway center, became a major site of this effort. The ability to participate in Sarekat Islam provided access to local networks that a small group of Dutch-speaking socialists could not have constructed rapidly on its own. Indonesian organizers did not simply relay European instructions; they interpreted socialist arguments in the circumstances of their own communities.

Semaun, who had worked in the railway service and participated in Sarekat Islam, became a leading figure in the Semarang branch and the labor movement. Darsono likewise emerged as an influential organizer and writer. The Semarang branch's increasing militancy brought questions of wages, prices, taxation and the power of officials into the broader discussion of social justice. The expansion of the Malay-language political press was vital to this process. A technical concept imported from Dutch or European socialist writing had to be rendered in terms comprehensible to readers and listeners familiar with quite different intellectual traditions. Oliver Crawford's research on the translation of Marxism is especially useful for understanding this process as the creation of a new political language rather than an act of simple diffusion.

The First World War intensified many of the grievances on which radical organizations drew. Rising living costs and uncertainty over employment affected workers and their families; news of the Russian Revolution supplied an internationally significant example of challenges to established authority. Different activists drew different lessons from these developments. Union organizers might seek recognition and better conditions; an ISDV activist might see successful economic organization as preparation for a revolutionary struggle. A member of Sarekat Islam could regard both efforts as consistent with Islamic obligations toward the poor. These overlapping identities help explain the rapid diffusion of radical ideas and the conflicts that subsequently arose when organizations attempted to impose more exclusive forms of membership.

Strikes and collective action were not signs that every worker had become a committed socialist. Workers weighed the risk of losing their livelihoods, judged the credibility of leaders and negotiated with employers. Some workplaces were easier to organize than others; rural, casual and domestic labor was particularly difficult to unite in stable dues-paying associations. John Ingleson's research on unions and Takashi Shiraishi's work on popular radicalism show why the pergerakan, or movement, should be studied as a social process. It encompassed political ideas, workplace institutions, public speech and contests over dignity. These experiences provided a constituency and a vocabulary for communism, but the decision to form a communist party introduced new demands of organizational discipline and ideological commitment that did not follow automatically from labor protest.

Historical Vocabulary I

Socialism, Trade Unionism, Class Consciousness, Internationalism

Socialism describes a range of political and economic doctrines concerned with social control of productive resources, class relations and equality. Socialists developed different views of reform, revolution and the role of the state. In the Indies, Marxist ideas were among the resources to which activists turned to understand the relationship between colonial rule and economic exploitation.

Trade unionism refers to the organization of workers to advance their shared interests, including conditions of work and pay. A union might pursue immediate practical aims without endorsing a political party. Class consciousness denotes an understanding of a shared social and economic position and the possible implications of that position for collective action. It does not mean that everyone within an occupational category necessarily holds the same belief. Internationalism describes solidarity or cooperation across national and imperial boundaries. It could be expressed through trade union ties, socialist organizations or anticolonial cooperation among national movements.

In reading a Malay political text, pay attention to whether a translated term preserves or modifies the assumptions of the original-language argument. Crawford's work shows how Indonesian translators and political writers made Marxist vocabulary intelligible to new audiences. The labor history of Ingleson provides a different evidentiary perspective, concentrating on workers and institutions. These approaches can be combined without presuming that participants in every strike understood Marxism in the same way.

Division II — Political Radicalization, Socialism, and Communism · Section 126.7

The Indonesian Communist Party and the Fragmentation of Sarekat Islam

The Russian Revolution altered the international environment of the Indies socialists, and the creation of the Communist International (Comintern) in 1919 offered parties a framework for international revolutionary cooperation. At the ISDV congress in May 1920, the association reorganized itself as a communist organization, known in Malay as Perserikatan Kommunist di India, with a corresponding Dutch name. Its initials, PKI, became associated with the Partai Komunis Indonesia, the name adopted in 1924. It was among the earliest communist parties organized in Asia. The chronology matters because the party emerged through a sequence of institutional decisions taken by activists already embedded in local politics, not through the sudden arrival of a foreign organization operating independently of the society around it.

Semaun became the first chairman, and Darsono and other Indonesian organizers assumed increasingly prominent positions. Tan Malaka, a Minangkabau teacher educated in the Netherlands, brought a different combination of educational experience and political thought. His work in Semarang included schools associated with Sarekat Islam, which attempted to provide education linked to the social conditions of the people who attended them. He also became a party leader and subsequently an important participant in international communist circles. The Comintern offered connections to activists and publications outside the Indies, but communication was difficult, surveillance extensive and agreement on strategy far from automatic. The Indonesian party's leaders confronted questions concerning the colonial state, Islam and the social composition of the Indies that could not be settled simply by repeating a European program.

For a time, dual participation in Sarekat Islam and communist organization permitted collaboration. Local branches provided venues for speeches and newspapers, helped disseminate news of labor struggles and permitted activists to build constituencies beyond the small number of people explicitly committed to communism. But this arrangement raised a question of political authority. If a local Sarekat Islam branch accepted instructions from a communist organization, could the central Islamic leadership require it to follow a different program? Leaders such as Tjokroaminoto and Agus Salim objected to communist efforts to redirect an Islamic association toward class-based politics. They were also concerned about the implications of a relationship with an international organization often identified with atheism. Their objections did not imply that Islamic leaders opposed all labor action or social equality.

In 1921 a Sarekat Islam congress adopted a rule requiring party discipline and restricting concurrent membership in rival political organizations. The decision created a more formal breach between the central leadership and communist activists. The labels Red and White Sarekat Islam came to identify competing currents, although adherents of each were diverse and political allegiances could change. In 1923 the pro-communist branches developed into Sarekat Rakyat, the People's Association. The effort to build a parallel network demonstrated the importance of existing local organizations: severing ties with the central Islamic body did not mean that the activists had lost every personal relationship or every constituency. Conversely, a political label placed upon a branch did not establish that all its members accepted a coherent Marxist philosophy.

The ideological disagreement concerned both immediate action and the desired shape of a future society. Communist leaders understood colonial domination in relation to capitalist exploitation and emphasized international class solidarity. Islamic activists could condemn exploitation on moral grounds and invoke the broader Muslim community as a source of solidarity without conceding the primacy of class conflict. Some activists attempted to combine elements of these languages. Tan Malaka, for example, defended the practical importance of understanding pan-Islamic solidarity in colonial societies rather than treating it as necessarily incompatible with anticolonial cooperation. Political debates thus involved questions of strategy, authority and interpretation, not an exhaustive choice between religious belief and economic grievance.

The communist movement also confronted the limits of legal organization. Deportation of leaders, restrictions on meetings and action against strikes made it increasingly difficult for activists to coordinate openly. The colonial state viewed the association of labor organization and revolutionary politics with particular suspicion. Official claims that unrest was the result of communist manipulation can obscure the independently intelligible concerns of workers and rural communities. Communist claims of broad revolutionary readiness can likewise exaggerate actual organizational capacities. The evidence of membership, strikes and local support must be read with these competing incentives in mind. The fragmentation of Sarekat Islam did not end Islamic politics, nor did the establishment of the PKI make the labor movement uniformly communist. It produced rival organizational centers with different strategies, a development that helps explain both the radicalization of the mid-1920s and the difficulty of building a coordinated revolt.

Multiple Perspectives I

Interpreting Political Radicalization

A communist organizer could view cooperation with Sarekat Islam as necessary because established local associations brought workers and rural constituencies into contact with political ideas. From this perspective, solidarity based on economic position need not exclude Muslim participants. The difficulty arose when organizers regarded instructions from their party as having priority over decisions of the Islamic association. Communists were also divided over international direction and domestic strategy.

An Islamic leader might support demands for social justice while opposing the subordination of the organization to a Marxist party or an international body associated with atheism. Religious authority and the autonomy of the association mattered in their own right. Views varied considerably among local leaders and their followers; criticism of communist party discipline was not necessarily approval of colonial inequality. The 1921 restrictions on concurrent membership forced activists who had previously participated in both environments to reconsider their institutional allegiances.

From the viewpoint of colonial authorities, the linkage between strikes, Islamic associations and revolutionary ideas presented a potential security problem. Reports could interpret a union or school as part of a political network. This standpoint was backed by unequal coercive power: officials could restrict organization, arrest people or expel activists. It should be studied through the records of the administration, not treated as the neutral standard by which the other positions are judged.

The different perspectives illuminate why the same membership rule could be presented as an act of self-protection, an unnecessary division of the movement or a welcome reduction in an apparent security threat. Crawford's study of the translation and circulation of Marxist ideas, alongside the organizational history by McVey, offers a basis for reconstructing these disagreements.

The Communist Uprisings of 1926–1927 and Their Repression

The communist uprisings of 1926–1927 were a decisive defeat for open revolutionary organization, but they were not a single national rebellion directed by an effective central command. Their background lay in the rapid growth of popular political associations, the conflict within Sarekat Islam, labor disputes and the contraction of legal opportunities. The railway strike of 1923 was especially important. Its defeat under the administration of Governor-General Dirk Fock damaged a major source of organized labor power. Dismissals and the removal of leaders weakened unions and made it difficult to maintain sustained public collective action. Some radicals concluded that more confrontational methods were necessary; others recognized that the organizational defeats made revolutionary action less feasible.

The party leadership was divided over timing and the prospects of an uprising. The people who might otherwise have coordinated activities were themselves scattered by arrests, restrictions and exile. Tan Malaka, operating abroad, opposed the decision to launch the revolt, arguing that conditions were not suitable for a successful revolutionary attempt. The Prambanan decision of late 1925 and subsequent meetings among leaders became points of dispute. Alimin and Musso sought support abroad; the Comintern did not direct the local outbreaks. Other leaders and local activists pursued plans for armed action. Decisions made under these circumstances reflected uncertainty about communications, the reliability of local support and the government's strength. They also reflected political conviction: some participants believed that postponement would allow colonial authorities to destroy the movement entirely. Neither disagreement among leaders nor the occurrence of repression establishes that those who favored action had a workable national plan.

In November 1926 uprisings broke out in parts of Java, including Batavia and Banten. In and around the capital, actions occurred in an environment shaped by urban networks, while the Banten events drew on a social world of village leaders, religious connections and rural grievances. Smaller disturbances elsewhere did not develop into a sustained archipelago-wide revolt. The colonial state could deploy concentrated armed forces against dispersed challengers and rapidly reestablish control over transport and administrative centers. Some planned actions failed to occur, and the chronology of the outbreaks itself reveals the limits of coordination. Local grievances and communist organization were related, but their combination varied substantially from one district to another.

The West Sumatran rising in January 1927 had its own regional setting. Silungkang and other districts of the Minangkabau region were connected to trading routes, mining and railway employment, Islamic educational networks and a lively vernacular press. Some religiously educated participants found radical social criticism intelligible in terms of their existing moral commitments. The movement's local leadership and the extent of participation therefore cannot be deduced solely from the doctrinal positions of party leaders on Java. The period separating the Sumatran disturbances from those in Java gave the colonial authorities additional opportunity to prepare a response. The disturbances were suppressed, leaving the revolutionary movement unable to sustain its public organization.

The repression had consequences much wider than the actual number of people who had engaged in armed action. Police investigations and arrests reached alleged supporters and people suspected of communist sympathies. Kankan Xie’s study of transnational communist networks, Estranged Comrades (2018), reports approximately 13,000 arrests for alleged participation and 1,308 people banished to Boven-Digoel, while distinguishing an additional category of people targeted for supposed communist tendencies. Such figures must not be treated as a verified roster of guilty individuals, and the interned should not be added to the arrested as if the categories were disjoint. The exact totals vary among accounts. The figures indicate the scale of repression, not the number of people who willingly participated in an uprising.

Boven-Digoel, in the southern part of Dutch New Guinea, became an instrument of administrative internment. Distance, difficult travel and an unhealthy environment separated detainees from established political and family networks. The deprivation imposed on those held there and the burdens borne by relatives were part of the political consequences of the revolt. The camp also illustrates how colonial emergency measures could operate outside the ordinary framework of trial and finite prison sentence. Yet communism did not vanish as an intellectual or political tradition. Exiles and dissidents pursued activities abroad, including Tan Malaka through networks independent of the official party leadership; underground contacts within the colony survived unevenly. The suppression shifted the possibilities for open politics and encouraged other nationalist organizations to distinguish their strategies from those associated with rebellion. It is this altered environment, not an inevitable succession of parties, that forms the immediate context for the emergence of the PNI.

Perhimpunan Indonesia and Nationalism Abroad

Association among students in the Netherlands provided an important but geographically unusual setting for Indonesian nationalism. When the Indische Vereeniging was founded in 1908, it was principally an association serving the social and cultural needs of people from the Indies who were studying in the metropolitan country. Such students constituted a tiny and relatively privileged minority compared with the population at home. They nonetheless occupied a revealing position. Living in the country that governed their homeland allowed them to compare Dutch public institutions and claims about political liberty with their own experience as colonial subjects. Meeting students from other regions of the Indies away from the hierarchies of their home communities encouraged them to reconsider how they defined their shared interests.

The association gradually became more explicitly political. Its journal Hindia Poetra, established during the First World War, provided a means of communicating with Indonesian readers and discussing the relationship between the colony and the Netherlands. Contacts with the Indische Partij exiles, among them Suwardi Suryaningrat, contributed to the intellectual atmosphere of student politics. The use of the name Indonesia was part of a broader shift toward an independent political identity. In 1922 the association adopted the name Indonesische Vereeniging, and during the 1924–1925 transition it adopted the Indonesian name Perhimpunan Indonesia (PI). Its independence-oriented principles stressed national unity, solidarity, noncooperation and reliance on Indonesian capacities. The change in name articulated a political relationship between the people speaking and the territory they claimed, instead of simply describing their geographic origin.

Mohammad Hatta, from Bukittinggi in West Sumatra, went to the Netherlands in 1921 to study economics. He became a major figure in this transformation, serving as chairman of the PI during a particularly active phase of its international work. Economic study influenced his understanding of colonial inequalities, but his politics also drew on Minangkabau experience, Islamic and ethical concerns and encounters with European socialist ideas. His contemporaries in the association included Ali Sastroamidjojo and other future Indonesian leaders. The periodical Indonesia Merdeka, which succeeded Hindia Poetra under its new title in the 1920s, conveyed the claim that the country should possess its own political authority. The publication of articles by students abroad could have consequences in the Indies when copies, ideas or returning organizers reached local associations.

The international dimensions of this work were significant. Hatta participated in the Congress against Colonial Oppression and Imperialism held in Brussels in February 1927, which established the League against Imperialism. He came into contact there with anticolonial activists associated with other parts of Asia and the wider world, including Jawaharlal Nehru. Cooperation in an international organization potentially enabled a small Indonesian association to present its cause as part of a shared challenge to imperial rule. Klaas Stutje's study of Hatta in Brussels cautions against assuming that the international stature of Indonesian representatives reflected an equally extensive organization at home. The PI itself had a very limited active membership. Hatta's prominence was made possible in part by relationships among activists and the international importance attached to colonial grievances and the recent communist revolt.

International alliances also presented difficulties. The League brought together communists and nationalists whose eventual political objectives were not the same. The PI wanted the Indonesian independence question to retain its own character and not be reduced to a component of a program determined elsewhere. Participation in common campaigns could benefit the association by bringing publicity, while association with organizations monitored by police increased the risk of criminal prosecution. The contradictions resembled those faced by activists within the Indies: political unity was both a resource and a problem of authority. An Indonesian nationalist overseas had to assess not only the intentions of possible allies but also how a relationship would be interpreted by Dutch officials and audiences at home.

In September 1927 Hatta and three colleagues were arrested in the Netherlands. They faced accusations connected with their political writings and activities. Hatta used the ensuing trial as an occasion to explain the claim to independence in his defense, remembered as Indonesië Vrij (Free Indonesia). The defendants were acquitted in March 1928. The result contrasted sharply with the risks of political prosecution under colonial conditions in the Indies, though the metropolitan state also possessed extensive powers of investigation and repression. The episode gave the PI considerable symbolic importance beyond its membership. Its publications, international contacts and insistence on an independence-oriented program influenced the ideas and strategic choices of organizations in the colony. The association was not the sole origin of Indonesian nationalism, but its experience demonstrates how nationalist movements could operate across imperial borders and acquire authority through intellectual work, legal argument and international relationships as well as domestic mass mobilization.

Sukarno and the Formation of a Nationalist Political Philosophy

Sukarno's political development occurred within the overlapping environments of professional education, Islamic organization, popular journalism and socialist debate. Born in 1901, he attended school in Surabaya, where he stayed in the household of Tjokroaminoto, the prominent Sarekat Islam leader. That household was one of several places in which young activists encountered competing currents of political thought. Later, at the Technical College in Bandung, Sukarno trained as an engineer. Engineering did not itself supply a program of national liberation, but his education and participation in study circles provided access to debates about economic development, colonial authority and the organization of modern society. As he entered political journalism, he addressed audiences whose experiences ranged from technical and professional training to religious education and work in the colonial economy.

The major intellectual problem that he confronted in the 1920s was the division of anticolonial politics. Sarekat Islam had fragmented; the communist movement was in increasingly severe conflict with the state; and early nationalist associations had struggled to achieve a broad constituency. Sukarno argued that rival tendencies could recognize a common interest in opposing colonial domination. In his 1926 essay Nasionalisme, Islamisme dan Marxisme, published in Suluh Indonesia Muda, he discussed nationalism, Islamic political commitment and Marxism as distinct forces with the possibility of cooperation. The essay treated mutual hostility as a political impediment rather than a necessary result of the differences among the three traditions. It therefore attempted an intellectual intervention in the organization of the movement as well as an explanation of its ideological composition.

His nationalism emphasized collective emancipation from foreign domination and rejected a narrowly imitative version of European nationalism that justified the domination of other peoples. Indonesian unity was to be an instrument of liberation, not a claim that every community already shared the same history or political outlook. His treatment of Islam emphasized the potential for solidarity and the pursuit of justice across social divisions. He did not propose that Muslim activists abandon religious commitments in order to participate in the movement. Marxism furnished an analysis of capitalism and imperialism that could help explain why colonial subordination was maintained through economic relationships, not merely by individual prejudice or an unjust administrator. Sukarno sought to distinguish this analysis from the assumption that accepting it required the abandonment of religious belief.

The proposed synthesis did not remove the substantive differences among the constituent forces. Communist leaders could insist that class antagonisms required political action even against wealthy indigenous interests. Religious leaders could understand the obligations of a Muslim political community in terms that were not reducible to territorial nationalism. Nationalists could insist that independence should take precedence over disputed social programs. These priorities affected choices about party membership, the distribution of leadership and the means of contesting colonial power. Agreement that imperialism should end did not imply agreement about how a national government should be constituted or which classes and institutions should exercise authority within it. Sukarno's essay can thus be read as evidence of the pressure for unity, not proof that unity had been achieved.

In the ensuing years Sukarno developed the political vocabulary of Marhaen and Marhaenism. His often-retold encounter with a small cultivator named Marhaen is part of the biographical and political tradition surrounding the concept; its value as a precise record of a particular meeting should be distinguished from the meaning Sukarno gave it. Many Indonesians possessed a little land or their own tools and were nevertheless impoverished by taxation, debt, market conditions and unequal power. They were not necessarily wage laborers who owned no productive resources. The category of the Marhaen allowed Sukarno to speak about the plight of such small producers as well as workers and other subordinated people. It connected an analysis of exploitation to the predominantly rural social composition of the Indies and offered a language of solidarity broader than a strict identification of the revolutionary subject with industrial workers.

Marhaenism developed through speeches, writings and organizational debate rather than appearing as a fully settled doctrine in 1926. Its appeal lay partly in the capacity to connect a national objective with the daily experiences of a wide population. Its limitations included the difficulty of reconciling the interests of poor cultivators with those of wealthier nationalists and the uncertainty of its institutional application. Sukarno's intellectual importance should therefore be evaluated alongside the work of Hatta, Sjahrir, Islamic reformers and communist writers. His later Guided Democracy formula known as Nasakom belonged to another historical situation and must not be projected backward as the established program of the 1926 essay. Here the central question is the formation of a political language under colonial rule, not the history of the independent state.

The Founding and Development of the Indonesian National Party

The founding of the nationalist organization in Bandung on 4 July 1927 represented an effort to translate the independence-oriented ideas already circulating in student and political circles into a permanent organization operating within the colony. Sukarno and colleagues associated with the Algemeene Studieclub (General Study Club), created in Bandung in 1926, recognized that study and publication alone could not mobilize sufficient political support. Study clubs gave educated participants opportunities to discuss the colonial economy, political representation and the future of Indonesia. The establishment of a wider association was an attempt to connect these activities to the organization of public opinion among people outside such circles. The body founded in 1927 was called Perserikatan Nasional Indonesia; it adopted the name Partai Nasional Indonesia (PNI) in 1928.

The context of the party's emergence mattered. The colonial authorities had suppressed the communist uprisings and destroyed much of the PKI's capacity for legal activity. The new nationalist organizers shared the communists' rejection of foreign domination but did not define their party according to exclusive commitment to a Marxist revolutionary program. They aimed to construct an Indonesian political movement whose principal objective was independence. This was a strategic as well as an ideological distinction. A message centered on national emancipation could appeal to participants whose religious commitments, occupations and social backgrounds differed. The party could not, however, simply disregard the grievances that had brought people into the labor and Islamic movements or the question of who would lead the new organization.

Noncooperation was a defining component of the PNI strategy. It rejected reliance on colonial representative bodies that lacked the authority to realize national independence. The Volksraad might offer opportunities to publicly criticize colonial policies, but its members could not obtain sovereignty simply by voting for it. The nationalists argued that political capacity had to be developed through organizations accountable to the Indonesian population rather than granted by Dutch officials. Self-reliance accordingly involved the financing and maintenance of independent associations and the cultivation of a political consciousness that would sustain an independence movement. Noncooperation did not mean that every member immediately withdrew from employment in the colonial economy or that the party refused every interaction with the authorities; as a political principle it addressed the source and objectives of collective action.

Institutional methods included branches, courses, public meetings, publications and fundraising. Political education was not an activity reserved for the later PNI Baru; it mattered to the original PNI as well. Organizers sought to explain how taxation, economic extraction and political subordination were connected, thereby moving from individual grievances to a claim that the structure of government should change. Speeches enabled people with limited literacy to participate in such discussions. The ability to collect dues and sustain local activities was important because prominent leaders could visit only a portion of the districts in which the party sought followers. Public mobilization required intermediary organizers who understood local conditions and could maintain an association when a celebrated speaker had departed.

The PNI also tried to build wider cooperation without dissolving existing organizations. In December 1927 representatives of nationalist associations participated in the establishment of the Permufakatan Perhimpunan-Perhimpunan Politik Kebangsaan Indonesia (PPPKI), a federation intended to coordinate political activity. Such efforts drew on the broader emphasis on unity associated with Perhimpunan Indonesia and Sukarno's political writings. Yet the organizations entering the federation possessed their own memberships, political programs and rivalries. Some were willing to cooperate with colonial representative institutions, while others rejected that route; Islamic organizations had commitments that a general nationalist program could not automatically absorb. Federation required decisions about common campaigns, resources and leadership, and agreement about a broad objective did not resolve these problems.

The party's membership policies also reveal limits to the inclusiveness proclaimed in nationalist rhetoric. Its independence-oriented definition of Indonesian membership did not reproduce the Indische Partij's cross-racial approach in every respect; restrictions on full membership for people treated as non-Indonesian exposed tensions between national solidarity and the colonial categories nationalists criticized. A party was not the same thing as the population of the future state, and debates over who should participate in political organization were not equivalent to a settled constitutional definition of citizenship. The PNI's achievement was to establish an influential public organization explicitly committed to Indonesian independence. It remained much smaller than the population it claimed to represent and depended on political conditions that its leaders could only partly control. Police scrutiny and the possibility of prosecution placed the party under a continuing threat, a vulnerability that became unmistakable at the end of 1929.

Historical Vocabulary II

Noncooperation, Self-Reliance, Mass Mobilization, Marhaenism

Noncooperation meant rejecting participation in colonial political institutions as the principal means of attaining nationalist objectives. The refusal to seek authority through the Volksraad did not necessarily mean refusing all contact with institutions operated by the state. It was a strategic and political judgment about the capacities and legitimacy of representative bodies under Dutch sovereignty.

Self-reliance emphasized the building of resources, knowledge and organizations that did not depend on the colonial government's willingness to confer political rights. Its practical manifestations differed between campaigns of economic self-help, independent schooling and party organization. Do not equate these distinct activities mechanically.

Mass mobilization describes the activity of drawing people into political participation through branches, meetings, campaigns, publications and other collective activities. A large crowd may indicate interest or the drawing power of a speaker, but is not a reliable count of party members. Marhaenism was Sukarno's evolving nationalist and social philosophy identifying exploited and impoverished Indonesians, including small owner-producers, as the constituency of emancipation. The concept is larger than the famous story of an individual cultivator and is not identical to Marx's category of the propertyless proletariat.

Apply these terms to the PNI's program. They concern different questions: cooperation addresses political strategy, self-reliance addresses resources, mass mobilization addresses participation, and Marhaenism addresses the social constituency and rationale of the movement. An organization could adopt one of these ideas while differing with the PNI on another.

Sukarno's Arrest, Trial, and Indonesia Menggugat

The arrest of Sukarno and other PNI leaders on 29 December 1929 revealed the extent to which colonial authorities treated nationalist political organization as a potential threat to security. The arrests occurred after the party had established branches, organized public meetings and participated in efforts at interparty cooperation. Officials were particularly sensitive to the possibility that a widely attended movement could become involved in unrest, especially following the experience of the communist uprisings. Nationalists rejected the implication that their demand for political independence amounted in itself to a conspiracy to commit criminal acts. The confrontation therefore raised the fundamental constitutional question underlying colonial political trials: whether the transformation of the colonial regime could legitimately be advocated under the laws of that regime.

Sukarno and his codefendants Gatot Mangkupraja, Maskun Sumadiredja and Supriadinata faced trial in Bandung in 1930. The colonial court, a Landraad, operated within a judicial system that did not place all inhabitants on an equal legal footing. The case raised issues under provisions of the criminal code concerning political incitement and participation in unlawful associations, including the articles cited by Sukarno in his defense. His response was not simply a denial of individual conduct. He sought to make the broader aims of the PNI and the economic and political conditions of the Indies central to the hearing. A proceeding intended to establish the criminal liability of particular persons thereby became an occasion for public discussion of the legitimacy of colonial government itself.

The defense was subsequently published as Indonesia Menggugat (Indonesia Accuses, also translated as Indonesia Indicts). In its opening Sukarno addressed the judges and discussed the public attention attracted by the raids and arrests. He argued that the importance of the case derived from its relationship to a political movement rather than the personal standing of its defendants. His argument about imperialism examined colonial extraction and the political institutions that made it possible. By presenting the pursuit of national emancipation as a response to actual inequalities, he attempted to redirect scrutiny from the supposed danger of nationalist doctrine to the conditions against which the party organized. The courtroom became a site of argument addressed to readers and listeners beyond those who were physically present.

A historian must nevertheless distinguish the defense text from the findings of the court and from an independent account of the party's activities. It is a persuasive document produced in an adversarial setting, and the surviving published text cannot be presumed to reproduce every spoken sentence of the trial exactly. Its analysis of economic exploitation is evidence of Sukarno's political reasoning, not a substitute for economic records. His account of the party's intentions must be compared with its statutes, publications, internal organization and the evidence presented by the prosecution. Conversely, allegations in a police report or the existence of a conviction do not establish that all nationalist activities constituted a credible plan for violence. A colonial trial is evidence about the regime's legal and political priorities as well as about the defendants.

Sukarno received a four-year prison sentence in December 1930. The remaining PNI leadership faced an immediate institutional problem: continuing openly in the existing form exposed members to the possibility of further prosecution, whereas dissolution threatened the loss of organizational continuity. Under Sartono's leadership the original party dissolved in April 1931. The decision was controversial among former members, some of whom considered it an unnecessary surrender of a party that should have survived the arrest of its leader. Partindo and the organization that became Pendidikan Nasional Indonesia (PNI Baru) emerged from the ensuing disagreement. These institutions were not simply successive names for the same party; their political strategies and approaches to organizing differed, as Section 126.17 explains.

Sukarno was released in December 1931, well before serving the full term of the sentence. His reputation had grown in part because the trial and defense were publicly discussed, but the destruction of the original PNI cannot be treated as a victory merely because its most famous leader gained recognition. Branch activists had lost an institutional home, some faced continuing surveillance, and the ability to sustain organized opposition depended on new leadership decisions. The trial thus illustrates a recurrent tension in colonial politics: repression might inadvertently publicize nationalist claims while materially diminishing the capacities of organizations. The political history of the next decade would be shaped by repeated efforts to resolve this tension, including both the reorganization of noncooperating parties and the pursuit of constitutional alternatives.

Documentary identification. Sukarno, Indonesia Menggugat, defense plea before the Bandung Landraad in 1930. The accessible Indonesian transcription reproduces a published witness to the speech. The title has been translated as Indonesia Accuses or Indonesia Indicts. The speech followed the arrests of 29 December 1929 and was written in response to allegations concerning the nationalist party's activities. The institution was a colonial court, not an independent Indonesian tribunal.

Passage-specific paraphrase. In the opening section (Pendahuluan), Sukarno recalls the June 1930 opening speech of the governor-general to the Volksraad announcing continuation of the prosecution. He then looks back to the raids of December 1929 and describes the political interest in the case within the Indies and the Netherlands. He represents that interest as a consequence of the significance of the movement rather than the individual personalities of the defendants. Later in the text he challenges the inference of unlawful intentions from public political activity and cites criminal provisions including Articles 153-bis and 169. These are the provisions invoked in the defense, not an independently reconstructed legal summary of the whole indictment.

Analysis. The sequential movement of the argument is important. Before developing his wider critique of imperialism, Sukarno establishes the political significance of the trial and contests the characterization of the defendants' intentions. The speech reframes the dispute as one involving the legitimacy of colonial rule. The defendant, while speaking within a colonial judicial process, addresses the broader public. His claims about extraction and exploitation should be read beside economic statistics and studies of the colony, not used as numerical evidence by themselves.

Perspective and limitations. This is an adversarial defense, not a transcript of testimony by neutral witnesses. The published material may differ from what was read aloud; the cited transcription is not the official trial record. Claims about the defendants' intended activities require corroboration in party documents, prosecution records and other evidence. The historical importance of the speech does not depend on accepting every factual or legal contention it contains.

Regional Youth Associations and the Formation of a National Generation

Youth associations arose in the Netherlands Indies partly because the expansion of schooling brought students from different districts into shared educational environments. Travel away from a family home could involve adjusting to new languages, expectations and forms of social life. Associations based on regional or cultural affiliation offered assistance in that adjustment. They might organize lectures, provide companionship, arrange cultural events and help members preserve ties to a home region. Such institutions did not have to begin with the objective of Indonesian independence in order to play a significant role in its development. Their members acquired experience in organizing meetings, keeping records, raising money and negotiating collective objectives outside the immediate supervision of family and school.

Tri Koro Darmo was established in Batavia on 7 March 1915 by young students associated with STOVIA, with Satiman Wirjosandjojo among its founders. Its initial orientation emphasized the cultural and social development of young people associated particularly with Java and neighboring cultural communities. At its first congress in June 1918 the association adopted the name Jong Java. The two dates must be distinguished, for the later name and effort to broaden participation were themselves part of the history of the organization. The change did not transform all its members into advocates of a fully developed territorial nationalism. It demonstrated that the associations in which future nationalists acquired organizational skills were themselves subject to debates about constituency and purpose.

Jong Sumatranen Bond, established in 1917, brought together students from Sumatra, many of whom were studying away from their places of origin. Its constituency was itself diverse: Sumatra contained communities with distinct languages, religious institutions and historical experiences. The association provided opportunities for discussing regional development across those differences. Young people associated with Minangkabau society, for instance, could meet others whose experiences of the colonial economy and education differed from their own. Youth associations linked to Sulawesi, Ambon, Batak communities, Sunda and Batavia likewise emerged. Their proliferation did not represent an obstacle that had first to disappear for Indonesian nationalism to become possible. The existing associations provided much of the organizational basis for interregional congresses.

Religious affiliation could cut across regional distinctions. Jong Islamieten Bond, established in 1925 after disagreements over religious education within youth organization, offered an environment in which students could relate Islamic commitments to contemporary intellectual and political questions. Its membership and aims overlapped with, but were not identical to, those of other youth associations. The question of the relationship between religious identity and national affiliation was therefore present even before an explicit interregional pledge. Young people could seek unity without consenting to the disappearance of distinctive religious institutions. Scouts, cultural clubs and educational societies also offered forms of organized participation in which physical training, discipline and service could be connected to the idea of a larger political community.

The formation of Perhimpunan Pelajar-Pelajar Indonesia (PPPI), the Indonesian Students' Association, in 1926 was an important example of an explicitly cross-regional orientation. Its members sought to strengthen Indonesian solidarity and provided organizational initiative for the Second Youth Congress of 1928. Such work required practical coordination among organizations with separate officers, meeting schedules and priorities. A conference did not occur merely because a generation had arrived at a common consciousness. Organizers had to agree on venues, a program, finances and the roles of speakers and delegates. The existence of newspapers and well-connected student residences in Batavia made that work possible, while the expense of long-distance travel and the uneven distribution of secondary schooling restricted who could participate.

Youth associations should not be mistaken for representative assemblies of all young people in the colony. Members predominantly came from the relatively small population with access to formal education and the resources required for urban associational life. Young people working on farms and in plantations, workshops or households could have quite different opportunities. Women participated in educational and youth associations, but leadership and access to schools remained unequal. The associations mattered because they cultivated durable relationships and practices of cooperation among the people able to join them, and because their publications and activities could have wider influence. The development of an Indonesian identity did not erase the cultural traditions that regional organizations sustained. Rather, the interregional cooperation examined in the next section made it possible to propose a national affiliation that could coexist with existing identities.

Continuity and Change II

Regional Youth Identities and Indonesian National Identity

Regional youth organization and national solidarity were not successive stages in which the first had to disappear before the second became possible. Tri Koro Darmo's establishment in 1915 and its adoption of the Jong Java name in 1918 show an effort to broaden participation while maintaining a strong cultural orientation. Jong Sumatranen Bond offered opportunities for people identifying with the many communities of Sumatra to cooperate, and religiously defined groups created affiliations crossing regional boundaries.

What changed was the capacity to coordinate among associations, formulate a common political vocabulary and propose an Indonesian national affiliation. What persisted were distinctive languages, religious commitments, home-region relationships and cultural interests. The practical work of convening congresses relied on officers and networks already developed within particular associations. The Second Youth Congress could therefore be a national event without having been convened by a single organization claiming to represent all young Indonesians.

The museum account of Jong Java documents debates about regional inclusion even after the change of name in 1918. The subsequent study of the Youth Pledge shows that the language provision was designed to facilitate unity without requiring the disappearance of other languages. The degree to which these changes reached young workers and rural communities must be investigated independently of the history of urban student organizations.

The Youth Congresses and the Youth Pledge of 1928

The First Indonesian Youth Congress, held in Batavia from 30 April to 2 May 1926, brought together representatives of organizations that had developed largely along regional and cultural lines. The agenda included the future of cooperation among these bodies and questions concerning language, education and a broader sense of belonging. Delegates did not reach a definitive agreement on the form of a unified youth movement. The congress nevertheless created relationships and an institutional precedent that allowed organizers to pursue the question of unity more systematically. Later accounts that concentrate only on the successful resolution of 1928 can obscure the significance of these earlier negotiations and the uncertainties that participants confronted.

The PPPI took a major organizational role in preparing the Second Youth Congress, which met in Batavia on 27 and 28 October 1928. The congress was chaired by Soegondo Djojopoespito, and Mohammad Yamin was among its principal figures and helped formulate the resolution. The gatherings took place in three venues, including an association hall at what is now Kramat 106, the site of the Youth Pledge Museum. The choice of several meeting places reflected the existing network of clubs and institutions that supported student activity. The program included discussion of national unity, education and scouting, and involved speakers from a range of youth organizations. The presence of multiple organizations was itself important evidence of the extension of political relationships across regional and religious affiliations.

The resolution subsequently known as the Sumpah Pemuda (Youth Pledge) was adopted on the final day. Its three declarations affirmed a common homeland, a common nation and the upholding of Indonesian as a language of unity. The third provision is particularly important because popular retellings often assimilate it to the grammatical form of the first two. The congress did not declare that Indonesian was the only language spoken by the population or that regional languages should cease to be used. It committed the participants to upholding a common language that could serve communication across regions. The use of a form of Malay as a language of public organization had precedents in the press and political associations; the term Bahasa Indonesia asserted a different political meaning for this shared medium.

The choice of language helped address a problem of equality among prospective members of the nation. Javanese was spoken by a large part of the colonial population, yet adopting a major regional language as the exclusive expression of Indonesian identity could be seen as privileging one community. Malay was already used in interregional communication and was not the home language of most Javanese. Its selection was therefore practical and politically consequential. It did not resolve questions about the resources needed to expand literacy, the standardization of vocabulary or the role of Dutch and other languages in professional education. Much work remained before a pledge made by a group of educated young people could become a broadly shared social practice.

The complete congress decision also identified common will, historical experience, language, customary law, education and scouting as bases upon which unity could be strengthened. It expressed a wish that the decision be published in newspapers and read at meetings of associations. These provisions show that the resolution was intended to guide organizational activity, not simply commemorate a moment of agreement. Participants recognized that unity had to be communicated and sustained through practical institutions. Before the congress closed, Wage Rudolf Supratman performed his composition Indonesia Raya on the violin. The musical event, alongside the resolution, illustrated the developing importance of cultural expression in making a wider national identity intelligible and emotionally meaningful.

The Youth Pledge did not establish a sovereign state, create a single nationalist organization or demonstrate the assent of the entire colonial population. Delegates were drawn from associations, and the degree of participation differed across districts, educational backgrounds and genders. Nevertheless, the resolution articulated a claim that later organizations could adopt and circulate without requiring every participant to belong to one party. In subsequent years youth organizations pursued further forms of coordination, including the formation of Indonesia Muda around the turn of 1930–1931. Their experience confirms that the pledge was part of a longer process of organizational development. Its enduring historical significance lies in the explicit articulation of an inclusive territorial and linguistic framework for political solidarity, alongside the continued diversity of the people for whom that framework was intended.

Women and the Indonesian National Movement

Women's organization in the Netherlands Indies developed in relation to the same transformations in schooling, communication and associational life that shaped other political movements. Yet the problems women confronted could not be reduced to the colonial status of the nation as a whole. Access to education, control of family resources, the age of marriage, divorce, polygamy and the recognition of women's work depended on colonial law, local custom, religious authority and unequal relations within households. Political independence promised a possible transformation of those conditions, but it did not in itself determine the distribution of power within a family or community. Women's associations consequently pursued objectives that intersected with nationalist activities while maintaining a distinct institutional and intellectual history.

Putri Mardika, founded in Batavia in 1912 with connections to the Budi Utomo milieu, promoted educational opportunity and provided support for girls and women seeking schooling. Other organizations emerged from religious and regional settings. Aisyiyah, established in 1917 within the Muhammadiyah environment, provided opportunities for women to organize educational, devotional and welfare activities. These activities are discussed in Chapter 125; here their significance includes the formation of leadership, the ability to maintain associations and the establishment of networks that could contribute to wider political cooperation. Independent educational initiatives, including those associated with Dewi Sartika and other women educators, similarly supplied precedents for the efforts to give girls greater control over their intellectual development. The forms of agency exercised by women were diverse, ranging from managing schools and publications to participating directly in political parties.

The First Indonesian Women's Congress met in Yogyakarta on 22–25 December 1928, less than two months after the Second Youth Congress. Among the organizers were R.A. Sukonto of Wanita Utomo, who chaired the meeting, Suyatin Kartowiyono and Nyi Hajar Dewantara. They brought together associations whose members differed in religious affiliation and conceptions of reform. The congress's agenda provides evidence that participants were concerned with practical questions of social and political power. Its recorded addresses discussed marriage and divorce, child marriage, education, the social position of women and the aspirations of the Indonesian women's movement. Siti Mundjiah of Aisyiyah was among the speakers. R.A. Sukonto's opening address and the other congress records offer historical evidence of the concerns that organizers themselves considered important, rather than relying on descriptions of women's activities by male political leaders.

The meeting led to the establishment of a federation, Perikatan Perkumpulan Perempuan Indonesia (also rendered Perserikatan in historical accounts; PPPI; its initials should not be confused with the students' association). Federation enabled participating organizations to cooperate without requiring that they abandon their particular religious or institutional commitments. The difficulty of reaching agreement on marriage reform was a significant test of this approach. Women who shared the goal of improved education could disagree over how marital practices should be changed, what role religious authorities should have and what legislation ought to accomplish. The ability to negotiate a common agenda was itself an exercise of political judgment. In the following decade women's congresses and associations continued to debate social legislation, welfare and their relationship to the independence movement.

Other women were active directly in confrontational nationalist politics. Rasuna Said, from West Sumatra, developed as a speaker and educator within Muslim reformist and nationalist circles, participated in Permi and faced colonial prosecution for her political speech in the early 1930s. Her career demonstrates that women could exercise political leadership outside the women's federation and that colonial authorities could treat their activism as a security concern. S.K. Trimurti, associated with radical nationalism, likewise used journalism and political education as instruments of participation. Women's ability to travel, publish and speak publicly depended on unequal access to education and family resources, and these prominent activists should not be presumed to represent the experience of all women in the colony.

The broader relationship between feminism, social reform and nationalism remained complex. Some nationalists welcomed women's participation chiefly because it could strengthen the common struggle or form a patriotic generation; women's organizations often insisted that questions affecting their own lives required attention in their own right. Religious women organizers did not necessarily regard religious commitments and emancipation as opposites, and participants in nonreligious associations did not all adopt the same position on marriage or family law. Susan Blackburn's studies of women and the state encourage attention to the independent organizational history of these movements. The national significance of the 1928 congress and subsequent activities lies not in the absorption of women into male-led parties, but in their development of institutions capable of acting, negotiating and formulating claims of their own.

Multiple Perspectives II

Women's Emancipation, Social Reform, and Nationalism

Women organizers could value national independence while identifying child marriage, educational exclusion, divorce and unequal family authority as problems demanding immediate attention. The addresses recorded at the 1928 Women's Congress show the diversity of subjects that women themselves brought to the national public sphere. Some issues required negotiation among organizations with different religious commitments and interpretations of custom.

Male nationalist leaders varied in their attitudes toward women's organization. Some provided support or expressed similar social reform objectives; others prioritized independence and expected women's organizations to support the larger movement. These positions did not necessarily settle questions about women's ability to govern their own associations. The objectives of an independent women's federation cannot be inferred solely from the program of a party with which some of its participants were affiliated.

Religious authorities and reformers were not uniformly opposed to women's political agency. Aisyiyah exemplifies the development of educational and welfare activity from within a religious environment. The issues of marriage and divorce nevertheless raised questions of doctrinal interpretation and the relationship between religious jurisdiction and legislation. Colonial officials could treat some welfare institutions as less directly threatening than noncooperating parties, but the prosecution of activists such as Rasuna Said demonstrates that women were not exempt from repression.

National Language, Symbols, Culture, and Political Communication

The movement for Indonesian independence required means of communicating with people who would never attend a party congress or read an extended political treatise. Its symbols, names, vocabulary and cultural practices were part of the practical process of building a national constituency. A person might first encounter the idea of Indonesia through a newspaper article, a school discussion, a speech or a song rather than through a statement of party doctrine. Repeated references to a common homeland associated people in different parts of the archipelago with a political project that reached beyond the locality. These cultural forms could circulate across boundaries that limited the membership of any single organization. Their effect nevertheless depended upon the local relationships that allowed them to be heard, read and interpreted.

The development of Bahasa Indonesia illustrates the relationship between inherited communication networks and new political objectives. Malay had long been used for trade and interregional correspondence and was employed in vernacular newspapers, religious publications and administrative activities. Its vocabulary and written forms varied according to audience and educational background. Nationalists who called it Indonesian changed the political implications of its use without creating a language from nothing. They could communicate with communities beyond Java without requiring all participants to learn Javanese or Dutch. The expansion of a national language required writers and readers to make choices about vocabulary, orthography and the expression of political and scientific concepts. It also required people with different linguistic abilities to participate in ways that did not always depend on formal schooling.

The press offered a particularly important environment for this work. Nationalist associations issued newspapers and periodicals that reported meetings, explained political ideas, advertised events and connected local experiences to a wider struggle. Publications could be read individually, passed from one reader to another or discussed aloud in a group. Circulation figures thus measure the distribution of copies, not necessarily the number of listeners or people who agreed with an editor. Newspaper production required capital, printing facilities, editors, correspondents and distribution networks. An organization able to afford a regular publication possessed a means of communicating consistently with members, whereas an association without such resources depended more heavily on personal travel and face-to-face contact. When authorities confiscated publications or banned periodicals, they disrupted these organizational relationships as well as the publication of particular ideas.

Music and visual symbols supplied different forms of communication. Wage Rudolf Supratman's Indonesia Raya, performed at the Youth Congress of 1928, offered a common anthem that could be recognized by people from many organizations. Its publication, circulation and performance made it a political symbol before it possessed the legal status of the anthem of an independent state. The red-and-white flag likewise came to symbolize the country claimed by nationalists, rather than a colonial administrative unit. Colonial restrictions on the display of nationalist symbols and performance of songs reflected official awareness of their power to express collective affiliation independently of an extended speech. A flag or melody could bind people to a shared cause, but its meanings could vary among those who used it. Displaying a symbol did not necessarily resolve the political disagreements between its bearers.

The development of literature also contributed to the expanding public discussion of Indonesian identity. State publishing through Balai Pustaka and independent journals made novels, essays and translated works available to new readers. The cultural journal Pujangga Baru, founded in 1933, provided a venue for discussions about literary modernization and the direction of Indonesian culture. Intellectuals disagreed over the relationship between inherited regional traditions and cultural forms associated with Europe or other parts of Asia. Such disputes should not be understood simply as opposition between people who supported tradition and people who wanted modernity. They concerned the kinds of education, social relationships and creative expression appropriate to an Indonesian future. Political independence was one important element of these debates, but writers also addressed the family, individual aspiration, social status and moral responsibility.

Everyday political communication was shaped by material constraints. People with little leisure, limited income or restricted access to education could not necessarily participate in the same way as urban intellectuals. Local officials monitored meetings, and workers risked employment consequences for public political involvement. Women could face additional restrictions on travel and public speech. The power of shared language and symbols lay partly in enabling participation beyond the formal meetings of organizations, but these forms of participation should not be exaggerated into universal support. They contributed to a national public that was increasingly recognizable by its language, publications, cultural references and claims of solidarity. This cultural achievement could endure even when the political organization responsible for a particular publication, meeting or song had been weakened or dissolved.

Everyday Life and Social History

Newspapers, Songs, Meetings, and Cultural Participation

Consider the life of a periodical rather than only its content. An editor had to obtain funds, a printer and paper, and arrange distribution. An urban subscriber might take a newspaper to a shop, a school, a workplace or a residence where other people encountered its contents. The ANU Press study by Lin Hongxuan discusses the importance of reading aloud and circulation beyond an individual reader. A periodical could therefore be socially influential without reaching every household or registering a particularly large print run. Political restrictions or financial failure could interrupt the chain at any stage.

A public meeting involved similar practical concerns. Organizers needed premises, speakers and means of advertising the event. Participants could listen to arguments, encounter a song or recognize a flag without becoming formal party members. The police might monitor the event, changing how speakers addressed their audience. The effects of public activity were shaped by the willingness of people to risk official attention and by their available time and financial resources.

These are analytically reconstructed examples of possible participation, not a diary of a named individual. The surviving materials needed to examine an actual occasion include event advertisements, newspaper reports, association accounts, police correspondence and memoirs. A newspaper article that describes the response to a speech may exaggerate its popularity; a police report may overstate its danger. Cultural participation must be studied through such evidence, alongside materials such as literary journals, musical publications and the records of the youth associations.

Division V — Dutch Repression, Political Reorganization, and the Road to 1942 · Section 126.17

Partindo, PNI Baru, Hatta, and Sjahrir

The dissolution of the original PNI in 1931 created divisions among activists who agreed on the importance of Indonesian independence but disagreed about how a movement could survive colonial repression. Sartono and other former leaders formed Partai Indonesia (Partindo) in 1931. Critics of the decision to dissolve the PNI wanted to build an organization less dependent on individual leaders and the immediate possibility of public agitation. Groups associated with these critics developed into Pendidikan Nasional Indonesia, commonly known as PNI Baru or the New PNI, also in 1931. The shared use of the initials PNI can obscure the differences between the original organization and the new educationally oriented movement. They constituted distinct institutional projects, not simply a continuation under a new name.

Partindo sought to rebuild a broad public movement and to communicate nationalist demands through visible forms of mobilization. After his release from prison in December 1931, Sukarno initially sought to overcome the division. On 1 August 1932 he joined Partindo and became a prominent public organizer. The distinction between the date of his release and the date of his affiliation matters because the period of uncertainty reflected genuine disagreements about the course the movement should take. Sukarno's capacity to draw audiences gave Partindo considerable advantages in public recruitment. Its activities sought to maintain contact with workers, small producers and other people whose aspirations could be expressed through the language of Marhaenism. Like the original PNI, the organization also relied on local leadership and political education, although its public style became especially associated with mass agitation.

Hatta returned from the Netherlands in August 1932. Together with Sutan Sjahrir, who had returned earlier, he became closely associated with Pendidikan Nasional Indonesia. Their emphasis was on educating members to develop an understanding of the political and economic structures they confronted. A cadre was conceived not simply as a member who obeyed orders but as a participant capable of sustained political judgment and organizational work. Printed materials, discussion groups and systematic instruction could create organizers able to maintain activity even when a prominent speaker was removed. Hatta and Sjahrir were concerned that mobilization dependent on charismatic leadership could leave followers vulnerable to the arrest of a small number of individuals. Their approach did not deny the importance of eventual mass participation; rather, it gave a different priority to the preparation of the people who would organize it.

The differing organizational strategies affected the size and composition of the parties. Lin Hongxuan’s study published by ANU Press, drawing on earlier research, presents reported membership in August 1933 of about 20,000 registered Partindo members in Java, roughly 1,000 Pendidikan members in twelve Javanese branches, and about 250 Pendidikan members in West Sumatra. It also reports approximately 10,000 members of Permi in Sumatra. These figures reflect the claims and geographic scope of the sources, not an independently audited census of nationalist support. Nevertheless, they illustrate that a movement with a relatively small body of trained members and a party oriented toward broad recruitment could differ greatly in numerical scale. The smaller organization also sought contact with agrarian laborers, especially in areas of the sugar economy. An emphasis on cadres should not be confused with an intention to confine politics permanently to educated urban elites.

Permi (Persatuan Muslim Indonesia), which developed from the West Sumatran educational and Islamic reform movement around 1930, offered another strategic and ideological possibility. Its combination of Islam and Indonesian nationalism attracted activists who did not regard religious commitment and independence-oriented politics as incompatible. Its growth and the subsequent prosecution or detention of its leading figures reveal that repression was not confined to secular nationalist parties in Java. Thus the argument about mass mobilization and cadre education unfolded within a wider political landscape of Muslim, regional and other nationalist networks. Neither Partindo nor the Pendidikan possessed exclusive authority over the independence movement.

The crackdown of 1933–1934 exposed the vulnerabilities of both approaches. Sukarno's removal deprived Partindo of its most prominent public leader, while the detention of Hatta and Sjahrir damaged the leadership and continuity of the Pendidikan. Repression could reach organizers regardless of whether they emphasized public meetings or sustained educational activity. Yet the strategic disagreements remained consequential. They concerned whether political movements should develop broad immediate participation, emphasize institutional resilience, or attempt to combine the two. The colonial setting made those decisions difficult because the very resources that increased an organization's visibility and reach also made its activities more readily identifiable by the police. The two parties' histories demonstrate that no organizational strategy could overcome colonial power automatically; nationalist organizers had to evaluate the benefits and risks of maintaining different forms of public opposition.

Historical Debate II

Mass Mobilization versus Cadre-Based Education

A movement centered on mass mobilization could make the claim to independence widely visible, motivate people to attend meetings and recruit a large membership. The reported growth of Partindo on Java after Sukarno's entry illustrates the attractions of this approach. Its effectiveness depended on experienced organizers, the ability to publish and meet, and leadership capable of addressing different audiences. Repression of popular leaders exposed its vulnerabilities.

An organization emphasizing cadre education might invest more resources in the knowledge and independence of individual members, preparing them for sustained political action. Hatta and Sjahrir's Pendidikan placed a special emphasis on that preparation. Smaller membership could be consistent with its strategy, but did not guarantee resilience. A limited membership could constrain recruitment and communication, and the authorities could target educationally oriented activists as well as mass speakers. Its efforts in agrarian districts also complicate a simple contrast between popular politics and an intellectual elite.

Colonial Surveillance, Political Exile, and the Limits of Opposition

The Dutch colonial government possessed a range of instruments for controlling political activity, and their differences matter. Criminal prosecution required bringing accusations under the law before a court, although proceedings operated within a colonial legal system. Administrative exclusion or internment permitted the executive to remove an individual considered dangerous without a conventional finding of criminal guilt. Press restrictions and controls on meetings could disrupt political communication even when the authorities did not prosecute the leaders of an organization. Intelligence services gathered information that helped officials choose which individuals and associations to monitor. These measures operated together, but a person sentenced to prison after a trial experienced a different legal process from one transported into administrative exile.

The extraordinary powers of the governor-general had a long history in the colonial constitutional framework. The affair of the Committee of Indigenous Sons in 1913 showed their use against politically outspoken intellectuals. The mass repression following the communist uprisings in 1926–1927 established Boven-Digoel as a major site of administrative internment. During the 1930s these powers were used against prominent nationalist figures whose public work did not necessarily involve armed rebellion. The government could characterize people as threats to public order and separate them from the places in which their political activities were effective. As the distinction between administrative and criminal processes indicates, the purpose was preventive control as well as punishment for conduct already established in court.

Surveillance was not confined to formal decisions taken in Batavia. Officials received reports on local meetings, speeches, publications and relationships among suspected activists. Police might be present at public gatherings, and the authorities could impose restrictions on assembly or intervene when they regarded a speech as unlawful. The Political Intelligence Service and the broader system of colonial police and local administrative reporting created a record of nationalist activity that remains important to historians. Such records were produced to serve official purposes. An intelligence report can preserve useful information about a meeting or branch, but its classification of an association as subversive cannot simply be accepted as an impartial judgment. Agents also possessed uneven knowledge of local languages and political relations. Informants might report selectively, and authorities could mistake a popular grievance for proof of centralized revolutionary direction.

The press was subject to criminal provisions and, under the press-ban ordinance of 1931, administrative restrictions on publications considered threats to public order (ordinance of 7 September 1931, Staatsblad 1931, No. 394). For a political association, loss of a publication could mean a loss of revenue, communication with members and opportunities to respond to accusations. Restrictions on meetings increased reliance on private contacts, letters and smaller study groups. These changes did not affect leaders alone. Local activists might lose their employment, encounter greater scrutiny from officials or find that an organization no longer provided financial and legal support. Families bore the costs of travel, interrupted earnings and separation. Women connected with the movement sometimes assumed responsibilities previously exercised by detained men, while their own public activity could also bring them under police attention.

Sukarno was arrested again in late July 1933 and sent into administrative exile in Ende, on Flores, in 1934. He was subsequently transferred to Bengkulu in 1938. His removal from Java restricted direct participation in the urban meetings and party networks that had sustained his political influence. During exile he continued to read, write, correspond where possible and engage with local social and religious life. Hatta and Sjahrir were arrested in February 1934 and transported to Boven-Digoel; in 1936 they were transferred to Banda Neira in the Banda Islands. Their experience likewise combined deprivation of freedom with limited possibilities for teaching and intellectual activity. The places of exile differed in living conditions and the extent of permissible contact. The ability of well-known detainees to continue some intellectual work should not obscure the severity of coercion or the far less documented experiences of ordinary internees.

By making it difficult to maintain open noncooperating organizations, repression altered the political environment of the late 1930s. Some activists shifted into legal associations or cooperative political work; others continued in less visible networks or withdrew from public activities. Exile also generated political symbols. Supporters could understand a detained leader's treatment as evidence of the injustice of colonial rule. Symbolic influence, however, could not replace all the practical capacities of a party whose organizers had been dispersed. The resulting situation helps explain why constitutional nationalists could become more prominent without eliminating the convictions of people who opposed cooperation. Dutch security policy was able to narrow the range of public political action; it did not resolve the underlying question of political legitimacy.

Multiple Perspectives III

Security Authorities, Political Prisoners, and Nationalist Communities

An official might describe administrative exile as a preventive measure intended to protect public order. In the colonial framework, the government claimed powers to intervene against people whose activity was considered dangerous without waiting for a conventional criminal conviction. The policy also reduced opportunities for open trials to provide platforms for nationalist arguments. The official rationale must be compared with the laws invoked and the evidence concerning particular people, rather than accepted as proof of guilt.

For detainees and their families, the same policy meant interrupted livelihoods, geographical separation, restricted movement and uncertainty about the duration of confinement. Letters and memoirs can reveal the practical difficulties that official reports omit. The capacity of a prominent internee to write or teach while detained does not eliminate the coercion imposed on that person. The circumstances of people transported to Boven-Digoel after the uprisings were not identical to those of every later nationalist transferred to a different location.

Other organizers experienced exile indirectly. They might lose access to speakers, editors or officials who had coordinated local networks. They could respond by continuing private contact, pursuing legal association work, developing new leaders or withdrawing from public activity. The removal of prominent individuals could also reinforce the public reputation of the detained, creating a political symbol that persisted after a party's organization weakened. Symbolic importance and actual operational capacity should be evaluated separately.

Constitutional Nationalism, the Volksraad, and GAPI

Constitutional nationalism became an especially prominent field of legal political activity during the latter part of the 1930s. The Volksraad, opened in 1918, provided elected and appointed representatives opportunities to debate colonial policy, but it was not a parliament exercising sovereign authority. Its powers changed over time, including an enlarged legislative role from the late 1920s, and its composition included representatives of legally classified population groups. The colonial executive retained decisive authority within the larger imperial constitutional system. Participation in the council therefore posed a strategic question. Activists who rejected the legitimacy of colonial government might refuse to join it. Others believed that the council could be used to articulate Indonesian demands publicly, demonstrate support for changes and challenge the government through the institutional channels that remained available.

The establishment of Partai Indonesia Raya (Parindra) in 1935 through a merger of Budi Utomo and Persatuan Bangsa Indonesia (PBI) reflected the development of an important cooperative current. Sutomo's work in Surabaya and the activities of PBI had linked political organization with projects of education, welfare and economic improvement. Parindra pursued national advancement through practical institutions as well as representative politics. Its willingness to operate in legal channels did not necessarily imply acceptance of permanent Dutch authority. The party's leaders had to assess the compromises and limitations involved in dealing with colonial officials. In the Volksraad, Mohammad Husni Thamrin became a prominent advocate for Indonesian interests, using debates to publicize issues that the authorities might otherwise have been able to contain within administrative decision-making.

The Soetardjo Petition of 15 July 1936 was a major constitutional initiative. Soetardjo Kartohadikoesoemo and five other council members proposed a conference between representatives of the Netherlands and the Indies on an equal basis to consider a gradual path toward autonomy within the constitutional relationship with the Dutch kingdom. The envisaged transition extended over approximately a decade; it did not establish a demand for immediate independent sovereignty. Among the signatories were Sam Ratulangi from Minahasa, the Catholic politician I.J. Kasimo, Datuk Tumenggung, Ko Kwat Tiong and Alatas. Their participation demonstrated that support for constitutional change could cross the communal categories and political associations represented in the council. The signatories nevertheless did not represent every nationalist organization, and some independence-oriented activists considered the proposal insufficient.

The petition provoked extensive debate. The Volksraad approved it by 26 votes to 20 in September 1936, and on 1 October it was sent to the metropolitan authorities. A favorable vote in the council did not make the proposal binding on the government. The rejection of the petition in November 1938 demonstrated how limited the institutional path to substantial constitutional change remained. It also presented a difficult problem for advocates of cooperation: if even a proposal for discussion of a gradual transition was unacceptable, what could legal intervention actually achieve? Some nationalists viewed the rejection as evidence that the colonial regime would not voluntarily concede meaningful self-government. Others regarded it as a reason to coordinate a stronger public campaign rather than abandon all use of constitutional institutions.

Gerakan Rakyat Indonesia (Gerindo), established in 1937, represented a current in which socialist and nationalist commitments could coexist with a willingness to pursue legal cooperation. Its leaders were especially concerned about the international growth of fascism and the threat of Japanese expansion. They recognized that the Netherlands and its colony might become involved in a wider war. The possibility of cooperation against fascism did not extinguish demands for political change; instead it raised questions about the relationship between the defense of the territory and the rights of its population. Parindra and Gerindo pursued distinct programs and had different political constituencies, but both participated in the broader search for coordination during the final years of Dutch rule.

On 21 May 1939 nationalist parties established Gabungan Politik Indonesia (GAPI), a federation that pursued the demand for an Indonesian parliament. Its leadership included Thamrin, Amir Sjarifuddin and Abikusno Tjokrosujoso, bridging Parindra, Gerindo and Islamic political currents, illustrating that a common constitutional campaign could bring together organizations with otherwise divergent priorities. The Indonesia Berparlemen campaign argued for representative political institutions rather than an isolated concession to an existing advisory body. The outbreak of war in Europe and German occupation of the Netherlands in 1940 gave renewed urgency to the question of political authority and defense. The colonial government appointed the Visman Commission to investigate political aspirations, but this did not establish the parliamentary system sought by GAPI. The widening war therefore confronted nationalists with a colonial order that required political support while remaining unwilling to concede the institutional transformation they sought. Constitutional nationalism had produced a broad field of public discussion and organization, but the ultimate distribution of sovereign power remained unresolved.

Historical Vocabulary III

Constitutional Nationalism, Cooperation, Autonomy, Sovereignty

Constitutional nationalism refers here to efforts to advance national political objectives through legal institutions, proposals and public campaigns for a changed constitutional relationship. It does not imply that the advocates accepted permanent colonial sovereignty. Cooperation was the strategic choice to participate in existing institutions, such as the Volksraad, even though these institutions were limited by Dutch authority. Some groups moved between cooperative and noncooperative positions in response to changing conditions.

Autonomy describes a degree of self-government within a wider political or constitutional relationship. In the Soetardjo Petition, the proposal for a conference and a gradual transition belonged to this framework. The proposal differed from an immediate demand for a fully independent state. Sovereignty denotes ultimate political authority, including the capacity of a political community to determine its constitutional order without subordination to another sovereign. Actual governmental authority can be constrained even where formal sovereignty is claimed.

GAPI's campaign for an Indonesian parliament focused on representative institutions and altered the scale of public demands during a period of growing international danger. The political consequences of its campaign cannot be reduced to whether the Dutch accepted a particular petition. A parliamentary demand could strengthen cooperation among parties, make constitutional questions salient and expose the limitations of the system, even if officials withheld the desired reform.

Competing Visions of Indonesia on the Eve of Japanese Occupation

On the eve of the Japanese invasion, Indonesian nationalism was recognizable as a broad field of political activity, even though it lacked a single institution capable of speaking authoritatively for the entire population. Since the early associations of the twentieth century, activists had developed a shared language of national emancipation, a body of political literature, methods of public communication and relationships across regions and social groups. Different organizations had created traditions of political leadership, mass participation, education and constitutional advocacy. The idea of Indonesia had acquired an institutional reality far greater than that of the early geographical name, but the existence of a national movement did not itself establish a sovereign nation-state. Its objectives and means remained contested.

Islamic political organizations drew on religious solidarity, schools and community institutions capable of operating in many areas in which the membership of explicitly nationalist parties was limited. Their leaders disagreed among themselves over doctrine, the relationship of religion to government, and the usefulness of particular political strategies. The establishment of the Majelis Islam A'la Indonesia (MIAI) in 1937 illustrated efforts at cooperation among Islamic organizations without establishing a single direction for all Muslim political activity. The history of Sarekat Islam, Muhammadiyah, Nahdlatul Ulama and Permi also demonstrates that the boundaries between religious association, social reform and nationalist politics were variable. They could cooperate on particular demands without agreeing on the future place of religion in a national constitutional order.

The communist tradition had suffered severe repression after 1926–1927. Open organization under its earlier party structure was drastically constrained, and activists were imprisoned, interned or driven into exile. Yet earlier socialist and communist activism had influenced the vocabulary of exploitation, economic inequality and international solidarity used more widely in nationalist politics. Separate currents survived through networks at home and abroad, including the Partai Republik Indonesia (PARI), founded abroad in 1927 by Tan Malaka and associates outside the official party structure, and later clandestine activity. These were not evidence of a restored mass communist party enjoying the freedom it had possessed earlier in the century. They show that ideas, personal relationships and political disagreements could outlast the institutions that once brought their adherents together.

The noncooperating nationalists had likewise experienced major defeats. The original PNI had dissolved, and the Partindo and Pendidikan experiments had suffered the removal of leaders and severe official restrictions. Sukarno, Hatta and Sjahrir nevertheless represented different approaches to the national movement that continued to have political significance. Their ideas would matter in a later historical environment, but their subsequent roles cannot establish that any particular strategy was destined to succeed in the 1930s. Constitutional nationalists had maintained a more visible field of legal activity, extending from the Volksraad to the GAPI federation. Their inability to obtain the reforms they sought did not make their organizational work irrelevant. Efforts to define representative government and formulate public demands helped establish questions that a future political order would have to address.

Nationalism also developed through forms of participation that cannot be assigned neatly to these party traditions. Youth associations worked across regions; women developed federations and independent agendas; teachers and journalists created public vocabularies; and artistic expression gave national claims meanings that could circulate beyond formal political institutions. The construction of a common language was important partly because it offered a way for activists to communicate across the different cultural environments in which they worked. Yet unequal access to education, the distance between political centers and rural communities, and disputes concerning racial and religious membership remained unresolved. A national movement is not evidence of unanimous national consciousness. Its claims must be evaluated alongside the institutions and social relationships that made them possible and the populations whose voices are least visible in surviving evidence.

In December 1941 the widening Pacific War dramatically transformed the international environment of the Netherlands Indies. In early 1942 Japanese forces invaded, and Dutch colonial military authority collapsed in March. This outcome depended on international military developments, not merely on the strength of Indonesian nationalism. Nor did the defeat of the Dutch establish Indonesian independence. The Japanese occupation introduced a new imperial regime with its own objectives, institutions and forms of coercion. Chapter 138 will explain how occupation altered the opportunities and constraints faced by Indonesian political actors. At the endpoint of the present chapter, the future remained uncertain. Nationalists had established a credible claim to sovereignty and developed organizational and intellectual resources with which to pursue it. They had not overcome all the conflicts concerning representation, religion, economic power and strategy that would confront any future government. The historical significance of their prewar struggles lies in these contingent achievements and unresolved problems, rather than in an imagined inevitability of the eventual declaration of independence.

Historical Debate III

Unity, Ideological Competition, and the Making of Indonesia

One interpretation stresses ideological fragmentation. Rival parties competed for members, disagreed about the authority of central leaders and adopted conflicting strategies. The split in Sarekat Islam weakened its ability to maintain a single mass constituency, and the conflicts within the communist movement preceded the failed uprisings. After 1931 the divergence between Partindo and the Pendidikan similarly complicated efforts to sustain a united noncooperating movement. From this perspective, the difficulty of achieving coordination under repression was a major source of weakness.

Another interpretation emphasizes that diversity supplied the movement with varied organizational capacities. Islamic schools and welfare institutions, labor unions, student associations, independent women's federations, parties, newspapers and constitutional campaigns reached different constituencies. Political thinkers drew selectively on religious, socialist, nationalist and other ideas. Lin's analysis of overlapping intellectual traditions challenges the assumption that each ideological current constituted a self-contained stream. This interpretation does not deny that disputes caused real damage; it asks whether the movement could have achieved comparable reach through one organizational model alone.

A third consideration is the power of the colonial state. Both rivalry and cooperation occurred in conditions in which colonial authorities could restrict organization, detain leaders and refuse proposed reforms. The collapse of Dutch authority in 1942 resulted from a wider war; it cannot be treated as a straightforward test of the relative effectiveness of prewar nationalist strategies. The divergent movements supplied political resources for later developments without determining those developments in advance.

Chapter Summary

At the beginning of the twentieth century the Netherlands Indies was a colonial jurisdiction, not a sovereign nation. Its population was diverse in language, religion, region and social position, and was governed through unequal legal and administrative arrangements. Political organizers faced the challenge of articulating national belonging across distinctions that mattered in the lives of the people they sought to mobilize. Older regional networks and the Malay language supplied resources for this work, while colonial conquest, education and communications transformed the relationships among communities.

The Ethical Policy expanded some educational opportunities without establishing equal political and social rights. Students and professionals used their educational experiences to develop new associations, as demonstrated by the foundation of Budi Utomo in 1908. Its early cultural and educational orientation was narrower than the later concept of an Indonesian independence movement. The commemoration of this association as a landmark in national awakening recognizes its importance while requiring care about its original purposes.

Sarekat Islam developed from local mutual-aid and commercial relations in Surakarta into an association of extraordinary social reach. Its growth brought people with diverse grievances into a common organizational environment. The association's Muslim identity and local networks aided recruitment, but the autonomy of branches and variation among constituencies made political unity difficult. Reported membership totals are not precise censuses and should be treated as distinct estimates. The Indische Partij advanced another approach through cross-racial Indies patriotism and explicit criticism of colonial domination.

Socialist and communist politics emerged from the interaction of international connections, local labor organization and existing Indonesian associations. The ISDV developed into a communist party, and controversy over concurrent organizational membership helped fragment Sarekat Islam. The failed uprisings of 1926–1927 occurred in different regional settings and were not an effective nationwide revolution. Subsequent repression severely damaged the open communist movement, but ideas, personalities and transnational connections persisted beyond the destruction of the earlier party organization.

Perhimpunan Indonesia made the claim to independence visible in the Netherlands and within international anticolonial networks. Hatta's political activities and trial demonstrated both the possibilities and risks of overseas organization. In the Indies, Sukarno sought an intellectual basis for cooperation among nationalist, Islamic and Marxist currents. His emerging philosophy of Marhaenism linked exploitation to the circumstances of impoverished small producers as well as workers. The original PNI translated independence-oriented nationalism into a party organized around political education, noncooperation and public mobilization.

The Bandung trial of 1930 gave Sukarno an opportunity to publicly criticize colonial rule. Its consequence was not simply an increase in his personal prominence: it also led to the institutional disruption of the PNI. After the original party dissolved, Partindo and the Pendidikan developed different priorities for reconstructing political organization. Their disagreement over mass mobilization and cadre education reflected practical as well as ideological questions about the movement's capacity to survive repression.

Youth associations, the two youth congresses and the Youth Pledge helped articulate a national identity that did not require the abandonment of regional languages and cultures. The 1928 resolution emphasized Indonesian as a language of unity and identified practical means of spreading the decision. Women's associations constituted another important form of political activity. Their congress and federation pursued education, marriage reform and other concerns that national independence alone could not settle. Women also exercised leadership within nationalist parties and encountered colonial repression.

Colonial coercion took the form of criminal proceedings, restrictions on meetings and publications, surveillance and administrative exile. The treatment of prisoners and their families and the disruption of associations were distinct consequences of these instruments. Detention did not necessarily eliminate intellectual activity or a leader's symbolic influence, but such continuity should not be mistaken for the preservation of a party's practical capacity. The government's increasingly restrictive policies narrowed the opportunities for public noncooperation in the 1930s.

Constitutional nationalists pursued alternative strategies through the Volksraad, Parindra, Gerindo and broader coordination. The rejection of the Soetardjo Petition in 1938 exposed the limits of metropolitan willingness to negotiate significant constitutional change. GAPI's parliamentary campaign brought organizations together amid growing international danger but did not obtain the desired transfer of authority before the invasion. Cooperation with legal institutions could be a nationalist strategy rather than a commitment to permanent Dutch sovereignty.

By 1942 Indonesian nationalism had created substantial organizational, intellectual and cultural capacities, but had not resolved the distribution of authority in a future state. Religious, socialist, regional, constitutional and noncooperating currents remained diverse. Dutch military defeat opened a new historical situation under Japanese imperial rule; it did not constitute independence. The subsequent occupation must be studied on its own terms, without projecting the future outcomes of the revolution onto the decisions taken by nationalists before the war.

Key Terms

Netherlands Indies
The Dutch colonial territory in the archipelago, consolidated through conquest and treaties. Its geographic extent and institutional arrangements changed over time; it should not be treated as an already independent Indonesia.
Indonesia
A name with nineteenth-century scholarly origins that became a political self-designation and a claim to national belonging and sovereignty during the nationalist movement.
Ethical Policy
Colonial policy orientation announced in 1901, commonly associated with education, irrigation and migration; it did not abolish colonial rule or educational inequalities.
Priyayi
Javanese aristocratic and bureaucratic strata with varied positions and relationships to colonial authority. Their educational advantages shaped many early associations.
Inlander
A period Dutch legal and administrative designation for persons classified as indigenous. The chapter uses it only to explain the hierarchy, not as a current descriptive category.
STOVIA
School tot Opleiding van Inlandsche Artsen, a Batavia institution for training indigenous physicians where Budi Utomo was founded.
Pergerakan
Movement: a historical term for the widening field of association, journalism, public activity and political organizing, not a single organization or party.
Budi Utomo
Association founded in 1908, initially oriented largely toward educational and cultural improvement in Java and Madura. Its subsequent political significance and commemoration differ from its original aims.
Rekso Roemekso
Surakarta mutual-protection network connected to the origins of Sarekat Islam. Its local form preceded the mass political association.
Sarekat Islam (SI)
Muslim association that developed into a mass political movement after 1912, with a diverse social base and conflicts over ideological and organizational direction.
Indische Partij
Political association founded in 1912 advocating Indies patriotism and challenging colonial racial distinctions and political authority.
Comite Boemi Poetra
Committee of Indigenous Sons, associated with criticism of the colonial celebration of Dutch liberty and the circulation of Suwardi's 1913 pamphlet.
ISDV
Indische Sociaal-Democratische Vereeniging, founded 1914; socialist association that developed close connections with Indonesian labor and SI activists.
VSTP
Association of Railway and Tramway Personnel, established in 1908; a major union in the development of labor organization and socialist politics.
Socialism
A broad family of political and economic doctrines concerning social control of resources and equality; different socialist movements disagreed on institutions and strategies.
Marxism
An intellectual and political tradition associated with Karl Marx and subsequent thinkers, emphasizing historical material relations, class and the analysis of capitalism. Its local application was debated.
Communism
A political tradition seeking a transformation of class and property relations; in the Indies a distinct party was organized in 1920 and later called PKI.
PKI
Partai Komunis Indonesia; the communist organization grew from the ISDV in 1920 and adopted the Indonesian party name in 1924.
Comintern
Communist International (1919–1943), an organization connecting communist parties internationally. Its authority and practical relationship with local activists must be evaluated separately.
Trade union
Organization of workers to pursue shared interests, including pay and working conditions; participation does not necessarily indicate party membership.
Class consciousness
Awareness of common economic and social relations and their implications for collective action, rather than uniform ideology among workers or cultivators.
Internationalism
Solidarity or collective action across national boundaries; it can be expressed through communist, socialist, religious and anticolonial networks.
Party discipline
Rules concerning membership, allegiance and adherence to organizational decisions. The SI controversy in 1921 concerned concurrent allegiance to rival bodies.
Sarekat Rakyat
People’s Association, created in 1923 from pro-communist sections of Sarekat Islam. Its history demonstrates continuity and separation of local networks.
Uprising
An episode of collective armed resistance to authority. The Java and West Sumatran outbreaks of 1926–1927 were regional and poorly coordinated.
Perhimpunan Indonesia (PI)
Student association in the Netherlands that evolved from Indische Vereeniging into an independence-oriented political organization during the 1920s.
League against Imperialism
International anticolonial organization formed at Brussels in 1927; Hatta participated as an Indonesian representative.
Nationalism
A political and cultural project identifying a people as a nation with claims to collective political authority; its meanings and boundaries differed among organizations.
Noncooperation
Refusal to rely on colonial representative and governmental institutions for nationalist objectives. This did not necessarily entail withdrawal from all colonial social or economic relations.
Self-reliance
The effort to create organizational, educational, economic and intellectual capacities independent of colonial grants of political authority.
Marhaenism
Sukarno’s evolving political and social philosophy emphasizing the liberation of impoverished and exploited Indonesians, including small producers who owned some means of production.
PNI
Perserikatan Nasional Indonesia, founded 1927, renamed Partai Nasional Indonesia in 1928. The original organization dissolved in 1931; it was distinct from PNI Baru.
PPPKI
Federation of Indonesian nationalist political associations founded in December 1927. Its coordinating purpose did not eliminate members' distinct programs.
Youth association
A body organized among young people, often students, around regional, cultural, religious or national purposes. Some became institutional foundations of interregional nationalism.
Youth Pledge (Sumpah Pemuda)
The resolution of 28 October 1928 asserting an Indonesian homeland and nation and upholding Indonesian as the language of unity.
Bahasa Indonesia
The national language developed from Malay; adopted as a language of unity rather than as a claim to eliminate other languages.
Women's federation (PPPI)
The association formed after the December 1928 Women's Congress, often rendered Perikatan Perkumpulan Perempuan Indonesia. Not to be confused with the students' PPPI.
Partindo
Partai Indonesia, founded after the PNI dissolution in 1931; associated with public mobilization and later with Sukarno's leadership.
PNI Baru (Pendidikan)
Pendidikan Nasional Indonesia, established in 1931; associated with Hatta and Sjahrir and an emphasis on cadre education.
Cadre education
Systematic political education and preparation of members for sustained organizational work; not merely attendance at public rallies or passive adherence to a leader.
Administrative exile
Executive removal or internment of people considered politically dangerous without ordinary criminal conviction. It should be distinguished from fixed prison sentences imposed in court.
Volksraad
Colonial representative council inaugurated in 1918, with elected and appointed members and changing but limited authority. It was not a sovereign parliament.
Soetardjo Petition
1936 initiative seeking negotiations about gradual autonomy within a Dutch constitutional framework; approved by the Volksraad but rejected by metropolitan authorities in 1938.
GAPI
Gabungan Politik Indonesia, federation founded in May 1939 and associated with the campaign for an Indonesian parliament.
Sovereignty
Ultimate political authority over a constitutional order, distinct from partial self-government or administrative autonomy.

Research or Library Extension

Independent Research Project

Constructing a Documentary History of an Indonesian Political Organization

Choose one organization active before 1942: Budi Utomo, Sarekat Islam, Indische Partij, the VSTP, PKI, Perhimpunan Indonesia, PNI, a youth or women’s association, Partindo, Pendidikan Nasional Indonesia, Permi, Parindra, Gerindo, or GAPI. Treat the organization as an institution operating in specific historical circumstances, not merely as the expression of a prominent leader or ideology. Prepare a 2,000–2,500-word research report and a source dossier.

  1. Define the organizational question and the years and geographical area you intend to study. Identify the institution’s formal name and changes of name, and distinguish related predecessor or successor groups.
  2. Locate at least two identifiable primary documents, such as an association constitution, congress resolution, dated article, letter, court decision or administrative report. Record the original date, publication edition, page or section, language and access route. If only a scholarly paraphrase is accessible, label it as mediated evidence.
  3. Use at least three scholarly works, including at least one specialist study concerned with the movement or institution. Distinguish its evidence from the interpretations it advances and identify where historians disagree.
  4. Reconstruct the organization’s governance, constituency, financing, and principal activities as far as the evidence permits. If membership numbers are available, classify them as registered members, active members, claims or estimates, and state the date and geographic scope. Do not treat missing data as zero.
  5. Investigate at least one relationship with another political or religious association. Determine whether the relationship involved a formal alliance, joint participation, overlapping membership, ideological exchange or rivalry, and support your classification through specific evidence.
  6. Examine one instance of interaction with colonial authority, including recognition, legal cooperation, censorship, prosecution or administrative exile. Identify the exact kind of official action rather than using repression as an undifferentiated category.
  7. Prepare a chronology of eight to twelve events, a short annotated bibliography, and a table comparing at least five characteristics with those of another movement. For the qualitative comparisons, identify the sources rather than invent quantitative scores.
  8. Conclude by assessing the organization’s significance as of the end of Dutch rule. Do not explain its value solely by reference to the careers that some members later pursued. Record unanswered questions and evidentiary limitations in a separate methodological note.

Evaluation: A strong report distinguishes primary evidence from later interpretation, explains institutional mechanisms, provides traceable references, and acknowledges gaps. It addresses competing explanations instead of assuming that all activity culminated inevitably in independence. A report that uses invented quotations, undefined membership statistics or unverified historical claims does not meet the documentary standard.

Chapter Reflection

The central lesson of this chapter is that political identity becomes historically consequential through institutions. An idea must be communicated, interpreted and sustained if it is to bring people into durable collective activity. Budi Utomo, Sarekat Islam, the communist and nationalist parties, youth associations, women's organizations and parliamentary campaigns all created different means of doing that work. Their histories invite students to examine such practical matters as who belonged to an organization, how it financed its activity, and how authority was allocated among participants.

The history also illustrates the difference between a shared objective and a shared political program. People may agree that colonial subordination should end yet disagree over how to reach that goal, what institutions should govern an independent society, or which social and religious interests should receive priority. Efforts toward unity were genuine and historically consequential; so were disputes, failures and exclusions. A textbook that ignores either dimension would lose sight of the historical conditions in which political actors made choices.

The surviving record of the nationalist movement requires critical examination. A published text can reveal a leader's political argument; a census can reveal aspects of the population counted by the colonial state; a prosecution can show both accusations and the exercise of judicial power. Each type of evidence has a different relationship to the experiences of people who left few written records. Studying the movement means asking how claims about the nation, party and public are supported, and whose experiences cannot be reconstructed from those claims alone.

The next chapter examines Vietnamese nationalism before the communist ascendancy, while subsequent chapters follow political developments across Southeast Asia. Comparative study should preserve the specific historical development of each movement. Indonesia's political institutions were shaped by the particular legal, social and geographical conditions of the Netherlands Indies. Their resemblance to institutions in other colonies can be analytically significant without making the national histories interchangeable. The immediate future in 1942 remained contested and uncertain.
