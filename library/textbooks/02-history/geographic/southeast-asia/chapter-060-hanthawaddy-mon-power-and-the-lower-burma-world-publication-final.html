Hanthawaddy, Mon Power, and the Lower Burma World

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Hanthawaddy was neither an ancient Mon homeland simply restored nor a port that foreign merchants built for their own convenience. It was a kingdom assembled, from the late thirteenth century onward, out of delta waterways, harbors, rice districts, local lords, Buddhist monasteries, and the pressures of war with Ava, and it was then remembered by its own court and by its rivals in ways that still complicate the evidence.

In the later 1470s, at Pegu in Lower Burma, a set of large stone slabs was inscribed in Pali and in Mon. Known today as the Kalyani Inscriptions, they record that King Dhammazedi (in Pali, Dhammaceti) sent monks across the Bay of Bengal to Sri Lanka, brought a reformed ordination lineage back to Pegu, and defined a ritual boundary, a sima, within which that lineage's monks could be ordained. In 1892 the colonial scholar Taw Sein Ko published a text and translation. The inscriptions matter for this chapter because they are something relatively rare in the study of Lower Burma: a direct, fifteenth-century document produced by the royal court whose history we want to understand.

Almost everything else about the early kingdom reaches us through later narratives. The conventional story begins around 1287, when a man known as Wareru, or Magadu, established an independent authority at Martaban on the Gulf of Martaban. It continues through a series of Martaban-based rulers, the transfer of the principal royal seat to Pegu around 1369, the long struggle with Ava under Razadarit, the reign of the queen Shin Sawbu, and the religious reforms of Dhammazedi. That story is useful, and this chapter follows it, but it is built from chronicles compiled long after the events, and modern scholarship has asked how far the Martaban-centered polity and the Pegu-centered kingdom should be treated as one continuous “Mon kingdom.”

Lower Burma's environment gave its rulers real resources and real problems. The delta and the coast produced rice, fish, salt, and timber, and its ports connected inland rivers with Sri Lanka, eastern India, the Malay Peninsula, and the wider Bay of Bengal. The same landscape of tidal channels, shifting rivers, and scattered towns made political integration difficult. Kings had to bargain with local lords and to hold together centers that were separated by water rather than linked by a single road system. Wealth from ports and agricultural districts allowed a court to patronize monasteries and maintain armies, yet it also exposed the delta to pressure from the more densely cultivated and militarily mobilized dry zone of Upper Burma.

That pressure took shape in the wars with Ava that Chapter 59 narrates from the Upper Burmese side. Here the same conflict is examined from Pegu, Martaban, and the western delta, where the strategic aims were to defend ports and river access, to retain the loyalty of local rulers, and to prevent Ava from dominating frontier towns. In the mid-fifteenth century, Shin Sawbu, a reigning queen, and Dhammazedi, a former monk who became king, gave the court a religious profile that made Pegu one of the leading Theravada Buddhist centers of mainland Southeast Asia.

The chapter therefore asks how Martaban and Pegu developed into the kingdom called Hanthawaddy, and how commerce, alliance, warfare, Mon-language institutions, and Buddhist patronage sustained it. It also asks how historians know. Inscriptions, chronicles, foreign records, and archaeology survive unevenly, and each carries its own purposes. Throughout, the chapter distinguishes the history of Mon-speaking communities, which was long and varied, from the independently documented history of a particular Mon-centered state. It treats Lower Burma as ethnically and linguistically mixed rather than homogeneous, and it keeps the narrative before the Toungoo conquest of 1539, examined in Chapter 84, in view only as a later outcome that no one in 1500 could have foreseen.

  3. Evaluate the strengths and limits of Mon and Burmese chronicles, inscriptions, foreign records, and archaeology as evidence for Hanthawaddy.
  4. Trace the political development of the kingdom from Wareru through Binnya U, Razadarit, Shin Sawbu, and Dhammazedi.
  5. Explain how maritime commerce and delta agriculture together supported royal power, without making either the sole explanation.
  6. Analyze the war with Ava from Hanthawaddy's strategic perspective, including the role of military ecology.
  7. Interpret Mon Buddhist institutions, Pali learning, and the Kalyani ordination reform as religious, political, and epigraphic phenomena.
  8. Compare the experiences of rulers, soldiers, merchants, cultivators, captives, and monastic communities in a wartime kingdom.
  9. Assess competing interpretations of ancient Ramannadesa and Mon historical memory.
  10. Distinguish contemporary evidence from later dynastic chronicles, religious foundation traditions, and later European commercial descriptions.
  11. Contextualize Hanthawaddy within the Bay of Bengal world and the Theravada mainland of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries.

Historical Context

By the late thirteenth century the kingdom of Pagan, which had dominated the Irrawaddy valley for generations and which earlier chapters have examined, was losing coherence under the strain of internal weakness and the pressure of Mongol-Yuan expeditions (see Chapter 46). Its fragmentation did not mean that earlier settlements, monasteries, or communities vanished. It meant that the central authority which had claimed overlordship over many districts could no longer enforce that claim. In Upper Burma, successor centers such as Myinsaing, Pinya, Sagaing, and later Ava emerged, as Chapter 59 explains. In the south, around the delta and the Gulf of Martaban, local rulers, port towns, and regional elites also acted on their own account.

Lower Burma was linked to larger worlds. The Bay of Bengal connected its harbors with eastern India and Sri Lanka, while overland and river routes tied it to the Tai-speaking polities discussed in Chapters 54, 55, and 57. Theravada Buddhism, whose spread across the mainland Chapter 53 traces, was well established in monastic and royal life, though in multiple lineages and with Sri Lankan connections that predated the Hanthawaddy period. Mon-speaking communities lived in Lower Burma and in parts of the Chao Phraya basin, where the earlier Dvaravati world is examined in Chapter 25. Burmese-speaking, Tai/Shan-speaking, Indian, Arab, Persian, and other communities also lived in or passed through delta towns, so that no simple ethnic label describes the population.

Historians must also avoid two errors. The first treats Hanthawaddy as the natural resurrection of an ancient Mon realm, an idea rooted in traditions about Ramannadesa and Suvannabhumi whose historical status is contested. The second treats the kingdom purely as a commercial contrivance. This chapter instead presents a polity that combined port revenue, rice districts, regional alliances, military capacity, and religious legitimacy, and whose reputation was later reshaped by religious and political projects, particularly under Dhammazedi, and by chroniclers writing from Mon and Burmese viewpoints.

Section 60.1

Reconstructing Hanthawaddy: Mon Chronicles, Burmese Records, and Inscriptions

The evidence for Hanthawaddy falls into four broad groups, and no single group suffices. The first is epigraphic. Stone inscriptions in Pali, Middle Mon, and Burmese record royal and monastic donations, religious boundaries, and the histories that courts wished to preserve. The Kalyani Inscriptions of the 1470s are a major royal religious record; the 1455 Kyaikmaraw dedication of Shin Sawbu and the 1485 Shwedagon inscription of Dhammazedi supply different contemporary perspectives on patronage. These are contemporary documents, but they are written by or for donors and religious institutions, and they say little about ordinary people, prices, or battles.

The second group consists of chronicles. The Mon tradition is represented by works such as the Razadarit Ayedawbon, an account of the king's reign, and by a Mon chronicle tradition printed in the early twentieth century under the title associated with the Rajadhiraj. Burmese royal chronicles, including U Kala's Maha Yazawin and the nineteenth-century Hmannan, include Lower Burma in their own narratives, often from the viewpoint of Ava or of the later Burmese monarchy. All were compiled long after the events they describe, and the surviving forms of the Mon traditions reflect copying, translation, and editorial processes whose history scholars such as Tun Aung Chain have studied. The third group is foreign: Ming Chinese diplomatic records, Tai chronicles, Sri Lankan Buddhist texts, and, only from the early sixteenth century, Portuguese and later Dutch accounts. The fourth is archaeological, though continuous urban occupation, river change, and the sack of Pegu in the mid-eighteenth century have limited what survives.

Several problems recur. Chronological retrospection means that the years given to early reigns are conventional: chronicles disagree on regnal lengths and on the order of some rulers. Dynastic legitimacy shaped narrative, since later kings and their historians wanted to link themselves to illustrious founders. Literary embellishment is visible in dramatic speeches, omens, and rhetorical duels that no contemporary could have recorded verbatim. And survival is uneven. Palm-leaf manuscripts decay in a humid delta, and later wars destroyed collections. None of this makes chronicles useless; it means that they are best used as evidence of political memory and, with caution, as sources for sequences of events that independent evidence can confirm.

A further distinction governs this chapter. The conventional narrative treats a “Wareru dynasty” as the ancestor of a continuous Hanthawaddy kingdom. Michael Aung-Thwin's Myanmar in the Fifteenth Century (2017) challenges that continuity and re-examines the kingdom's foundations. This chapter therefore speaks of a Martaban-centered political order in the late thirteenth and fourteenth centuries and of a Pegu-centered kingdom from about the later fourteenth century, using “Hanthawaddy” as a convenient name for the latter and noting where chroniclers treat the two as one. The history of Mon-speaking communities is longer and broader than the history of either state.

Foreign evidence deserves its own note. Ming Chinese records mention Ava and Pegu among the polities of the southwest, and they show that the Chinese court distinguished the two kingdoms and received missions from both, but they say little about internal politics. Tai chronicles remember relations with the Gulf of Martaban in their own terms. Sri Lankan Buddhist texts matter chiefly for the history of ordination. Because each outside source reflects its author's interests, its value lies in independence from the Lower Burmese court, not in neutrality. Taken together, these sources allow some claims to be checked against one another, and they allow a historian to say which parts of the chronicle narrative are corroborated, which are plausible, and which rest on a single retrospective tradition.

Section 60.2

The Geography of Lower Burma: Rivers, Delta, Coast, and Trade Routes

Lower Burma is a landscape of rivers entering the sea. The Irrawaddy divides into many channels across a broad delta; to the east the Sittang flows south into the Gulf of Martaban, and between them the Bago River drains the region around Pegu and meets the channels that lead to the coast near Dagon and the later Rangoon. Farther east, the Salween (Thanlwin) reaches the Gulf near Martaban. Sediment carried by these rivers builds land and fills channels, so the coastline and the navigability of particular waterways changed over time. Historians therefore use present maps cautiously for the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries.

Three centers illustrate the region's contrasts. Martaban, on the gulf, was a port oriented toward the sea and toward the Tai-speaking states and the Salween approaches. Pegu, inland on the Bago River, lay between the eastern delta and the Sittang basin and could draw on rice-growing districts while remaining reachable by water. Bassein, in the western delta, looked toward the Bay of Bengal and was linked to the Irrawaddy channels. None of these was a simple twin of Ava's dry-zone core, where irrigated rice and a more compact population supported a different kind of state. The delta offered fertile land and abundant fish, but much of it required drainage, embankment, or clearing, and floods could destroy crops as readily as they could renew soils.

The monsoon system shaped travel. Seasonal winds carried ships across the Bay of Bengal in set periods, and the rainy season transformed the delta's channels and flooded fields, affecting both commerce and military movement (see Chapter 4). Tides moved far upriver, and rivers served as roads. For rulers, this meant that communications were by water, that control of boats and ports mattered, and that a single decisive victory on land might not translate into control of scattered districts. Geography did not dictate political outcomes, but it set conditions within which kings, local lords, merchants, and monks made choices.

The delta was also a disease and resource environment. Mangrove coasts, tidal flats, and freshwater swamps supported fish, shellfish, and salt-making, while seasonal flooding spread silt but also brought standing water and the health risks that accompany it. Soils differed from district to district, and not all land could be cultivated without drainage or protection from tides. Rice farming therefore expanded unevenly, concentrated where water could be managed. This variety helps explain why the delta supported many small centers rather than a single dominant core, and why a king who wished to rule it needed local knowledge and local cooperation as much as armies.

Section 60.3

Wareru and the Political Formation of Martaban, c. 1280–1307

The figure known as Wareru, or Magadu, stands at the head of the conventional Hanthawaddy narrative. Later traditions describe him as having a Tai or Shan background and as having had a connection with the Sukhothai political world, which Chapter 55 examines. They place his seizure of authority at Martaban and give 1287 as the founding year of an independent realm, the year that tradition also associates with the end of Pagan's overlordship. These traditions must be treated critically. Accounts of his humble origins, his marriage and Sukhothai recognition, and his acquisition of power follow familiar patterns of founder stories, in which an outsider of modest standing rises through cunning and favor. They may preserve genuine memories of ties between Martaban and Tai-speaking regions, but they also served the legitimacy of the dynasty that claimed descent from him.

What can be said with more confidence is general. In the decades around 1300, a political authority centered on Martaban emerged and extended influence across parts of the Gulf of Martaban coast. Its position near the Salween estuary made it a port and a point of contact with the Tai world to the east. It coexisted with other centers, among them Pegu and districts of the western delta, which traditions associate with rulers such as one called Tarabya of Pegu. Accounts of alliances and conflicts among these centers differ, and the later image of a single ruler gathering “the Mon country” reads backward from later consolidation.

The ethnic and linguistic character of the early Martaban order was mixed. A ruler of Tai/Shan origin by tradition governed a population in which Mon speakers were prominent but not exclusive, and the court drew upon several languages and traditions. This is why the chapter avoids calling Wareru's authority a “Mon kingdom” without qualification. Mon language and institutions were important in the region, and later rulers drew on them, yet the early polity should be described by its political center and its connections rather than by a single ethnic label. Traditions place Wareru's death in 1307, in a conspiracy within his family, and his succession by his brother Hkun Law, though chronicle accounts of these events differ in detail.

Wareru's emergence coincided with the years in which Pagan's authority collapsed under internal weakness and Mongol-Yuan pressure, discussed in Chapter 46. Whether he acknowledged any overlord, and in what form, is unclear from the sources, and later accounts that describe recognition by Sukhothai or by Pagan's successors may reflect the interests of later narrators. The safest conclusion is that a capable leader of mixed connections took advantage of a political vacuum at a strategic port. The use of a royal title, and the memory of that title in later centuries, show how quickly a new authority sought the vocabulary of kingship and how important such vocabulary was to its legitimacy.

Primary Source Window

Mon and Burmese Traditions of Wareru's Rise

Creator: Anonymous Mon and Burmese chroniclers and later compilers · Date: compiled centuries after the events; surviving forms from later periods · Type: Dynastic chronicle tradition

Context: Accounts of Wareru appear in Mon chronicle traditions and in Burmese royal chronicles such as the Maha Yazawin and the Hmannan. They were written to explain the origin of a ruling line and, in the Burmese case, often to place Lower Burma within a wider royal history. Surviving texts have passed through copying and editing. The present window is an editorial synthesis based on modern historical scholarship and the transmission described by Tun Aung Chain in A Chronicle of the Mons (Yangon, 2010), translated into English from a Burmese intermediary derived from Mon. It does not purport to reproduce a specific Mon or Burmese manuscript passage.

Editorial paraphrase (not a verbatim quotation):

In general terms, the traditions describe a man of Tai or Shan origin, known as Magadu, who is associated with the Sukhothai sphere, who gains a following at Martaban, who becomes ruler there, and who takes the royal title Wareru. They place this rise at the time of Pagan's decline, and they often present it as the beginning of a line of kings. Details of his background, of Sukhothai's role, and of the order of events vary from account to account.

Analysis: The paraphrase captures what is shared at a general level. The stories show that later writers, whether Mon or Burmese, treated Wareru as a founder. They also suggest memories of connections between Martaban and the Tai world.

Limitations: The accounts cannot establish the date of 1287 as an exact year, the facts of Wareru's origin, or the extent of Sukhothai's involvement. Founder narratives tend to follow literary patterns.

Perspective: The surviving accounts reflect the viewpoints of later courts and chroniclers. Local cultivators, merchants, and monastic communities of the Martaban region are largely absent, as is any independent Tai record of the events.

Section 60.4

Martaban, Thaton, Pegu, and the Early Organization of Lower Burmese Authority

After Pagan, authority in Lower Burma was layered. A king at Martaban might claim overlordship, but towns such as Pegu, Thaton, Dagon, Myaungmya, and Bassein were governed by local rulers or governors who held their own followings and revenues. Relationships between a king and such rulers were personal and often fragile. They rested on kinship, marriage, shared religious patronage, tribute, and the ruler's ability to reward loyalty or punish rebellion. Titles and ranks in the chronicles hint at hierarchies, but the sources rarely allow a clear reconstruction of offices.

One valuable clue is the tradition of thirty-two administrative districts, or myeo, in the medieval Mon kingdom. H. L. Shorto's study of the “32 Myos” examines this tradition and its relationship to Mon political geography. The number thirty-two has symbolic overtones and should not be treated as an exact census of units at any particular date. Even so, the tradition suggests that people of the region conceived of their land as a set of districts linked to a royal center. It also indicates how later Mon writers imagined order in the delta.

The Wareru Dhammathat is another legacy. A dhammathat is a legal-moral text in the Pali-Sanskritic tradition, adapted in Burma into collections of rules on inheritance, marriage, debt, theft, and procedure. A text attributed to Wareru appears in later manuscripts and was copied and revised over generations. Its existence shows that the memory of Wareru was associated with royal justice. It does not prove that every surviving rule dates to his reign: later scribes updated, combined, and rearranged material. Historians therefore treat the text as evidence for the law of Lower Burma as transmitted, and for the authority that later generations gave to the founder's name, not as a verbatim record of thirteenth-century legislation.

Together these points show that early Lower Burmese authority was less an administrative machine than a negotiated order. Kings sought to bind local lords, monasteries, and port communities through patronage and justice. The weak points were clear: the separation of centers by water and the competing ambitions of regional rulers. These features explain both why the Martaban order survived for decades and why succession disputes repeatedly threatened it.

Thaton and other older towns of the coast retained prestige in later memory, and rulers of such places did not simply disappear when a new king arose at Martaban. Monasteries, too, formed part of the political landscape: a king who endowed a monastery gained the support of its community and its lay patrons, while a monastery that received royal gifts gained land and protection. Hierarchies of title and rank appear in the sources, but their meanings changed over time, and the same title could signify different powers in different reigns. Historians therefore reconstruct authority from patterns of action, such as who gave gifts, who commanded troops, and who paid tribute, more than from lists of offices.

Historical Vocabulary
Ruler, Myeo, Dhammathat, and Political Allegiance

Ruler

Definition: In the sources, a king or lord holding royal or local authority, whose titles vary. Why it matters: A “king” of Martaban or Pegu might be one of several rulers claiming authority at once. Potential misunderstanding: Modern ideas of sovereignty with fixed borders do not fit.

Myeo

Definition: A Burmese term (with Mon counterparts) for a town or district and the surrounding territory. Why it matters: The thirty-two myeo tradition shapes how the Mon kingdom was imagined. Potential misunderstanding: The number is a traditional framework, not a precise administrative census.

Dhammathat

Definition: A legal-moral text on social and legal rules in the Buddhist tradition of mainland Southeast Asia. Why it matters: The Wareru Dhammathat shows the link between founder and royal justice. Potential misunderstanding: Attribution to a founder does not date every provision.

Political allegiance

Definition: The bonds, personal, ritual, and tributary, by which local rulers acknowledged a higher king. Why it matters: Allegiance could shift with circumstance. Potential misunderstanding: It was not equivalent to modern citizenship or exclusive subjecthood.

An Essential Qualification

The emergence of Hanthawaddy should not be described as the immediate restoration of an ancient, continuously unified Mon kingdom. Traditions link Lower Burma to the old realm of Ramannadesa and to Buddhist legends of Suvannabhumi, but their historical status is contested, as Section 60.17 examines. Aung-Thwin argues that important elements of those traditions took their recognizable form through later religious and political projects, especially under Dhammazedi. Other scholarship places more weight on long-standing Mon cultural and settlement networks. The sound approach is to separate the history of Mon-speaking communities from the independently documented history of a Mon-centered state.

Section 60.5

Wareru's Successors and the Struggle for Lower Burma

The decades after Wareru are known chiefly through an abridged chronicle sequence featuring Hkun Law, Saw O, Saw Zein, and Binnya E Law, with intervening short-lived claimants such as Zein Pun and Saw E in some accounts; the order and regnal lengths vary by recension. The conventional picture is of a dynasty beset by assassinations, rebellions, and competing claims within the royal family. The pattern is credible, since a realm whose centers were separated by water and held by local lords was vulnerable to conspiracy. Yet the details, including who killed whom and in which year, should be read as the stories that later writers told about instability rather than as secure reconstruction.

The dynasty's difficulties had structural roots. Martaban, as a port, could generate revenue from trade, but a king's real power depended on the cooperation of governors at Pegu, Dagon, Myaungmya, Bassein, and other towns. Each had its own population, harbor or river access, and followers. When a king died, rivals within the family could seek the backing of one or another of these lords. Conflict was therefore regional as well as dynastic. In the western delta, rulers had their own ambitions and relationships with the Bay of Bengal; in the east, the Pegu region lay near the Sittang basin and the approaches to Upper Burma.

Outside powers added complexity. Tai-speaking polities to the east, whose rise Chapters 55 and 57 examine, had connections with Martaban, and the successor centers in Upper Burma discussed in Chapter 59 had their own interests in the south. Under Binnya E Law, tradition holds, the dynasty regained a measure of stability and prepared the way for his successor Binnya U. These accounts must not be read as a story of steady progress toward a unified kingdom. The consolidation that did occur was uneven, reversible, and dependent on persons and circumstances. Their contribution to the long-term story is to show that Lower Burma could sustain a recognized royal tradition despite repeated disruption.

The reliability of the succession narrative is limited by the nature of the sources. No single contemporary account survives, and later chronicles tend to compress long and complicated conflicts into tales of murder and revenge. Some rulers may have held power only in part of the delta, so that two kings could be reigning at once in different centers, a situation that chronicle sequences, which present a single line, tend to obscure. A careful historian therefore treats the neat succession of rulers as a convention of dynastic memory. What the evidence does support is a pattern of recurring contest and eventual stabilization, with Binnya E Law and Binnya U representing the more successful phase.

Section 60.6

Binnya U and the Emergence of Pegu as the Royal Capital

Binnya U, whose reign is conventionally dated 1348–1384, is the first ruler of the line about whom the tradition suggests substantial consolidation. He is associated with measures to secure the loyalty of local elites, to defend the realm, and to relocate the principal royal center from Martaban to Pegu around 1369. The date is conventional and the circumstances are explained differently in different accounts, but the move is one of the most significant political developments in the history of the kingdom.

Pegu's advantages were considerable. It lay between the eastern delta and the Sittang basin, close to rice-producing districts and connected by river to the coast. It was less exposed than Martaban to pressures from the east and could serve as a hub for communication among delta towns. Tradition also invests Pegu with legendary prestige through the foundation story of Hamsavati, the “city of the sacred goose,” from which the name Hanthawaddy derives. Whether that story was already prominent in the fourteenth century or acquired its recognizable form later is a question that scholarship, including the work discussed in Section 60.17, has raised.

The change of capital signaled a political reorientation rather than a mere change of residence. A court at Pegu relied more on agricultural districts and the inland waterways; a court at Martaban had depended more on gulf trade and Tai relations. Moving the court meant moving the pattern of patronage: new palaces, monasteries, and officials, a different geography of alliance. It also placed the king closer to the frontier with Upper Burma, where Ava was rising. Binnya U's rule thus laid the geographic basis for the kingdom that Razadarit would defend, and Martaban retained importance as a port and as a center of authority rather than disappearing.

Binnya U's relations with local elites were central to his success. Chronicles credit him with measures that reconciled rivals and with building or restoring religious foundations, and these acts match the broader pattern of royal strategy: binding local lords through patronage and bringing the monastic community into a close relationship with the court. Pegu's religious prestige, whatever the age of its foundation legend, helped such a policy, because a king who resided at a sacred city could present himself as its protector. These actions suggest that the move to Pegu was not only a response to military and geographic circumstances but also a deliberate effort to build a new center of loyalty and legitimacy.

Section 60.7

Maritime Commerce, Port Cities, and the Bay of Bengal World

Lower Burma's ports connected the mainland to the Bay of Bengal. Martaban, Bassein, and Pegu received ships and goods linked with eastern India, Sri Lanka, the Malay Peninsula, and maritime Southeast Asia, and exported products of the delta and the inland river system. The kingdom's rulers benefited through taxes, tolls, tribute, and the ability to direct commerce. The Islamic and Indian Ocean commercial networks discussed in Chapter 62 formed part of this world, and merchants from several communities visited or lived in the ports.

The best-known commodity associated with the region is the Martaban jar. Pamela Gutman's survey of the “Martaban trade” shows that the name, applied to large stoneware containers found across Asia, came from the port through which many passed, and that evidence exists for ceramic production around the Gulf of Martaban and for wider markets. The chapter must, however, distinguish pottery actually manufactured in Lower Burma from goods transported through its ports. Vessels called Martaban jars were made at several kiln areas, and the label does not prove origin. Nor should students assume that the arrangements known from later centuries, including the large-scale commerce described by sixteenth-century Portuguese observers and later Dutch records, were already fully developed in the fourteenth.

What the fourteenth- and fifteenth-century evidence supports is a pattern: ports served as entrepôts and as outlets for local products such as rice and forest goods, while imported textiles and other goods arrived. Ming records and Buddhist texts show links with Sri Lanka and Bengal. Agriculture mattered as much as commerce; rice districts provided food and revenue, and port wealth alone cannot account for the kingdom's strength. The commercial foundation explains how a king could finance monasteries and armies, but it also exposed the ports to disruption in wartime and to competition from other harbors on the Bay of Bengal and the Malay Peninsula. Lower Burma's wealth was real but not equivalent to the dominance of the dry-zone military state.

Archaeological and documentary study of ceramics has shown that the story of the Martaban jar is complicated. Kiln sites in more than one area have been associated with large stoneware jars, and the vessels were used to carry water, oil, and other goods on ships, so that their presence in a distant site shows that a jar passed through the trade, not where it was made. Gutman's survey also emphasizes the long chronology of the trade, from before the period of this chapter to well after it, which is a reminder that Hanthawaddy's commerce was part of a long-running system and not a sudden creation of the fourteenth century. The kingdom's rulers benefited from the system but did not control it.

Section 60.8

Rice Cultivation, Fishing, Craft Production, and Everyday Life in Lower Burma

Most people of Lower Burma lived by farming and fishing. Wet-rice cultivation depended on rainfall, floodwater, and local management of embankments and channels, and the delta offered fertile alluvial soils where clearing and drainage allowed expansion. Rice supplied both subsistence and surplus for markets and for the royal court. Fishing and salt production were also important, drawing on tidal creeks and coastal flats. The sources rarely describe these tasks directly, so reconstruction draws on later records, comparative evidence, and the environment, and must be read as plausible rather than fully documented for the period.

Craft production included boatbuilding, essential in a region of water roads, and ceramics, as the Martaban trade shows. Markets in port towns and at Pegu connected farmers, fishers, artisans, and merchants. River transport carried grain and other goods, and monsoon floods shaped the seasons of work and travel. Religious communities relied on donations, and monasteries received land, labor, and goods that supported their activities. Women worked in agriculture, trade, and household production; the importance of a reigning queen such as Shin Sawbu should not obscure the fact that evidence for ordinary women is thin.

War and instability affected productive life. A campaign in the rainy season could destroy crops or disrupt river traffic, and captured populations might be resettled. Famine and displacement are plausible consequences of prolonged conflict, though the sources document them unevenly. Slavery and debt bondage existed in mainland societies of this period (see Chapter 91 for the early modern period), but the precise forms in fifteenth-century Lower Burma are not well documented. Political stability, conversely, could encourage agricultural expansion and trade. The relationship between politics and everyday life ran in both directions: the royal court depended on the labor and surplus of ordinary people, who in turn faced the demands of tax, service, and war.

Boats were the connecting technology of the delta. Cargo boats carried rice and salt, smaller craft moved people between villages and markets, and seagoing vessels linked ports to the Bay of Bengal. The building and repair of such craft required timber, skilled labor, and sheltered yards, and the demand for boats in wartime must have been high. The seasonal rhythm of the monsoon governed when boats could safely sail, when fields were planted and harvested, and when armies could move. Daily life therefore followed a calendar set by water, and political decisions, such as when to campaign, had to take that calendar into account.

Section 60.9

Razadarit's Accession and the Unification of Lower Burma

Binnya U's reign ended, conventionally, in 1384, and the succession was contested. Razadarit, whose reign is traditionally dated from about 1383–1384 to 1421, emerges in the chronicle tradition as the son who prevailed over rivals and who then faced rebellion and invasion at the same time. The sources describe rival claimants and lords who resisted him, including, in the western delta, a ruler centered on Myaungmya. The tradition makes this period a story of a young king's determination, a framing that deserves skepticism but may preserve genuine memories of a succession crisis.

Consolidation meant bringing the major Lower Burmese centers under a single royal authority. That process involved military campaigns, negotiation, marriage alliance, and the management of local elites. Pegu, Dagon, Martaban, Myaungmya, Bassein, and other towns needed to be bound to the king. The chronicles present these developments as steps toward unity, but they were uneven, and rebellions recurred. A modern account should not assume that the delta was ever administered as a centralized territory; Razadarit's authority rested on a combination of force, patronage, and recognition of local power.

The principal narrative for his reign is the Razadarit Ayedawbon, whose title refers to a royal campaign chronicle. It is a retrospective narrative containing political memory and literary elaboration, not a contemporary account. Its rich detail, including dialogues, stratagems, and omens, makes it vivid, but vividness is not proof that every conversation or battle occurred as written. The Mon tradition behind it and its Burmese transmission have a documented history of copying and editing, and later Burmese chronicles treat the same events differently. Historians therefore use the work as a source for the sequence of major conflicts, for the values attributed to kingship, and for the way Mon and Burmese writers remembered the king.

Legitimacy was a continuing concern. A king who gained the throne through conflict needed to show that he was rightful, and the chronicles accordingly stress descent from Binnya U, the support of ministers, and the king's piety. Marriage alliances with leading families and with the families of provincial rulers helped to bind regional elites to the new king. Such alliances were not merely personal; they created obligations among families and gave local lords a stake in the success of the royal house. The combination of force, kinship, and religious patronage that marked Razadarit's consolidation became a pattern for his successors.

Section 60.10

The Forty Years' War: Hanthawaddy's Conflict with Ava

The conflict between Hanthawaddy and Ava is conventionally dated from the mid-1380s to the early 1420s, though “Forty Years' War” is a modern label for a set of campaigns separated by intervals of truce and by changes of ruler. Chronicles differ about dates and about the sequence of some operations. The main protagonists were Razadarit on one side and, on the other, Ava's kings Swa Saw Ke and Minkhaung I and the latter's son Minye Kyawswa, whose campaigns Chapter 59 describes. This section examines the war from Hanthawaddy's side and avoids repeating Chapter 59's account of Ava's campaigns.

For Hanthawaddy, the first strategic aim was defense. Ava's armies approached by the Irrawaddy and its overland routes toward Pegu and other delta centers, and a king who lost Pegu risked losing the loyalty of local lords. Razadarit's task was to protect the capital, to hold Martaban and the western delta, and to keep the revenue of the ports and the rice districts. A second aim was to influence frontier towns, such as those along the route between Upper and Lower Burma, whose allegiance shifted between the two kingdoms. These towns served as buffers, sources of manpower, and points of access, and control over them could determine whether a campaign succeeded.

The war moved through phases. Early in the 1380s and 1390s, the chronicles describe Ava's invasions and Hanthawaddy's resistance. After a period of truce, conflict resumed in the first decades of the fifteenth century, when Minkhaung I and Minye Kyawswa led renewed campaigns, and fighting continued until the early 1420s. Other powers affected the contest: the western coast of Arakan, relations with Tai-speaking polities, and diplomatic contact with Ming China all appear in the sources, though the evidence is limited and the extent of their influence is debated. Hanthawaddy preserved its independence, but it could not conquer Ava, and its frontier remained contested.

Interpretation depends on how one reads the sources. Chronicles celebrate battles and personal combat, but a historian looks for the strategic logic beneath them: Razadarit repeatedly used delta geography, negotiated with rivals, and kept key towns, while Ava's armies suffered from the problems of supply and terrain that Section 60.11 explains. Neither kingdom could eliminate the other, and the result was a stalemate that left both with their distinct bases, an agrarian dry zone and a commercial delta. The war also had costs: displacement of populations, destruction of crops and towns, and the capture of people for resettlement, whose full scale cannot be measured.

Historians have approached the war from different angles. Some emphasize the personalities of kings and commanders, as the chronicles do. Others, following the ecological framework discussed in the next section, emphasize recurring problems of terrain and supply. Still others stress the role of frontier towns and local lords, whose shifting allegiance determined the fortunes of campaigns. These approaches are not mutually exclusive, but they lead to different emphases. The present chapter treats the war as an episode in the formation of a delta kingdom, since it forced the rulers of Pegu to build alliances, to rely on defensible waterways, and to define their kingdom against an expansionist neighbor.

Section 60.11

Military Organization, Delta Geography, and the Limits of Conquest

Warfare in Lower Burma was conducted on rivers as well as on land. Boats moved troops and supplies through tidal channels, and fortified towns controlled crossings and confluences. Defensive positions, including walls, stockades, and moats, were important, and the defenders' knowledge of local waterways gave them advantages. Armies relied on elephants, infantry, and cavalry in varied proportions, and on supplies gathered from the districts through which they passed. The sources do not allow precise statements about army sizes, and this chapter does not give numbers that the evidence cannot support.

Recruitment drew on local manpower from the delta and coast, organized through lords and officials who owed service to the king. The population of Lower Burma was less densely concentrated than the irrigated heartland of Upper Burma, and its military obligations were tied to the networks of allegiance described earlier. Provisioning was a persistent problem: a king needed grain from rice districts, boats from river towns, and men from many places. The wealth of ports could buy supplies and allies, but could not always replace manpower.

Jon Fernquest's research offers a framework for relating environment to the pattern of Burman–Mon warfare. In general terms, an army effective in the dry zone, where campaigns could be organized around concentrated populations and overland movement, could face serious difficulties in the delta: flooded ground in the rainy season, tidal channels, disease risks for troops unaccustomed to the environment, and the challenge of maintaining supply lines far from base. Conversely, Lower Burmese forces could defend their waterways but struggled to establish lasting authority farther north, where they lacked the same local support and logistical base. The environment did not decide the outcome, but it constrained both sides' options.

Fortified frontier towns formed the hinge of the military system. A town on a river crossing could block the movement of an army, supply it, or serve as a base for raids, and its garrison and governor mattered as much as the armies of kings. Such towns were often held by local lords who bargained with both kingdoms, so that control was always provisional. Campaigns were therefore as much about securing the cooperation of these lords as about winning battles. This helps explain why even decisive victories did not produce lasting conquest, and why peace, when it came, took the form of mutual exhaustion and a recognition of existing positions rather than a clear settlement.

Section 60.12

Razadarit's Political Achievement and the Societies of a Wartime Kingdom

Razadarit's reign, conventionally ending in 1421, is traditionally regarded as a high point of consolidation. He is credited with defeating rivals, drawing the principal Lower Burmese centers under his authority, managing local elites, and defending the kingdom against Ava. Evaluating these achievements requires attention to how he secured loyalty: through military success, marriage and kinship, grants of office or revenue, and religious patronage. A kingdom held by such means could be strong, yet it depended on the ruler's continuing ability to reward followers and on stability at the center.

Royal revenue came from port dues, rice districts, and service. Sustaining it during prolonged war meant protecting river access and the ports, and keeping cultivators and boatmen in place. Chronicles praise his battlefield skill, but the political accomplishment lay equally in preserving a coalition of local powers over decades. The difference between the martial feats praised by chronicles and the broader social consequences of warfare is crucial: a campaign that appears as a triumph in narrative could mean displaced villages, lost harvests, and captives marched to new settlements.

The kingdom he left was not free of problems. His death in 1421 was followed by a succession of short reigns, and the political stability of the next decades was not guaranteed. Yet the institutions he preserved, the royal court at Pegu, the network of local lords, and the claim to independence from Ava, formed the basis for what followed, including the religious patronage under Shin Sawbu and Dhammazedi examined in Division IV.

The decades after Razadarit's death show how fragile the consolidation remained. His successors reigned briefly, and the chronicles record disputes and the transfer of power through several hands before Shin Sawbu took the throne. Yet the kingdom endured, which suggests that the institutions Razadarit had preserved, notably the coalition of local lords and the court's religious and commercial resources, were more durable than the persons of kings. Monasteries, too, continued to receive support, and the religious patronage of later rulers drew on the legitimacy that the earlier wars had secured by preserving independence.

Section 60.13

Theravada Buddhism and Mon Religious Institutions

Theravada Buddhism was central to the religious life of Hanthawaddy, but it was not a uniquely Mon religion, and it did not descend in a single uninterrupted line of institutions. Earlier chapters, especially Chapter 53, trace its growing influence across the mainland. In Lower Burma, as elsewhere, monks lived in communities with differing lineages, practices, and relationships to rulers. Some monasteries were village-centered, others forest-dwelling; some stressed scholarship, others meditation or ritual service. Royal patronage gave certain communities wealth and prestige, and rivalry among lineages sometimes took political form.

Monastic education centered on Pali, the language of the canonical texts, and on the study of scripture, commentary, and law. Manuscript copying, usually on palm leaf, preserved these traditions, though humidity limited survival. Ordination, the rite by which a man entered the community of monks, required a properly constituted assembly within a recognized boundary, and the legitimacy of a lineage depended on whether its ordinations were considered valid. Laypeople supported the sangha through donations of land, labor, food, and buildings, and they gained merit by doing so. Kings used these practices to present themselves as protectors of religion, a role that carried both moral prestige and political advantage.

Sri Lanka held special significance as a center of Buddhist tradition, and the prestige of its Mahavihara lineage made its ordination attractive to reformers elsewhere. Contacts between Lower Burma and the island predated the fifteenth century and traveled by sea, through the same ports that carried commerce. The diversity of monastic lineages in Hanthawaddy, and the existence of disputes over the validity of ordination, formed the background for the reforms examined in Section 60.16. Historians should not assume that all monks or laypeople shared the court's views, and the sources largely reflect the court-linked monastic elite.

Lay donation supported monasteries in practical ways. Gifts of land and of the labor of dependent families, as well as of buildings and manuscripts, are recorded in inscriptions and form part of the economic history of the delta. Such endowments created long-term relationships between donors and monasteries and removed some resources from direct royal control, which kings sometimes resented and sometimes encouraged. The relationship between the sangha and the state was thus not one of simple subordination. A monastery could gain wealth and independence through donations, and a king could try to regulate this independence through reform, as Dhammazedi's program shows.

Sangha, Sima, Upasampada, Sasana, and Merit

Sangha

Definition: The community of Buddhist monks (and, in other traditions, nuns). Why it matters: Rulers sought the sangha's support and its recognition of their legitimacy. Potential misunderstanding: The sangha was not a single unified body; lineages differed.

Sima

Definition: A consecrated boundary within which formal acts of the sangha, notably ordination, are performed. Why it matters: The Kalyani sima defined where reformed ordination could take place. Potential misunderstanding: It is a ritual boundary, not a political frontier.

Upasampada

Definition: The higher ordination that makes a man a full monk. Why it matters: Validity depended on lineage and procedure. Potential misunderstanding: Disputes about it concerned authority as well as doctrine.

Sasana

Definition: The Buddha's dispensation, meaning the religion, its teachings, and its institutions. Why it matters: Kings spoke of purifying or protecting it. Potential misunderstanding: “Purity” claims were historical arguments, not neutral facts.

Merit

Definition: Spiritual benefit earned through generous and virtuous acts, especially donation. Why it matters: It motivated temple building and endowment. Potential misunderstanding: Merit-making also expressed social status and political loyalty.

Section 60.14

Mon Language, Literacy, Court Culture, and Sacred Landscapes

Mon is an Austroasiatic language with a long written history. Scholars conventionally distinguish periods of the language, and inscriptions in what is called Middle Mon, written in a script derived from Indian models, survive from the later medieval centuries, including the Hanthawaddy period. These texts record donations, boundaries, and royal proclamations. Because they are among the few contemporary writings from the court, their linguistic and historical value is great. Mon was also used alongside Pali and Burmese, and the Kalyani Inscriptions, with texts in both Pali and Mon, illustrate this multilingual environment.

Royal and monastic patronage supported literature, religious architecture, and ceremonies. Kings endowed shrines and monasteries, sponsored copying of manuscripts, and presided over rituals that displayed their merit. Court culture drew on Indian-derived models of kingship, adapted locally, and on practices of the delta and coast. Because many surviving artifacts cannot be dated securely to a particular reign, historians must be cautious in attributing monuments, sculpture, or texts to individual rulers. Later rebuilding, especially of famous shrines, also obscures earlier forms.

Sacred geography gave Lower Burma its distinctive religious character. Pegu was associated with the Hamsavati foundation tradition and with major shrines, among them the Shwemawdaw. Dagon, later part of Rangoon, was the site of the Shwedagon, which tradition holds to enshrine relics of the Buddha and which became one of the most important Buddhist sites on the mainland. Foundation legends tie such places to Buddhist history and to the claims about Suvannabhumi and Ramannadesa examined in Section 60.17. They should be read as religious traditions that shaped how people understood their landscape, not as independent evidence for the antiquity of the sites. The cultural production of the period, from inscriptions to shrine building, shows a court and monastic community investing heavily in religious identity, even as the exact chronology of individual works often remains uncertain.

Middle Mon inscriptions are valuable not only for their content but for what they imply about literacy and language. Their existence shows that Mon was used for formal, public purposes, and their coexistence with Pali and Burmese shows that the court operated in several languages. It does not follow that every inhabitant of Lower Burma spoke Mon: Burmese, Tai, and other languages were also in use, and the use of Mon on stone reflects the choices of donors and scribes. Linguistic study of the inscriptions also helps date them, though the dating of individual texts can be difficult, and scholars continue to refine the conventional periodization of the language.

Section 60.15

Shin Sawbu: Queenly Authority, Patronage, and Political Consolidation

Shin Sawbu, whose reign is conventionally dated from 1453 to around 1471, is one of the few women to have ruled a mainland Southeast Asian kingdom in her own right. Tradition describes her as a daughter of Razadarit who, after a series of short reigns by male kin, was placed on the throne by court officials. Her accession shows that royal descent and the support of ministers and the royal family could outweigh the expectation of male succession in particular circumstances, though the sources do not explain the reasons in detail and later narratives embellish them.

Her reign is remembered as a period of stability. She is credited with maintaining order, supporting the sangha, and presiding over an active program of religious building and donation. Chronicle accounts associate her with the Shwedagon, to which she is said to have made lavish gifts and whose enlargement is credited to her, and with other Buddhist foundations. Later biographical traditions also describe her choice of a successor, the monk-turned-ruler Dhammazedi, whom some accounts present as her son-in-law, and her retirement to Dagon. These details come from narratives whose reliability varies, and the epigraphic study below shows how historians separate what donor records can prove from what later tradition adds. One firmer witness is the Kyaikmaraw I inscription, dated 1455, recording a land dedication to a shrine by Shin Sawbu, called Bañā Thau in the Middle Mon text. H. L. Shorto published the inscription in 1958. By contrast, the trilingual Shwedagon inscription of 1485 was erected on Dhammazedi's orders and records earlier patronage retrospectively; it should not be mistaken for a document issued by Shin Sawbu herself.

Her authority rested on several supports: lineage from Razadarit, the cooperation of ministers and local lords, control over revenue from ports and rice districts, and religious merit. The relative calm of her reign should not be mistaken for an absence of politics; managing a kingdom of scattered centers required continual negotiation. Her patronage helped define Pegu and Dagon as religious centers and prepared the ground for Dhammazedi's reforms. The fact of a reigning queen also deserves comparison with the experience of women in other mainland courts, though the evidence is limited and the chapter does not assume that her status implied broad changes in women's position.

The sources for Shin Sawbu's reign require particular care because the later image of an idealized, pious queen suited the purposes of subsequent writers. Her reign was long by the standards of the period and apparently peaceful, but chronicles of peace are often thin, and the absence of recorded war should not be taken as evidence of political simplicity. Comparisons with other women who held power in Southeast Asian courts show that female rule was possible in particular dynastic situations, but each case had its own circumstances, and the evidence for Shin Sawbu cannot be used to generalize about women's status in Lower Burma.

Section 60.16

Dhammazedi, Sri Lanka, and the Kalyani Ordination Reform

Dhammazedi, conventionally reigning from 1471 to 1492, came to the throne after a career in the monastic community, according to tradition, and his reign is the best documented of the kingdom. His rule combined political management with an ambitious religious program. He patronized Buddhist institutions, supported learning, and undertook a reform of ordination that linked Pegu with Sri Lanka and made the royal court the guardian of a purified lineage. The reform began in the 1470s: a mission of monks traveled to Sri Lanka, received ordination there according to the island's tradition, and returned to Pegu, where the Kalyani sima was established in 1476.

The reform must be distinguished from the inscriptions that memorialize it. The activities of the mid-1470s are the reform itself; the documentary complex of the Kalyani Inscriptions is conventionally associated with 1476–1479, with differences in scholarly references reflecting the stages of the program and the dating of the stones. Taw Sein Ko's 1892 edition, The Kalyānī Inscriptions Erected by King Dhammaceti at Pegu in 1476 A.D., uses the earlier date in its title, and students should recognize that later study has refined the chronology. The inscriptions are written in Pali and Mon on stone slabs and provide a royal account of the monks' journey, the establishment of the boundaries, and a history of Buddhism that places Lower Burma within it.

The reform served religious and political purposes at once. It aimed to unify the diverse monastic lineages of Lower Burma under a single recognized ordination, resolving disputes about validity. By receiving ordination from Sri Lankan authorities, the court tied its monks to a prestigious tradition. By defining the sima and requiring that monks be ordained within it, the king also positioned himself as the arbiter of religious legitimacy. Claims of restoring Buddhist purity thus reinforced royal authority. The reform also shows the importance of maritime links: monks traveled by ship, using the same routes as merchants, and Sri Lankan connections gave the reform its legitimacy. Later consequences spread beyond Pegu, since reformed lineages influenced monastic networks elsewhere on the mainland, though the extent and pace of that influence were uneven and are examined in other chapters.

Sri Lanka's role deserves emphasis. The Sri Lankan monastic community, whose ordination lineage was widely regarded as authoritative, provided the validation that the Pegu court sought, and Sri Lankan ritual practice and textual tradition shaped the form of the reform. The fifteenth-century reform was not the first contact between Burma and Sri Lankan Buddhism, but it was among the most carefully documented and politically significant. Its long-term influence on monastic lineages, and the way the Kalyani sima became a reference point in later Burmese religious history, show that the reform had effects beyond the reign of its sponsor, though those later developments belong to other chapters.

Section 60.17

Ancient Ramannadesa, Mon Historical Memory, and the Problem of Royal Legitimacy

Traditions about Lower Burma's ancient past connect the region with Ramannadesa, with the city of Thaton, and with Suvannabhumi, the “land of gold” of Buddhist and Indian texts. In these accounts, Buddhist missionaries brought the religion to Lower Burma in the time of the emperor Ashoka, and a Mon kingdom of Thaton preserved the Pali scriptures until it was conquered by Pagan's king Anawrahta, who carried its learning northward. This narrative gave Lower Burma an ancient Buddhist pedigree and a place at the origin of Burmese Buddhism.

The historical status of these claims is contested. Michael Aung-Thwin, in The Mists of Rāmañña (2005) and in later work, argues that important elements of the tradition acquired their recognizable form through later religious and political projects, especially under Dhammazedi, whose Kalyani Inscriptions present a history of Buddhism that includes ancient missions to Suvannabhumi. On this reading, the “legend that was Lower Burma” was a construction that served to legitimate the Pegu court and later shaped chronicles and modern histories. Other scholarship preserves a stronger emphasis on long-standing Mon cultural and settlement networks, on the evidence of Mon-language epigraphy and material culture in earlier periods, and on the plausibility that some historical memory underlies the traditions. The identification of Suvannabhumi with Lower Burma is not an established geographical fact; the term was used in Indian texts for a distant, golden land, and scholars have proposed locations across mainland and island Southeast Asia.

Dhammazedi's reforms and the chronicles that followed shaped Mon royal historical memory. The Kalyani Inscriptions tied the court to ancient missions and to Sri Lankan authority, and later Mon and Burmese chroniclers adapted these claims. Distinguishing the history of Mon-speaking communities from the history of a Mon-centered state is therefore essential. Mon speakers have a long documented presence in Lower Burma and beyond; whether a continuous Mon kingdom existed from antiquity to Hanthawaddy is a separate question, answered differently depending on how one reads the evidence.

The older historiography, much of it written in the colonial period, accepted the Thaton story as the history of a Mon kingdom conquered by Pagan, and it described Pagan's debt to Mon culture as a settled fact. Later scholarship, informed by closer study of inscriptions, chronologies, and the sources of the traditions, has reassessed these claims, and the debate summarized in this section is part of that reassessment. The significance of the debate extends beyond medieval history, since ideas about Mon antiquity have also entered modern discussions of identity. The responsible course for a student is to separate the questions of language and community from those of statehood and to ask of each claim what evidence supports it and when it first appears.

Historical Debate

Ancient Mon Kingdom or Later Historical Construction?

The question: Did Hanthawaddy inherit an ancient, continuous Mon Buddhist realm in Lower Burma, or did later courts and chroniclers construct that ancient past to legitimate their own rule?

Interpretation A: a later construction. Aung-Thwin and scholars who follow his approach argue that the Ramannadesa tradition, as an account of a unified ancient kingdom, took form in fifteenth-century and later religious and political projects, notably under Dhammazedi, and that earlier independent evidence for such a kingdom is thin.
Interpretation B: continuity of Mon cultural networks. Other scholars emphasize long-standing Mon-speaking settlement, religion, and literacy in Lower Burma and see the traditions as preserving some real memory, even if embellished, while accepting that political unity was not continuous.

Evidence: Both sides use the Kalyani Inscriptions, Mon-language epigraphy, chronicles, archaeological evidence, and the testimony of Indian and Sri Lankan Buddhist texts, but they weigh the age and independence of the sources differently.

Points of disagreement: The debate concerns chronology (when traditions arose), interpretation of the sources, terminology (what “Mon kingdom” means), and scale (how much continuity can be shown). It is not a dispute over whether Mon speakers lived in Lower Burma.

Why it matters: How historians answer shapes whether Hanthawaddy is read as the heir of an ancient state or as a fifteenth-century political and religious achievement, and how Mon identity and history are understood today.

Student task: Identify what kinds of evidence, such as securely dated pre-fourteenth-century inscriptions, could clarify whether the tradition is older than Dhammazedi, and explain why neither a complete acceptance nor a complete rejection of the traditions is required.

Section 60.18

Hanthawaddy's Commercial and Diplomatic World in the Late Fifteenth Century

By the late fifteenth century, Hanthawaddy's ports and court were connected with a broad network of exchange and diplomacy. Contacts with Sri Lanka, strengthened by the Kalyani reform, joined commercial links with eastern India and the Bay of Bengal. Relations with the Malay Peninsula and with Ayutthaya, the central Thai kingdom examined in Chapter 57, involved trade, diplomacy, and at times rivalry. The Islamic commercial world of the Indian Ocean, whose growth Chapters 62 to 66 examine, also included merchants who visited Lower Burmese harbors.

Merchant communities, among them Indian, Arab, Persian, Malay, and Tai traders, used the ports, and Buddhist monks, pilgrims, and ambassadors moved along similar routes. The court's interest in these exchanges was political as well as economic: maintaining access to ports and shipping routes was essential to revenue and to the monastic ties that supported royal legitimacy. Diplomacy might take the form of gifts, letters, and missions, with the exchange of monks and texts serving as a distinctive channel of international contact.

The evidence requires care. Direct fifteenth-century documentation for trade is limited, and the better-documented commerce of the sixteenth century, described by Portuguese observers and later by Dutch and other sources, cannot simply be projected backward. Ming records and the Kalyani Inscriptions confirm some contacts, and the later accounts may reflect continuities, but they also reflect changes, including the arrival of European ships. A careful account therefore describes a plausible network of ports and routes while acknowledging that volumes, prices, and organization are mostly beyond reach. What is clear is that Hanthawaddy's rulers did not merely receive foreigners but acted as patrons of religious exchange, regulators of ports, and diplomats in their own right.

Buddhist exchange was a distinctive channel of diplomacy. Monks and texts traveled between Pegu and Sri Lanka, and such exchanges conveyed prestige, legitimacy, and information. The court's reception of Sri Lankan monks, and its dispatch of Burmese monks to the island, were acts of religious diplomacy that also served to display the kingdom's resources and standing. Commercial and religious travel shared ships and routes, and the same harbors that handled cargo received pilgrims. The relationship between trade and religion should therefore not be treated as separate spheres: religious networks followed commercial ones and added to them, and rulers who profited from commerce could use religion to enhance their status.

Section 60.19

Hanthawaddy around 1500: Stability, Prosperity, and Political Limits

Dhammazedi died, conventionally, in 1492 and was succeeded by Binnya Ran II, whose reign extended into the early sixteenth century. The kingdom he inherited had a well-established court at Pegu, a network of ports and rice districts, a reformed Buddhist establishment, and a reputation as a center of religious learning. By conventional accounts it enjoyed relative stability and prosperity. Because the surviving sources are heavily religious and royal, the picture is partial, but it is consistent with the evidence of the Kalyani Inscriptions and the continuing commerce of the Bay of Bengal.

The kingdom's strength rested on the interaction of several resources. Port revenues provided funds for patronage and defense; agricultural districts supplied food and labor; religious patronage bound the sangha to the court; and relationships with local lords and neighboring rulers helped preserve autonomy. Frontier diplomacy with Ava and with Tai and Arakanese neighbors aimed at preventing renewed conflict. The kingdom did not need to conquer in order to survive, and its rulers invested in religion and commerce rather than in expansion.

There were limits. Authority over scattered districts depended on continuing negotiation, and the delta's political fragmentation never disappeared. Revenue from ports could fluctuate, and the neighboring kingdoms retained their own strengths. Historians must avoid reading the later conquest of Lower Burma by the Toungoo dynasty in the sixteenth century, examined in Chapter 84, or the sixteenth-century Portuguese presence, examined in Chapter 68, back into the situation around 1500. For people living in the 1490s and the first years of the new century, Hanthawaddy was a stable and respected kingdom whose future was open. The chapter therefore stops at this point and does not narrate the events of 1539.

Local autonomy remained an important feature of the kingdom. Governors of provincial towns held authority in the king's name, but their loyalty depended on rewards and on the king's reputation. A strong ruler could command them; a weak or disputed succession could allow them to assert independence. The long reigns of Shin Sawbu and Dhammazedi gave stability to this system, and the death of a king always raised the question of whether the coalition would hold. Historians should keep in mind that stability and prosperity, in this context, did not mean the absence of risk, and that the outcome in later decades was shaped by events that lay outside the period of this chapter.

Section 60.20

Hanthawaddy and the Lower Burma World: Commerce, Kingship, Buddhism, and Historical Memory

The kingdom's development ran from a Martaban-centered political order of the late thirteenth century to a Pegu-centered kingdom of the fifteenth. In that time rulers consolidated scattered ports and districts, resisted Ava through a long war, and built a court that combined royal authority with Buddhist patronage. The decisive features were the combination of delta agriculture and maritime commerce, the management of local elites, and the use of religious institutions to express legitimacy. Mon-language and religious institutions gave the court a distinctive profile, but the population and the political order were not exclusively Mon.

Shin Sawbu and Dhammazedi gave the kingdom a particular religious identity. Their patronage and reform made Pegu a leading center of mainland Theravada Buddhism and tied it to Sri Lanka and the wider Buddhist world. At the same time, the Kalyani Inscriptions and later chronicles shaped how the kingdom was remembered, projecting an ancient pedigree and a story of unbroken Mon kingship. Later historians, including colonial and Burmese writers, transformed the kingdom's reputation again, sometimes treating it as a commercial or ethnic polity in contrast to Ava, a simplification that this chapter has tried to avoid.

Later historiography shaped the kingdom's reputation in contrasting ways. Burmese royal chronicles tended to treat Lower Burma as a subordinate or rival realm, while Mon traditions presented it as a center of religion and culture, and modern writers have drawn on both. Colonial scholarship sometimes read the contrast between Mon and Burmese as a stable ethnic opposition, a reading that recent work has questioned. For students, the chapter's central lesson is methodological as well as historical: to understand a kingdom, one must understand both what it did and how it has been remembered, and to keep the two distinct while recognizing that each shapes the other.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the Mon-centered kingdom of Hanthawaddy developed in Lower Burma after Pagan's fragmentation. Its central argument is that Hanthawaddy emerged through the consolidation of regional centers linked by delta waterways and maritime trade, and that its rulers drew on Mon-speaking communities, local elites, agricultural resources, merchant networks, military power, and Buddhist institutions to establish a kingdom that resisted Ava and became a major center of mainland Theravada Buddhism.

The chapter began with sources. Inscriptions are contemporary but religious and elite; chronicles are rich but retrospective, copied, and embellished; foreign records are brief; and archaeology survives unevenly. These limits led to a key distinction between a Martaban-centered political order, conventionally founded by Wareru in 1287, and a Pegu-centered kingdom from about 1369, a distinction challenged and refined in Michael Aung-Thwin's work.

Geography shaped everything. The Irrawaddy delta, the Sittang basin, the Bago River, and the Gulf of Martaban offered rice, fish, and ports, but also shifting channels and scattered districts that made political integration difficult. Martaban, Pegu, and Bassein had different ecological and commercial settings, and the monsoon governed seasonal navigation and campaigning.

Wareru's rise is known through founder traditions that connect him with the Tai world and Sukhothai; they must be treated critically, and the early polity was ethnically and linguistically mixed. Early authority was layered and negotiated, relying on kinship, tribute, and patronage. The thirty-two myeo tradition and the Wareru Dhammathat show how later generations imagined order and justice, without proving that every detail dates to the founder.

Wareru's successors faced instability, but Binnya U consolidated authority and moved the principal seat to Pegu around 1369, a political reorientation toward the rice districts and the eastern delta. Maritime commerce, including the trade in so-called Martaban jars, brought revenue, yet agriculture and ports interacted, and fifteenth-century evidence must be distinguished from later Portuguese and Dutch descriptions. Everyday life, centered on rice, fishing, boatbuilding, and markets, is reconstructed indirectly.

Razadarit's accession and reign brought the unification of Lower Burma and a long war with Ava. From Hanthawaddy's side, the war was a defense of Pegu, Martaban, and the western delta, a struggle over frontier towns, and a contest shaped by military ecology. The Razadarit Ayedawbon preserves political memory but is a retrospective, literary narrative. The perspectives of soldiers, merchants, cultivators, captives, and monks show that royal achievement came with social costs.

Buddhist institutions, Pali learning, and Mon-language inscriptions gave the court a distinctive religious profile. Shin Sawbu, a reigning queen, is directly attested by the 1455 Kyaikmaraw land dedication and associated with the Shwedagon in later testimony; subsequent biographies embellish. Dhammazedi's reform, beginning in the 1470s with a mission to Sri Lanka and the establishment of the Kalyani sima in 1476, and recorded in inscriptions conventionally dated 1476–1479, tied Pegu to Sri Lankan lineages and reinforced royal authority.

The tradition of ancient Ramannadesa and Suvannabhumi remains debated. Aung-Thwin argues that it took form through later religious and political projects, especially under Dhammazedi, while other scholars emphasize long-standing Mon networks. Identification of Suvannabhumi with Lower Burma is not an established fact, and the history of Mon-speaking communities should be separated from the history of a Mon-centered state.

Around 1500, Hanthawaddy was a stable and respected kingdom connected to Sri Lanka, the Bay of Bengal, and neighboring states, though its authority depended on negotiation with local lords. The chapter has avoided projecting the Toungoo conquest or sixteenth-century European commerce backward. The kingdom's significance lies in its combination of port-centered commerce, delta agriculture, military power, and Buddhist legitimacy, and in the way later memory shaped its reputation.

Chapter Reflection

Hanthawaddy matters because it shows how a mainland kingdom could be built from water, trade, rice, alliance, and religion rather than from a single dominant agrarian core. It complicates any view of Southeast Asian polities as either passive recipients of outside influence or products of timeless ethnic nations. Its rulers selected and adapted Sri Lankan Buddhist authority, managed maritime exchange, and resisted a powerful neighbor.

The chapter also demonstrates how historical memory is made. Foundation stories, ancient pedigrees, and royal chronicles shaped the way later generations understood the kingdom, and modern historians have had to separate documented history from legitimation. The Kalyani Inscriptions are valuable precisely because they are both evidence and an act of political and religious construction.

Later developments built on these foundations. Lower Burma's ports and Buddhist institutions continued to matter in the sixteenth century, when new powers, examined beginning in Chapter 68 and Chapter 84, transformed the political landscape. The contrast between maritime Lower Burma and agrarian Upper Burma remained a recurring theme in mainland history, though it should never be treated as a fixed destiny.

In the larger textbook, this chapter pairs with Chapter 59 to show two different paths of recovery after Pagan, and it prepares the transition to Chapter 61, where another mainland kingdom was transforming. Together they illustrate the diversity of the Theravada mainland and the importance of reading each society on its own terms.
