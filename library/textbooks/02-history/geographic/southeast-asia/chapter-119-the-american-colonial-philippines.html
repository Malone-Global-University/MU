The American Colonial Philippines

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Between 1901 and 1914 the United States turned a war of conquest into a working colonial state in the Philippines, with schools, courts, a constabulary, municipal councils, an elected assembly, and a customs union with the American market. Filipinos staffed, used, bargained with, criticized, and sometimes fought these institutions. The same structures that secured foreign sovereignty also trained the leaders and supplied the arguments that would be turned against it.

On July 4, 1901, in Manila, William Howard Taft took the oath as the first American civil governor of the Philippine Islands. The ceremony was meant to mark a change in the character of American rule, from the authority of army officers to the authority of civilian officials operating under law. It did not mean that the fighting was over. Armed resistance continued in several regions, and the army kept its own governor in districts not yet declared pacified. The new civil government and the old military campaign therefore existed at the same time, and much of this chapter concerns how the two were joined.

The preceding chapter, Chapter 118, examines the Philippine-American War itself: conventional and guerrilla campaigns, repression, atrocities, and disease. This chapter begins where that account leaves off, with the question of how conquest became administration. The answer lies in a set of legal instruments and institutions created within a few years, most of them in the first months of 1901. They include the municipal and provincial governments, the Judiciary Act and the Supreme Court, the Philippine Constabulary, the public school system, the Philippine Organic Act of 1902, land and tariff legislation, and finally the elections of 1907 and the opening of the first Philippine Assembly.

Three features of this history make it easy to misdescribe. First, it is tempting to tell it as a simple story of benevolent modernization, in which American teachers, engineers, and physicians improved a backward colony. That account takes American self-description at face value and treats Filipinos as recipients. Second, it is equally tempting to tell it as pure domination, in which every school and road was a device of subjugation. That account ignores the real demand of Filipino families for schooling, the real effects of sanitation and public health work, and the active use Filipino politicians made of every opening. The chapter avoids both versions by treating colonial institutions as arenas in which unequal parties pursued different aims.

Third, geography and class mattered enormously. The colonial experience of an educated Manila lawyer was very different from that of a tenant farmer in Central Luzon, an Ifugao rice cultivator, or a Tausug household in Sulu. Muslim Mindanao and Sulu and the Cordillera of northern Luzon were governed under special arrangements that differed sharply from the lowland provinces, a point developed in Section 119.16. The population recorded by the census of 1903, about 7.6 million people, did not encounter “American rule” as a single thing.

Historians draw on a wide range of evidence for this period. Legislative acts of the Philippine Commission and the United States Congress survive in print and are now available through official legal repositories. The annual reports of the Philippine Commission, the Bureau of Education, the Bureau of Health, and the Constabulary supply statistics and administrators' self-portraits. Newspapers in English, Spanish, and Philippine languages record public argument. Photographs and exposition publications show how Americans chose to display Filipinos. Filipino voices are present in petitions, editorials, party platforms, and later memoirs, but they are thinner for rural tenants, women, and highland and Muslim communities, so absences in the record need to be kept in mind throughout.

The chapter also takes up the arguments historians have developed about this period. Paul A. Kramer has stressed how race shaped colonial policy and the representation of Filipinos; Warwick Anderson has examined colonial medicine and hygiene as instruments of both care and classification; Alfred W. McCoy has emphasized policing and surveillance in building the colonial state; Benedict Anderson characterized the resulting political order as “cacique democracy,” in which provincial landed elites captured the new electoral institutions. Others, notably Glenn A. May, have questioned how far American social engineering actually transformed Philippine society. These positions are introduced where they bear on the evidence rather than at the end of the chapter.

The chronological focus is roughly 1901 to 1914. Developments after 1913, especially the Jones Act of 1916, the growth of a Filipino-led administration, independence negotiations, and the Commonwealth, belong to Chapter 131. The comparative economic history of plantations and mines across Southeast Asia belongs to Chapter 122. Chapter 119 concentrates on the early framework, and its central argument is a paradox: the colonial government expanded education, administration, and representation while retaining foreign sovereignty, and those expansions in turn strengthened Filipino claims to self-government.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace the transition from American military occupation to civil government between 1900 and 1902, and explain why the two overlapped.
  2. Explain the structure and powers of the Philippine Commission and the Philippine Organic Act of 1902, distinguishing legal rights, representation, and sovereignty.
  3. Analyze how municipal and provincial governments, courts, and the Constabulary worked together as a system of colonial administration.
  4. Evaluate the purposes and effects of the public school system created by Act No. 74, including the role of English and of Filipino teachers.
  5. Assess American policy toward the Roman Catholic Church and the significance of the Iglesia Filipina Independiente and Protestant missions.
  6. Describe public-health and sanitation campaigns and assess both their benefits and their coercive and unequal features.
  7. Examine infrastructure investment and explain whose interests shaped its location and purpose.
  8. Explain how tariff legislation of 1902, 1909, and 1913 tied Philippine exports to the American market and created economic dependence.
  9. Interpret the friar-land purchases and land legislation, and explain why they did not produce broad peasant landownership.
  10. Analyze the 1907 elections, the First Philippine Assembly, and the political parties, with attention to franchise limits and elite power.
  11. Analyze racial ideology in American colonial rule, including the 1904 St. Louis exhibition, and Filipino responses.
  12. Compare colonial governance in lowland Luzon, the Cordillera, and Muslim Mindanao and Sulu, and evaluate the question of whether early institutions consolidated empire or prepared self-government.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did a military conquest become an enduring civilian colonial government, and what did American officials hope civilian rule would accomplish?
  2. What is the difference between legal protection, political representation, and sovereignty, and why does the difference matter for the Organic Act?
  3. Why did American authorities recruit Filipino elites, including former revolutionaries, into colonial institutions, and what did those elites gain and risk?
  4. How did schools, hospitals, and sanitation campaigns serve both genuine public needs and colonial purposes at the same time?
  5. Who benefited from infrastructure, preferential trade, and land reform, and who did not?
  6. How did a very limited franchise shape the character of the First Philippine Assembly and the parties that contested it?
  7. How did racial classification shape American policy and the way Filipinos were shown to American audiences?
  8. Why was colonial rule so different in the Cordillera, Mindanao, and Sulu from rule in the lowland Christian provinces?
  9. How should historians distinguish collaboration, accommodation, and strategic participation in colonial institutions?
  10. Did institutions created to sustain foreign rule more strongly consolidate it or prepare their own supersession?

Historical Context

American rule rested on a prior revolution and a prior colonial order. Spanish rule in the Philippines, examined in Chapters 73 through 76 and 115, had left a parish-centered administration, powerful religious orders that held large landed estates, a Chinese-mestizo and indigenous landowning elite, and an export economy that had grown rapidly in the nineteenth century. The Philippine Revolution of 1896 to 1898, examined in Chapter 117, produced a revolutionary government and the short-lived republic proclaimed at Malolos in 1899. By then the Spanish-American War had brought American forces to the islands, and in the Treaty of Paris of December 1898 Spain ceded the archipelago to the United States for a payment of twenty million dollars. Filipino leaders were not parties to the treaty. The war between the Philippine Republic and the United States followed, as Chapter 118 describes.

By early 1901 conventional resistance had collapsed and the war had become a dispersed guerrilla conflict. The capture of Emilio Aguinaldo in March 1901 and his oath of allegiance to the United States encouraged many officers to surrender. Resistance nevertheless continued in parts of Luzon, in the Visayas, and especially in Samar, where the aftermath of the Balangiga attack of September 1901 shaped American conduct. In July 1902 President Theodore Roosevelt proclaimed the insurrection ended and announced an amnesty. The proclamation did not extend to the Muslim south, where fighting of a different kind continued.

American policymakers disagreed about what the Philippines were for. Some wanted a permanent possession and naval base on the edge of Asia; some argued that annexation violated American republican principles; others saw trade and strategic advantage. President William McKinley's proclamation of “benevolent assimilation” in December 1898 had already framed the occupation as a project of tutelage. In 1900 the Second Philippine Commission, headed by Taft, arrived with instructions to build civil institutions, and the Spooner Amendment of March 1901 authorized the president to exercise civil authority until Congress acted. Congress passed no definitive statute until 1902. In the interval, a great deal of colonial law was written by executive instruction and by the Commission itself.

Students should keep in mind several structural facts. The Philippines had no single constitutional status in American law. Federal courts later described the islands as unincorporated territory, so that not every provision of the Constitution applied. The Philippine government, called the Insular Government, was financed largely from local revenues, especially customs duties. The apparatus relied on an American civil service at senior levels and on Filipino clerks, teachers, police, and local officials at lower ones. And the colony was divided by religion, language, class, and region in ways that American planners understood only partly and often used for their own ends.

Section 119.1 · Division I — Building the American Colonial State

From Military Occupation to Civil Government

American authority in the Philippines began as a military occupation. After the capture of Manila in August 1898, army commanders issued regulations, collected customs, ran municipal affairs, and fought the Philippine Republic. Two bodies were sent to study and then to govern. The First Philippine Commission, led by Jacob Gould Schurman of Cornell University, investigated conditions in 1899 and recommended a civil government with significant local participation. The Second Commission, headed by Taft, arrived in June 1900. On September 1, 1900, it took over legislative authority from the military governor, Major General Arthur MacArthur, while the army remained responsible for executive power and for the war.

The reasons for the change were practical as well as ideological. Officers who had handled municipal affairs reported that local cooperation depended on visible institutions of ordinary government, and American officials argued that only a civilian regime offering offices, schools, and legal rights could separate moderate Filipinos from the armed movement. Political considerations at home mattered as well. Critics of annexation in the United States described the islands as ruled by force, and a civilian government with schools and elections gave the administration an answer. Taft was also convinced that the Filipino elite would cooperate with a government that offered power in return for acceptance of American sovereignty, a belief that proved partly right.

The transition was gradual and uneven. In March 1901 the Spooner Amendment authorized the president to establish civil government. On July 4, 1901, Taft became civil governor, while General Adna Chaffee, who had replaced MacArthur, remained military governor for areas where resistance continued. Provinces were transferred from military to civil control one at a time as they were judged pacified, so that in 1901 and 1902 a visitor might cross from a province governed by civilians to one under army commanders. Where civil government was established, the military often continued to supply garrisons and to assist the new Constabulary. The army also kept control of the Muslim regions of Mindanao and Sulu, which were not declared under civil control and were placed under a distinct military-led structure in 1903 (Section 119.16).

The Commission worked with extraordinary speed. By the end of 1901 it had enacted hundreds of laws, covering the civil service, municipal and provincial government, the judiciary, education, taxation, public works, and the police. The first Filipino Commissioners, Benito Legarda, José Luzuriaga, and Trinidad Pardo de Tavera, were added in 1901, though Americans held a majority and the key departments. The principle was one of the clearest features of early rule: Filipinos could be included in government, but the American president and Congress held final control.

Historians have asked what this civil government actually was. Julian Go and Anne Foster, among others, have described it as a colonial state created through a tension between American ideals of self-government and the realities of rule over a population that did not consent. The decision to rely on Filipino cooperation shaped everything that followed, because it required offices to give and a legal framework that could be defended as just.

Colonial authority rested on a chain of delegations that could be difficult to reconcile with the language of popular government. Congress possessed ultimate authority over the acquired territory; the president acted through an executive apparatus in Washington; and the Philippine Commission legislated in Manila while provincial and municipal officers administered the resulting rules. Decisions that appeared local—whether to levy a municipal tax, fund a schoolhouse, or transfer a district from military jurisdiction—could therefore depend on an official far removed from the people affected. This arrangement also created rivalries among American administrators. Military commanders, whose first priority was security, could regard an early transfer to civilian rule as premature, while civilians argued that unending military administration perpetuated hostility. The transfer of a provincial capital to civil rule did not necessarily mark the disappearance of patrols, political prisoners, or guerrilla sympathizers in its hinterland.

There were practical difficulties in creating a bureaucracy during wartime. The state needed interpreters, surveyors, bookkeepers, school supervisors, judges, and constables, and it could not employ Americans in every subordinate post. The colonial civil service consequently became a venue for Filipino advancement, with appointments and examinations presented as safeguards against favoritism. Yet recruiting locally also created the risk, from the commissioners' perspective, that government employees would place family, provincial, or nationalist loyalties ahead of instructions from Manila. For Filipino applicants the calculation was different: a government salary might provide family security, mastery of new administrative methods, and an opportunity to influence the treatment of neighbors. These choices should not be read backward as settled judgments about ultimate independence. They were made amid political uncertainty about how long American rule would last and what institutions Filipinos might eventually control.

Historical Vocabulary
Colonial sovereignty, military occupation, civil government, insular government, centralization, legitimacy

Colonial sovereignty means ultimate legal authority held by a distant state over a territory whose inhabitants do not control that authority. Military occupation is rule by an army, bound by applicable laws of war as well as military regulation. Civil government is administration by civilian officials under written law. Insular Government is the official name of the colonial government of the Philippines, taken from the American term “insular possessions.” Administrative centralization describes the concentration of authority in Manila, in contrast to the parish-based and local arrangements that preceded it. Political legitimacy is the acceptance of authority as rightful; colonial governments always faced a legitimacy problem because the governed had not chosen them.

Potential misunderstanding: “Civil government” did not mean self-government. The Commission was appointed by the American president, and civilian rule coexisted with armed coercion.

Section 119.2 · Division I

The Philippine Commission and the Philippine Organic Act of 1902

The Philippine Organic Act, passed by Congress and signed on July 1, 1902, and often called the Cooper Act, supplied the first statutory framework for civil government. Until then the Commission's authority rested on presidential instructions and the Spooner Amendment. The Act approved and confirmed the actions of the president and of the Commission, declared that the inhabitants of the islands were citizens of the Philippine Islands entitled to the protection of the United States, and set out the structure of the government.

The executive was a governor, formally styled Governor-General from the mid-1900s, appointed by the president with the consent of the Senate. The Commission was similarly appointed, and it acted as the legislature and, through its American members, as the heads of the executive departments, among them the Interior, Commerce and Police, Finance and Justice, and Public Instruction. The Act also provided for a second chamber. After a census of the islands had been taken and peace had been maintained for two years, a Philippine Assembly would be elected, and the legislature would then consist of the Assembly as the lower house and the Commission as the upper house. Two Resident Commissioners, chosen by the legislature, would represent the islands in Washington as non-voting delegates. Acts of the legislature remained subject to the veto of the governor and to annulment by Congress.

The Act also contained a bill of rights. Most protections found in the American Constitution were extended to the islands, such as due process, freedom of speech and the press, freedom of religion, and prohibitions on slavery. It omitted trial by jury and the right to bear arms. This was a significant choice. The first reflected the view that Filipino jurors could not be relied upon; the second reflected the view that civilian arms were a threat to order. The Act also dealt with land. It limited the amount of public land that could be sold or leased to individuals and corporations, and it authorized the government to buy the friar estates, a matter treated in Section 119.11. A section on franchises and concessions required congressional oversight.

It is important to separate three things that the Act joined together. Legal protection meant that the government could not lawfully do certain things to individuals. Political representation meant the possibility of electing some officials. Sovereignty meant ultimate control over the law itself. The Organic Act gave the first fairly broadly, the second in strictly limited form, and the third not at all. Filipino leaders therefore saw it as an advance and a reminder of subordination at the same moment. The Sedition Act, Act No. 292, had already been enacted on November 4, 1901, before the Organic Act. It criminalized specified forms of sedition and, during the legally defined condition of war or insurrection, certain advocacy of Philippine independence; the Brigandage Act followed in 1902. That chronology, and the tension between formal guarantees and coercive legislation, recur throughout this chapter.

The delay in creating the Assembly shows how conditional representation was. The census was taken in 1903, but a formal declaration of peace was needed, and the Assembly was not elected until 1907. In the meantime the Commission remained the only legislature, and its Filipino members were appointed rather than elected.

The legal settlement was provisional in a deeper sense than the word organic sometimes suggests. The Philippine Islands were not brought into the Union on the same terms as an American state. The Supreme Court's Insular Cases developed the doctrine that certain territories could belong to the United States without being incorporated into it, a distinction that shaped constitutional questions concerning tariffs and legal safeguards. The doctrine did not resolve the moral argument about consent; rather, it enabled the courts and Congress to exercise considerable discretion about which federal constitutional requirements applied. Filipino lawyers and legislators had reason to study that distinction closely, for it meant that rights established by statute could be interpreted or altered within a hierarchy controlled outside the islands.

Institutional duality made everyday legislation especially revealing. A proposed appropriation for a school, a road, or a court might command support in the elected Assembly after 1907 but still require approval from the appointed Commission. An impasse over the budget was not merely procedural: it exposed the mismatch between electoral claims to represent the population and the constitutional power reserved to Washington's appointees. The bill of rights, meanwhile, did not by itself guarantee a neutral legal environment. Officials could invoke public order in prosecuting alleged sedition or organizing constabulary surveillance, and defendants could argue that such measures contravened the promised liberties. Historians should examine the precise dates and texts of these measures: the Sedition Act, Act No. 292, was passed in November 1901, before the Organic Act, and its independence-advocacy restriction was tied to the official condition of war or insurrection. A legal guarantee and the actual conditions under which a person could exercise it were not the same historical fact.

Primary Source Window

The Philippine Organic Act of July 1, 1902

Creator: The Congress of the United States, with a framework worked out with the Taft Commission · Date: July 1, 1902 · Type: Statute establishing a colonial constitutional framework

Traceable source records: Philippine Organic Act of 1902, Official Gazette constitutional archive · Act No. 292 (Sedition Act, November 4, 1901), Philippine Senate

Context: Congress passed the Act after prolonged debate between those who wanted permanent retention of the islands, those who wished for eventual independence, and those who objected to annexation entirely. The text was written in Washington, not Manila, and Filipino leaders did not take part in drafting it.

Paraphrase (not a verbatim excerpt):

The Act confirms the earlier acts of the president and the Commission; declares the inhabitants citizens of the Philippine Islands under United States protection; extends a list of civil rights while omitting jury trial and the bearing of arms; keeps appointment of the governor and Commission in presidential hands; provides for an elected Assembly after a census and two years of peace; and provides for two Resident Commissioners in Washington.

Analysis: The text shows how a colonial constitution could define rights and representation while locating power elsewhere. It also shows the American assumption that self-government was a stage to be reached after tutelage and demonstrated peace.

Limitations: The statute shows intention, not practice. It does not reveal how rights were enforced in provincial courts or how often the Sedition Act and similar laws limited them.

Perspective: The source expresses the perspective of the American legislature. It does not record Filipino views of the Act, which appear in newspapers, petitions, and political speeches.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the Act say about rights, representation, and sovereignty?
  2. Who wrote it, and in what circumstances?
  3. What purposes might have shaped the omission of jury trial and the right to bear arms?
  4. What can historians learn about American assumptions from the conditions attached to the Assembly?
  5. What cannot be established from the statute alone?

Section 119.3 · Division I

Municipal Government, Provincial Administration, and Filipino Political Elites

Local government was the point at which most Filipinos met the colonial state. The Municipal Code (Act No. 82, January 1901) established elected municipal councils and presidents in towns, and the Provincial Government Act (Act No. 83, February 1901) established provincial boards. Municipal officers were chosen by voters who met qualifications of property, taxation, previous office, or literacy; in a typical town these voters were a small minority of adult men. In the provinces the governor was chosen by the municipal councilors, while the provincial treasurer and supervisor were American appointees who held the financial and engineering functions. The result was a hybrid: Filipino elected officials held visible local power, but Americans controlled revenue and public works.

The new structures drew on a base of existing notables. Under Spanish rule, the principalia, a local elite of past officeholders and landowners, had governed towns. Americans did not invent local elite power; they reorganized and in many cases confirmed it. Former revolutionaries were prominent among the first governors and presidents, often because they commanded the loyalty of their districts. Civil officials considered this a feature rather than a problem. Offering office to men who had fought against the United States was a way of demonstrating that the new order was open and of making them guarantors of order.

The bargain was mutual, and it was not equal. Filipino notables gained legal office, access to patronage, and the ability to shape local taxation and public works. In return their authority became bound up with an American-controlled administration. Local elected officials were expected to collect taxes, maintain roads, report to American supervisors, and, in many places, assist the Constabulary. Benedict Anderson later argued that this arrangement amounted to “cacique democracy,” in which a narrow landowning class used electoral institutions to extend its own power. That interpretation fits much evidence, though it can underplay the real contests among elite families and the pressure some officials placed on the American supervisors.

The reach of these institutions was uneven. In provinces where the war had been fiercest or the population was scattered, administrative reports describe weak councils, incomplete tax rolls, and officials who did not meet the American standards of record-keeping. In the Cordillera and the Muslim south, local rule was organized quite differently (Section 119.16). Even in the lowlands, local custom, kinship ties, and personal loyalties mattered more in practice than the code. Evidence on these matters comes mainly from American inspection reports, which describe local officials from the viewpoint of those who supervised them, and from Filipino newspapers and later memoirs.

Taxation was the central practical issue. The cedula, or personal registration certificate, and the land tax fell heavily on small cultivators, while customs duties produced most of the central revenue. Municipal councils decided on local public works, and the burden of road labor, still required in many places, was resented. Taxation shows that local government was not simply a symbolic space of participation but an apparatus of extraction and obligation.

A municipal president's authority was also constrained by the resources available to the town. The council might wish to repair a bridge after flooding or expand a primary school, but a small tax base, unpaid assessments, or disputes over jurisdiction could leave the project stalled. The provincial treasury and engineering services connected such decisions to appointed officers and administrative standards. Financial accountability was one genuine governmental concern: colonial reports regularly criticized irregular bookkeeping and the absence of reliable cadastral information. But the demand for formal records could also disadvantage poorer residents who lacked documentary proof of property or could not afford the costs of complying with the new system. Local fiscal reform, in other words, linked administrative efficiency with an unequal capacity to participate.

Political families could adapt quickly. A household that had supplied a principalía official under Spain, financed a revolutionary unit in 1898, and subsequently provided an elected municipal president was not necessarily changing its underlying social base at each political transition. Land, credit, kinship, and personal authority often survived a change of sovereign. Nevertheless, the arrival of elections and official procedures introduced new possibilities for intra-elite competition and petitioning. Defeated candidates could challenge returns, rival notables could appeal to provincial supervisors, and residents could attempt to present grievances in the vocabulary of statutory rights. Such opportunities did not make the system democratic for women or the majority of men excluded from the franchise. They help explain, however, why local politics cannot be reduced to either a seamless American imposition or an unchanging landed oligarchy.

Section 119.4 · Division I

Courts, Police, the Philippine Constabulary, and Colonial Order

The Judiciary Act (Act No. 136, June 11, 1901) created the Supreme Court, courts of first instance, and justices of the peace. Cayetano Arellano became the first Chief Justice, and the Court included both Filipino and American justices. The system combined American procedure and public institutions with a body of Spanish civil and criminal law that was revised only gradually. Filipinos therefore entered a judicial system whose language of argument shifted over time from Spanish to English, which affected who could practice law. The courts handled ordinary disputes over land, contracts, and crime, and they were an important arena in which Filipino lawyers, many of whom entered politics, established themselves.

Policing grew from the same wartime needs. Act No. 175 of July 18, 1901 established the Insular Constabulary, later known as the Philippine Constabulary, under the American officer Henry T. Allen. It was a centralized, military-style police force with American officers and Filipino enlisted men, and it was designed to replace army garrisons in the provinces and to suppress brigandage and insurgency. Its recruits were often drawn from the same regions in which they served, and in many places they fought former comrades. Alfred McCoy has argued that the Constabulary and associated intelligence and records systems were a distinctive feature of American colonial state-building, one that created lasting patterns of surveillance and political policing.

Legislation supplied the legal tools. The Sedition Act of November 1901 punished advocacy of independence and seditious expression. The Brigandage Act of 1902 allowed armed resisters to be treated as bandits rather than as political combatants, which is how Macario Sakay, who proclaimed a Tagalog Republic, was described until his surrender in 1906 and execution in 1907. Surveillance and the use of informers belonged to the same toolkit. The Flag Law of 1907, which prohibited display of the Katipunan and revolutionary flags, extended the logic to symbols. These measures show that legal order and political repression could be pursued through the same institutions.

Prisons followed the same dual pattern. Bilibid Prison in Manila was reorganized, and penal colonies were established, among them Iwahig in Palawan, which officials described as a rehabilitative experiment. Evidence for conditions comes largely from official reports that stress reform, and critics have noted the difficulty of confirming such claims independently. The point for students is that the same state could present itself as a reforming and impartial legal order while also relying on coercion.

The Constabulary also shows the limits of American control. Its officers and men were often accused of abuses, and its reliance on local recruits made it dependent on Filipino loyalty. By the end of the decade, as insurgency declined, its role shifted toward ordinary policing in the lowlands and continued campaigns in regions such as Samar and Leyte against the Pulahan movement, a religiously inspired peasant resistance that persisted until about 1907.

Courts and police were closely connected, but their responsibilities should not be collapsed. Judges adjudicated civil suits and criminal accusations under laws that combined inherited Spanish codes with new procedural rules. The constabulary investigated suspects, escorted prisoners, pursued armed groups, and reported to a command structure that could be heavily influenced by security priorities. A land dispute could reach a courthouse as a contest between claimants; a refusal to pay taxes, a suspected guerrilla connection, or possession of revolutionary emblems could draw the attention of police and military intelligence. Because many ordinary Filipinos could not afford skilled counsel, the practical availability of justice depended on wealth, language, travel, and local influence as well as statutes. The legal profession nevertheless offered nationalist politicians an instrument for challenging official actions in a forum that recognized petitions, appeals, and documentary argument.

The constabulary's composition deserves care. Its ranks were recruited extensively among Filipinos, although American officers initially held many supervisory positions; over time Filipinos acquired increasing authority. Local knowledge could improve investigations, but proximity to community rivalries also made the force vulnerable to denunciations, personal vendettas, and selective enforcement. Officers described some enemies as ladrones or bandits, while opponents could understand themselves as revolutionaries, millenarian adherents, or participants in local struggles against landlords and officials. Classifying them as criminals rather than political adversaries changed the legal and public meaning of coercion. The resulting system was neither simply an army in civilian uniform nor a politically neutral police force. It was a hybrid institution working at the contested boundary between ordinary criminal justice and the unfinished task of making American sovereignty effective. The consequences can be examined through court cases, constabulary annual reports, complaints from provincial residents, and the changing use of sedition and brigandage legislation.

Long-term causes

The inherited Spanish structures of parish, town, and landed elite; the American imperial decision to retain the islands; and the assumption that colonial rule needed institutions of tutelage.

Short-term causes

The costly guerrilla war, the need to separate moderates from insurgents, domestic American criticism, and the practical need for administration.

Taft's civil governorship in July 1901, the Spooner Amendment, and the pace at which provinces were declared pacified.

Section 119.5 · Division II — Education, Religion, Public Health, and Social Transformation

The Establishment of the American Public School System

Act No. 74, passed on January 21, 1901, created a centralized, secular, tax-supported public school system under a General Superintendent of Instruction. Fred W. Atkinson was the first holder of the office. The Act provided for primary instruction, for the use of English as the medium of instruction, for the recruitment of American teachers, and for the establishment of a normal school in Manila to train Filipino teachers. Schooling in the Spanish period had been limited and was often tied to parishes, though the Educational Decree of 1863 had set out a system of primary schools. American officials presented the new system as a break with the past, though they also built on a demand for schooling that already existed among many Filipino families.

American goals were several. Officials saw education as a means of social uplift, of preparing a citizenry fit for eventual self-government, of building loyalty to American institutions, and of reducing the divisions created by many languages. English was chosen as a common medium partly for administrative convenience, since Filipino languages were numerous, and partly out of a conviction that English was the language of progress. The curriculum emphasized reading, arithmetic, hygiene, civics, and industrial and vocational training. Textbooks were typically written for American or generic audiences, and their content reflected American assumptions.

After approximately forty-eight American teachers had arrived aboard the Sheridan in July 1901, 540 educators reached Manila on August 21 aboard the army transport Thomas, and they became known as the Thomasites. Over the following years roughly a thousand Americans served as teachers, supervisors, and administrators. They worked alongside many more Filipino teachers, who made up the bulk of the teaching force from the beginning, often with limited training and in make-shift buildings. American teachers were sent to provincial towns where they faced disease, isolation, and in some cases hostility. Several died. The Thomasites became a symbol in later American and Philippine memory, which has tended to magnify their role relative to that of Filipino teachers.

Funding and access were uneven. Municipalities were expected to provide school buildings, and the Gabaldon Act of 1907, named for the Filipino Assemblyman Isauro Gabaldon, provided national funds for school construction. Enrollment increased rapidly within a decade, though far more children entered the early grades than completed them, and only a small proportion reached secondary education. Many rural children left school to work. The system was therefore broad at the base and narrow at the top.

Education served several purposes at once: genuine literacy, vocational training, social advancement for individuals, preparation of clerks and teachers for the colonial administration, and the spread of American cultural and political assumptions. Historians have emphasized different aspects. Some have stressed the opportunities schools created for ordinary Filipinos. Others, following the critical tradition, have emphasized “colonial education” as a means of securing hegemony. Both claims are supported, and the evidence suggests that families often used the schools for their own ends regardless of official intention.

A distinction between policy and classroom practice is essential. English was the officially preferred medium, but teachers could not instantly replace the languages students used at home or the Spanish literacy that some older pupils already possessed. Lessons required explanations, examples, and a practical bridge between the language of the curriculum and a child's everyday speech. Filipino teachers supplied much of that bridge, sometimes informally translating concepts even where English-only instruction was promoted. School inspectors might celebrate the extension of English in annual reports without capturing the difficulties of pronunciation, attendance, or the use of books unfamiliar to local pupils. These discrepancies make classroom records and recollections particularly valuable alongside the legislative framework.

Education also altered the social geography of provincial towns. A central school could become a focal point for examinations, teacher meetings, celebrations, and contact with officials from Manila. Pupils who remained in school might qualify for clerical employment or additional training unavailable to their parents. The cost of attendance was not limited to fees: children lost time that families depended upon for planting, fishing, marketing, or domestic work. Publicly funded primary schooling thus created an opportunity that could not be used equally by all households. Administrative measures of progress—buildings erected, pupils enrolled, teachers appointed—need to be distinguished from learning achieved, sustained attendance, and advancement beyond the elementary level. This is especially important where optimistic official accounts omit the children who never entered a classroom or who left before completing the course.

Section 119.6 · Division II

Teachers, Students, English, and the Emergence of a New Educated Generation

The classroom was where policy met daily life. Pupils learned to read English from primers and readers that offered images of American households, seasons, and holidays unfamiliar to most children, and they also learned hygiene, gardening, and manual skills. Instruction in the first years often relied on recitation and on teachers with limited English themselves. Students spoke Tagalog, Cebuano, Ilocano, Hiligaynon, and many other languages at home, and the use of English in school created both a practical advantage for those who mastered it and a gap between school and home.

Girls attended in significant numbers, though fewer than boys, and teaching became one of the first professions open to Filipino women outside the household. The Philippine Normal School, established under Act No. 74, trained teachers, and by the end of the decade Filipinos filled most teaching posts. Teaching offered a salary and social standing, though salaries were lower than those of Americans in comparable roles, a disparity that Filipino teachers noticed.

Secondary and higher education were limited and selective. Provincial high schools and specialized institutions, such as the Philippine School of Arts and Trades, opened in the first decade. In 1903 the government established the pensionado program, which sent a small but influential number of Filipino students to American universities at government expense. Many returned to hold senior posts. The University of the Philippines, created by Act No. 1870 in June 1908 and beginning instruction in 1909, offered professional training in medicine, law, agriculture, and the arts. Alongside it, the Ateneo de Manila and the University of Santo Tomas, institutions with Spanish-period roots, continued to educate elites.

Education created a new generation with skills the colonial state needed: clerks, teachers, lawyers, doctors, engineers. It also created a public able to read newspapers, understand American constitutional language, and use it. When Filipino politicians cited American ideals of self-government in support of independence, they drew on a literacy and a vocabulary that the schools had helped to spread. The paradox was recognized at the time, and some American officials worried that educated Filipinos would become dissatisfied with subordination.

The reach of the system should not be overstated. Many rural children had limited access, and literacy in a Filipino language or in Spanish often preceded English literacy. Glenn A. May has argued that the social impact of American education, including its ideological effect, was smaller than its promoters claimed. The evidence suggests that schools had real effects but that those effects were mediated by class, region, and family strategy.

The normal school and higher institutions supplied a route from schooling into occupations that the colonial economy increasingly rewarded. The government required teachers, translators, medical workers, surveyors, and clerks; employers sought people able to handle correspondence and accounts in English. Pensionado scholarships sent selected Filipinos to study in the United States, but those beneficiaries represented a small fraction of the population and often had advantages of prior education and family support. Their later careers could include public administration, law, medicine, and education. A scholarship designed to produce technically capable colonial employees could also equip a graduate to argue publicly that Filipinos were capable of governing themselves. Colonial training and anticolonial political ambition were not mutually exclusive.

Women entered the teaching profession in growing numbers, gaining a respectable form of paid work and a public role beyond the household. This did not erase family expectations or the gender hierarchy governing salaries, officeholding, and the franchise. Nor did the rise of English remove older intellectual traditions. Spanish-language journalists remained prominent in the early political press, while Tagalog and other Philippine-language newspapers and dramatic performances addressed audiences beyond the narrow circle of English-speaking professionals. The educational transformation should therefore be described as a reorganization of linguistic authority, not the simple replacement of one national language by another. Evidence of a school's founding is likewise not evidence that an entire province became literate. Census categories, differing definitions of literacy, and uneven coverage must be examined before making broad numerical claims about educational improvement.

A Filipino Teacher in the Early Colonial School

Consider the position of a young Filipino teacher, drawn from a family of modest means in a provincial town, who finished the normal school course and took a post in a primary school. The salary was regular and carried standing in the community, and the position opened a path out of agricultural life. The teacher was also expected to teach in English, which he or she might speak imperfectly, to follow a curriculum designed in Manila, and to defer to an American supervisor who earned more. Section 119.7 · Division II

Religion, Church-State Relations, and Cultural Authority

Spanish rule had joined church and state, and the religious orders, the friars, had been among the largest landowners and most powerful local figures. Their conduct had been a central grievance of the revolution. The American government separated church and state, guaranteed religious freedom, and declined to support any church from public funds. The Organic Act extended freedom of religion. This ended the legal privilege of the Catholic Church, but it did not end the Church's social influence, and Catholicism remained the faith of the large majority of lowland Filipinos.

The first major issue was the future of the friars and their property. Taft traveled to Rome in 1902 to negotiate with the Vatican over the purchase of the friar estates and the withdrawal of Spanish friars. Negotiations helped address ecclesiastical and political objections; the eventual December 1903 contracts were executed with corporate estate holders and the Recollect order, not with the Vatican as seller. The withdrawal of Spanish friars remained incomplete. The Catholic hierarchy was also reorganized, with American bishops appointed in a number of dioceses, and in 1906 Jorge Barlin was named the first Filipino Catholic bishop. These changes reflected a shift in Church leadership and an effort to defuse anti-friar anger.

A second development was the emergence of an independent Filipino church. In 1902 the Iglesia Filipina Independiente was founded under the leadership of Gregorio Aglipay, a former Catholic priest and revolutionary officer, with Isabelo de los Reyes as an important organizer. It combined nationalist sentiment, anticlericalism, and Catholic liturgy, and it attracted large numbers of followers, especially in the Ilocos region and parts of the north and center of Luzon. A Philippine Supreme Court decision of 1906 awarded many of the church buildings that the new church had taken over to the Roman Catholic Church, which weakened the new church materially, though it continued to exist.

Protestant missions arrived with the American occupation, and the major denominations reached an informal agreement, the comity arrangement of 1901, dividing regions among themselves. Missionaries opened schools, hospitals, and printing presses, and they attracted a modest but significant following. American Protestant missions often cooperated with the public school system and shared its assumptions about moral and social reform, so they became part of the wider “uplift” project. In the Muslim south, Protestant and Catholic missions had a limited impact, and the Americans were cautious about interfering with Islam, a policy discussed in Section 119.16.

Whether American policy broke with the Spanish pattern or reproduced a new form of institutional control is a real historical question. The disestablishment of the Church and the end of the friar monopoly altered the legal setting. But the American state also actively sought to shape belief and behavior through schools, hygiene, and missions, and it continued to classify populations by religion and level of “civilization.”

The separation of church and state posed a difficult question about property as well as doctrine. The friar estates were an economic problem because tenant farmers and parishioners had opposed clerical landlords before the American conquest. The public purchase of those lands was negotiated through legal and financial channels, but disputes over priests, church buildings, and local religious authority could not be settled by a commercial agreement alone. In some communities the Iglesia Filipina Independiente attracted supporters seeking both religious self-government and a distinctly Filipino national church. Contests over parish buildings could become bitter precisely because access to a church represented more than the legal ownership of stone walls and land: it signified the right to lead worship and to speak for a local religious community.

American Protestant missions introduced additional schools, hospitals, publications, and congregations, but it would be misleading to assume that the population changed religion en masse. Catholic institutions continued to be influential across much of the lowlands, and individual Filipinos could embrace Protestant or independent church organizations for varied spiritual, educational, or political reasons. In the Muslim south, religious authority and law raised questions that the religious experience of Luzon could not answer. The constitutional principle of free exercise therefore operated inside a religiously diverse archipelago and could be implemented differently according to the demands of security and colonial administration. This diversity also complicates an easy succession narrative in which American secularism simply replaces Spanish clerical power. Older institutions remained powerful while new institutions entered an already contested landscape.

Section 119.8 · Division II

Public Health, Sanitation, Medicine, and the Colonial Reform Agenda

The war and its aftermath left the islands exposed to epidemic disease. Bubonic plague appeared in Manila in 1899, smallpox remained endemic, and a severe cholera epidemic between 1902 and 1904 killed many people; official cholera registers recorded 109,461 deaths among 166,252 cases from March 1902 to March 1904, while larger retrospective population estimates, around 200,000, employ different methods and remain debated. Rinderpest in 1902 devastated draft animals, killing a very large proportion of carabao in affected regions, which in turn hurt rice cultivation and rural incomes. Disease interacted with war, displacement, poor sanitation, and malnutrition.

The colonial government built a health administration to respond. A Board of Health was created in 1901 and later reorganized as the Bureau of Health in 1905. Under officials such as Victor Heiser, the Bureau carried out vaccination campaigns, quarantine, inspection, and sanitary regulation. The government also created the Bureau of Science in 1905 for laboratory research and medical training, the Philippine Medical School in 1907, and the Philippine General Hospital, which opened in 1910. Improvements to water and sewerage in Manila, together with tropical-disease research, reflected the same effort. Culion in Palawan became a segregation colony for people with leprosy after 1906.

Warwick Anderson has analyzed the intellectual setting of these programs in Colonial Pathologies. American medical and sanitary officials saw the tropics as an environment that endangered white bodies and attributed disease to habits of the people, so that hygiene became a means of both care and classification. This view led to coercive measures: forced inspections, destruction of property, isolation in cholera camps, and the compulsory segregation of leprosy patients. Many Filipinos resisted them, sometimes violently, and some objections reflected valid concerns about family separation and the cost of compliance. Others welcomed vaccination or clean water.

Access was unequal. Manila and larger towns received hospitals, water systems, and doctors; rural communities often saw only periodic vaccination teams. The urban poor, rural tenants, and the people in the Cordillera and the south generally received fewer services. Filipino physicians, many trained at the new medical school, gradually took over posts, and they sometimes argued for different priorities, such as a greater emphasis on rural health and treatment rather than inspection. Historians therefore see both real benefits and a style of intervention that reflected colonial hierarchy.

Mortality is hard to measure, because statistics were incomplete, especially in rural districts. The long-term decline in some epidemic diseases, notably cholera, after the early 1900s is well documented, but its attribution to sanitation, vaccination, changed behavior, or the end of wartime disruption remains a matter of judgment.

The cholera epidemic illustrates both the promise and the limits of the colonial health state. Official records for the epidemic beginning in March 1902 and ending in March 1904 recorded 166,252 cases and 109,461 deaths. Larger estimates derived from subsequent population investigations have been cited, but the totals do not count precisely the same observations, and neither can be read as a flawless national register. Epidemics were unevenly reported in rural districts, the diagnosis of illness varied, and families sometimes avoided inspectors because removal to isolation facilities threatened livelihoods and family ties. The historian must distinguish a documented public-health response from a demonstrated reduction in mortality and must avoid aggregating cholera deaths mechanically with Philippine-American War casualty figures.

Sanitary measures also reveal the distribution of colonial power. Regulations could require the cleaning or destruction of an infected dwelling, the inspection of food or water, or the relocation of people considered a danger to public health. A homeowner, tenant, or small vendor might face considerable loss even when the stated objective was scientifically defensible. American medical writers sometimes portrayed resistance as ignorance, but the economic and social costs of intervention provide another explanation. Filipino health professionals were crucial intermediaries: they communicated with families, challenged assumptions about local behavior, and increasingly staffed government facilities. The broader history is not a choice between medical improvement and colonial domination. It concerns which communities received services, whose knowledge counted in defining public-health problems, who bore the cost of sanitary action, and how medical authority became associated with the larger colonial bureaucracy.

Section 119.9 · Division III — Infrastructure, Land, Trade, and the Colonial Economy

Roads, Railways, Ports, Communications, and the Transformation of Space

American officials regarded infrastructure as proof of good government and as a means of integrating the archipelago. Their projects had varied purposes: military mobility, administrative control, commerce, public services, and the comfort of the American community. Understanding which purpose drove a given project helps explain where investment went.

Railways illustrate the mix. The Manila-Dagupan line had been built under Spanish rule by a British company. In 1905 the government offered guarantees on bonds to private investors, and the Manila Railroad Company expanded lines in Luzon, including extensions toward the south, though full completion of southern routes came later. The railways carried rice, sugar, and passengers, and they connected Manila to Central Luzon and Bicol. They also reinforced Manila's role as the hub of the colony, with the Visayas and Mindanao tied more to coastal shipping than to rail.

Roads were a central project. Governor-General W. Cameron Forbes, earlier Commissioner of Commerce and Police, was a leading advocate, and the government built and repaired provincial roads and bridges, often using municipal labor obligations. The most celebrated example was the Benguet Road, completed in 1905 and later known as Kennon Road, which connected the lowlands to the mountain town of Baguio. Baguio was developed as a hill station and summer capital after 1903, to a plan prepared in 1905 by the American architect Daniel Burnham and his associates, who also produced a plan for Manila. The project reflected American assumptions about the dangers of lowland tropical climates, and the road made Baguio accessible to officials while also opening Benguet to mining, vegetable cultivation, and migration. It was built in part with Igorot and Chinese labor and was costly in lives and money.

Ports and communications formed another group. Manila's harbor was improved with breakwaters, dredging, and new piers, which supported the export trade. The Bureau of Posts expanded postal service, telegraph and telephone lines linked provincial centers, and in July 1903 the Commercial Pacific Cable reached Manila, connecting the islands to San Francisco by way of Honolulu, Midway, and Guam. Manila gained electric tramways and a modernized water supply, while many provincial towns saw few such changes.

Not all benefits were shared equally. Roads and railways concentrated in Luzon and in zones of commercial agriculture, and many roads served strategic purposes in the military districts. Mobilizing road labor imposed obligations on rural people, and construction sometimes disrupted farming. Infrastructure also increased the reach of the state, tax collection, and the Constabulary. At the same time, roads, ships, and mail enabled Filipino farmers to reach markets, students to reach schools, and politicians to reach constituencies.

Transport improvements should be examined as a network rather than a list of celebrated engineering projects. The Manila–Dagupan railway, inherited from the Spanish period, connected the capital with agricultural districts of Central Luzon, but it did not represent a rail system extending uniformly across the islands. Extensions and concessions proceeded unevenly, with delays arising from finance, land acquisition, challenging terrain, and competing regional priorities. Elsewhere coastal steamships remained indispensable. A port improvement that shortened unloading time could matter to exporters and merchants even if it offered little immediate help to an inland household whose road became impassable in the rainy season. The key geographical question is not merely where construction occurred but which connections changed costs and access.

The Baguio project presents a similar contrast. Establishing a seasonal government center in the highlands entailed road construction, land acquisition, public expenditure, and the movement of workers and supplies. It made an upland setting more accessible to officials who believed a cooler climate would protect them from tropical illness; it also facilitated commerce, tourism, and new claims to land. Workers, including Filipinos recruited from different communities and migrants from overseas, experienced the construction site differently from administrators who later traveled the road. The expansion of the postal system, cable connections, and telegraph lines likewise sped official communications, commercial contracts, and personal letters, but fees, language, and access constrained use. Infrastructure increased the capacity of the government to circulate information and deploy police; it simultaneously expanded the possibilities for Filipino businesses, newspapers, and political organizations to maintain connections beyond their immediate locality.

Section 119.10 · Division III

Tariffs, Commercial Integration, and Economic Dependence on the United States

The Philippine economy was already oriented toward export when the Americans arrived. Sugar, abaca (Manila hemp), tobacco, and coconut products had grown rapidly in the nineteenth century, and the main markets had been Britain, China, and Spain as well as the United States. American policy reoriented this pattern by aligning Philippine commerce with the American customs system, and by 1913 the two economies were bound by free trade.

The steps were gradual. Initially Philippine exports to the United States paid a reduced tariff, set at a fraction of the American rate by acts of 1902, while American goods entering the islands paid Philippine duties. Congress was divided, since American sugar and tobacco growers opposed competition. The Payne-Aldrich Tariff of 1909 established free trade between the two territories, subject to quotas on Philippine sugar and tobacco products, and with rice still dutiable. The Underwood-Simmons Tariff of 1913 removed those quotas, opening the American market without limit to Philippine goods, while American goods entered the islands free of duty. Coinage reform in 1903 had also tied the Philippine peso to the gold standard through a fixed exchange rate to the dollar.

The effects differed by sector. Export-oriented sugar, coconut products, and later tobacco expanded, benefiting landowners and processors in areas such as Negros, Pampanga, and the Cagayan valley. Abaca, mostly sold to American and European rope manufacturers, remained important. Imports of American manufactured goods, such as cotton textiles, flour, and canned goods, grew and competed with local artisan production, notably in weaving. The Philippines was therefore increasingly a producer of raw materials and a market for American manufactures. Customs duties, which funded the Insular Government, also shifted as free trade reduced some revenue.

The outcome created what economists and historians call dependence. Producers of export crops gained income but became vulnerable to American policy and prices, and powerful sugar interests later had reason to fear independence because it might end preferential access. Landowners and exporters thus came to hold economic interests that conflicted with the nationalist demand for immediate independence. At the same time, many Filipino leaders had argued that the free-trade arrangement was beneficial and could be defended on economic grounds. Historians continue to debate how far dependence resulted from American design and how far from existing export patterns and landowner interests.

Investment from the United States was modest in this period, compared with other colonial economies in the region, and much of the capital in sugar milling, trade, and shipping remained Spanish, Chinese, British, or Filipino. Chapter 122 compares this pattern to plantation and mining economies elsewhere in Southeast Asia.

Tariff policy is best understood as a set of negotiated compromises rather than a single decision to grant free trade. American producers of sugar and tobacco feared competition, while importers, shipping interests, and some colonial officials sought a more open commercial connection. The 1909 legislation created duty-free privileges with quantity restrictions on important commodities; the 1913 changes removed major remaining barriers to Philippine products entering the American market. These arrangements were not symmetrical in their consequences. A producer whose crop could enter the United States on favorable terms might welcome the preference, while a household buying imported manufactured goods encountered new competition with local crafts or existing suppliers. The effects differed by crop, region, ownership, and the ability to transport produce to an export port.

Dependence has several meanings that students should distinguish. It can refer to the proportion of exports sold in one market, the source of capital and machinery, the currency and credit arrangements under which trade occurred, or the political vulnerability of producers to changes in customs law. The opening of the American market could raise returns and encourage investment while giving exporters a reason to resist any political change that might jeopardize the tariff advantage. Yet not all firms were American-owned, nor did trade policy erase established Chinese-Filipino, Spanish, British, and domestic commercial networks. The Philippine economy had already been tied to global commerce under Spain. The distinctive early American development was a closer legal and commercial orientation toward the United States, creating an interest group that would later shape debates over political independence and tariff separation.

Historical Vocabulary
Tariff, preferential trade, export agriculture, customs revenue, investment, dependence

A tariff is a tax on imported goods. Preferential trade gives certain partners lower duties than others. Export agriculture refers to crops grown mainly for sale abroad. Customs revenue is government income from duties and was the main source of Insular Government funds. Foreign investment is capital supplied from outside the territory. Economic dependence describes reliance on a particular market or supplier, so that the dependent economy is exposed to decisions made elsewhere.

Potential misunderstanding: Dependence did not mean that the economy stagnated. Exports grew; the point is that growth took a form that tied key interests to the American market.

Section 119.11 · Division III

Friar Lands, Land Registration, and the Agrarian Question

Land was among the most contentious inheritances of Spanish rule. The religious orders held large estates, especially around Manila and in Central Luzon, and tenants on those estates had long complained of rents and evictions. The revolutionaries had demanded the removal of the friars and, for many, the return of land. American officials considered the friar question a source of unrest that had to be settled to stabilize the colony.

After negotiations that included the Vatican and holders of religious estates, the government contracted with several corporate landholding companies and the Recollect order in 1903 to buy friar lands totaling approximately 164,127 hectares (about 405,570 acres), for $7,239,784.66 under contracts signed December 22, 1903, financed through public borrowing. The Friar Lands Act, Act No. 1120 of April 26, 1904, set rules for managing and selling the purchased land. Tenants were to have preference in purchase, and sales were limited to modest holdings, with payments in installments. The government acted as landlord in the meantime, collecting rents from tenants.

Other legislation attempted to create a system of secure title. The Land Registration Act (Act No. 496, 1902) introduced the Torrens system of registered title, and the Public Land Act of 1903 allowed homesteads of limited size for individuals and capped corporate holdings at 1,024 hectares. The aim was to make property secure, encourage small farmers, and prevent American corporations from accumulating large plantations, an aim shaped by the memory of land concentration in other territories.

The results were mixed. Registration and survey required money, legal knowledge, and time, which small farmers often lacked, and those with education and capital found it easier to register land, often including land that others had cultivated customarily. Many friar tenants could not afford the purchase price, and a substantial portion of the estates was bought by wealthier individuals or remained under government management for years. Where tenants did purchase, they sometimes found themselves in debt. The registration regime also privileged written title over customary claims, which affected upland and non-Christian communities.

The central point for students is the difference between reform objectives and outcomes. Removing land from the religious orders changed who was the landlord, but it did not guarantee that cultivators would own land. The agrarian question remained unresolved and reappeared in the peasant movements of the 1920s and 1930s, as Chapter 131 will show. Historians differ on how far the land program was a missed opportunity and how far it was constrained by cost and elite interests.

The friar-land transaction was large enough to demand precise historical accounting. Contracts dated December 22, 1903 covered approximately 164,127 hectares—roughly 405,570 acres—for an aggregate price of $7,239,784.66 in U.S. currency, as Act No. 1120 subsequently recorded. The sellers included corporate holders of former religious estates and the Recollect order; the purchase should not simply be characterized as a direct payment to the Vatican. The legal figure describes the land covered by contracts, not a universal census of every estate or an assurance that every tract was immediately surveyed and transferred without dispute. Government officials still had to determine boundaries, titles, occupants, and the terms on which parcels could be sold or leased.

For a tenant family the decisive question was less whether the government had acquired an estate than whether the family could secure land in its own name. Installment terms could appear manageable in an official schedule but become onerous after crop failure, illness, or a fall in prices. Disputes over boundaries and the expenses of registration favored claimants able to secure lawyers, surveyors, and documentary evidence. The Torrens registration system aimed to create certainty through official titles, but a formal record could reproduce an existing inequality when poorer cultivators lacked the resources to assert their customary claims. Some tenants did gain property; others remained tenants or encountered new landlords. The reform cannot be judged from acreage purchased alone. It requires examining the eventual purchasers, their financing, the treatment of occupants, and the regional variation of ownership outcomes.

Section 119.12 · Division III

Farmers, Tenants, Laborers, and the Unequal Distribution of Colonial Growth

For most Filipinos, the economy of this period meant farming. In Central Luzon, tenancy was widespread, and rents were commonly paid as a share of the crop. In the sugar areas of Negros and Pampanga, large estates employed seasonal and permanent laborers. In abaca and coconut regions, small holders sold to traders. In much of the Visayas, Mindanao, and the uplands, people continued mixed subsistence farming and sold surplus in local markets. Growth in export sectors therefore did not lift all boats equally.

Several factors worked against small cultivators. Credit was scarce and expensive, and borrowing from landlords, traders, or moneylenders at high interest was common, which tied tenants to creditors. The loss of draft animals to rinderpest forced many farmers to borrow for replacements. Transportation costs reduced the share of the final price that reached producers. Land taxes, cedulas, and road labor fell on rural households. Where landowners had political power, as they did through the municipal and provincial structure, they could shape these conditions to their advantage.

Wage labor also shifted. Sugar plantations, abaca and coconut harvests, and construction projects employed seasonal laborers. From 1906 the Hawaiian Sugar Planters' Association recruited Filipino workers, known later as sakadas, for Hawaii, beginning a pattern of migration linked to American territories. Within the islands, laborers moved to sugar regions and to new commercial zones. Wages were low, and organization was difficult, though early labor associations emerged in Manila (Section 119.18).

Chinese and Chinese-Filipino merchants remained central to commerce, as rice dealers, shopkeepers, and moneylenders, and as links in export chains. American policy extended the Chinese Exclusion Act to the islands in 1902, limiting new Chinese immigration, though established residents and their families continued in trade. This community was not uniform: wealthy merchants, small shopkeepers, and laborers occupied very different positions, and the mestizo elite of Chinese descent was increasingly integrated into the Filipino landowning and political class. Resentment against Chinese middlemen existed among some rural people, but it should not be treated as a single phenomenon.

The central question is who benefited. Landowners and exporters, merchants, and some professionals gained most. Tenants, laborers, and subsistence farmers had fewer gains and bore costs. The picture is complicated by uneven evidence, since official statistics measure exports and revenue more reliably than rural living standards. What can be said is that growth and inequality advanced together, and that the agrarian tensions of the period shaped later movements.

Social class was experienced in working relationships, not simply in the census categories used by administrators. An agricultural tenant might cultivate a plot, borrow money or seed from a landlord or merchant, pay rent in produce, and face obligations that limited the ability to move or negotiate. A landowner involved in export agriculture could sometimes borrow against property or receive preferential access to new transport links, while a smallholder had fewer assets to secure credit. The shift toward cash transactions and export markets did not automatically free cultivators from older social obligations. In some districts the commercial value of land encouraged tighter landlord control and disputes over tenancy, particularly where cadastral surveys or new title procedures made existing possession newly contestable.

Wage earners also encountered a changing economy. Port workers, printers, tobacco-factory employees, railway laborers, domestic servants, and construction crews worked under different arrangements for hiring, pay, and discipline. Their work was shaped by seasonal demand, the costs of rice and housing, and access to collective organization. Chinese and Chinese-Filipino merchants occupied important positions in retail and regional distribution, but it is historically misleading to treat either population as a unified wealthy class; businesses differed greatly in scale and relationship to producers. Urban growth created employment and consumer markets without guaranteeing secure wages. A balanced explanation of economic change therefore links macroeconomic reports to household budgets, landholding records, petitions, strikes, and local studies. Official claims about rising trade should not be used as proxies for general improvements in living standards unless independent evidence supports that conclusion.

The First Philippine Assembly and Electoral Politics, 1907

After the 1903 census and the proclamation of peace in 1902 in the affected provinces, the Commission scheduled elections for the Philippine Assembly. They were held on July 30, 1907, in thirty-four provinces where civil government was in place, and the Assembly of about eighty members opened on October 16, 1907, in Manila. The ceremony was attended by Secretary of War Taft and by American and Filipino officials, and it was widely regarded at the time as a significant step.

The franchise was narrow. Voters had to be men of at least twenty-three years who held or had held municipal office under the Spanish or American regimes, or who owned property of a specified value or paid a specified amount of taxes, or who could read and write English or Spanish. Women were excluded, as were non-Christian populations in areas under special administration. Approximately 104,966 men registered, with roughly 98,000 voting according to historical election tabulations, a small fraction of a population of about seven and a half million. The electorate was therefore dominated by landowners, professionals, officeholders, and the educated.

The Assembly was the lower house of a bicameral legislature, whose upper house was the Commission, still appointed by the American president and with an American majority. Legislation required the approval of both houses, so the Commission could block any Assembly act. The governor held a veto, and Congress could annul laws. The Assembly controlled no executive branch and no appointments, though it could pass appropriations bills, which gave it some leverage, and it quickly used its power over the budget to press its priorities.

Sergio Osmeña of Cebu was elected Speaker, and Manuel L. Quezon of Tayabas became the Assembly's majority floor leader and a rising political figure. The Nacionalista Party, which called for immediate independence, won a majority of the seats, while the Progresista Party, the successor to the Federal Party, took a smaller share. Independent and other candidates held the remainder. The Assembly's work included education funding, public works, and appropriations, but it also served as a platform for national debate and for the training of a political class.

The Assembly was an important milestone in representative government, and Filipino leaders used it effectively. It should not be equated with national sovereignty or universal suffrage, and its powers were bounded by colonial law. Both sets of facts must be held together.

The election law supplies a precise measure of the limits of participation. Act No. 1582, enacted January 9, 1907, restricted voting to men at least twenty-three years old who met the residence rule and one of several alternative qualifications: qualifying pre-1898 municipal service, ownership of property worth at least five hundred pesos or payment of specified taxes, or the ability to speak, read, and write English or Spanish. Literacy in a Philippine language alone did not satisfy that last alternative. The categories therefore favored people with established officeholding, taxable resources, or access to particular forms of schooling. Approximately 104,966 voters were registered and roughly 98,251 votes were cast in the 1907 Assembly contest, although historical compilations differ in reporting methods. These figures should not be mistaken for the number of all adults eligible to have a political opinion or a stake in the laws passed.

The Assembly nevertheless mattered as a public stage. Candidates organized campaigns, parties issued platforms, newspapers reported disputes, and elected representatives debated appropriations. The body provided a site where national politicians could demand greater control of education, taxation, and appointments, even as the appointed Commission could block legislation. A legislative deadlock might expose the contradictions of a constitution that encouraged electoral participation without granting the elected body final authority. Students should distinguish the expansion of institutional experience from a simple story of democratic enfranchisement. The new system elevated certain voices while withholding the vote from women and most men; it could strengthen claims to Filipino competence and independence while reproducing local hierarchies of land, patronage, and education.

Section 119.14 · Division IV

Political Parties, the Nacionalistas, and the Struggle over Self-Government

Parties took shape quickly. The Federal Party was formed in December 1900 by figures such as Trinidad Pardo de Tavera, Benito Legarda, and Cayetano Arellano and favored cooperation with the Americans, with an eventual aim of statehood in the American Union. Its leaders were prominent among the wealthy and educated, and it enjoyed American favor and offered a path to appointments. It was, however, criticized by nationalists as too accommodating, and it later dropped its statehood aim and became the Progresista Party.

The Nacionalista Party was organized in 1907 by Osmeña, Quezon, and others, and it called for immediate independence. It campaigned for the Assembly and won decisively. The party included many former revolutionaries and used the language of national dignity. Yet its leaders accepted that independence would come by American consent, and in practice they negotiated within colonial institutions. Competing factions, such as those favoring immediate independence and those favoring gradual autonomy, appeared within and outside the parties, and the Sedition Act and Flag Law limited what could be said.

Party politics depended heavily on patronage and local networks. Landowning families controlled votes in many districts through tenancy, credit, and kinship, and party organization rested on provincial leaders rather than ideology. The characterization of this system as cacique democracy captures this dependence. The social and regional diversity of the population, with its many languages and economic circumstances, made it difficult for any party to claim to represent the whole, and the narrow franchise excluded most tenants and laborers.

Osmeña and Quezon built different strengths. Osmeña, a lawyer from Cebu, was cautious and skilled at institutional bargaining. Quezon, a former revolutionary officer from Tayabas, was a forceful orator and a shrewd political organizer who became Resident Commissioner in Washington in 1909. Their partnership and rivalry shaped Nacionalista politics for decades.

The central point is that cooperation with the colonial system did not mean abandoning independence as an aim. Many leaders saw the Assembly and the parties as a means of gaining experience and leverage and of making the case for independence in a manner the Americans could not easily dismiss. Others used participation to secure personal and family advantages. Both motives frequently coexisted in the same person.

Parties emerged within an institutional framework that rewarded organizations capable of winning provincial districts and managing relations with influential families. The Nacionalistas' appeal for independence offered a language that could unite representatives who differed on questions of taxation, patronage, and the proper speed of constitutional reform. Other groups accepted a longer period of American supervision or emphasized immediate administrative improvements. Alignments were not fixed ideological blocs in the modern sense. Local political rivalry, kinship, and the distribution of offices could be as important as the wording of a national platform. A politician might campaign for independence and simultaneously negotiate with an American governor for a road, court appointment, or school allocation.

The development of a party system also changed the meaning of nationalism. During the revolution, political authority had been asserted through a republic at war; after 1907, elected leaders could claim to speak for constituents and demonstrate Filipino capacity for parliamentary administration. These were different institutions serving a related goal, and neither ought to be treated as an inevitable step toward the Commonwealth. The United States could alter the rules, and Filipino leaders disagreed about whether confrontation or cooperation would secure concessions. Party debates should be read alongside the Assembly's proceedings, the press in several languages, and provincial electoral practices. Without those sources it is too easy to mistake the rhetoric of leading politicians for the preferences of everyone living under colonial rule.

Section 119.15 · Division IV

Racial Ideology, American Imperial Culture, and the Representation of Filipinos

American rule was justified through ideas of race and civilization. Many officials and writers described Filipinos as children in need of tutelage, a view often summarized in the phrase “little brown brothers,” associated with Taft. They divided the population into “civilized” Christian Filipinos and “non-Christian tribes,” a classification that placed Muslims and highland peoples in a separate and lower category. Paul A. Kramer, in The Blood of Government, has shown how racial thinking shaped the structure of colonial policy, including the timing of self-government and the treatment of different groups.

Knowledge-gathering served these ideas. The Ethnological Survey, established in 1901, conducted fieldwork among non-Christian peoples. Dean Worcester, who had earlier traveled in the islands as a zoologist, was a leading figure, and his photographs and writings, including articles in National Geographic, presented the highland and Muslim peoples to American readers. Government photography, censuses, and museum collections organized peoples into types and gradations. These materials were valuable to anthropologists, but they were also instruments of classification.

The most visible display was the Philippine Exhibition at the Louisiana Purchase Exposition in St. Louis in 1904. The Philippine Reservation covered some forty-seven acres and brought together more than a thousand Filipinos, including Igorot, Bagobo, Moro, and Visayan people, along with Philippine Scouts and a Constabulary band. Villages were reconstructed, and visitors observed people in dress and activities selected by the organizers. The exhibit aimed to present the islands as a showcase of American progress, with a graded hierarchy from “wild” to “civilized.” Controversy arose over the Igorot, whose supposed customs drew sensational attention.

Filipinos did not respond uniformly. Some educated Filipinos objected that the display reduced the nation to its least familiar parts and distorted their identity, and some Igorot participants and others described their own experience in terms that did not match the organizers' framing, though the surviving record on this point is limited. Elite Filipinos were also implicated in these hierarchies, since many shared assumptions that distinguished them from highland and Muslim peoples. This shows that racial classification worked within Filipino society as well as between colonizer and colonized.

Racial ideas also shaped practical policy. They underlay arguments that Filipinos required a long period of tutelage before independence, and they shaped the unequal treatment of Muslims and highlanders in administration, as the next section shows. The same ideas were challenged by Filipino writers, politicians, and teachers who demonstrated, in the press, in the Assembly, and in schools, the capacity that the ideology denied.

Exhibitions were not neutral windows onto an unfamiliar society. Their organizers selected people, buildings, crafts, agricultural products, and labels to tell visitors what kind of territory the United States had acquired and what sort of government it supposedly required. The 1904 Philippine Exhibition at St. Louis presented distinct communities through displays categorized by geography and what administrators imagined to be levels of civilization. It included commercial and educational achievements as well as highly staged representations of highland and Muslim communities. Visitors could encounter Filipinos as students and artisans in one setting and as ethnographic specimens in another. The contrast helped sustain the argument that some inhabitants were ready for collaboration while others needed coercive supervision and prolonged tutelage.

A photograph of the exhibition is evidence of a carefully arranged scene, not direct evidence of unmediated life in a Philippine village. The camera records selected participants, costumes, building materials, and the visitors' vantage point; it does not establish how typical the display was of its purported source community or how participants understood their own presence. Contemporary illustrated publications further framed those images through captions that could be exoticizing or openly derogatory. The Library of Congress preserves the 1904 publication The Complete Portfolio of Photographs of the World's Fair, St. Louis, 1904, including material concerning the Philippine reservation. A defensible source exercise should identify the publication and examine its caption alongside the photograph, treating both as products of the fair's official and commercial display culture. Filipino objections, newspaper coverage, and individual participants' accounts are necessary checks on the apparent authority of the picture.

Section 119.16 · Division IV

Muslim Mindanao, Sulu, the Cordillera, and Uneven Colonial Rule

American rule was not uniform. In Muslim Mindanao and Sulu and in the highlands of northern Luzon, authorities used special arrangements justified by theories of racial and cultural difference and by the practical difficulties of control. Understanding them is essential to avoiding the mistaken impression that the Philippine colonial state was one thing across the archipelago.

In the south, the first American approach was negotiation. The Bates Agreement of August 1899 with Sultan Jamalul Kiram II of Sulu recognized the Sultan's position in exchange for acknowledgment of American sovereignty, and American officers initially avoided interfering with Islam or slavery. The agreement was never approved by Congress, and the American government abrogated it in 1904. Chapter 76 gives the longer background of Spanish-Moro relations and the history of the term “Moro.”

In 1903 the Commission created the Moro Province, including the districts of Cotabato, Davao, Lanao, Sulu, and Zamboanga. It was governed by army officers, with a legislative council dominated by Americans, and its governors were Leonard Wood (1903–1906), Tasker Bliss (1906–1909), and John J. Pershing (1909–1913). The province's officials sought to extend administration, outlaw slavery and certain customs, collect taxes, and build roads and schools, while working through datus and other local leaders where they could. Datu Piang of Cotabato was among those who cooperated, while others, such as Datu Ali, resisted and was killed in 1905. Resistance often took the form of fighting against taxes, loss of authority, and perceived threats to religion.

The violence was substantial. In March 1906, American forces under Wood's authority attacked Tausug people who had taken refuge in the crater of Bud Dajo on Jolo. Over several days in early March, the American assault killed several hundred people, probably over six hundred by most accounts, including women and children, and the Americans lost about twenty men. The event provoked protest in the United States, notably from critics of the war and the army, and it remains a central episode in Moro historical memory. In June 1913, at Bud Bagsak, also on Jolo, forces under Pershing attacked another fortified position and killed hundreds of defenders. Both episodes differed in circumstance, as the first was a refuge of a community under pressure and the second a more clearly military stronghold, but in both civilians and fighters were killed in large numbers.

In 1913 the military-run province was replaced by the Department of Mindanao and Sulu, under civilian leadership, which took effect in 1914 and reflected a policy shift toward integration with the rest of the colony. In the Cordillera, the Commission created the Mountain Province in 1908, which grouped subprovinces such as Benguet, Bontoc, Ifugao, and others under an appointed governor, with administration through appointed officials and a distinct legal and tax regime. American administrators, Worcester among them, considered highland peoples a separate category under the heading of “non-Christian tribes” and promoted a policy of gradual development, with schools, roads, and labor, which also reinforced classification and often disrupted local economies. Highland communities adapted and resisted in varied ways, and some individuals became teachers and officials. The path of integration thus differed profoundly from that of lowland Luzon.

The separate administrative geography of the islands was a political choice, not simply a consequence of distance from Manila. Colonial officials commonly divided the population into Christian-majority lowland provinces and communities classified administratively as non-Christian. The latter designation combined religion, geography, and racial assumptions in ways that obscured the diversity of Igorot societies, Muslim sultanates, and other communities. The Moro Province incorporated several districts with different languages, political relationships, and histories of interaction with Spain and the Philippine Revolution. Military governors relied selectively on datus and other local authorities, but revenue measures, anti-slavery policies, forced disarmament, and the extension of patrols could also challenge existing power and provoke resistance. No single account of a uniform Moro response is credible.

The 1906 assault on Bud Dajo should be situated within this pressure on the Tausug communities of Jolo. Accounts often report approximately six hundred or more local deaths, including women and children, while different contemporary sources distinguish American fatalities from the total killed and wounded. The case requires careful reconstruction of the assembly of people in the crater, the military decision to assault, the composition of those killed, and the ensuing public controversy. At Bud Bagsak in 1913, forces commanded by John J. Pershing confronted fortified Tausug resistance under different immediate circumstances; the two operations should not be treated as identical events. In the Cordillera, appointed officials, ethnographic classification, and infrastructure projects reorganized relationships with upland villages, whose agricultural and political systems were not interchangeable. Comparison reveals a colonial state that claimed general sovereignty while varying its practical methods radically according to region, religion, military priorities, and assessments of the population.

Section 119.17 · Division V — Collaboration, Resistance, Political Mobilization, and the Approach to Self-Government

Elite Collaboration, Continuing Resistance, and the Limits of Colonial Legitimacy

The word collaboration is often used loosely. In this period Filipinos who worked with American institutions did so for very different reasons, and the distinctions matter. Some believed American rule to be beneficial or inevitable and cooperated willingly. Some accepted office under pressure after military defeat, seeking to protect family, property, and community. Others participated deliberately as a way of advancing nationalist aims through available institutions. These motives could overlap and could change over time, and the label “collaborator” carries a moral judgment that historians have learned to apply cautiously.

American officials relied on cooperation at every level. Former revolutionaries became municipal presidents, governors, judges, and members of the Assembly. Business relationships with Americans provided capital and contracts. The Federal Party institutionalized collaboration, and its leaders held seats on the Commission. Participation brought advantages, including office, patronage, access to education for children, and legal protection for property. It also brought costs, because officeholders were expected to enforce unpopular measures and were vulnerable to the accusation of betraying the revolution.

Armed resistance did not end in 1902. Macario Sakay, a former Katipunero, proclaimed the Tagalog Republic (Katagalugan) and led a force in the hills of the Tagalog region. The authorities denied it political status under the Brigandage Act, and treated its members as outlaws. In 1906 Sakay accepted an amnesty offered through an intermediary, partly on the understanding that he would receive recognition for his cause, but he was arrested, tried as a bandit, and hanged in September 1907. His case divided later opinion, some regarding him as a patriot and others as a rebel who was misled. In Samar and Leyte the Pulahan movement carried on until about 1907, and in the Muslim south, resistance, as noted, continued to 1913 and beyond. Chapter 118 gives the principal military history.

Between collaboration and armed resistance lay a wide range of other responses. Many people avoided the state where possible, evaded taxes and labor obligations, or used the courts and the press to contest particular decisions. Religious movements, including peasant millenarian groups and the Iglesia Filipina Independiente, provided other vehicles of dissent. Colonial rule operated through a combination of negotiation, repression, public services, political incorporation, and coercive institutions. It rested less on consent than on the combination of inducement and force, and its legitimacy among Filipinos remained partial and contested, even among those who held office.

The central historical issue is the long-term effect. Cooperation gave a generation of Filipinos experience in administration and legislation, which later underpinned claims that Filipinos were ready for self-rule. It also embedded dependence on American goodwill. Both effects belong to the same process.

The vocabulary of collaboration needs historical restraint. A former revolutionary who accepted a provincial appointment might be seeking personal advancement, protecting constituents from renewed repression, building influence for a future autonomy campaign, or combining several of these motives. The available records often preserve the American official's judgment but not the private calculation of the Filipino participant. Moreover, municipal officeholding carried immediate duties that affected people regardless of national allegiance. A town president might be judged by whether the market functioned, a school was supplied, or a constabulary patrol behaved abusively. Public service could produce limited legitimacy for an institution that remained foreign-controlled, without eliminating the underlying sovereignty dispute.

Continuing resistance took different forms. Armed bands associated with the revolutionary tradition persisted after 1902, but not every episode labeled brigandage was a direct continuation of Aguinaldo's republic. Agrarian grievances, religious expectations, protection of local patrons, and community opposition to taxation could overlap with nationalism. The case of Macario Sakay illustrates the state's power to define resistance as criminal, while the disputes that surrounded his surrender and execution reveal that classifications were contested. Political resistance also operated without arms: lawyers brought challenges, newspapers criticized administrators, elected representatives opposed policies, and organizations pressed for new rights. Such actions did not necessarily share a single program. They show why the consolidation of government cannot be measured only by the absence of major battles.

Section 119.18 · Division V

Newspapers, Civic Associations, Women, Labor, and New Political Publics

Under American rule the press grew rapidly, in English, Spanish, and Philippine languages. Newspapers such as El Renacimiento, founded in 1901 and edited by Teodoro Kalaw, provided a forum for nationalist commentary. On October 30, 1908 it published an editorial by Fidel A. Reyes widely known as “Aves de Rapiña” (Birds of Prey), which attacked Dean Worcester. Worcester pursued libel proceedings against people associated with its publication; the criminal case United States v. Ocampo was decided by the Philippine Supreme Court in December 1910 and related proceedings reached the United States Supreme Court in 1914. The legal campaign contributed to the newspaper's ruin. The case showed both the existence of a lively press and the limits of critical expression under colonial law.

Associational life expanded. Professional groups of lawyers, teachers, doctors, and civil servants developed, and student organizations linked schools to politics. In February 1902 Isabelo de los Reyes and others founded the Union Obrera Democratica, often regarded as the first labor federation in the islands. Dominador Gomez was among its leaders. The organization led strikes and demonstrations, and its leaders were prosecuted under the Sedition Act, but it set a precedent for urban labor politics. Workers in Manila's ports, tobacco factories, and printing shops had begun to organize around wages and hours.

Women took part in new ways. They entered teaching and nursing, formed civic associations, and began to argue for rights. The Asociación Feminista Filipina, founded in 1905, was among the early organizations that promoted women's education and public roles. Women's suffrage was not achieved in this period; the franchise remained restricted to men, and the question was not resolved until later decades. It would be an error to describe the early colonial period as one of mass political participation or women's political equality.

Expanding literacy and urban growth created readers and audiences. Manila grew as a center of administration, trade, and education, drawing students and workers from the provinces. Political arguments circulated in several languages, with English increasingly the language of national politics and Spanish still prominent among elders and in the press, and Tagalog and other vernaculars used in popular publications and theater. Yet participation was constrained. Colonial censorship and the Sedition and Flag Laws limited expression, and the franchise excluded most people. These publics were therefore real but narrow, concentrated in towns, among men and women with schooling.

The controversy over El Renacimiento is especially revealing because it involved a newspaper operating in the legal order American authorities claimed to protect. On October 30, 1908 it published Aves de Rapiña, attributed to Fidel A. Reyes, employing the image of predatory birds in a critique associated with Interior Secretary Dean C. Worcester. Criminal libel proceedings followed, involving the newspaper's editorial and publishing leadership; the Philippine Supreme Court decided United States v. Ocampo on December 22, 1910, and proceedings associated with Martin Ocampo and Teodoro M. Kalaw later reached the United States Supreme Court in 1914. The legal history should distinguish the writer of the editorial, those charged with publication, and the several proceedings rather than attributing every act to a single editor. For historians, the case shows that newspapers could sustain highly visible criticism while still facing ruinous legal and financial consequences.

Associational politics extended beyond the better-known elite newspapers. The Unión Obrera Democrática connected organizers across trades and pursued demands concerning working conditions and labor dignity. Women's groups linked educational opportunity with claims to social reform, public responsibility, and eventually political rights. Teachers, students, and professionals organized public lectures and reading circles; popular theater and Philippine-language publications transmitted arguments to audiences who were not eligible to vote. The sources are uneven: an official report may record a strike because police intervened, while the social work of a women's organization may survive in a commemorative account written later. Such differences in documentation should be made explicit. Early political mobilization was substantial, but it was not yet mass electoral democracy, and activism could cross the boundaries between reform within the colonial state and opposition to its continued existence.

Section 119.19 · Division V

Washington, Filipino Political Leaders, and the Road toward Greater Autonomy

The Philippine government was ultimately answerable to Washington, and Filipino leaders learned to work there. Under the Organic Act two Resident Commissioners sat in the House of Representatives as non-voting delegates. The first, Benito Legarda and Pablo Ocampo, took their seats in 1907. Manuel Quezon followed in 1909 and used the post to lobby Congress and the press for independence legislation. Their position was weak formally but gave Filipinos a continuing presence in the American capital and a means of influencing debate.

American opinion was divided. The Republican administrations of Roosevelt and Taft treated the Philippines as a long-term responsibility and emphasized tutelage, though Taft as president (1909–1913) also supported gradual increases in Filipino participation. Democrats, and some Republicans, were more willing to promise eventual independence, and the Democratic platform of 1912 favored a declaration of intention to grant it. Anti-imperialist organizations kept the question alive. In the Philippines, the Assembly and the Commission clashed over budgets and appointments, with the Commission able to block Assembly bills and the Assembly able to hold up appropriations. This institutional friction marked the whole period.

The victory of the Democrat Woodrow Wilson in 1912 brought a change. Francis Burton Harrison arrived as Governor-General in October 1913 and announced a policy of increasing Filipino control. In 1913 the Commission's composition changed so that Filipinos held a majority, and Harrison began to replace American officials with Filipinos in bureaus and provincial posts. This policy became known as Filipinization. It accelerated after 1913, and its results belong to Chapter 131.

Filipinization should not be confused with independence. It meant a growing Filipino share of administrative and legislative power within a colonial structure that remained under American sovereignty, and it was reversible by American decision. The Jones Act of 1916 would take the next major institutional step by replacing the Commission with an elected Senate and stating the American intention to grant independence once a stable government was established, but that act and its implementation are reserved for later. What matters for this chapter is that by 1913 the structure created in 1901 to 1907 had become the base for a significantly expanded Filipino role.

The resident commissioners occupied an unusual place in American constitutional politics. They could speak for Philippine interests, maintain relationships with members of Congress, and seek amendments to legislation, but they did not cast binding votes on the floor of the House. Their influence depended on negotiation, publicity, committee work, and the interests of American political parties. For Filipino nationalist politicians this situation was both an obstacle and a means of action. A delegation to Washington could put independence demands before the legislature controlling the islands while also revealing how limited Filipino authority remained. Trade advocates, religious groups, administrators, and American anti-imperialists brought competing petitions into the same political arena.

Harrison's arrival in 1913 sharpened the disagreement over how rapidly government should be transferred to Filipinos. Filipinization involved changes in appointments and the balance of influence within existing departments rather than a transfer of sovereignty. Greater opportunity for Filipino administrators could increase the colonial government's knowledge of local conditions and improve its legitimacy. It could also provoke concern among Americans who believed the existing civil service needed their continued supervision and among Filipinos excluded from the circles receiving appointments. The subsequent Jones Act belongs to a later chapter, but it should not be foreshadowed as an unavoidable consequence of developments by 1914. American elections, competing colonial interests, and Filipino political bargaining remained open variables. The important achievement of this early period was the formation of institutions and leadership networks capable of pressing claims, not the predetermined success of any particular constitutional program.

Section 119.20 · Division V

The American Colonial Philippines by 1914: Institutional Transformation and Unresolved Contradictions

By 1914 the Philippines had a structure of government that differed substantially from either the Spanish colony or the revolutionary republic. There was a civilian administration headed by a governor-general, an appointed upper house with a growing Filipino membership, and an elected lower house whose leaders had substantial experience. Municipal and provincial governments operated across the lowlands, courts and a Constabulary supported them, and a system of public schools had enrolled large numbers of children. The Manila Railroad, roads, ports, and cables tied the colony together and tied it to the United States, and free trade had made the American market the main destination for exports. Public health bureaus, a medical school, and a university had been established, and newspapers, associations, and labor groups had begun to form a new public.

Much remained unresolved. Sovereignty rested with the United States, and Congress and the president held the final word. Political participation was restricted by property, literacy, and sex. Rural land inequality was largely intact, and tenancy and debt were widespread. Governance differed sharply between the lowlands, the Cordillera, and the Muslim south, where violence and special rule had marked the period. Racial hierarchy structured American policy and knowledge. And Filipino expectations about independence conflicted with American uncertainty and with the economic interests that preferential trade had created.

Institutions designed to stabilize colonial authority also produced Filipino political leadership, administrative capacity, and claims to self-government. Schools produced the educated men and women who staffed the bureaucracy and argued for national rights. The Assembly trained a political class. The courts produced lawyers who used American legal language to question American policy. The same developments also bound some Filipino interests to colonial arrangements, which complicated later struggles. The next stage, examined in Chapter 131, would build directly on this base, through the Jones Act of 1916, the expansion of Filipinization, the independence missions, and the establishment of the Commonwealth in 1935.

The record of early American rule is strongest when achievements and costs are placed side by side in the same institution. A school could teach literacy and the assumption that American rule was a necessary stage toward civilization. A road could improve access to markets while facilitating police operations and increasing land speculation. A court could offer a mechanism of appeal while using procedures and languages that disadvantaged a poor litigant. A Philippine Assembly could publicly debate the government budget while remaining subordinate to an appointed body and to Congress in Washington. These are not contradictions that must be resolved by choosing either praise or condemnation. They are the specific historical relationships that made colonial institutions useful, contested, and unequal.

The scale of the archipelago is equally important. The experience of an elected politician in Manila, a teacher in a provincial town, a lowland tenant, a Cordillera community negotiating an appointed governor's instructions, and a Tausug family confronting armed patrols does not fit a single developmental sequence. Administrative reports often measured progress in roads, tax receipts, school enrollment, and the number of towns deemed peaceful; independent scrutiny asks which groups were represented by those measures and what alternative outcomes might have been possible. By 1914 the institutions created since 1901 had made Filipino officials and political organizations more capable of acting at national scale, while preferential trade and elite patronage created constituencies with stakes in the existing order. The debate over future self-government thus unfolded inside political and economic arrangements shaped by conquest, accommodation, and uneven reform, not outside them. That unresolved situation, rather than any claim that colonial rule automatically produced independence, provides the appropriate transition to the later developments examined in Chapter 131.

Historical Debate

Did Early American Institutions Consolidate Imperial Rule or Prepare Self-Government?

The question: Were the institutions of 1901–1914 primarily instruments of imperial consolidation, or did they primarily lay the foundations for Filipino self-government?

Interpretation A — consolidation: Schools, police, courts, elections, and infrastructure secured American control, co-opted elites, classified populations, and tied the economy to the United States. Representation was limited by design.
Interpretation B — foundation for self-government: The same institutions trained administrators, politicians, and professionals and gave Filipinos legal and political tools to claim independence; Filipino actors reshaped them for their own purposes.

Evidence: Legislation, administrative reports, election and Assembly records, school and employment statistics, press and party materials, and later political outcomes.

Points of disagreement: Scholars differ over the emphasis given to American intent versus Filipino agency, and over how far later independence reflected the institutions themselves or the later circumstances of the 1930s and 1940s.

Why it matters: The question shapes how the American period is placed in Philippine and imperial history. The strongest answer recognizes that the two outcomes were not mutually exclusive: consolidation and preparation occurred through the same institutions.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 119 has examined how the United States turned the conquest described in Chapter 118 into a functioning colonial state between 1901 and 1914. Its central argument is that the colonial government expanded education, administration, and political participation while retaining foreign sovereignty, and that those expansions simultaneously consolidated American rule and strengthened Filipino demands for self-government.

The state was built quickly. The Second Philippine Commission took over lawmaking in 1900, Taft became civil governor in July 1901, and the Commission enacted municipal and provincial government, a judiciary, a school system, and a constabulary within months. The Organic Act of 1902 supplied a statutory framework that extended civil rights, retained appointed executive authority, and promised an elected assembly after conditions were met. The distinctions among legal protection, representation, and sovereignty explain why Filipinos could regard the Act as both an advance and a limit.

Local government and policing show how the system operated in practice. Filipino notables, including former revolutionaries, staffed municipal and provincial offices under American supervision, in a bargain that gave them influence and gave the colonial state legitimacy. The Constabulary, courts, and laws on sedition and brigandage combined ordinary governance with coercion, and historians such as Alfred McCoy have stressed the importance of policing in the formation of the colonial state.

Social transformation was real but uneven. Act No. 74 created a public school system in which English served as the medium and Filipino teachers formed the bulk of the teaching force; the pensionado program and the University of the Philippines created a small educated elite. Religious policy disestablished the Catholic Church, produced the Iglesia Filipina Independiente, and opened the way for Protestant missions. Public health campaigns and new medical institutions helped to control epidemics, though they also involved coercion and unequal access, as Warwick Anderson's work emphasizes.

The economy was reoriented toward the United States. Roads, railways, ports, and cables integrated the colony and strengthened Manila, though investment followed strategic and commercial priorities. Tariffs culminating in free trade in 1913 expanded exports and created dependence. The friar-land purchase and land registration laws changed the formal ownership of land but did not produce broad peasant ownership, and tenants, laborers, and small cultivators gained less from growth than landowners and merchants.

Electoral politics emerged under restricted conditions. The First Philippine Assembly of 1907 was a significant milestone, elected by roughly a hundred thousand men and subject to the Commission and the governor. The Nacionalista Party won with a platform of immediate independence while working within colonial institutions, and patronage-based politics, which Benedict Anderson called cacique democracy, shaped party life. Whether participation advanced independence or entrenched elite collaboration remains debated.

Racial ideology influenced both policy and representation. The division between “civilized” and “non-Christian” peoples, the activities of the Ethnological Survey, and the 1904 St. Louis exhibition expressed ideas that Paul Kramer and others have shown to be central to American imperial thinking. Filipinos criticized and reshaped these representations, though elites were also implicated in hierarchies of their own.

Colonial rule was uneven across the archipelago. The Moro Province of 1903, the violence at Bud Dajo in 1906 and Bud Bagsak in 1913, and the Mountain Province of 1908 show how the Americans governed Muslim and highland communities differently from the lowlands, relying on military administration and ethnographic classification. The replacement of the Moro Province by a civilian department in 1914 marked a change of policy rather than an end to these differences.

Collaboration and resistance coexisted. Filipinos cooperated for many reasons, from conviction to pressure to strategy, while armed resistance, such as Sakay's, lingered until 1906 and 1907 and in the south until 1913. New publics of newspapers, labor groups, professional associations, and women's organizations developed, though participation remained narrow.

By 1914 Washington's policies and Filipino pressure had begun to shift toward Filipinization under Governor-General Harrison. The chapter closes with an unresolved paradox: the institutions that sustained foreign rule had also created the leaders, skills, and arguments through which Filipinos would claim self-government, a story continued in Chapter 131.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how colonial rule works after conquest. Armies establish control, but states require courts, clerks, schools, taxes, and the cooperation of at least some of the governed. Examining the early American Philippines shows that this cooperation was bargained for and contested, and that those who took part were not passive.

The chapter also reveals a paradox that shaped the rest of the twentieth century in the Philippines. The institutions that brought the islands under American sovereignty trained the political class that would demand independence, spread the language in which the demand was made, and created networks of patronage that would endure well beyond the colonial period. The roads and trade laws that integrated the economy also created interests that complicated independence.

Regional and social variation is a further lesson. The Philippines of 1914 was not governed uniformly, and the experience of Manila professionals, tenant farmers, Cordillera communities, and Muslim households differed greatly. Evidence about elites is far fuller than evidence about the rural majority, and good history must say so.

The developments of 1901 to 1914 set the stage for Chapter 131, which follows the movement from Filipinization and the Jones Act toward the Commonwealth. They also connect to the wider story of this textbook, in which colonial states across Southeast Asia, from Dutch Indonesia to British Malaya and French Indochina, built institutions that they could not fully control, and whose inheritors shaped the postcolonial states of the region.
