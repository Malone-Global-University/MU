Borobudur and the Buddhist Landscape of Java

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Borobudur is not a palace, a tomb, or a conventional temple with an interior chamber. It is a vast stepped monument of volcanic stone, raised around a natural hill on the Kedu Plain of Central Java, whose walls carry thousands of sculpted panels and whose upper platforms carry scores of perforated stupas. Its builders left no surviving foundation inscription explaining who commissioned it, when it was finished, or exactly how it was used, yet the monument itself is one of the richest sources for the religion, art, labor, and political ambition of ninth-century Java.

A visitor approaching Borobudur from the surrounding countryside encounters first a mass: a pyramid-like structure of gray stone whose lower levels are square in plan, whose upper levels are circular, and whose summit is crowned by a large, closed stupa. Only at close range does the surface resolve into its details: long corridors with relief-covered walls, balustrades pierced by niches, staircases aligned with the four directions, and, on the upper terraces, rows of bell-shaped latticed stupas, each of which encloses a seated Buddha image. The monument today stands roughly thirty-five meters high, and its base measures on the order of a hundred and twenty meters on each side. These dimensions matter less than the density of the sculpture: archaeological inventories commonly count 1,460 narrative relief panels and 1,212 decorative panels, and the monument's niches and stupas held 504 Buddha statues in its original design.

Borobudur belongs to the world examined in Chapter 30, when the Sailendra dynasty and other Central Javanese rulers patronized Buddhist and Śaiva foundations on the volcanic plains of the island's interior. But where Chapter 30 follows the dynasty and its politics, this chapter takes up the monument itself: its setting, plan, engineering, sacred imagery, and the kinds of historical reasoning it supports. The monument invites a question that organizes the whole chapter: how did the builders of Borobudur transform a Central Javanese landscape into a monumental expression of Buddhist teachings, royal patronage, artistic knowledge, and social organization?

The question is easier to ask than to answer, because the evidence is both extraordinary and incomplete. The monument preserves thousands of sculpted scenes, a sophisticated architectural plan, and drainage, foundations, and stonework that testify to specialized engineering. Yet no single surviving inscription unequivocally records its foundation. Royal charters from the ninth century mention Buddhist foundations and members of the Sailendra court, but how those records relate to Borobudur is a matter of scholarly interpretation. This chapter therefore treats Borobudur as a historical archive that must be read with method: it can reveal a great deal about the society that made it without supplying a complete account of its builders, its founders, or its ritual life.

That distinction runs through every section that follows. The visible structure can be measured and described; the religious meanings assigned to its levels are interpretations, some widely shared and some contested. The reliefs depict Buddhist narratives drawn from texts, not photographs of eighth-century life, though they preserve knowledge of dress, houses, boats, and social relations that artists could draw upon. Even the monument's present appearance reflects nineteenth- and twentieth-century clearance, documentation, and reconstruction, so a responsible historian must always ask which parts are original ninth-century material, which are conserved, and which are modern restoration.

Borobudur also illustrates themes introduced in Chapters 1 and 19. Javanese rulers, artists, and religious specialists did not simply receive Indian Buddhism; they selected texts, adapted iconography, and rendered the Buddha's story in a local stone vocabulary, with Javanese dress, architecture, and landscapes visible in the carvings. The monument is thus an example of localization in practice. It likewise belongs to the Indian Ocean and maritime Buddhist networks examined in Chapters 18 and 29, which carried texts, teachers, and images across the Bay of Bengal and the Java Sea.

Finally, Borobudur is a monument with a long afterlife. Its later fortunes, its documentation in the nineteenth century, the international conservation campaign of the twentieth, and its inscription on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1991 form part of its history. This chapter therefore ends not with the ninth century alone but with the question of how a monument built by one society becomes an archaeological source, a conservation challenge, and a living site of heritage for others. The chapter that follows, Chapter 32, turns to Prambanan, Mataram, and the shift of political power toward East Java.

Learning Objectives

  3. Explain the relationship of Sailendra patronage to Borobudur while distinguishing established evidence from scholarly inference.

Historical Context

By the eighth century, the interior of Central Java had become a zone of consolidating political authority. Volcanic soils, reliable water from mountain streams, and wet-rice agriculture supported dense rural populations. Inscriptions from the late seventh and eighth centuries, composed in Sanskrit and Old Javanese, show rulers and elites adopting Indian-derived titles, religious vocabulary, and ritual practice while framing them in local institutions of land, labor, and tax. This is the world of the early Javanese kingdoms introduced in Chapter 27 and the Mataram-period political competition examined in Chapter 30.

Two religious currents are central to the chapter. The first was Mahāyāna Buddhism, whose texts and teachers moved along maritime routes linking Java to eastern India, Sri Lanka, the Malay world, and China, a network discussed in connection with Srivijaya in Chapter 29. The second was Śaiva religion, which received royal patronage on the same plains and would produce monuments such as Prambanan, examined in Chapter 32. These traditions coexisted and interacted. Rulers and elites could patronize both, and religious foundations could be tied to land grants, ritual obligations, and local communities.

The Sailendra dynasty, whose political history is treated in Chapter 30, is associated in surviving inscriptions with Buddhist patronage in Central Java during the late eighth and early ninth centuries. Whether the Sailendras were a distinct lineage, a title adopted by a ruling house, or something else is a matter that Chapter 30 addresses; this chapter needs only the essential point that Buddhist foundations on the Kedu Plain were created under political patrons whose authority drew on religion, land, and organized labor.

Students should also keep in mind the broader building tradition of Central Java. Stone temples, called candi in Javanese usage, were constructed in volcanic andesite across the central plains between roughly the eighth and tenth centuries. Borobudur differs from most of them in scale, in plan, and in the absence of a conventional enclosed interior sanctuary, yet it belongs to the same craft environment of stone quarrying, carving, and construction. Comparisons with later monumental religious architecture, such as the Khmer temples discussed in Chapters 35 through 37 and the Buddhist foundations of Pagan discussed in Chapter 41, illustrate that monumental religious building was a recurring means by which Southeast Asian rulers and communities expressed devotion and authority, while taking different local forms.

Finally, historians must contend with a modern layer of context. Borobudur was documented, cleared, photographed, partly dismantled, and reconstructed during colonial and postcolonial periods, and modern scholarship has repeatedly revised older assumptions. The chapter therefore pays continuous attention to the distinction between ninth-century evidence and modern interpretation.

Section 31.1

Reconstructing Borobudur: Archaeology, Inscriptions, Art History, and Historical Interpretation

Historians reconstruct Borobudur's history from several kinds of evidence, none of which alone is sufficient. The first is the monument itself: its stone fabric, plan, joints, drainage, and foundations, studied by archaeologists, architects, and engineers. The second is the sculpture, thousands of relief panels and freestanding images that can be analyzed for iconography, style, and relationship to Buddhist texts. The third is epigraphy. Borobudur carries short incised labels, particularly on the concealed lower foot, which scholars have used for palaeographic dating, that is, comparing letter forms with those of dated inscriptions. The fourth is a body of royal inscriptions from Central Java that mention Buddhist foundations and members of the Sailendra court. The fifth is later written evidence, including Javanese chronicle traditions and the reports of nineteenth-century surveyors.

What is missing is as important as what survives. No known inscription clearly records the monument's foundation, its date of completion, its original name, or the intentions of its founders. The name "Borobudur" itself is attested only in later sources, and its etymology and relation to any ninth-century designation are uncertain. Some scholars have proposed that a Sanskrit-derived name appearing in ninth-century records referred to Borobudur, but such proposals depend on interpreting inscriptions whose relation to the monument is not explicit. Historians therefore speak of the monument's date as a range and of its patronage as a probable association rather than a documented fact.

This absence has methodological consequences. Chronologies built from palaeography depend on a limited number of comparison inscriptions. Chronologies built from art history rely on stylistic comparison with other Central Javanese monuments whose dates are themselves partly inferred. Architectural phasing, which identifies stages of construction from structural seams and changes in plan, offers relative sequence rather than calendar dates. Each method answers a different question, and each carries its own uncertainty.

Students should also separate original evidence from modern layers. Colonial-era investigators cleared vegetation and volcanic debris, photographed and drew the monument, and in some cases made interpretive claims that later research has challenged. Twentieth-century restorers dismantled and rebuilt large sections, replacing fill, adding concrete and drainage layers, and re-setting stones. These interventions are part of Borobudur's modern history, and historians must consult conservation records to know which parts of the visible monument are original ninth-century placement and which are reconstruction.

The governing principle of the chapter follows from this survey: a monument can preserve exceptional evidence about a historical society without providing a complete account of its builders or intended use. Borobudur reveals engineering knowledge, Buddhist textual learning, artistic skill, and the capacity to organize resources. It does not by itself tell us the identity of every patron, the daily routines of its religious community, or the precise ceremonies conducted there.

Source Method

Archaeology, Inscriptions, Texts, and Art History: What Each Can and Cannot Show

  • Archaeology and architecture establish physical structure, construction sequence, materials, and later modification. They cannot by themselves name patrons or date events in years.
  • Inscriptions can supply names, dates, titles, and endowments. They survive unevenly, are often formulaic, and may refer to foundations that cannot be securely identified with a surviving monument.
  • Buddhist texts help identify what the reliefs depict. A close match to a text does not prove the sculptors used a particular manuscript, and carved scenes often depart from written versions.
  • Art history and iconography compare styles and symbols across monuments. Stylistic dating gives relative sequences; it requires fixed points from inscriptions or other evidence.
  • Later records and photographs document the monument's condition before restoration. They are valuable but reflect the interests of the observers who made them.

Section 31.2

The Kedu Plain: Mountains, Rivers, Agriculture, and the Sacred Landscape

Borobudur stands in the Kedu Plain, in what is now Magelang Regency in Central Java. The plain lies in an interior basin framed by volcanic mountains and hills: Merapi and Merbabu to the east and northeast, Sumbing and Sindoro to the north and northwest, and the Menoreh Hills to the south. The monument sits near the point where the Elo River flows into the Progo, a juncture that places it within a network of watercourses draining the surrounding highlands. The environmental background to such landscapes is introduced in Chapter 5, which discusses volcanism, soils, and rivers across the region.

These conditions matter historically in specific ways. Volcanic ash and river sediment produce fertile soils, and reliable streams support irrigated rice cultivation, so the plain could sustain dense rural populations capable of producing agricultural surplus. Rivers also supplied water for workers and, in all likelihood, a source of the andesite stone used in construction, which is a volcanic rock available in boulders and outcrops in the region. Roads and river routes connected the plain with other parts of Java. A large construction project required food, tools, timber for scaffolding and ramps, and the coordination of many people over a long period. The Kedu Plain provided the agrarian basis for that effort.

It would be a mistake to move from this to determinism. Fertile plains existed elsewhere in Java and Southeast Asia without producing a Borobudur. What made the monument possible was the interaction of environmental resources with political authority, religious patronage, craft knowledge, and social organization. The environment created possibilities; people with institutions and purposes realized one of them.

The mountainous setting also invites interpretation. Many scholars have suggested that the surrounding peaks and the hill around which the monument was built contributed to its sacred meaning, echoing a widespread Southeast Asian and South Asian association of sacred places with mountains. This is plausible, and the monument's builders did choose to shape a natural hill into a stepped structure. But no surviving text states that the site was selected because of a particular visible mountain or alignment. Historians should therefore distinguish three things: demonstrable construction decisions, such as using a natural hill as a core; plausible symbolic readings, such as the monument evoking a sacred mountain; and speculative claims, such as precise astronomical or geomantic planning, which require independent evidence.

A further caution concerns the landscape's changes. Archaeologists and geologists have debated whether a body of water or marshy area lay near the monument in antiquity, and whether volcanic and fluvial deposition altered the surrounding terrain. Such arguments bear on how the monument appeared in its own time, but they remain contested and should not be assumed in the narrative of its construction.

Section 31.3

The Sailendras, Royal Patronage, and the Construction of Borobudur, c. 780–850

Borobudur is conventionally associated with the Sailendra rulers who patronized Buddhism in Central Java in the late eighth and ninth centuries. The full political history of the dynasty, including questions about its origins and its relations with Srivijaya, is treated in Chapter 30. For present purposes the essential point is that ninth-century inscriptions connect Sailendra-period rulers with Buddhist foundations, and that the scale of Borobudur implies patronage by an authority able to mobilize extensive resources.

The most commonly cited construction range extends from about 780 to about 830, though some accounts extend work into the mid-ninth century. These are scholarly estimates, not recorded dates. Architectural studies commonly identify several building phases: shaping and stabilizing the hill; constructing the square galleries and balustrades; adding a base-enlarging structure that concealed the original foot; and modifying or completing the upper circular platforms and stupas. The number and exact character of the phases are debated.

Samaratungga and Pramodhawardhani are the royal figures most often linked to the monument. Samaratungga is traditionally placed in the early ninth century as a Sailendra ruler, and Pramodhawardhani is described in inscriptions as a royal daughter and is often treated as a patron of Buddhist foundations. In some modern narratives Samaratungga is called the builder of Borobudur and Pramodhawardhani its completer. This is a convenient shorthand, not a documented conclusion. It rests on interpreting inscriptions that mention these figures in connection with a Buddhist sanctuary, and on assumptions about how long construction took and in which reigns it occurred.

The Karangtengah or Kayumwungan inscription, dated 824 CE, is central to this problem. It names Samaratungga and Pramodhawardhani and refers to a Buddhist sacred foundation, but whether that foundation is Borobudur, Mendut, another nearby monument, or a complex that cannot be identified is debated. Some scholars have proposed that a place-name in later royal documents referring to a sacred foundation endowed with land refers to Borobudur, but these readings rely on connecting texts across decades without a decisive link. The inscription is therefore best treated as important contextual evidence for Buddhist royal patronage, not as an unambiguous foundation document.

Even if the chronology remains uncertain, the monument shows what such patronage involved. A project of this scale needed planners able to conceive an integrated architectural and iconographic program, sculptors able to execute thousands of panels, and agricultural and administrative systems able to sustain the work for years or decades. Patronage here means not only a ruler's will but the capacity of a court, its officials, and its rural base to organize labor and supplies. The social dimensions of that capacity are developed in Section 31.15.

The Karangtengah (Kayumwungan) Inscription, 824 CE

Creator: Court-sponsored inscription from Central Java · Date: 824 CE (Śaka 746) · Type: Bilingual stone inscription in five surviving fragments; Sanskrit and Old Javanese in Old Javanese script

Context: The surviving inscription comprises five stone fragments, dated Śaka 746 (824 CE). Its opening twenty-four lines are in Sanskrit, followed by Old Javanese. It names Samaratungga and Pramodhawardhani in connection with a Buddhist jinalaya and refers to Śrīmad Venuvana, literally a bamboo-grove name associated with a sacred establishment. The Old Javanese portion records a protected sīma grant involving rice land at Kayumwungan. These particulars are described in the Borobudur Conservation Office's digital reference entry; the identification of the named sanctuaries with Borobudur or other surviving temples remains disputed.

Editorial paraphrase (not a translated quotation):

The Sanskrit portion names Samaratungga and Pramodhawardhani in relation to a Buddhist sacred foundation, using the terms jinalaya and Venuvana. The Old Javanese portion records a grant involving Kayumwungan rice fields. The inscription joins religious patronage to a concrete arrangement concerning land and privilege.

Analysis: The bilingual structure allows historians to distinguish a courtly Sanskrit dedication from an Old Javanese land-grant record. Both matter: a sacred foundation required not only praise and consecration but also material support. The inscription anchors a securely dated Buddhist endowment in 824, though not a securely dated phase of Borobudur's construction.

Limitations: The terms jinalaya and Venuvana are not unambiguous modern site labels. J. G. de Casparis associated Venuvana with Mendut; Soekmono proposed Ngawen. Other identifications have also been advanced. This edition does not equate the named establishment automatically with Borobudur or treat the inscription as a foundation charter for the monument.

Historical Debate

Dating and Patronage of Borobudur

The question: Who commissioned Borobudur, and when was it built, given that no unequivocal foundation inscription survives?

Interpretation A — Sailendra patronage in the late eighth and early ninth centuries: Many scholars connect construction with Sailendra rulers, citing stylistic parallels with other Buddhist monuments, palaeographic dating of the incised labels, and the 824 inscription naming Samaratungga and Pramodhawardhani. On this view work took place roughly between 780 and 830, perhaps continuing later.
Interpretation B — Greater caution about linking specific rulers to specific stages: Other scholars stress that the inscriptions do not name Borobudur, that construction may have spanned several reigns, and that architectural phasing allows for different sequences. They argue for describing the monument as the product of Sailendra-period Buddhist patronage without assigning founder and completer roles.

Evidence: Both positions use palaeography, architectural analysis, art-historical comparison, and the inscriptions. They differ in how much weight to place on possible identifications of place-names.

Points of disagreement: Chronology (start and end), interpretation of inscriptions, and the number of construction phases.

Why it matters: If construction was brief and associated with one reign, Borobudur is a single royal statement; if prolonged across reigns, it reflects continuing institutional commitment and shifting priorities.

Student task: Identify the strongest evidence for each position, then propose one kind of new evidence, such as a dated inscription found in situ, that would most reduce uncertainty.

Section 31.4

Mendut, Pawon, and the Wider Buddhist Sacred Landscape

Borobudur was not an isolated structure. To its east lie two smaller Buddhist monuments, Pawon and Mendut. Pawon is a small temple lying relatively close to Borobudur, and Mendut lies farther along, at a distance of a few kilometers. The three monuments are arranged along a roughly straight line, a relationship recognized in UNESCO's World Heritage designation, which treats them as a group. Mendut is notable for a large interior sanctuary containing a monumental seated Buddha flanked by two bodhisattva figures, in contrast to Borobudur's absence of an enclosed chamber. Pawon is smaller and its original image is not preserved.

The relationship among the three has prompted several interpretations. One proposes that they formed a coordinated ritual landscape, with devotees moving from one monument to another. Another emphasizes their shared Buddhist character and nearby locations while allowing that they were built at different times and for different purposes. Scholars have also suggested that Mendut may be the sanctuary referred to in the Karangtengah inscription, but this is not agreed. The evidence for an unbroken ancient ceremonial route linking the three is not conclusive: the alignment is observable, but no excavation or text has demonstrated a continuous processional road used in the ninth century. Modern Buddhist observances in the area, such as annual celebrations that link the monuments, are an important contemporary tradition but cannot be projected back as proof of ancient practice.

Beyond these three, Central Java contains other Buddhist and Śaiva foundations. Buddhist complexes on the plains near the later Prambanan area, discussed with Chapters 30 and 32, show that Buddhist patronage extended across the region, not only at Kedu. This regional pattern supports a broader point: Borobudur was part of a network of religious foundations, each tied to patrons, land, and communities. It was not the only expression of Central Javanese Buddhism, though it was exceptional in scale and sculptural program.

Treating the monuments together also clarifies a methodological issue. If Mendut, Pawon, and Borobudur formed parts of a single plan, then evidence from one may help interpret the others. If they were separate projects whose alignment arose later or incidentally, such inferences are weaker. Historians therefore describe the alignment as a significant observed relationship and a plausible basis for sacred-landscape interpretation, while stopping short of claiming a documented, planned processional axis.

Division IIArchitecture, Engineering, and Buddhist Cosmology
Section 31.5

Borobudur's Architectural Plan: Terraces, Galleries, Stairways, and Stupas

Borobudur is a stepped structure built around and over a natural hill. Its lower portion consists of a base platform and five successive square terraces, four of which form galleries: corridors bounded on the inside by a wall carved with reliefs and on the outside by a balustrade. Above these rise three circular platforms, bearing rows of perforated stupas, and at the center a large principal stupa. Counts of the monument's levels vary depending on whether the base and enclosing structure are counted separately, so students should check how a given source counts.

Staircases at the four cardinal directions connect the levels, passing through gateways whose archways were once decorated with guardian and ornamental motifs. A visitor walking the galleries moves along corridors that turn at each corner, so that the reliefs are encountered in sequence, and then ascends to the next level. Because the corridors are narrow and framed by walls, the experience on the lower levels is enclosed and inward-looking, directing attention to the carved panels. On the circular terraces the walls and balustrades fall away, and the visitor stands in the open air among the stupas, with the landscape visible on all sides.

The architecture thus orders movement. Entry is possible from each direction, but the narrative reliefs are arranged to be read while walking in a clockwise direction, with the monument on the walker's right. This pattern corresponds to pradakṣiṇa, the circumambulation of a sacred object, a widespread Buddhist and Hindu practice, and the layout of galleries invites repetition of the circuit at each level. How this design was used in practice is addressed in Section 31.13.

It is important to separate observation from interpretation. That the monument has square lower terraces, circular upper terraces, and a central stupa can be directly observed and measured. That the progression from square to circular, and from carved to relatively plain surfaces, corresponds to a Buddhist cosmological scheme is an interpretation, widely discussed but not recorded in a surviving contemporary explanatory text. Similarly, the description of the plan as a mandala, a sacred diagram, is a scholarly reading, which Section 31.7 examines. Students should therefore learn to state claims in two registers: what the structure is, and what it has been taken to mean.

One further feature is the absence of an interior sanctuary. Unlike Mendut or many Central Javanese temples, Borobudur has no chamber in which an image is enshrined and worshipped. Its central stupa is solid, or at least closed. This has led some scholars to describe the monument primarily as a stupa and others to emphasize its distinctness from temples, a classification problem taken up in the vocabulary feature of Section 31.7.

Section 31.6

Engineering Borobudur: Andesite Stone, Drainage, Foundations, and Construction Phases

Borobudur is built mainly of andesite, a volcanic stone available in the river valleys and slopes of the region. Estimates of the total volume of stone are commonly cited in the tens of thousands of cubic meters, but such figures depend on assumptions about the hill core and the fill, and they should be treated as approximate. The stones were cut into blocks and fitted without mortar, held by their weight and by interlocking joints and shaped connections. Evidence from the monument suggests that blocks were set in place and that the relief carving was frequently completed afterward, on the assembled walls, which explains why some panels show unfinished areas and carving marks.

The central problem for such a structure is stability. A heavy stone monument on a hill in a high-rainfall tropical environment is vulnerable to water. Borobudur incorporated drainage: channels and spouts carry rainwater from the terraces to the exterior, and the surviving system includes numerous waterspouts, often carved as stylized sea-monster forms. Modern restoration found that the interior fill had absorbed water and that drainage had failed in places, leading to bulging and displacement of stones. The twentieth-century restoration therefore added new drainage layers, a reminder that engineering challenges persisted across the monument's life.

Transporting and raising the stone required organized effort, though the specific methods are not recorded. Historians infer the use of ramps, sledges, levers, and scaffolding from general knowledge of premodern construction and from traces on the stone, but without textual descriptions, reconstructions of the process remain provisional. Claims about the number of workers or the duration of work need particular caution and are addressed in Section 31.15.

Archaeological Study

The Hidden Foot and Construction Changes

What was found: In 1885 an investigator recorded that the monument's base concealed an earlier lower structure bearing carved reliefs. This original foot had been covered by an additional encasing structure. Its reliefs, 160 panels, were photographed in 1890–1891 before the foot was covered again. Only a portion at the southeast corner remains visible today.

Competing explanations for the encasement:

  • Structural explanation: The original base was prone to spreading or settling under the weight of the upper monument, and builders added an enclosing structure as a buttress. Evidence cited includes signs of stress and the need for stabilization.
  • Religious or design explanation: The builders altered the plan, perhaps to conform to a revised program or to conceal the scenes of karmic consequence beneath a new base, so that the monument's visible narrative began with the Buddha's life.
  • Combined explanation: A structural problem may have prompted a change that also allowed a revised iconographic program.

How to evaluate: Look for evidence of settlement in the original foot, the relationship of the added base to the existing structure, and whether the new base was planned or reactive. The question remains unresolved.

Student exercise: Draw a simple cross-section showing a hill core, original foot, and added base. Then explain which features of your drawing are observed and which are hypothesized.

The addition of the encasing base is not the only evidence of change. Architectural studies identify alterations in the plan of the upper levels, including modification of the circular terraces and the arrangement of stupas, and changes in the form of the balustrades and gateways. These suggest that the monument's design evolved during construction, whether through changing resources, a revised religious program, or engineering problems encountered along the way. Recognizing this prevents the error of treating Borobudur as the unmodified realization of a single master plan.

The modern restorations are themselves an engineering chapter. The first major campaign, early in the twentieth century, cleaned and stabilized the monument but did not fully solve the drainage problem. The later campaign, from the 1970s, dismantled the upper levels and much of the galleries, installed concrete support and drainage layers, and reassembled the stones using a method in which original elements are returned to their positions where possible. The visible monument today is thus an original structure substantially re-set on modern foundations, an essential consideration when assessing its fabric as historical evidence.

Section 31.7

Buddhist Cosmology and the Three Realms: Kāmadhātu, Rūpadhātu, and Arūpadhātu

A common interpretation of Borobudur reads the monument as an expression of a Buddhist model of the universe and of the path through it. In many Buddhist traditions, existence is described as divided into three realms: the realm of desire (kāmadhātu), where beings are driven by craving and experience the consequences of their actions; the realm of form (rūpadhātu), where beings have refined bodies and states of meditative concentration; and the formless realm (arūpadhātu), the most subtle states of consciousness. Beyond these lies liberation or enlightenment. In Mahāyāna Buddhism, the ideal practitioner is the bodhisattva, who seeks awakening for the benefit of all beings and progresses through stages of realization.

Under the traditional reading, the hidden foot corresponds to kāmadhātu, with its reliefs of karmic cause and effect; the square galleries correspond to rūpadhātu, with their narrative of the Buddha's life and the bodhisattva path; and the circular platforms with their latticed stupas and the closed central stupa correspond to arūpadhātu, a realm without form. A visitor ascending the monument would thus symbolically ascend through levels of existence and understanding. This interpretation is attractive because it matches several observable features: the shift from carved to plain surfaces, from enclosed corridors to open terraces, and from narrative images to abstract forms.

Yet the reading has limits. The monument's architectural divisions do not map neatly onto every aspect of the scheme. For example, the hidden foot was concealed, so the visible monument did not begin with the kāmadhātu reliefs; the square galleries contain a range of narratives not all limited to what one would call "form"; and no surviving contemporary text states that the monument was designed to embody the three realms in this way. The scheme is therefore best treated as a scholarly interpretation, supported by correspondences, rather than a demonstrated blueprint. Other readings emphasize the sequence of reliefs and the bodhisattva path without insisting on a rigid cosmological mapping, or they view the monument as a combination of stupa, mountain, and mandala.

For students, the lesson is about method. Cosmological interpretations can be heuristically powerful, but each claimed correspondence needs to be checked against the evidence: what is carved where, what texts illuminate it, and whether the interpretation explains features that alternatives do not. Not every architectural element has a single universally accepted theological meaning, and the richest readings tend to acknowledge multiple layers of significance.

Historical Vocabulary
Stupa, Candi, Mandala, and Cosmology

Stupa is a Buddhist monument, originally a mound, associated with relics or commemoration and used as a focus of devotion and circumambulation. Candi is the Javanese term commonly applied to ancient stone temples, Hindu and Buddhist; Borobudur is often called a candi in popular usage, although it lacks a typical interior sanctuary and is often described by scholars as a stupa or a stupa-like monument. Mandala, in a Buddhist religious sense, refers to a sacred diagram representing a cosmic or spiritual order; some scholars read Borobudur's plan as mandala-like. This differs from the political "mandala" model introduced in Chapter 1 and associated with O. W. Wolters, a modern analytical concept for patterns of overlapping political authority. Cosmology here means a structured account of the universe and its levels of existence.

Why it matters: The terms point to different aspects of the monument: its religious type, its Javanese architectural context, and its symbolic organization.

Potential misunderstanding: Using "mandala" for both a Buddhist diagram and a political model risks confusion. Calling Borobudur a "temple" without qualification may also obscure its distinctive form.

Section 31.8

Buddha Images, Mudrās, Perforated Stupas, and the Symbolism of the Summit

Buddha images are distributed throughout the monument. Archaeological studies describe 504 Buddha statues in the overall design. Of these, 432 sit in niches along the balustrades of the square terraces, and 72 sit inside the perforated stupas of the circular platforms, which UNESCO records as 72 openwork stupas surrounding the principal stupa. The perforated stupas are arranged in three concentric rows, with fewer stupas on each higher row. Their latticed form allows the seated images within to be glimpsed from outside.

The images are distinguished by hand gestures, or mudrās, which in Buddhist iconography convey aspects of the Buddha's teaching, actions, or states. A common reading associates different gestures with the four directions and with the upper levels, giving the monument a directional symbolism as visitors circle it. The details of these associations, and their relation to Buddhist systems of directional Buddhas, are discussed in the iconography feature below. The images on the upper terraces appear relatively uniform and are enclosed within stupas, which scholars interpret as emphasizing a transition from the visible, narrative lower levels toward a more abstract, interiorized stage.

At the center stands the principal stupa, larger than the others and closed. Reports from the nineteenth century described a chamber or contents within, including an unfinished statue, but the interpretation of those reports is contested, and the question of whether the central stupa held a deposit or image remains uncertain. Some interpreters view the closed form as emblematic of the formless; others stress that the central stupa's meaning cannot be settled without more evidence about its interior and original completion.

The present condition of the sculpture differs from the original program. Many Buddha statues have lost their heads or have been damaged, a result of weathering, collapse, and removal. During the nineteenth century, some pieces were removed from the monument and presented as gifts to or collected by visitors, and thus now reside in collections elsewhere. Restoration has re-set surviving elements but could not replace all that was lost. Counting statues today therefore yields different totals from the original design, and students should be clear whether a source describes the original program or the current condition.

Division IIIReading Borobudur's Reliefs as Buddhist Literature and Historical Evidence
Section 31.9

The Hidden Foot: Karmavibhaṅga and the Moral Consequences of Human Action

Archaeological inventories commonly count 1,460 narrative relief panels and 1,212 decorative panels. The narrative panels do not form a single continuous story. They belong to several distinct textual and artistic programs, arranged on different walls and levels, and read in sequence they take the visitor from the consequences of ordinary action, through the life of the Buddha and exemplary stories of earlier lives, to a long narrative of spiritual seeking. This section begins with the now-concealed lower foot and the study of karmic consequence.

The concealed original foot carries 160 relief panels associated with the Karmavibhaṅga tradition, a body of Buddhist teaching on the analysis of karma: how actions produce results in this and future lives. In Buddhist thought, intentional actions, whether physical, verbal, or mental, have consequences for the one who performs them, and texts of this type classify actions and their outcomes in detail. The Borobudur panels illustrate this teaching in scenes that pair deeds with their results, such as generosity leading to favorable rebirth and cruelty or greed leading to suffering.

These panels are important to historians because they depict a wide range of social situations. Human figures appear as householders, rulers, servants, merchants, hunters, and others, and the scenes include acts of charity, violence, dishonesty, and devotion, as well as depictions of punishment in hells. Because the point of the scenes is moral instruction, they dramatize extremes, and they should be read as didactic representations, not documentation of specific events. Nevertheless the artists drew on observed or familiar details to make the scenes intelligible: houses, clothing, tools, animals, and social relationships appear in recognizable form, giving the panels value for social history, a subject taken up in Section 31.16.

The hidden foot also illustrates the relationship between doctrine and depiction. The same Buddhist teaching can be expressed in a text, a sermon, and a sculpted cycle, and the sculpted version can differ from texts in emphasis and detail. Some scenes correspond closely to known textual descriptions, while others appear to adapt or condense them. The short incised labels on the foot, which have been interpreted as guides for the carvers identifying the scene to be sculpted, provide a rare glimpse into the workshop process, and their script has been used to propose a date range.

Why was the foot enclosed? Section 31.6 presented the competing explanations. Whatever the reason, the effect was that the visible monument began with the Buddha's life rather than karmic consequence, and the Karmavibhaṅga reliefs were hidden from view for centuries. Their accessibility to scholarship owes much to the photographic documentation of 1890–1891, which recorded the panels before the encasing structure was replaced. Without these photographs, researchers could consult only the small exposed portion routinely, even though the other carved stones remain beneath the encasement.

The arrangement of the hidden-foot cycle raises a question that matters beyond Borobudur: how does a sculptor turn a classification of moral actions into images that can be followed? A written analysis of karma may distinguish intention, action, and result in categories that are difficult to see directly. A relief can instead place several figures in a connected scene, use gesture to identify an action, and contrast settings associated with beneficial or harmful outcomes. Yet a single panel need not correspond to a single passage, and an image may represent more than one moment. This complicates attempts to reconstruct a lost manuscript by treating the reliefs as a line-by-line transcription. The monument preserves a sophisticated act of interpretation, not merely an illustrated copy of an imported book.

The numbered photographic record also allows historians to make their arguments testable. In the modern inventory the hidden-foot series is designated O; a photograph catalogued as O.109, for example, was connected by N. J. Krom to passages 27 and 28 of a Mahākarmavibhaṅga tradition. The attribution is a scholarly comparison rather than a label identifying a text carved on the panel itself. Later researchers, including Hariani Santiko, have compared relief episodes with different Chinese translations, especially texts conventionally catalogued as T80 and T81. Differences among possible textual parallels make it important to record the photograph, its panel number, the relevant passage, and the reason for an identification before claiming that the artists followed one particular recension.

Section 31.10

The Lalitavistara Reliefs: The Life of the Buddha and the Teaching of Enlightenment

On the upper part of the main wall of the first gallery, 120 panels are associated with the Lalitavistara, a Mahāyāna Buddhist text, extant in Sanskrit, that recounts the life of the Buddha. The sculpted sequence begins with preparations in the heavenly realm, including the Buddha's descent from the Tuṣita heaven and his conception, and proceeds through his birth, his youth and princely life, his encounters with suffering that prompted his renunciation, his years of ascetic practice, his struggle with the temptations of Māra, his enlightenment, and his first teaching. The sequence concludes at that first sermon, so the sculpted narrative does not follow the whole of the Buddha's later career.

The reliefs adapt a literary narrative into a visual idiom. The sculptors used compositional conventions to convey the sequence: repeated figures, groups arranged around a central figure, architectural settings, and attributes identifying the Buddha-to-be and other characters. The setting is rendered with Javanese features, including buildings, clothing, and landscape elements, so that the story takes place in a world visually familiar to a Javanese audience while preserving its Indian narrative geography. This is a clear example of localization, discussed in Chapter 19: the story is retained, but its visual vocabulary is local.

Comparison with surviving texts shows both correspondence and variation. The sequence of major episodes generally follows the textual tradition, but the sculptors condensed some episodes and expanded others, and it is not safe to assume the carvings reproduce a single recension exactly. Some textual details cannot be shown in stone, while some visual details have no textual counterpart. Because the surviving manuscripts of Buddhist texts are of different dates and recensions, it is also difficult to establish which version would have been available in ninth-century Java. The evidence supports the conclusion that the planners knew the Lalitavistara tradition well, but not that they worked from a particular manuscript.

The presence of the Buddha's biography at the first gallery is significant. By beginning the visible narrative with the Buddha's own life, the monument presents an exemplar of the path before the visitor encounters stories of other beings and of the bodhisattva Sudhana. The life of the Buddha provides the model of liberation that the later reliefs elaborate.

The order of the 120 panels provides a practical way to study artistic planning. A commonly used photographic catalogue groups panels 1–27 around descent, conception, and pregnancy; 28–52 around birth and youth; 53–69 around the signs that prompt renunciation; 70–95 around encounters and spiritual striving; and 96–120 around awakening and the beginning of teaching. These groupings are editorial aids to a continuous sculptural sequence, not labels carved by the ninth-century artists. They enable a student to choose a manageable cluster, establish its narrative order, and ask how a story becomes legible when a viewer encounters one stone scene after another while walking a gallery.

The image of the Buddha's awakening makes this method especially useful. A written narrative can describe inner resolve, temptation, and transformation through speech and narration. A sculptural composition must establish its actors, their relative importance, and the decisive moment through posture, placement, attributes, and surrounding figures. The same episode may consequently occupy more or less visual space than it does in surviving texts. Comparing the carved sequence with a published translation of the Lalitavistara reveals both fidelity to a recognizable sacred narrative and the creative work of Javanese artists. It does not establish exactly which written copy, oral explanation, or teacher's interpretation guided the workshop.

Section 31.11

Jātaka and Avadāna Narratives: Compassion, Generosity, and Moral Example

Jātaka stories recount the Buddha's previous lives, in which, as a bodhisattva, he appears as an animal, a king, a merchant, or another being and practices virtues such as generosity, patience, and self-sacrifice. Avadāna stories are related narratives of exemplary deeds by various figures, which illustrate moral and religious lessons and the results of action. Together they form a large body of narrative literature in Buddhist traditions, widely circulated in South and Southeast Asia and beyond.

At Borobudur such stories appear on the lower parts of the first gallery wall and on balustrades, in panels that offer a visual anthology. The compositions are often compact, centering on a key moment, such as an act of sacrifice or an encounter that tests virtue. Animals, rulers, families, merchants, and ordinary villagers appear in the scenes, and the stories use these figures to convey moral examples. A hare offering itself, a king giving away his possessions, or a merchant facing danger on a voyage are among the kinds of narratives found in such collections, and students should consult specialized catalogues for the specific identified panels.

Interpretation requires care. Identifying a panel with a particular named story depends on comparing it with texts, and in some cases several text traditions contain similar stories with differing details. Some panels have not been securely identified. The selection of stories, which of the great number available were carved, reflects choices by the planners, and these choices may tell us about the virtues they wished to emphasize, though we cannot recover their reasoning directly.

The narrative panels also demonstrate how sculpture functions as both a religious and a social source. As religious communication, the panels present models of compassion and generosity. As social evidence, they show how artists imagined and represented human relationships, from rulers and courtiers to farmers and sailors, in a visual language comprehensible to contemporaries. The next sections show how this evidence can be used, with appropriate caution.

The distinction between Jātaka and Avadāna deserves more attention than a simple list of moral themes. A Jātaka identifies the exemplary protagonist with a former existence of the Buddha, whereas an Avadāna may center another person whose deeds produce consequences across lives. That difference affects how an audience understands the authority of a story. When a bodhisattva sacrifices wealth, safety, or comfort, the narrative presents an ideal that may exceed ordinary human expectations; when another figure receives the results of generosity or cruelty, the lesson may focus on the moral order within which ordinary decisions take place. The sculptors could place both kinds of narrative on a monumental Buddhist route without implying that the two genres were interchangeable.

A second issue is the problem of identification. Animals, courtly gifts, dangerous journeys, and acts of charity occur in more than one Buddhist story collection. An isolated photograph of a ruler, elephant, or boat may therefore support several plausible readings. Secure interpretation requires attention to neighboring panels, the apparent sequence, distinctive attributes, and corresponding passages in textual sources. Even a confident story identification does not make the relief an unfiltered illustration of Java's social conditions. Artistic conventions can transform an Indian narrative setting into a landscape familiar to Javanese viewers, while patrons may select those episodes that best express particular religious values. The relative prominence of generosity, kingship, and self-sacrifice is itself evidence of curatorial choice, although the planners' deliberations are not recorded.

Section 31.12

The Gaṇḍavyūha, Sudhana's Journey, and the Bodhisattva Ideal

The upper galleries of the square terraces present the Gaṇḍavyūha, a Mahāyāna Buddhist text in which a young seeker named Sudhana travels from teacher to teacher in pursuit of enlightenment. Each teacher, a figure the text calls a spiritual friend, instructs him in a different aspect of the path and then sends him onward. The teachers include monks, laypeople, deities, and others of varied backgrounds, which underscores the text's message that wisdom may be found in many kinds of beings. The journey culminates in encounters with major bodhisattvas, and in a final vision of the vow and practice associated with Samantabhadra, which Borobudur's reliefs represent in a separate series of panels.

The Gaṇḍavyūha reliefs occupy a large share of the monument's narrative space and are among its most celebrated sculptures. The sequence is long, and the carvings convey it in successive scenes showing Sudhana in conversation with each teacher in settings that vary from houses and courts to forests and cities. The artists thus portray a wide world through which the pilgrim moves. The text's themes of progressive realization, humility before teachers, and the ideal of acting for the benefit of all beings are central to the bodhisattva path.

The relationship between narrative and architecture is suggestive. Sudhana's journey is itself a pilgrimage, and the visitor who walks the galleries, circuit after circuit, may be seen as accompanying him. Many scholars therefore propose that the monument's design intentionally paralleled narrative and physical movement. This is a plausible reading, but it remains an inference: we have no record of how visitors were instructed to understand the reliefs, and the experience of reading long narratives while walking is not documented.

The upper terraces above the galleries contain no narrative reliefs, only the open circular platforms with their stupas. Interpretations of this transition differ. Some see it as the culmination of the path, where narrative gives way to the silence of the formless; others emphasize practical or constructional reasons or the possibility of changes in plan. The contrast between the richly carved galleries and the plain summit is among the monument's most striking features, and any reading of it should be presented as interpretation, informed by the monument's structure and by Buddhist teaching, and open to revision.

The scale of this program distinguishes it from a decorative selection of sacred anecdotes. Jan Fontein's close study of the Gaṇḍavyūha and associated upper-gallery reliefs examines a corpus of 460 carved panels against three early Chinese translations of Sanskrit-text traditions. That comparison matters because surviving versions differ in details and sequence; a scene that seems unfamiliar beside one translation may make better sense beside another. The young pilgrim's progress also provides a different educational model from the Buddha's completed biography. Rather than beginning with a fully enlightened teacher, it follows a learner who repeatedly admits the limits of his understanding, seeks instruction, and continues onward.

The teachers encountered by Sudhana represent varied places and social roles, making the pursuit of wisdom a journey through a complex imagined world. Some encounters stress moral discipline; others emphasize compassion, insight, or the capacity to perceive reality differently. The architectural fact of ascending galleries invites a comparison with that cumulative learning, but the comparison should remain a hypothesis about intended experience. The reliefs were not equipped with surviving explanatory notices specifying what each ninth-century viewer had to say or do at every station. Their significance lies in the combination of a carefully organized narrative, a traversable monument, and an intellectual program that cannot be reduced to a single slogan about enlightenment.

Division IVRitual Experience, Religious Communities, and Central Javanese Society
Section 31.13

Walking the Monument: Circumambulation, Ritual Movement, and Pilgrimage

The architecture of Borobudur provides for clockwise circumambulation, pradakṣiṇa, a practice in which devotees walk around a sacred object keeping it on their right. The galleries are continuous corridors, their staircases are placed at the four directions, and the reliefs are laid out to be read in a clockwise circuit. The visitor can thus walk each gallery, ascend, and repeat, passing in sequence through the monument's narratives.

What the architecture makes possible is not the same as what historians can document. It is reasonable to infer that the monument was used for devotion, instruction, and merit-making: walking among images of the Buddha, listening to or recalling stories, and making offerings are consistent with Buddhist practice and with the design. Some interpreters suggest ceremonial processions or pilgrimages to the monument at particular times. But no surviving ninth-century description records the rituals performed at Borobudur, who took part, how often, or what rules governed access. We cannot assume that all visitors walked the entire monument, that all followed the same sequence, or that all understood the reliefs in the same way.

The site's openness invites further questions. Were all levels accessible to all? Were some parts reserved for religious specialists or royal participants? The architecture does not answer these questions. The narrowness of galleries may have limited the number of people moving at once, and the upper terraces provided open space, but any claim about social restriction or ceremonial hierarchy would require evidence not currently available. Likewise, modern observances at Borobudur, including large Buddhist gatherings in the region, are a living tradition that illuminates continuing significance, yet they are not evidence of ninth-century ritual.

Movement through the galleries was also physical work shaped by the building's dimensions. Turning corners repeatedly, negotiating stairways, and encountering panels in changing light would have produced a different experience from studying a modern photograph or a flat diagram. The juxtaposition of carved walls and balustrades could focus a visitor's attention on nearby scenes, while the open circular terraces would have changed the sense of enclosure. These contrasts are observable properties of the building. Whether a particular ritual leader used them to teach stages of a doctrinal path cannot be demonstrated without additional evidence. Architecture creates opportunities for practice; it does not by itself preserve a full liturgy.

Equally important is the question of who could read the monument. Following a sequence of carved scenes would not necessarily require literacy in Sanskrit, but identifying every episode and drawing elaborate doctrinal lessons might have required instruction. A knowledgeable guide could make a long narrative accessible to an audience with differing experience; a visitor might also recognize familiar stories without following a systematic curriculum. Neither possibility is recorded for Borobudur, so historians must distinguish comparative evidence from assertions about local custom. This matters for evaluating the monument as a public religious environment: participation may have ranged from acts of devotion and offering to learned reflection, without every participant approaching the reliefs in the same way.

Section 31.14

Monks, Teachers, Buddhist Learning, and Religious Knowledge in Central Java

The monument's relief program implies a considerable body of religious knowledge. Selecting and arranging scenes from the Karmavibhaṅga, Lalitavistara, Jātaka and Avadāna literature, and the Gaṇḍavyūha required planners familiar with these texts and their relationships, and with the traditions of Buddhist iconography. Such knowledge was likely held and transmitted by learned religious specialists, including monks and teachers, perhaps in association with manuscripts and with oral instruction. The presence of Sanskrit-derived terminology and textual traditions in Central Javanese inscriptions indicates that Sanskrit learning existed in courtly and religious circles.

Connections with the wider Mahāyāna world supplied both texts and teachers. Buddhist communities across South Asia, Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia, and China exchanged texts, images, and personnel along the maritime and overland routes discussed in Chapters 18 and 29. Pilgrims and scholars traveled to Buddhist centers, and travelers from other regions are known to have visited Southeast Asian ports. Central Java participated in this network as an active center of patronage and learning, not merely a recipient. The Borobudur program, with its distinctive selection and arrangement of narratives, suggests creative engagement with inherited traditions.

It is, however, easy to overstate what can be concluded. The monument does not reveal the exact curriculum of study, the size of any monastic community, its rules, its sources of income beyond general patronage, or its relationship to other foundations. No contemporary monastic record from Borobudur is known. Scholars sometimes assume that a major Buddhist monument implies an adjacent monastery, and this is plausible, but the archaeological remains of such a community have not been conclusively identified. The safest statement is that the monument demonstrates deep Buddhist learning and implies the existence of religious specialists, while leaving the organization of that learning undocumented.

The relationship between textual knowledge and carved images was probably mediated through several specialists. Someone had to select which narratives belonged on particular walls; someone had to determine how long stories were divided into scenes; designers had to coordinate the images with the shape of the galleries; and sculptors had to make the resulting instructions intelligible in stone. The short incised labels observed on portions of the concealed foot suggest one means of communicating subject matter within a workshop, although their exact function remains debated. Such coordination implies planning and interpretation across artistic and religious domains. It does not prove that the same people designed the architecture, read the texts, and carved the panels.

Nor should the word 'Buddhist' conceal differences among textual traditions. The biography associated with the Lalitavistara, the moral classifications illustrated on the concealed foot, and Sudhana's encounters in the Gaṇḍavyūha present different genres and modes of learning. Their coexistence at one monument suggests that Javanese patrons and teachers could bring diverse strands of Buddhist literature into a coherent visual environment. But we cannot identify the precise monastic lineage of every participant or impose later sectarian boundaries on ninth-century practice. Surviving Sanskrit and Chinese versions help researchers establish textual relationships; they do not establish which language a visitor heard during instruction at Borobudur.

Section 31.15

The People Who Built Borobudur: Labor, Artisans, Agricultural Resources, and Royal Authority

Behind the monument lay a large and varied workforce. Stone had to be quarried or collected, shaped, transported, and lifted into place. Planners had to design the structure and the relief programs. Sculptors had to carve thousands of panels with a consistent but not uniform style, as variations in quality and manner suggest the work of different hands. Others had to prepare tools, provide timber, and maintain the supply chain. Supporting all of them were agricultural producers whose surplus fed workers and craftspeople, and ritual authorities who consecrated the work.

Royal authority connected these elements. Inscriptions from Central Java indicate that rulers could grant land and revenue to religious foundations and could assign obligations to communities, which suggests mechanisms through which labor and resources might have been directed toward construction and maintenance. Chapter 30 and later chapters discuss such institutions. But the specific arrangements used at Borobudur are not documented. We do not know whether workers were paid, conscripted, working in fulfillment of obligations, or acting from religious motivation, and different groups may have worked under different arrangements. We also do not know the size of the workforce at any time; modern estimates depend on assumptions about duration and productivity, and they should be treated cautiously.

What the evidence does show is specialization. The consistency of stone fitting, the planning of drainage, the variation of relief quality, and the incised labels that appear to guide carvers suggest a division of labor between designers, supervisors, and craftsmen. The scale of the monument implies an organization able to sustain the project over a long period and to coordinate efforts across multiple trades. That is a form of historical evidence about state and religious capacity, even though the people involved are largely anonymous.

A monument built over many seasons would also have required decisions about the timing of work. Rice cultivation occupied agricultural households at certain points of the year, while stone transport, masonry, and finishing demanded specialist time and equipment. It is reasonable to ask whether different tasks were organized seasonally and whether highly skilled sculptors worked continuously while other laborers were mobilized intermittently. Yet these remain questions rather than conclusions: no surviving payroll, workforce register, or building contract identifies the people employed at Borobudur. The most responsible reconstruction begins with the surviving tasks and works outward to possible arrangements, instead of starting with a guessed headcount and multiplying it into a spurious total.

Religious endowment was another form of organization. Central Javanese inscriptions record the designation of land and revenues for religious purposes, sometimes through sīma arrangements that altered a locality's fiscal obligations. Such charters demonstrate institutions through which rulers and communities supported sacred establishments. They do not state that a particular grant funded each gallery or sculpture at Borobudur. The difference is essential: an institutional practice documented in the same political culture provides context, not a missing construction ledger. The people who maintained the site after completion may also have differed from those who built it. Cleaning drainage channels, repairing damage, receiving offerings, and supporting religious personnel would have required continuing resources, but the specific scale of these obligations is unknown.

Section 31.16

Everyday Life in Borobudur's Reliefs: Clothing, Houses, Boats, Markets, and Social Relations

Borobudur's reliefs show a great many details of material life: garments and ornaments, buildings, tools, plants, animals, musical instruments, vehicles, and watercraft. Houses appear raised on posts, with roofs of various forms; figures wear wrapped lower garments, jewelry, and elaborate headgear; people carry goods, tend animals, and work in fields and kitchens; and ships with outriggers appear on water. Social relations are visible in the arrangement of figures: rulers and courtiers shown with attendants and parasols, families grouped together, and servants and laborers placed in supporting roles.

These images are valuable, but their evidentiary status requires care. The reliefs illustrate Buddhist narratives set in India or in imagined worlds, and the artists depicted them in a visual language drawn from Javanese experience and artistic convention. A scene may therefore reveal that artists knew a certain type of boat or house or garment, and that such objects were intelligible to viewers. It does not follow that every depicted activity occurred in the way shown, or that a given scene records a particular historical event in Java. Some features may reflect Indian models or sculptural convention rather than local observation. Buddhist narrative scenes are not photographs of eighth-century life.

Used critically, the reliefs can be compared with other evidence: archaeological finds of tools and ceramics, inscriptions concerning land and taxation, and later or foreign descriptions. Where several kinds of evidence converge, historians can speak with more confidence. For example, the representation of ocean-going outrigger vessels is consistent with other evidence of Javanese and Southeast Asian seafaring discussed in Chapters 14 and 18. A replica ship modeled on a Borobudur relief was sailed from Indonesia to West Africa in 2003–2004, an experiment that tested what such a design could do, though it cannot prove that actual ninth-century vessels matched the sculpture in every detail.

The carvings can be especially productive when several details are studied together. A vessel is not identified only by the presence of a sail: the hull, steering arrangement, outriggers, crew positions, and cargo all deserve separate observation. Likewise, a building's roof, posts, doorway, and surrounding activity may communicate different information about materials or social setting. Comparing repeated forms across panels can distinguish an established artistic convention from an unusual depiction. Such a method is more reliable than using one attractive scene to assert how all Javanese households or ships looked. Even apparently ordinary details may be chosen to clarify a moral narrative rather than to document technical design.

The evidence also has social blind spots. Elite figures are often made visually prominent, and servants or workers may appear only in supporting positions. The presence of women, children, or craft specialists in a scene does not automatically reveal their legal rights, working conditions, or frequency in the population. Scholars can compare the reliefs with inscriptions about donations or land and with archaeological finds, but each source has its own selection bias. A useful classroom exercise is to distinguish three statements about one panel: what can be seen directly, what could plausibly be inferred from a comparison, and what would require evidence from elsewhere. This method connects artistic interpretation to social history without turning religious storytelling into a statistical survey.

The monument's builders, too, belong to this social history, though they left no portraits or statements. Their presence is visible only through their work, and recognizing that absence is part of reading the monument responsibly.

Division VPolitical Meaning, Historical Change, Rediscovery, and Legacy
Section 31.17

Borobudur as a Royal and Political Statement

A monument of Borobudur's scale could not have been built without a political authority capable of organizing resources, and its existence communicated that capacity. Building a major sacred monument demonstrated piety, wealth, artistic sophistication, and the ability to command labor and materials. In many premodern societies, including those of Southeast Asia examined in Chapters 35 through 38 on Angkor, religious building was an important means by which rulers and elites expressed legitimacy and acquired merit.

Scholars have emphasized different aspects of this relationship. Some stress Buddhist devotion and the accumulation of religious merit as the primary motivation, noting that the monument's program is centered on teaching and the path to awakening. Others stress royal legitimacy, arguing that patrons used the monument to connect rulers with the Buddhist cosmos or with an ideal of the world-ruling, bodhisattva-like king. Still others suggest ancestral or commemorative dimensions, though evidence for funerary or ancestral functions at Borobudur is limited and debated. A balanced reading recognizes that these motives were not mutually exclusive. For patrons in ninth-century Java, religious merit and political standing were intertwined.

It would be misleading to call Borobudur simply royal propaganda. The monument's complex textual program, its lengthy narratives, and its emphasis on moral teaching and spiritual progress suggest purposes beyond display. Equally, it would be wrong to see it as detached from politics: its construction depended on royal resources, and its prestige reflected on its patrons. The relationship between faith, kingship, and monumental construction is better understood as one of mutual reinforcement, in which sincere religious commitment and political advantage could coexist.

The monument's political meaning also varied by audience. For rival courts, it could be a sign of Sailendra-period strength; for local communities, it might be a source of employment, obligation, and religious opportunity; for Buddhist networks beyond Java, it could be evidence of a flourishing Mahāyāna patronage. We lack direct testimony of these responses, and historians should present them as plausible roles rather than documented reactions.

A royal project also involved a relationship with the communities around it. The devotion of distant visitors, the prestige of religious specialists, the obligations of agricultural villages, and the employment of craftspeople could have intersected at a monument whose benefits and costs were not distributed evenly. Some people may have experienced its construction chiefly as religious service; others may have encountered it through provisioning or work. These are analytical possibilities, not preserved testimonies. Recognizing that distinction allows the political discussion to include people outside the court while avoiding a fictional account of their reactions. It also clarifies why the monument's value was not exhausted when a particular dynasty's fortunes changed.

Section 31.18

Buddhist and Śaiva Traditions in Central Java: Continuity and Change

Central Java in the ninth century was a landscape of both Buddhist and Śaiva foundations. Inscriptions and monuments indicate that Śaiva and Buddhist communities coexisted, and rulers and elites supported both. Foundations could receive land grants, benefactors might patronize more than one tradition, and artistic and architectural practices were shared. Craft communities that carved Buddhist stone images and Śaiva temples worked in related technical traditions.

The ninth century nevertheless saw changes in patronage and political emphasis. Monuments associated with Śaiva dedication, including those of the Prambanan area, became prominent, and the political history of the Mataram-period courts shifted. Chapter 32 examines these developments, including Prambanan and the later movement of political authority toward East Java. This chapter notes only that the relationship between the religions cannot be told as a simple replacement of Buddhism by Hinduism. Buddhist foundations continued to exist and be maintained for some time, and the relationship between religious change and political change is complex, with dynastic politics, endowment patterns, and local communities all playing roles.

The later religious history of Java, including the spread of Islam examined in Chapters 62 through 65, further transformed the island's religious landscape, but Buddhist and Hindu traditions left lasting traces in language, literature, art, and place-names. Borobudur belongs to this longer history of religious layering.

The material landscape offers evidence for overlap rather than an abrupt sequence. Buddhist and Śaiva monuments were constructed and maintained within neighboring Central Javanese regions, using related skills in stone cutting, sculptural planning, and the administration of religious support. The presence of one kind of sanctuary does not, by itself, establish the disappearance of another. Nor can the later prominence of Prambanan be read backward as proof of a sudden ninth-century victory by one religious community over another. Patterns of endowment, the dates of inscriptions, and the chronology of surviving structures must be compared before claims about patronage can be made.

The same caution applies when the political center moved toward East Java. Changes in royal residence or court sponsorship could alter the resources available to particular foundations, but religious practices and local communities did not necessarily relocate on the same timetable. The disappearance of a building program from written sources may mean that records have not survived rather than that worship ceased immediately. This distinction between political chronology and religious continuity helps students approach Chapter 32 without treating Buddhism and Śaivism as opposing teams in a single dynastic contest.

What remained continuous? Royal patronage of religion, the use of land grants and endowments, shared craft traditions in stone, and the coexistence of multiple religious communities.

Section 31.19

Borobudur through Time: Changing Use, Rediscovery, Archaeological Research, and Conservation

What happened to Borobudur after its early history is only partly known. The monument seems to have lost the regular royal patronage that sustained it, as political centers shifted. Later Javanese texts have been interpreted by some scholars as referring to the site, and chronicle traditions mention a place with a similar name, but the identification of these references and what they imply about maintenance and use are debated. The causes of its eventual deterioration and partial burial under volcanic material and vegetation are likewise uncertain. Volcanic eruptions in the region have been proposed as a factor, but historians should not present Borobudur's changing fortunes as the demonstrated result of a single eruption or a single decision to abandon it. A gradual combination of reduced upkeep, natural processes, and changing religious and political circumstances is more consistent with the evidence, and local memory of the site persisted.

Modern documentation began in the early nineteenth century. In 1814 H. C. Cornelius undertook an early inspection and clearance on instructions from Thomas Stamford Raffles, during the British administration of Java (see Chapter 100). Local knowledge of the site predated that colonial investigation. Further documentation followed under Dutch administration. In the nineteenth century, pieces of the monument were removed or given to visitors, reflecting attitudes of the time toward antiquities. Scholars produced descriptive studies and drawings, and in 1885 an investigator discovered the hidden foot, whose reliefs were photographed in 1890–1891 by Kassian Céphas. The first major restoration, directed by Theodoor van Erp, took place in 1907–1911.

The twentieth-century campaign proved decisive. By the middle of the century, water infiltration and structural movement threatened the monument. UNESCO documents the international conservation campaign launched in 1972, and a large restoration project, carried out chiefly from the early 1970s to 1983 with Indonesian leadership and international support, dismantled much of the monument, installed new foundations and drainage, and reassembled the stones. In 1985 bomb attacks damaged several stupas, which were later repaired. The monument was inscribed on the World Heritage List in 1991. Conservation continues, addressing weathering, visitor impact, and the effects of volcanic ash from eruptions in the region.

For students, the point is that the visible monument is a layered historical object. Its stones are largely original but were re-set; its drainage is partly modern; its condition reflects centuries of change; and modern interpretations have shaped how it is presented. Historians who use Borobudur as evidence need to consult conservation records and distinguish original features from reconstruction.

The familiar description of Borobudur as 'discovered' in 1814 obscures the history of local knowledge. The monument was known to people living in the region, while the British administration's intervention under Thomas Stamford Raffles initiated a new phase of colonial survey and clearance. Raffles sent the engineer H. C. Cornelius to inspect the overgrown structure and direct early work; neither man was the first human being to know of the place. This distinction is not semantic. It shifts attention from a dramatic European rediscovery to the changing institutions through which a locally known monument entered colonial records, collecting practices, and eventually archaeological scholarship.

The rediscovery of the hidden foot likewise involved both evidence and intervention. J. W. IJzerman identified the concealed relief-bearing structure in 1885. Kassian Céphas then photographed the panels in 1890–1891 under difficult working conditions; the surviving photographic series includes 160 panel views and additional contextual photographs. Once the foot was covered again, these images became indispensable for routine study, even though the original carvings continued to exist beneath the encasement. Students should therefore distinguish the survival of the stone from the accessibility of the evidence, and ask how selection, framing, lighting, and later cataloguing affect interpretations of historical images.

Section 31.20

Borobudur as Architecture, Sacred Landscape, and Historical Archive: Chapter Synthesis

The chapter has examined Borobudur from five perspectives. As an engineered monument, it demonstrates sophisticated use of volcanic stone, a stepped design built around a hill, drainage systems, and construction phases that changed during the work. As a Buddhist religious environment, it combines architecture, sculpture, and text to guide movement and reflection, from the moral analysis of action through the Buddha's life and exemplary stories to the seeker Sudhana's pilgrimage and the open summit. As a product of royal patronage, it reveals the capacity of Central Javanese courts to organize resources and to express religious and political authority. As a source for Central Javanese society, its reliefs preserve knowledge of dress, houses, boats, and social relations, though filtered through religious narrative. And as an archaeological landscape, it has been shaped by centuries of change, rediscovery, and restoration.

Each kind of evidence has strengths and limits. Archaeology and architecture establish structure and sequence but not names or motives. Inscriptions supply names and dates but cannot be tied securely to the monument. Texts identify the narratives depicted but do not show which manuscripts were used or how the reliefs were taught. Art history provides relative chronologies and connections but depends on fixed points. Modern records document recent history but reflect modern interests. The most reliable conclusions come from the convergence of several kinds of evidence, and the most honest statements acknowledge where they do not converge.

Borobudur's value does not lie solely in its monumental scale. It preserves evidence of the interaction of religion, political authority, specialized labor, artistic knowledge, and economic resources in historical Southeast Asia. It shows Javanese rulers, artists, and religious specialists participating actively in Buddhist networks that stretched across Asia, transforming what they received into a distinctive monument. Chapter 32 turns to the Hindu religious patronage of Prambanan, the political institutions of Mataram, and the movement of power toward East Java, continuing the story of Central Java's religious and political transformation.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined Borobudur as an architectural achievement, religious monument, political statement, pilgrimage environment, and archaeological source for Central Javanese society. Its central question asked how the builders transformed a Central Javanese landscape into an expression of Buddhist teaching, royal patronage, artistic knowledge, and social organization. The answer developed through attention to the monument's setting, structure, imagery, and history, and always with attention to what the evidence can and cannot support.

The chapter began with sources. Borobudur has no surviving, unequivocal foundation inscription, so its date, founder, and original name are reconstructed by inference from architecture, palaeography, art history, and royal inscriptions of the period. The governing principle is that a monument can preserve exceptional evidence about a society without giving a complete account of its builders or use. Historians must also distinguish original ninth-century material from modern restoration.

The setting in the Kedu Plain, framed by volcanoes and watered by the Progo and Elo rivers, provided fertile land, water, and stone. These conditions made construction feasible but did not cause it; political authority, religious commitment, and craft knowledge turned possibility into achievement. Symbolic readings of the mountainous setting are plausible but must be distinguished from demonstrable construction decisions.

Sailendra-period Buddhist patronage provides the political context. Samaratungga and Pramodhawardhani are traditionally linked to the monument, and the 824 Karangtengah inscription names them in connection with a Buddhist foundation, but whether that foundation is Borobudur is debated. Construction is conventionally placed from roughly 780 to 830 or later, in several phases whose number and sequence are contested. Nearby Mendut and Pawon, aligned with Borobudur, suggest a wider Buddhist sacred landscape, although a documented ancient processional route has not been established.

Architecturally, Borobudur is a stepped monument built around a hill, with a base, five square terraces forming galleries, three circular platforms bearing 72 perforated stupas, and a closed central stupa. Engineering evidence includes andesite masonry, drainage, and the concealed original foot enclosed by an added base, for which structural and religious explanations compete. The traditional interpretation in terms of the three realms of desire, form, and formlessness is illuminating but remains an interpretation, not a documented blueprint. The monument's 504 Buddha statues, with their gestures and directional arrangements, and the summit's symbolism extend this architectural reading.

The reliefs form the monument's most important textual archive. The 160 hidden-foot panels illustrate the Karmavibhaṅga teaching on karma; 120 panels on the first gallery present the life of the Buddha in the Lalitavistara tradition; Jātaka and Avadāna stories offer examples of compassion and generosity; and the Gaṇḍavyūha narrates Sudhana's pilgrimage and the bodhisattva ideal. Together the narrative and decorative panels number 1,460 and 1,212 in standard inventories. The programs are distinct, they adapt textual sources in a Javanese visual idiom, and they do not form a single continuous story.

Turning to society, the monument's architecture makes circumambulation and ascent possible, and it implies religious specialists with deep textual knowledge, but specific rituals, monastic organization, and the circumstances of labor are not documented. The scale of construction implies specialization and organized resources, which the cause-and-effect analysis tied to agricultural surplus, royal authority, craft knowledge, and religious motivation. The reliefs preserve knowledge of dress, houses, boats, and social relations, but as artistic representations of Buddhist narratives, not photographs of daily life.

Politically, Borobudur expressed piety, legitimacy, and capacity, and a balanced reading rejects both the view that it was mere propaganda and the view that it was independent of politics. Buddhist and Śaiva traditions coexisted in Central Java, and the ninth century saw shifts in patronage and political emphasis that Chapter 32 addresses, without a simple replacement of one religion by another.

Finally, the monument's later history involves reduced patronage, gradual deterioration, and local memory, followed by nineteenth-century documentation, the 1885 discovery of the hidden foot and its photography, the restorations of 1907–1911 and the 1970s–1983, the 1985 bomb damage, and World Heritage inscription in 1991. The visible monument is therefore a layered object whose evidentiary value depends on knowing what is original and what is modern, and Borobudur remains valuable because it preserves evidence for the interaction of religion, authority, labor, art, and economy in historical Southeast Asia.

Chapter Reflection

Borobudur matters because it joins evidence that historians usually find separately: engineering, religious thought, art, political ambition, and the organization of labor. A single monument allows students to see how a society could draw on agricultural wealth, specialized skill, and wide Buddhist networks to create a work that was at once devotional and political. At the same time, it teaches how much remains unknown, and that an honest history must say so.

The chapter also reveals larger processes. The monument shows Southeast Asian societies as active participants in Asia-wide religious exchange, selecting and transforming texts and images, a theme introduced in Chapter 1 and developed in Chapter 19. It shows how sacred landscapes are created by combining architecture, narrative, and movement. And it shows that monuments themselves have histories: of use, neglect, rediscovery, and restoration.

Later chapters build on these themes. Chapter 32 turns to the Hindu patronage of Prambanan and the movement of political power eastward, while the later chapters on Angkor, Pagan, and Majapahit return to monumental religious building as a recurring expression of authority and devotion. Borobudur's own continuity beyond its first centuries, as a conserved monument, a site of contemporary religious observance, and a heritage landmark, reminds us that the past is not only studied but inherited.

For students, the lasting lesson is methodological. Reading a monument requires distinguishing what can be observed from what is inferred, what is original from what is restored, and what a source shows from what it merely suggests. Those habits will serve throughout the rest of this textbook.
