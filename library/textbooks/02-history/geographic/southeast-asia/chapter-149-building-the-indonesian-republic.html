Chapter Opening

Sovereignty was recognized in December 1949, but a state is not built by recognition alone: the first decade of Indonesian independence was a struggle to turn a revolutionary movement into a legitimate, representative, economically viable, and territorially integrated republic.

At the end of December 1949 Indonesia received recognition of its sovereignty from the Netherlands. The ceremonies ended four years of armed and diplomatic struggle, but the leaders who had won independence now had to govern. Destroying colonial political authority and constructing a viable national government are different tasks, and the second proved at least as difficult as the first.

The new state had to answer basic questions. Would Indonesia be federal or unitary? Who would govern, through what institutions, and with what constitutional limits? How would a state that spanned thousands of islands, hundreds of languages, and several major religions create a shared citizenship? How could an economy built for colonial export be made to serve national purposes? And what role would the armed forces, born in the revolution, play in civilian politics?

This chapter follows these questions through the first decade of independence, roughly 1949 to 1959. It begins with the sovereignty settlement and the dissolution of the federal system, and examines the provisional constitution and the administrative institutions of the unitary state. It then analyzes political parties, coalition cabinets, the 1955 elections, and the debate over a permanent constitution; the problems of regional diversity, citizenship, maritime territory, and national language; the economy and everyday life; foreign policy; and finally the armed forces, Darul Islam, and the regional rebellions of the late 1950s.

Historians draw on constitutional texts and statutes, parliamentary records, election materials, newspapers, government and economic records, military and diplomatic documents, regional studies, memoirs, and later scholarship. Each has limits. Legal texts show intentions rather than practice, official records emphasize the viewpoint of the state, and the experiences of villagers, women, and minority communities often have to be inferred from indirect evidence.

The chapter treats the period as neither a story of inevitable failure nor one of uninterrupted democratic success. Indonesian leaders and citizens made real choices under heavy constraints, and different outcomes were possible. The decade produced durable institutions, a national language system, a competitive electoral precedent, and a new international role, while leaving open problems that shaped the next phase of Indonesian history.

Taken together, the first decade of independence was a period of significant institutional experimentation. Its achievements and unresolved tensions set the stage for the transition examined in Chapter 180, but they are best understood first on their own terms.

Historical Context

Dutch colonial government had ruled the archipelago through a centralized administration at Batavia, with Dutch officials at senior levels and indigenous officials in the lower ranks, a dual legal order, and an economy organized around plantation and mineral exports. Indonesian nationalism emerged in the early twentieth century, and nationalist organizations competed under colonial repression. The language of unity, the national idea, and leaders such as Sukarno and Hatta were products of this period.

The Japanese occupation (1942-1945) destroyed Dutch rule, mobilized Indonesians for labor and military service, and trained auxiliary armed forces. It also disrupted the economy, created famine in some areas, and expanded Indonesian roles in administration. The proclamation of independence in August 1945 followed the Japanese surrender, and is examined in Chapter 147.

The National Revolution of 1945-1949, examined in Chapter 148, combined diplomacy and armed struggle against the returning Dutch. It created the Republic's first institutions and the armed forces, and it produced a heterogeneous political landscape, including Republican officials, local revolutionary leaders, and Dutch-sponsored states. The revolution ended with the Round Table settlement and the transfer of sovereignty in December 1949.

The settlement created a federal Republic of the United States of Indonesia, left the status of Western New Guinea to later negotiation, and bound Indonesia to financial and economic arrangements that protected Dutch interests. It was a compromise reflecting international pressure and the balance of forces, and it satisfied few Indonesian nationalists.

The armed forces inherited from the revolution were an amalgam of Republican army units, local militias, and former colonial soldiers. The economy remained oriented toward exports controlled largely by foreign firms. These inherited conditions provide the starting point for the chapter. Developments assigned to Chapters 147 and 148 are summarized only as far as necessary.

The international setting also changed after 1949. The end of European empires in Asia and the sharpening Cold War created opportunities for diplomatic cooperation, but exposed the new state to competing pressures. Indonesia’s leaders sought room for independent action while negotiating trade, aid, territorial questions and foreign recognition. This international setting connects the domestic institutional history of the chapter with Indonesia’s emerging role in Asian and African affairs.

Section 149.1

Indonesia at the Beginning of Independence

The transfer of sovereignty on 27 December 1949 was marked by ceremonies in Amsterdam and Jakarta, but it transferred internationally recognized sovereign authority, without automatically resolving the existing state’s institutional weaknesses. The federal Republic that emerged from the Round Table settlement received Dutch recognition; recognition by other countries had begun during the revolution and widened after the settlement. It had not yet created a unified army, a trained national civil service, a stable currency, a secure revenue base, or a settled constitutional order. This section asks what it meant to hold sovereignty over an archipelago whose government had, until recently, been run by someone else.

The Republic inherited the physical and human costs of a decade of upheaval. Japanese requisitioning, wartime forced labor, and the revolutionary struggle had damaged plantations, railways, ports, and irrigation works in many regions, and had displaced large numbers of people. Many Dutch and Dutch-trained officials, who had staffed the senior ranks of the colonial bureaucracy, left or were about to leave, while experienced Indonesian administrators were few relative to the tasks ahead. Dutch firms, banks, shipping lines, and plantation companies continued to hold a dominant place in the modern economy, and the financial terms of the settlement bound the new state to colonial-era obligations.

The settlement was also constitutionally provisional. The federal structure created at the Round Table Conference joined the revolutionary Republic with a number of states and territories whose formation had been encouraged by the Dutch during the conflict. The armed forces combined units that had fought for the Republic, former Royal Netherlands East Indies Army personnel, and local militias with different loyalties. Governing these diverse institutions, across thousands of islands with uneven communications, was the immediate task of the first Indonesian governments.

Popular expectations were high and not uniform. For many villagers, merdeka meant an end to forced requisitions and foreign overlordship; for former fighters it implied recognition and employment; for urban nationalists it meant Indonesian control of ministries, firms, and schools; for religious leaders it raised the question of the place of Islam in public life; for regional elites it meant a fair share of the revenue their territories produced. These expectations could not all be met at once. The gap between formal sovereignty and practical state capacity defined the political history of the following decade.

The transition was not a transfer from a single colonial bureaucracy to an empty successor office. For much of the revolution, two governments had administered different parts of the country, collected different taxes, and recognized different instruments of payment. The Republic in Yogyakarta had acquired experience in governing under military pressure, while administrations established under Dutch protection possessed records, personnel, buildings, and access to commercial networks. The new federal authorities had to combine these resources. An official appointed in one system could find that his qualifications, seniority, or pension were not recognized in the other. The question was not simply whether an official was Indonesian, but which institution had authorized him to act and whether residents would accept his authority.

The federal cabinet was organized before the actual sovereignty ceremonies. Hatta brought experience from the Republican government into the new national administration, while Sukarno provided continuity in the presidency. At the same time, the revolutionary Republic continued to exist as a constituent unit. Thus independence involved overlapping levels of authority from the outset. One government could negotiate internationally while another continued to administer its own territory. This arrangement was legally intelligible but politically difficult for nationalists who had understood the proclamation of 1945 as establishing a single Republic.

The distinction between recognition and effective power can be illustrated by public finance. A ministry could approve a budget, but unless customs officers could inspect cargo, tax officials could identify taxpayers, and banks could transmit payments, the budget remained a statement of intention. A government might promise to open a school in a distant district, yet have neither a qualified teacher nor a reliable route for delivering building materials. Such limitations affected the everyday credibility of independence. Officials had to decide which services could be maintained, which could be expanded, and which claims would have to wait. The competing priorities could not all be satisfied from the same limited pool of revenue.

The new leadership also had an asset that cannot be measured through government accounts: the political legitimacy of a successful anticolonial revolution. Citizens organized collections, associations, and political meetings that made government possible even where formal administration was weak. Local initiative was therefore an essential resource, not simply a substitute for an absent state. It could also produce conflict when national leaders sought to standardize practices or restrict revolutionary organizations. The first decade would repeatedly reveal this tension between mobilization from below and consolidation from above. A viable national government had to turn wartime loyalties into peacetime institutions without extinguishing the participation that had made the Republic possible.

The new state was not built on a blank slate. It used the ministries, courts, tax offices, and police forces of the colonial government, but sought to use them for a national rather than an imperial purpose. That adaptation, uneven and contested, is a theme of the entire chapter.

Historical Vocabulary
Sovereignty and State Capacity

Definition. Sovereignty is the recognized legal authority of a state to govern its territory and conduct foreign relations. State capacity is the practical ability of a government to collect revenue, enforce law, deliver services, and command the loyalty of its officials and armed forces.

Historical context. In December 1949 Indonesia secured Dutch recognition of the first, while the second had to be built over years through training, administration, and political bargaining.

Why it matters. The distinction explains why a state can be internationally recognized and still be unable to administer remote regions or control its own security forces.

Potential misunderstanding. Recognition by other states did not by itself produce obedience within the territory. Equally, limited capacity did not mean that the Republic lacked legitimacy; many Indonesians strongly supported the state even where it was administratively thin.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Colonial Administration to National Government

What Changed

  • The purpose and personnel of the state shifted from imperial rule to national government, with Indonesians replacing Dutch officials at senior levels over time
  • Ministries, courts, and tax offices answered to an Indonesian cabinet and parliament rather than to Batavia and The Hague
  • Public expectations changed: the state was now expected to deliver education, employment, and welfare

What Remained Continuous

  • Many inherited legal codes, administrative divisions, and office routines continued in use
  • Dutch firms retained a large role in banking, trade, and plantation production
  • Java remained the demographic and administrative center, and the outer islands remained the principal export-earning regions

When and how: Change was gradual and uneven across the 1950s rather than a single transition in December 1949.

Who experienced change most directly: Senior administrators, military officers, and educated urban Indonesians experienced the change most directly. Villagers often experienced greater continuity in the form of the same tax collectors and local office-holders, now serving a different government.

Why the pattern matters: The pattern shows that decolonization changed who held authority before it changed the institutions through which authority was exercised.

Section 149.2

The United States of Indonesia and the End of Federalism

The Round Table Conference produced the Republic of the United States of Indonesia (Republik Indonesia Serikat, RIS). Sukarno became its president and Mohammad Hatta its prime minister. The RIS comprised the Republic of Indonesia, centered on Yogyakarta, together with a set of constituent states and autonomous territories, including East Indonesia, Pasundan, and East Sumatra. The Dutch had encouraged many of these units during the revolution, as part of a strategy to create a federal Indonesia in which the Republic would be one component among several.

Federalism cannot be reduced to that strategy. Some regional leaders had sincere reasons to favor a federal structure: they feared Javanese dominance, wished to protect local administrative traditions, or distrusted a centralized state controlled by Republican revolutionaries. Others had been closely associated with the Dutch administration, and some depended on it. The federal units were not identical in institutions, social base, or political aims, and their leaders held a range of views about the Republic.

The federal system faced severe pressure from its first months. Republican nationalists regarded federalism as a colonial construction that divided the nation and diluted the revolution's achievement. Popular demonstrations in several federal units called for union with the Republic. Armed episodes in 1950 deepened the sense of crisis, among them an attempted coup in Bandung led by the former Dutch officer Raymond Westerling, a revolt by Andi Aziz in Makassar, and the proclamation of a South Moluccan republic in Ambon. These events convinced many leaders that the federal framework was unstable and associated with those seeking to reverse the revolution, although the Moluccan movement also reflected local anxieties about Ambonese soldiers' futures that were not simply Dutch-inspired.

The federal states dissolved or merged into the Republic in stages during the first half of 1950. In April 1950 Mohammad Natsir presented a parliamentary motion urging a return to a unitary state, and a May 1950 agreement between the RIS government and the Republic of Indonesia set out the path to unification. On 17 August 1950, five years after the proclamation of independence, Indonesia was formally reconstituted as a unitary republic with a provisional constitution.

The federal constitution distinguished between full constituent states and other autonomous entities. The federal legislature included a House of Representatives and a Senate in which constituent units were represented. That institutional design was intended to provide a national arena and a voice for the component territories. In the revolutionary Republic itself, the national claim of 1945 was still powerful. For those who regarded the unitary Republic as the legitimate expression of the nation, having the Republic sit beside other territories in a federal arrangement represented an unwelcome concession. For some leaders in eastern Indonesia, however, federal representation promised influence they feared losing in a legislature based more closely on population.

The political collapse of federalism was not uniform. In West Java, pressure for the dissolution of Pasundan was linked to popular hostility to the colonial political arrangements that had created it. In East Sumatra, the interests of estate owners, aristocratic groups, and Republican activists were shaped by the social conflicts of the preceding revolution. In East Indonesia, the federal government encompassed communities that differed considerably in religion, economic structure, and relationship to the former colonial army. The experience of the South Moluccan movement illustrates that the transition did not secure the consent of everyone. The Republic of South Maluku (RMS), proclaimed in April 1950, rejected incorporation; central government forces reestablished control over Ambon later that year, while resistance continued. The political consequences outlasted the formal abolition of the federation.

Natsir's Integral Motion of 3 April 1950 gave the transition a constructive parliamentary form. Rather than require each surviving entity to declare a unilateral surrender, the proposal sought an agreed national solution. The joint charter of 19 May 1950 between the federal and Republican governments made unification a constitutional undertaking. Negotiators had to work out the status of existing legislatures and the basis on which the unitary constitution would operate. The legal instrument implementing the transition, Federal Law No. 7 of 1950, amended the provisional federal constitutional framework into the constitution of the unitary Republic. This institutional sequence matters: unification was not accomplished simply by a presidential announcement.

The success of the unitary movement nevertheless left unresolved how provincial governments would participate in national decisions. Federalism concerns the constitutional division of authority, whereas decentralization within a unitary state concerns powers conferred on regional institutions under the national framework. Ending the federation did not logically require the abolition of meaningful regional autonomy. Subsequent governments could still have distributed resources and authority among provinces in different ways. The regional crises of the late decade should therefore be understood as new disputes over political and fiscal arrangements rather than as an automatic revival of the federal entities of 1949.

The outcome was achieved through negotiation and integration rather than a simple change of name. Constituent units were merged, their legislators and officials absorbed into national structures, and their territories reorganized into provinces. Whether a durable federal arrangement might have accommodated regional demands better than the unitary state later did is a question discussed in the debate below, and it recurs in the regional conflicts of the late 1950s.

Historical Debate

Was Indonesian Federalism a Colonial Instrument or a Viable Constitutional Alternative?

The question: Did the federal arrangements of 1949 chiefly serve Dutch efforts to retain influence, or did they represent a legitimate and workable way to organize a diverse archipelago?

Interpretation A — federalism as a colonial instrument: Republican nationalists at the time, and many later historians, emphasized that the Dutch sponsored the creation of states during the revolution to isolate and weaken the Republic, and that several federal leaders were dependent on Dutch support. In this view the federal structure was designed to preserve Dutch economic and political leverage.
Interpretation B — federalism as a genuine constitutional option: Other historians stress that federalism also drew on real regional concerns about centralization and Javanese predominance, and that a number of regional leaders favored autonomy for reasons of their own. On this reading, the speed of the federal collapse reflected nationalist momentum and security crises as much as the intrinsic unworkability of federal government.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on Dutch policy documents, records of federal-state legislatures, accounts of the 1950 unrest, and the memoirs of participants.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns motive and causation: how much weight to give Dutch strategy relative to regional interests, and whether the federation's failure was structural or contingent on the circumstances of 1949-1950.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand later regional grievances. If federalism was only a colonial tool, those grievances appear as separate problems; if it addressed real concerns, the unitary settlement left an unresolved constitutional question.

Section 149.3

The Provisional Constitution of 1950

The unitary state was governed under a provisional constitution that took effect in August 1950. It replaced the federal constitution of the RIS, and drew on elements of the 1945 revolutionary constitution and of the federal charter. As its name indicated, it was intended to serve until a permanent constitution could be drafted by an elected Constituent Assembly. That expectation shaped the political life of the decade.

The constitution established a parliamentary system. Executive authority was exercised by a cabinet headed by a prime minister, and the cabinet was responsible to a legislature, the House of Representatives (Dewan Perwakilan Rakyat, DPR). A cabinet that lost the confidence of parliament was expected to resign or be reconstituted. The president, with a vice president, held a constitutional position that was largely representative: he appointed the formateur of cabinets and symbolized the state, but did not govern through ministers who answered to him. The provisional constitution also contained guarantees of rights and freedoms and provided for political participation.

Sukarno's constitutional position was therefore narrower than his political stature. He was the Proclamation's best-known voice and a national leader of immense symbolic authority, but under the parliamentary framework he had limited formal power over cabinet policy. The tension between his revolutionary standing and his constitutional role was a continuing source of political friction, and became important in the later 1950s.

The initial parliament was not elected by a national vote. It combined members of earlier representative bodies of the Republic and the federal system, with additions to broaden representation. Its legitimacy therefore rested on the revolution and on the expectation of elections, which would not take place until 1955.

The constitution was enacted through Federal Law No. 7 of 1950 and came into effect on 17 August. Its provisions made ministerial responsibility a central principle. Article 83 distinguished the personal inviolability of the president and vice president from the responsibility of ministers for government policy, collectively and individually. In institutional terms, this meant that ministers could not ordinarily escape political accountability by invoking the wishes of the president. The presidency was important in appointing governments and in representing the state, but it was not the same kind of executive presidency established under the restored 1945 constitutional framework in 1959.

The relationship between parliament and government can be understood through the process of cabinet formation. When a ministry could no longer sustain political support, consultations determined which leader might assemble a successor. A formateur negotiated with parties over a program and ministerial portfolios. The resulting cabinet needed support to enact its budget, defend legislation, and survive criticism. A party could support a government from outside without holding a ministerial position, while a coalition partner could threaten to withdraw if its interests were disregarded. Such arrangements made policy the outcome of continuous negotiation rather than the unilateral decision of a single electoral winner.

The text also devoted extensive attention to rights. Its protections included legal equality and freedoms relating to religion, expression, association, and assembly. These provisions belonged to a postwar constitutional culture in which the rights of citizens had become central to international and national political discussion. Their practical force depended on courts, accessible legal remedies, administrative behavior, and the conditions under which political activity took place. A constitutional right to assemble was not the same as the ability to assemble safely in a district affected by rebellion or emergency rule. This difference between formal guarantee and actual protection was particularly important by the later 1950s.

The permanent constitution was the responsibility of a body distinct from the ordinary legislature. Article 134 gave the Constituent Assembly and government the task of establishing the constitution that would replace the provisional one. The Assembly was not an upper chamber of the DPR, nor was it a committee subordinate to the cabinet. Its elected members had a separate mandate. This division permitted legislation and administration to continue while constitutional deliberation took place. It also created a problem when a broad political settlement could not be reached: the state could continue under the provisional framework, but uncertainty remained over its ultimate constitutional foundation.

A provisional constitution had advantages. It allowed the state to be organized quickly and left the most divisive questions, including the relationship between religion and the state, to a later body. It also had costs: because the foundations of the state were formally unsettled, political actors could contest the framework itself, and the long delay in elections left governments dependent on coalitions of parties that no electorate had tested.

Parliamentary Government

Definition. A system in which the executive (the cabinet) is drawn from and politically responsible to the legislature, and can be removed if it loses the legislature's confidence, while the head of state holds a mainly symbolic role.

Section 149.4

Building the Institutions of the Independent State

Building a national administration involved adapting existing structures as much as creating new ones. The Republic organized ministries on the pattern of departments inherited from the colonial government, and recruited staff from several sources: veterans of the Republican administration of the revolutionary years, officials who had served under the Dutch or the Japanese, and graduates of new schools and universities. The result was a civil service that combined different training and loyalties, and in which party affiliation increasingly mattered for appointments.

Administrative Indonesianization proceeded as Dutch personnel departed and Indonesians took senior posts. The speed of replacement outran the supply of experience in some technical fields, including engineering, finance, and law. The inherited administrative corps in the regions, which in Java and the outer islands had long mediated between the central government and rural communities, continued under national authority, though the principles that governed its appointments and promotion were contested.

Local government remained an unsettled question. The state was divided into provinces and lower administrative units, and legislation addressed the powers of regional councils, but the balance between central appointment and local election was disputed. Regional leaders pressed for fuller autonomy, while central ministries often preferred supervision. These disagreements fed the regional grievances examined in Division V.

The judiciary relied on colonial-era codes that remained in force under transitional provisions, administered by judges trained in the earlier system. This preserved continuity but also left a legal order in which colonial categories and customary law coexisted uneasily. Tax collection, customs duties, and export taxes provided the main revenue but were administered with limited capacity, and police and local security institutions often had to improvise.

A civil service is not merely a collection of officials. It depends on procedures for hiring, training, promotion, recordkeeping, and accountability that allow work to continue when an individual minister leaves office. The frequent turnover of ministries made these procedures especially valuable in the 1950s. Permanent officials were expected to keep departments operating across changes of cabinet, yet appointment decisions were also a means by which parties could extend their influence. The boundary between legitimate political direction and partisan distribution of public employment was difficult to establish when institutions themselves were being reorganized.

The Republic inherited differences among regions in the character of district and village administration. In densely settled Java, government could often work through established hierarchies of local officers. In parts of the outer islands, officials had to negotiate with traditional leaders, religious authorities, and communities with different customary institutions. National law could provide a common framework, but its application required knowledge of local conditions. Administrative uniformity was therefore neither a realistic immediate objective nor necessarily a desirable one. What mattered was whether citizens in different settings could receive services, obtain legal protection, and communicate their demands to higher levels of government.

Local government reform was one attempt to address these issues. Law No. 1 of 1957 on the principles of regional government provided a framework for autonomous regional institutions. Its promulgation came at a particularly difficult moment, as disputes over regional authority and revenue were becoming increasingly acute. Written decentralization provisions could not alone solve questions over control of officials, financial allocations, or military jurisdiction. The emergency measures introduced that same year further complicated efforts to make civilian regional government effective. The relationship between legal reform and actual political authority therefore needs to be studied together with the regional crises in Division V.

Law presented a related challenge. Transitional provisions preserved many existing rules rather than abruptly abolishing the entire colonial legal order. Courts had to reconcile inherited legislation with the constitution and with new laws enacted by the Republic. Customary law retained importance in questions of land, family, and community relations. The objective of equal citizenship did not mean that every legal distinction disappeared immediately. Legal professionals, universities, and ministries played a part in developing the new system, while citizens experienced the process through concrete questions such as property claims, employment disputes, and registration of civil status. The ability to settle such disputes predictably was itself a form of state capacity.

Institutional development therefore proceeded on multiple timescales. A ministry could be reorganized by decree; training a generation of engineers, judges, teachers, and accountants required years. The state's achievements should be assessed against those different timescales rather than simply against an ideal of instant administrative transformation.

Transportation and communication posed practical constraints. Railways and roads in Java were comparatively well developed, but inter-island shipping, postal services, and telegraph links were weaker and, in the early 1950s, still depended heavily on Dutch-owned companies. National policy was often announced faster than it could be implemented. The institutional history of the early Republic is therefore a story of adaptation under shortage.

Section 149.5

Political Parties and the Indonesian Political Landscape

Political life in the 1950s was organized around a large number of parties, newspapers, and associations. Four parties emerged as the most influential. The Indonesian National Party (PNI) drew support from many civil servants, officials, and nationalists, particularly in Java, and presented itself as the heir of the pre-war nationalist movement. Masyumi, a broad Muslim party with strong modernist leadership associated with leaders such as Mohammad Natsir, had considerable backing among reformist Muslims, traders, and many communities in the outer islands. Nahdlatul Ulama (NU), a traditionalist Muslim organization built around religious scholars and rural schools, left Masyumi in 1952 to compete as a separate party, with strength especially in rural Java. The Communist Party of Indonesia (PKI), rebuilt after the Madiun crisis under younger leaders including D. N. Aidit, grew rapidly through its organization of peasants, workers, and women.

Other parties and groups mattered out of proportion to their size. The Indonesian Socialist Party (PSI), led by Sutan Sjahrir, had influence among intellectuals and officials but limited electoral support. Christian parties, Catholic and Protestant, represented minority communities. Smaller nationalist and regional parties competed in particular localities. Beyond parties, the political landscape included trade union federations, some affiliated with parties; religious associations such as Muhammadiyah; women's organizations; youth groups; and newspapers, many of which were openly partisan.

Scholars have sometimes described Indonesian society using categories of political and cultural orientation, linking parties to broader social milieux: nationalist and Javanist, modernist Muslim, traditionalist Muslim, and communist or socialist. These categories help explain patterns of support, but they must be used cautiously. People did not belong to permanently fixed blocs; individuals changed allegiance, families included members in different parties, and regional, class, and personal factors cut across ideology.

Party leadership, organizational membership, and electoral support were also distinct. A leader might hold views that differed from many of the voters who supported the party, and parties used patronage and networks of local notables to attract votes as well as programmatic appeals. Parties also maintained links to the armed forces, to government ministries, and to business networks, which affected their access to resources.

Political parties were more than electoral labels. They connected national politicians with organizations through which people already conducted their social lives. Religious education, professional associations, trade unions, neighborhood groups, and newspapers provided channels for recruitment and political communication. A national party could appear powerful in the capital but possess only a thin organizational network in rural districts. Conversely, a party whose leaders had little access to high office could develop an extensive membership among peasants or workers. The organizational structure helps explain the difference between parliamentary influence before 1955 and electoral performance afterward.

Masyumi was initially an umbrella of Muslim political organizations rather than a simple association of one school of religious thought. Its internal differences became more visible when NU withdrew in 1952. NU's network of religious scholars, pesantren, and followers gave it an organizational base independent of the modernist associations prominent in Masyumi. This split had consequences for coalition bargaining: parties that shared support for an Islamic constitutional foundation could still compete over ministries, religious administration, and political leadership. It is important to distinguish the religious identities of voters from the positions adopted by particular party organizations.

The revival of the PKI is another example of the importance of organization. Under Aidit and other leaders, the party sought to rebuild itself as a legal participant in national politics after the defeat of 1948. It expanded links with the Indonesian Peasants' Front (BTI), labor organizations such as SOBSI, and women's organizations. Its political language joined demands for land, wages, and anti-imperialism to a claim to represent ordinary citizens. Its growth cannot be explained simply as the importation of a foreign ideology; its leaders adapted their program to Indonesian conditions and participated in elections. Its opponents, particularly some Muslim and military leaders, regarded this growth as a major political danger.

The PNI similarly drew on more than Sukarno's personal popularity. Nationalist organizations cultivated connections with local government personnel, teachers, and rural leaders. The PSI, by contrast, illustrates how intellectual influence and administrative expertise did not necessarily translate into a mass following. Smaller parties such as the Indonesian Christian Party (Parkindo), the Catholic Party, and the Islamic Union Party represented constituencies that could be important in particular regions or legislative coalitions. Their significance cannot be inferred only from their nationwide vote share.

These networks gave democracy a social infrastructure. They also meant that competition for policy could become competition over employment, land, education, or control of local institutions. Understanding both dimensions makes it possible to explain the strength of party democracy and the tensions associated with it without treating political mobilization itself as a pathology.

The multiplicity of parties reflected genuine diversity in religion, region, and social experience. It also made it hard for any one party to govern alone, a condition that shaped the cabinet politics described in the next section.

Section 149.6

Coalition Cabinets and the Difficulties of Parliamentary Government

Between 1950 and 1957 Indonesia had a series of cabinets, each a coalition requiring the support of multiple parties. The first, led by Mohammad Natsir of Masyumi (1950-1951), pursued economic stabilization and administrative consolidation without the participation of the PNI, and fell over regional government regulations and a dispute with parliament. The cabinet of Sukiman Wirjosandjojo (1951-1952), a Masyumi-PNI coalition, took a strong line against communist activity and fell over an agreement concerning American aid that critics said compromised Indonesia's independent foreign policy.

Wilopo's cabinet (1952-1953), led by the PNI, faced the civil-military confrontation of October 1952 and economic difficulties as export prices fell after the Korean War boom. It resigned after a dispute over a land-use incident at Tanjung Morawa in Sumatra. Ali Sastroamidjojo's first cabinet (1953-1955), a PNI-NU coalition that excluded Masyumi, prepared the elections, hosted the Bandung Conference, and fell after a dispute within the army leadership.

Burhanuddin Harahap, of Masyumi, led a cabinet (1955-1956) that presided over the elections and reinstated military leaders, and which pursued corruption cases that unsettled some politicians. Ali Sastroamidjojo's second cabinet (1956-1957), formed after the elections as a PNI-Masyumi-NU coalition, was weakened by regional unrest and the resignation of Vice President Hatta in December 1956. In April 1957 Djuanda Kartawidjaja, a non-party technocrat, formed an extra-parliamentary 'working' cabinet under conditions of martial law, marking the end of the parliamentary period's coalition model.

Several features explain cabinet fragility. No party commanded a majority, so every government depended on bargaining, and parties could leave coalitions over narrow disputes. Cabinets lacked the authority of an elected mandate before 1955. Disagreements over policy, including economic strategy and relations with the United States, divided partners. The army and regional leaders often acted independently of cabinet wishes, limiting what any government could promise.

The official cabinet chronology records the terms of seven ministries from Natsir's installation in September 1950 to the replacement of Djuanda in July 1959. These dates refer to periods of formal service rather than necessarily the day a cabinet lost its political support. A resigned cabinet might remain responsible for routine government until a successor could be installed. This distinction prevents a common chronological mistake: measuring cabinet duration from a political crisis without accounting for the caretaker interval.

The Natsir government confronted the difficult task of completing the administrative integration of the federation. Its dependence on a narrow coalition made the question of regional representative councils particularly divisive. Sukiman's ministry encountered a different problem. The United States Mutual Security arrangements became politically controversial when critics understood the exchange of notes with Washington as an implication of alignment. The government's resignation demonstrates that foreign policy was not an exclusive matter for diplomats or the president: it was subject to parliamentary criticism and could determine the survival of a cabinet.

During the Wilopo ministry, social and military disputes intersected with the government's economic difficulties. The conflict at Tanjung Morawa involved the restoration of access to estate land and the claims of cultivators who had occupied it during the years of disruption. A confrontation with police made the land dispute a national parliamentary issue. The episode is instructive because a cabinet could face pressure not simply over abstract political doctrine but over the distribution of land and the behavior of state authorities. The first Ali cabinet, meanwhile, demonstrated that a government vulnerable to coalition conflict could still carry out major initiatives, including preparations for elections and the Bandung Conference.

Burhanuddin Harahap's ministry conducted the long-awaited elections and oversaw a reorganization of military leadership, including Nasution's return. The second Ali cabinet was founded on a stronger representative basis, but the electoral outcome did not provide a single governing majority. Economic and regional disputes, disagreement over the role of the president, and the departure of Masyumi ministers weakened the coalition. Its fall showed the limits of electoral legitimacy in the absence of agreement over the distribution of authority. Djuanda's cabinet represented an institutional experiment rather than just another party coalition: it was designed to draw on technical expertise and to address emergencies under conditions in which ordinary parliamentary bargaining had become more difficult.

The sequence reveals both policy continuity and instability. Governments changed, but the civil service, legislative institutions, and many long-term programs continued. The appropriate measure of parliamentary performance is therefore not merely how many cabinets fell. It is also whether governments could formulate policies, sustain public services, accept accountability, and resolve disputes without displacing constitutional institutions.

It would be mistaken to treat cabinet turnover as proof that parliamentary institutions could not function. The cabinets enacted significant policies, held free elections, maintained a lively press, and preserved civilian primacy for most of the period, and the average life of a cabinet reflected specific pressures rather than an inevitable defect. At the same time, the repeated need to rebuild coalitions drew attention from long-term policy, and contributed to public frustration with party politics, a frustration that critics of parliamentary government exploited in the late 1950s.

Section 149.7

The General Elections of 1955

Indonesia's first nationwide general elections were held in 1955, after repeated postponements. The election law adopted in 1953 provided for proportional representation and for two separate contests: one in September 1955 for the DPR, the national legislature, and one in December 1955 for the Constituent Assembly, the Konstituante, which was charged with drafting a permanent constitution. The two bodies had different responsibilities, and voters chose members of each separately.

The logistical task was considerable. Registration, ballot printing, and the transport of materials had to reach islands with poor communications, with a large electorate that included many first-time voters and many people with limited literacy. Symbols were used to identify parties on ballots. Officials, party workers, and the armed forces all played roles in preparing and securing the elections, and large numbers of citizens took part.

Campaigning was vigorous and highly organized. Parties mobilized through their affiliated associations, newspapers, rallies, religious networks, and village notables, and the campaign made religious and ideological differences more visible. Disputes occurred, including local intimidation and arguments about whether campaigning had divided communities, but contemporaries generally regarded the elections as largely orderly and competitive.

In broad terms, four parties, the PNI, Masyumi, NU, and PKI, won the largest shares, and no party approached a majority. Support varied regionally: Java returned strong support for the PNI, NU, and PKI, while Masyumi's support was comparatively stronger outside Java. The results demonstrated the strength of religious and nationalist mobilization, the PKI's organizational reach, and the limited electoral appeal of parties such as the PSI.

The legal framework was Law No. 7 of 1953, governing elections for both the DPR and the Constituent Assembly. The national electoral administration worked through territorial committees, and the organization of electoral districts had to account for the country's geographical and demographic differences. Party and candidate lists, voter registration, ballot distribution, and counting were distinct administrative operations. The use of recognizable symbols on ballots helped make participation possible for citizens who could not easily read a list of names. The challenge was not merely that many people had never voted before, but that the state had to develop an electoral bureaucracy capable of treating voters in very different settings as participants in the same national process.

The two contests took place on 29 September and 15 December 1955. According to the Indonesian General Elections Commission (KPU), the four leading parties in the DPR election won 57 seats for the PNI, 57 for Masyumi, 45 for NU, and 39 for the PKI. The results revealed a fragmented electorate rather than a mandate for one ideology. PNI and Masyumi received similar representation despite different social and geographical bases; the strength of NU confirmed the electoral importance of its independent organization, while the PKI's performance demonstrated its recovery from the political defeat of 1948. The electoral success of religious parties did not mean that all their voters supported an identical constitutional program. Likewise, votes for the PNI or PKI cannot be reduced to a single motive. Some citizens may have been responding to particular candidates, local leaders, labor or land grievances, or the prestige of party networks. The elections expanded the legitimacy of national representative institutions, yet they could not determine how the military would respond to civilian authority or how the government would distribute revenue among regions. Nor did a legislative plurality settle the much more demanding constitutional task entrusted to the Assembly. This is a useful example of the difference between electoral democracy and the broader problem of constitutional government. Elections enable citizens to authorize representatives and replace governments. Durable democracy additionally requires a shared commitment to procedures, rights, and the settlement of disagreements through institutions rather than coercion.

The elections conferred a form of legitimacy that no earlier government had possessed, because the legislature and the Constituent Assembly now reflected a popular vote. They did not resolve conflicts about the structure of the state, about regional claims, or about the army's role. The post-election cabinet still required coalition bargaining, and tensions between the center and the regions continued to grow.

Pancasila, Islam, and the Search for a Permanent Constitution

The Constituent Assembly convened in Bandung in November 1956. Its task was to write a permanent constitution, and its debates concerned the nature of the state itself. Delegates drew on the legacy of 1945, when the Proclamation-era constitution and the formulation of Pancasila, the five principles of belief in God, humanity, national unity, democracy, and social justice, had been adopted as the revolutionary foundation.

The most intense dispute concerned the basis of the state. Islamic parties, including Masyumi and NU, argued that Islam should be the foundation of the constitution, while nationalist, Christian, communist, and other delegates supported Pancasila. The disagreement was linked to older arguments over the so-called Jakarta Charter, a 1945 draft formula that would have obliged Muslims to follow Islamic law and had been dropped before the constitution was finalized. Delegates also debated the relationship between religious authority and citizenship, the position of religious minorities, and the guarantees of rights.

The debate was not a simple contest between religious and secular forces. Muslim delegates differed about what an Islamic basis would require in law, and some accepted Pancasila as compatible with Islam. Secular and non-Muslim delegates differed in their concerns, and some supporters of Pancasila were themselves devout. Parties also bargained over economic provisions, regional relations, and rights, on which considerable agreement had been reached.

The Assembly's difficulty was in the threshold: adopting a constitution required a large majority, and the key question of the state's basis lacked sufficient support on either side. The distinction between ordinary legislative conflict, which parliament resolved by majority, and foundational disagreement, which needed broad consensus, is central to understanding the deadlock.

The Constituent Assembly's work was substantial even though no permanent constitution resulted. Delegates addressed principles of rights, the design of representative government, and the constitutional relationship between individuals and the state. The institution gave members a forum in which they could explain how their political traditions understood citizenship, democracy, social justice, and religious obligation. Surviving proceedings are therefore important evidence for the intellectual history of independent Indonesia. A narrative that treats the Assembly only as a failed institution obscures the extent of the deliberation that took place.

Pancasila was itself a historical formulation, not a synonym for the exclusion of religion from public life. The version in the preamble to the 1945 Constitution began with belief in the One and Only God. Its supporters interpreted that principle alongside national unity, humanity, representative deliberation, and social justice. For many Muslim advocates of Pancasila, such a formulation was consistent with religious conviction while permitting citizens of different faiths to belong to the same state. Proponents of an Islamic foundation could argue that a Muslim-majority society should give Islamic principles a more explicit constitutional role. The institutional consequences of these positions depended on the specific legal provisions proposed, not merely on their labels.

The Jakarta Charter remained a reference point because its original formulation had included an obligation for Muslims to observe Islamic law. Its alteration in August 1945 had enabled a broader national agreement, but it had not removed the constitutional issue from political debate. Some advocates later regarded restoration of the formulation as a matter of historical justice; others feared that it would create different legal standings or unsettle the national compact. Students should distinguish what the 1945 draft proposed from the text actually enacted and from subsequent proposals made in the Assembly.

Under Article 137 of the Provisional Constitution, constitutional adoption required a quorum and a two-thirds majority of members present. Such a threshold was intended to ensure a broadly accepted settlement, but made it difficult for rival constitutional programs to prevail without agreement across party lines. The dispute over the return to the 1945 Constitution in 1959 became entangled with the changing political role of Sukarno and the armed forces. The outcome cannot be explained by a simple vote count alone. It was shaped by the interaction of the Assembly's rules, political disagreements, emergency government, and pressure from institutions outside the Assembly. That interaction provides the bridge to Chapter 180.

The Assembly's eventual failure to adopt a permanent constitution was not inevitable. Compromise formulas were discussed, the delegates continued to work through the late 1950s, and the outcome was shaped by the wider political crisis involving the army, the regions, and Sukarno's own proposals. How the Assembly was brought to an end, and the 1959 return to the 1945 constitution, are examined in Chapter 180.

Section 149.9

Regional Diversity and the Geography of Indonesian Government

Indonesia's political geography was unusual. A very large share of the population lived on Java, the seat of the capital and of most national institutions, while many of the country's export-earning resources lay elsewhere. Java combined dense rural settlement, long-established wet-rice agriculture, and the main universities, newspapers, and ministries. Its political weight shaped the fears of regional leaders that national government meant Javanese government.

Sumatra was the principal producer of plantation crops such as rubber and of petroleum and other minerals, and it contained strong Muslim reformist traditions, important port cities, and political communities with separate historical memories, including Aceh and the Minangkabau highlands. Kalimantan had oil, timber, and sparsely populated interiors, linked to the coast by rivers. Sulawesi included Makassar's trading tradition, the coconut-producing north, and areas of strong local political leadership. Bali had a distinctive Hindu religious culture and a strong local aristocratic and village structure. Nusa Tenggara, including the eastern Lesser Sunda islands, was poorer, drier, and more weakly connected to national markets. Maluku had been a center of the spice trade and contained substantial Christian and Muslim communities and a history of colonial military recruitment.

Colonial experience also varied. Some regions had been governed closely and some indirectly, through local rulers whose positions survived into the revolution and after. These variations affected the quality of administration, the number of educated officials available, and local attitudes to the central government. The distribution of religious institutions, from Muslim schools to Christian missions and Hindu temples, likewise shaped local leadership and access to education.

Distance did not automatically mean isolation. Trading networks, migration, pilgrimage, and shipping connected islands, and some outer-island regions were more closely linked to Singapore and international markets than to Jakarta. A region could be geographically distant yet politically integrated through party organizations, the army's territorial commands, schools, and radio; another could be near the capital but poorly served by administration.

The economic geography of the archipelago made the division of public revenue especially controversial. Rubber exports originated in both plantations and smallholder production, and substantial quantities of oil came from Sumatra and Kalimantan. The proceeds of these exports helped provide the foreign exchange necessary to purchase machinery, medicines, and other imports. Revenue collected through customs and export arrangements did not necessarily remain in the district in which the commodity had been produced. A central government might argue that these resources were national and should support services across Indonesia. Regional leaders could respond that the people and infrastructure supporting production were not receiving an adequate return. Both arguments concerned the organization of the national state, not simply loyalty or disloyalty to it.

Java itself was far from uniform. The capital's expanding administrative functions differed from the economic role of the industrial and port city of Surabaya, while the densely settled agricultural districts of Central and East Java faced different land and employment problems from the plantation zones of West Java. Similarly, Sumatra was not one political community: Aceh's religious and revolutionary institutions, the Minangkabau region's political and educational networks, and the estate economy of East Sumatra created differing demands on government. The expression outer islands can be useful as a description of their relationship to the center, but it can obscure the diversity of those regions and should not be treated as an ethnic category.

The geography of government was also a geography of transport. A steamship connection could be more important to the delivery of official correspondence than the straight-line distance between two places. Ports concentrated customs administration, warehouses, commercial information, and access to imported supplies. In inland areas, roads and rivers determined how far an official could travel and what produce could be carried economically to market. When shipping schedules failed, local markets could encounter shortages even if goods were available elsewhere in the country. Investment in these networks was consequently a political question as well as an engineering problem.

People were active agents in these networks. Merchants and migrant families linked distant ports, students traveled to educational centers, and religious teachers maintained relationships across provincial boundaries. These connections existed before the Republic and did not automatically follow its new administrative map. National integration involved building on them and sometimes redirecting them. The success of a policy announced in Jakarta depended on whether regional institutions and local communities possessed the resources and incentives to put it into practice.

Geography created administrative constraints, notably in transport, communications, and revenue collection, but not an insurmountable barrier to integration. The relationships that mattered were political: who controlled export revenue, who staffed local administrations, and how regions were represented in the center.

Citizenship, Ethnicity, Religion, and National Belonging

Indonesian nationalism had been formulated in the 1920s as a project that joined people from many ethnic and religious backgrounds. After independence the state had to give that idea legal form. Citizenship was not the same as ethnic identity: the national community was defined by a common state and history, not a single ethnicity, and Indonesian identity was expected to coexist with local, linguistic, and religious loyalties rather than replace them.

The colonial legacy complicated this. Dutch law had classified the population into legal categories, broadly Europeans, 'Foreign Orientals,' and natives, with different courts and rights. The new state rejected this hierarchy as a principle of citizenship, but inherited social distinctions, differences in education and wealth, and the practical effects of the old categories persisted. Indigenous and minority communities, including uplands peoples and small island societies, had their own relationships with the state, and the extent to which national policy reached them varied.

Chinese Indonesians were the most politically sensitive case. Many families had lived in the archipelago for generations; others were recent arrivals; and their political loyalties, languages, and occupations varied widely. Some were prominent traders, many were shopkeepers, artisans, or laborers, and some had supported Indonesian independence. Citizenship arrangements agreed after 1949 allowed many people of Chinese descent to acquire Indonesian nationality, but Indonesian and Chinese law could both claim the same individuals, raising the problem of dual nationality. A 1955 agreement between Indonesia and the People’s Republic of China sought to resolve overlapping nationality claims. A citizenship law in 1958 then set the framework for nationality in the independent state.

Citizenship debates intersected with economic nationalism, discussed later, because commercial roles were often discussed in ethnic terms. Treating Chinese Indonesians as a homogeneous economic group obscures their diversity and exaggerates the share of wealth held by the few. Arab Indonesians, Indo-Europeans, and others faced their own choices; many Indo-Europeans and Ambonese associated with the colonial army left for the Netherlands.

The sovereignty settlement included nationality arrangements because independence changed the relationship between inhabitants and the states that claimed their allegiance. A legal determination of nationality might affect eligibility for public employment, residence rights, travel, and political participation. This was especially consequential for families whose members had been assigned different legal statuses under colonial rule. The transition did not simply make every resident a citizen or every person of foreign ancestry a foreigner. Individual nationality could depend on earlier agreements, descent, declarations, and administrative records.

The Sino-Indonesian dual-nationality agreement signed at Bandung on 22 April 1955 sought to resolve competing claims under Indonesian and Chinese nationality rules. It concerned people who possessed both nationalities, not everyone of Chinese ancestry. Its implementation extended beyond the date of signature; Indonesian Law No. 2 of 1958 approved the agreement, and the nationality-selection process followed ratification. It is therefore important not to describe the 1955 agreement as immediately requiring all Chinese Indonesians to make a choice. Nor should nationality be used as a shorthand for political allegiance. A citizen could maintain familial, linguistic, or commercial connections with China while identifying strongly with Indonesia.

Law No. 62 of 1958, promulgated on 1 August, gave a more systematic national legal framework. It recognized persons already Indonesian under earlier legislation and agreements, defined acquisition of nationality through family relationships and other specified circumstances, and provided routes to naturalization. Its rules reflected a significant preference for avoiding dual nationality. They also included gendered distinctions: the nationality of a child in a legally recognized paternal relationship was often determined through the father, with maternal transmission available in particular circumstances. Thus the law's implications for families depended on sex, marital status, descent, and prior nationality as well as place of birth.

Citizenship rights in practice could be affected by access to documentation. Registration offices, consular officials, and courts were important intermediaries. A person eligible for a legal status might still face practical difficulties in proving it, especially where records had been disrupted by occupation, displacement, or revolution. Political debates about economic ownership could also distinguish citizens categorized as indigenous from citizens of other ancestry, showing that formal citizenship and equal economic treatment were not necessarily identical. The challenge for the Republic was to establish legal membership in a common political community while addressing inequalities inherited from colonial society without turning ancestry into a permanent measure of national belonging.

National equality and inherited distinctions therefore coexisted uneasily. The state aimed to treat all citizens equally while administering a society in which language, religion, wealth, and region continued to shape opportunity.

Section 149.11

Maritime Sovereignty and the Integration of National Territory

The inherited colonial rule on maritime space treated each island as surrounded by its own narrow belt of territorial water, with stretches of high seas between islands. Under a 1939 colonial ordinance, territorial waters extended a short distance, three nautical miles, around each island. For an archipelagic state this left wide corridors of sea between the islands open to foreign ships and aircraft and outside Indonesian jurisdiction.

The sea was central to Indonesian integration. Inter-island commerce, communication, and the transport of officials and troops all depended on shipping, which in the early 1950s was dominated by a Dutch company until its eventual displacement. Security concerns added urgency: the regional crises of 1956-1958, tensions with the Netherlands over Western New Guinea, and the passage of foreign warships raised questions about control of the waters between the islands.

On 13 December 1957 the government of Prime Minister Djuanda issued a declaration asserting that the waters around, between, and connecting the islands of the archipelago formed part of the national territory under Indonesian sovereignty. It proposed measuring the territorial sea from lines linking the outermost points of the outermost islands, an approach very different from the colonial pattern, and it affirmed that innocent passage by foreign ships would continue.

The declaration marked the emergence of an archipelagic understanding of territory, in which islands and seas were treated as a unit. Foreign governments, including maritime powers, objected or reserved their position, since the claim restricted what they considered high seas. Indonesia developed the principle further in domestic legislation and in international negotiations over the following decades, and the concept was eventually recognized in the 1982 United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, which created a regime for archipelagic states. The declaration itself did not settle the legal question in 1957; it was an assertion that required years of diplomacy and persuasion.

The colonial ordinance of 1939 was a product of an international legal order that had developed around continental and coastal states. Its treatment of territorial waters as a belt around individual islands was not necessarily designed to establish a coherent Indonesian national territory. Once the archipelago became independent, however, the continued existence of waters outside national jurisdiction between many of its islands became a matter of political concern. It raised questions about the country's ability to regulate resource use, customs, maritime security, and the passage of foreign vessels across waters separating its population centers.

The Djuanda Declaration gave this problem a specific legal response. The government proposed drawing straight baselines between the outer points of the outermost Indonesian islands and measuring a territorial sea twelve nautical miles outward from these lines. The waters enclosed between the islands were claimed as part of the national territory. The proposal was accompanied by a provision for peaceful passage of foreign ships, subject to the security and sovereignty of the Republic. These elements were connected: Indonesia sought both territorial unity and a workable relationship with international navigation. The declaration should not be interpreted as a claim that every movement of foreign ships would automatically be prohibited.

The significance of a baseline is that it establishes the reference from which the extent of a maritime zone is measured. If a country uses the coasts of individual islands as the reference, narrow territorial belts may leave broad spaces outside its sovereignty. If a legally permissible archipelagic baseline encloses the islands as a group, the waters on its landward side may acquire a different legal status. This distinction has consequences for regulation, navigation, and the exploitation of resources. A historical diagram can explain the principle without pretending to establish legally valid coordinates or to represent the precise maritime geography of Indonesia.

Indonesia's claim faced resistance because other states had interests in the use of international sea lanes. The development of the modern legal regime therefore required diplomatic negotiation and refinement of the original proposal. In 1960 Indonesia followed the declaration with national legislation; the international regime codified in Part IV of the 1982 Convention on the Law of the Sea recognized archipelagic waters subject to specific conditions and navigation rights. Those later rules were not already in force in 1957. The distinction between the original political declaration and the later multilateral legal settlement is essential to understanding this achievement. In the context of this chapter, the declaration demonstrates the Republic's attempt to translate the political claim of unity into a coherent spatial conception of the state.

Western New Guinea, which the Netherlands had retained in 1949 pending further negotiation, remained an unresolved part of Indonesian claims to national territory. Indonesia pressed the matter in international forums during the 1950s without success. Its subsequent history belongs to Chapter 180.

Bahasa Indonesia, Education, and the Construction of National Identity

National integration depended on everyday communication as well as constitutional design. Bahasa Indonesia, derived from Malay, had long served as a language of trade across the archipelago, and nationalists had declared it the language of unity in 1928. After independence it became the language of government, schools, newspapers, and radio. It was a second language for many Indonesians, who spoke Javanese, Sundanese, Minangkabau, Balinese, Bugis, and hundreds of other languages at home, and regional languages continued to be used in daily life and, often, in early schooling.

Education expanded rapidly. Primary schooling grew, teachers were recruited and trained, and new secondary schools and universities opened, including Gadjah Mada University in Yogyakarta, which helped train a generation of officials and professionals. The expansion exceeded the supply of qualified teachers and facilities, and many children, particularly in rural and remote areas, had limited access. Muslim schools, Christian mission schools, and other private institutions continued alongside state schools.

Print and broadcast media created a shared public sphere. Newspapers, magazines, and publishers circulated writing in Indonesian, and Radio Republik Indonesia, created during the revolution, carried news and cultural programs to listeners across the islands. Writers and artists explored national themes and the memory of the revolution. National ceremonies, symbols, and anniversaries, including Heroes' Day, tied the new state to revolutionary memory.

Youth organizations, scouting, student associations, and women's organizations drew people into public life. Women voted and stood for office, worked as teachers and in health services, and organized through associations of different political and religious orientations, though women remained a minority among political leaders.

The adoption of Indonesian as a national language offered a practical response to linguistic diversity. Its roots in the widely used Malay language meant that it was not simply a decision to make the language of the largest ethnic population the official language. The nationalist movement had already identified Indonesian as a language capable of connecting people of different backgrounds. Government use after independence extended that role into administration, legislation, education, and communication. This transition required the creation or adaptation of terminology for such subjects as economics, engineering, constitutional law, and medicine. Translators, teachers, publishers, and civil servants contributed to the development of a vocabulary in which national institutions could operate.

Education was simultaneously a social aspiration and a means of building government capacity. The Republic needed literate clerks, trained teachers, health workers, engineers, and legal professionals. Expanding the school system therefore affected both the lives of individual students and the ability of the state to function. Gadjah Mada University, formally established in December 1949, and other higher-education institutions became important sources of trained personnel. The University of Indonesia also developed out of institutions reorganized in the period surrounding the sovereignty transfer. Such institutions helped reduce dependence on foreign expertise, although the preparation of highly skilled professionals required sustained investment over many years.

The transition was uneven because the distribution of classrooms, teachers, textbooks, and public funds differed across regions. A child in a well-connected urban center might have access to secondary education that a child in a remote rural district did not. Families also made decisions about whether children could remain in school rather than contribute to household work. Girls' access to education expanded but continued to be affected by economic circumstances and social expectations. The existence of a national educational program therefore cannot be equated with equal access to education. Religious schools and private organizations remained important participants in educational development.

Cultural life also became an arena in which the meaning of independence was debated. Literature and the arts explored revolutionary experience, social inequality, and the tension between older cultural forms and modern national institutions. Radio made it possible for speeches and reports from the capital to circulate beyond the readership of newspapers. Yet local languages and regional cultural traditions continued to shape how people interpreted national events. The emerging national public was not a uniform audience receiving identical messages. It was a set of overlapping communities that could increasingly communicate with one another through a shared language while retaining distinctive experiences and traditions.

Integration in this sense did not mean cultural homogenization. National language, schooling, and ceremony offered a common framework while local languages, religions, and customs continued. The reach of these institutions was uneven, and many villages experienced the nation chiefly through a teacher, a radio set, or a local official.

Section 149.13

The Economic Inheritance of Colonialism and Revolution

The Republic inherited an economy built for export. Plantation crops such as rubber, sugar, tea, coffee, and tobacco, together with petroleum, tin, and copra, earned foreign exchange, while a large share of the population grew food on small farms. The modern sector was dominated by foreign firms: Dutch trading houses, banks, plantation companies, and shipping lines, together with British and American oil companies. Indonesian entrepreneurs existed but held a smaller share of large-scale commerce.

Occupation and revolution had damaged plantations, transport, and industry, and production in some sectors recovered slowly. Java imported rice to feed its population. The sovereignty settlement obliged the state to assume obligations connected with the colonial government, which burdened public finance. Government revenue depended heavily on export and import taxes and thus on world commodity prices.

The early 1950s illustrated this dependence. A surge in demand for raw materials during the Korean War raised export earnings in 1950 and 1951, easing the budget, but prices fell afterward, and governments faced deficits, inflation, and pressure on foreign-exchange reserves. Monetary instability affected wages and food prices, which mattered to urban workers and the salaried middle class.

Regional differences were large. Export-producing regions in Sumatra, Kalimantan, and Sulawesi earned much of the foreign exchange, while Java contained most of the population and the national government. Producers in the outer islands often felt that their earnings supported spending elsewhere, a grievance that fed the regional conflicts of the late 1950s.

The inherited economy combined large enterprises serving overseas markets with small-scale production for local and regional consumption. Plantation rubber and tobacco, petroleum, and other primary exports supplied foreign exchange. Many rice cultivators, artisans, fishers, and traders operated outside the large corporate sector but depended indirectly on the prices of imported goods and the condition of domestic markets. An increase in export revenue could improve the government's ability to import equipment without automatically improving the incomes of all households. Conversely, an external price decline could weaken public finances even in regions not directly producing export commodities.

The Korean War commodity boom provided a temporary favorable environment for exporters in the early 1950s. Demand for strategic raw materials supported prices, particularly for rubber. The government could use part of the resulting foreign exchange to finance imports and reconstruction. When prices fell from their peak, the weakness of a budget dependent on primary exports became more apparent. Ministries faced competing claims for expenditure on roads, schools, security forces, and administration. The difficulty was not unique to Indonesia: newly independent states whose exports were concentrated in a limited range of products faced considerable uncertainty in financing long-term commitments from short-term movements in world markets.

Monetary consolidation was another immediate problem. The revolution had produced overlapping systems of currency, while the new federal government inherited inflation and fiscal pressures. In March 1950 Finance Minister Sjafruddin Prawiranegara introduced the measure known as Gunting Sjafruddin, or Sjafruddin's scissors. Specified higher-denomination notes were divided, reducing the amount available for immediate circulation and converting part of their value into a government borrowing instrument. The policy was intended to restrain excess purchasing power and strengthen the government's financial position. It illustrates the difficulties of monetary integration in a state that lacked a fully developed domestic market for public borrowing. Its impact on households depended on what forms of currency and wealth they held.

The debt settlement imposed additional constraints. Indonesia assumed obligations inherited from the colonial state under the Round Table arrangements. The political dispute concerned not simply the size of these obligations but the legitimacy of requiring a newly independent country to service debts associated with the previous administration. Payments and negotiations competed with reconstruction needs and contributed to the deterioration of relations with the Netherlands. Indonesia repudiated outstanding elements of the settlement in 1956, but this did not remove the country's need for foreign exchange, equipment, credit, and technical expertise.

Economic reconstruction thus required more than restoring prewar output. The Republic sought to change the distribution of ownership and the purpose of investment while keeping the productive and financial systems functioning. These objectives sometimes reinforced one another and sometimes conflicted, as the next section demonstrates.

Political independence therefore did not bring economic independence. Foreign ownership, export dependence, debt obligations, and limited Indonesian managerial experience constrained policy. Governments could change the rules of trade and ownership, but had to do so within these inherited limits.

Section 149.14

Economic Nationalism, Indonesianization, and Development Policy

Economic nationalism aimed to increase Indonesian ownership and management of the economy. Its advocates argued that political independence meant little while foreign firms controlled trade and finance. One early policy, the Benteng ('fortress') program launched in 1950 under economic planners including Sumitro Djojohadikusumo, gave import licenses and credit preferentially to indigenous (pribumi) firms, intending to create a class of Indonesian entrepreneurs able to compete with established importers.

Implementation was difficult. Licenses were valuable, and many recipients lacked capital and experience; some sold or shared licenses with established merchants, including Chinese Indonesian traders, in arrangements sometimes called 'Ali-Baba' partnerships. Political patronage influenced who received licenses. The program produced some new entrepreneurs, but critics argued that it also encouraged rent-seeking and strained relations among communities. Assessments differ about its balance of success and failure.

The state built institutions for finance and development. The colonial-era central bank was placed on a path to nationalization in 1951 and succeeded by Bank Indonesia in 1953, giving the government greater control over monetary policy. State banks and development agencies were created, and the government prepared development plans, including a five-year plan in the mid-1950s. The state expanded its role in enterprises, and in 1956 Indonesia unilaterally ended parts of the Round Table financial arrangements with the Netherlands.

In December 1957, amid the dispute with the Netherlands over Western New Guinea, workers and then military authorities took over Dutch-owned enterprises, and the government moved to nationalize them. This greatly expanded public ownership, but placed many enterprises under managers, often military officers, with limited commercial training, and disrupted trade and shipping.

The Benteng Program addressed a specific imbalance in the structure of commerce. Large established firms possessed capital, access to international suppliers, knowledge of import procedures, and relationships with banks. A prospective Indonesian-owned importer might have none of these advantages. Preferential licensing and credit were intended to overcome barriers to entry. The policy therefore represented an early attempt to use public authority to change the distribution of economic opportunity rather than simply rely on market competition to reverse colonial-era inequalities.

The problem was that a license did not itself create the other resources needed to operate an importing business. A license holder needed access to foreign currency, knowledge of shipping and insurance, customers, warehouses, and the ability to arrange payment. Partnerships with established firms could provide these capabilities, but they could also reduce the intended beneficiary to a nominal participant receiving a payment for access to a license. Political connections could influence the allocation of licenses. These outcomes illustrate why the institutional design of an affirmative economic policy matters as much as its announced objective. The existence of such arrangements does not demonstrate that every participant was fraudulent or that all partnerships were unsuccessful.

In banking, the purchase and nationalization of the formerly Dutch-controlled De Javasche Bank began in 1951. Bank Indonesia commenced operations as the national central bank on 1 July 1953 under Law No. 11 of 1953. Its establishment provided the state with an institution responsible for monetary management, although its role also encompassed functions that differed from those of a modern independent central bank. The bank's creation was a concrete institutional achievement: monetary policy and banking development could increasingly be directed by Indonesian authorities. It did not, by itself, establish price stability or solve the budgetary problems of government.

Planning institutions sought to coordinate public investment in transport, energy, education, and productive facilities. In a country with limited capital and technical personnel, project selection was important. Investment in a port or irrigation facility could facilitate production for many years, whereas poorly designed projects could tie up funds without increasing capacity. The Five-Year Development Plan prepared in the 1950s reflected the government's desire to move from emergency reconstruction toward coordinated development. Implementation was disrupted by political instability, foreign-exchange constraints, and security emergencies.

The takeovers of Dutch firms in December 1957 were followed by the legal nationalization framework of Law No. 86 of 1958. Distinguishing the two stages clarifies how workers' actions, military supervision, and government legislation interacted. The acquisition of enterprises transferred ownership and control, but operations required skilled managers, working capital, spare parts, and access to markets. Some military officers obtained important administrative and economic responsibilities in this process. Economic nationalism thus contributed not only to changes in ownership but also to the redistribution of institutional power within the Republic.

The policies show the tension between rapid change and limited capacity. Policy objectives, implementation, and outcomes must be distinguished: the aims of building Indonesian ownership were widely shared, the instruments chosen had mixed results, and the resulting economy remained dependent on commodity exports. Nationalization did not by itself produce successful development.

Rural Society, Urban Life, and the Social Expectations of Independence

Most Indonesians lived in villages. Rural life centered on agriculture, with wet-rice farming predominant in Java and Bali, and a mix of rice, upland crops, tree crops, and fishing elsewhere. Land was unequally distributed in many places, and in densely populated Java many households held very little land, rented land, or worked as laborers. Plantation workers, living in estate settlements, labored under conditions inherited from colonial production and organized unions, some linked to political parties.

Revolution had raised expectations. Many villagers hoped for secure land and fairer treatment, and some former fighters expected recognition, employment, or land. Disputes arose when plantation land that villagers had cultivated during the occupation and revolution was claimed by companies or the state, as in the Tanjung Morawa incident in Sumatra in 1953. Demands for agrarian reform were raised by peasant organizations and parties, though comprehensive land legislation came later and belongs to Chapter 180.

Cities grew as people migrated for work and schooling. Jakarta, Surabaya, Medan, and other cities attracted migrants and struggled with housing shortages and limited services. Urban employment included government office work, small trade, factory and dock labor, and informal work. Inflation affected salaried officials and workers, and labor unions organized strikes and campaigns for wages.

Education and public health improved in some places but remained uneven. Schools and clinics expanded, yet rural areas lacked teachers and medical staff. Food security depended on harvests and on imported rice, and regional shortages occurred. Women worked in agriculture, markets, schools, and health services, and women's organizations campaigned on education, marriage law, and political rights.

Agrarian disputes were shaped by the difference between formal title and actual occupation. During the Japanese occupation and the revolution, some estate land ceased to be cultivated under its previous management, and residents began farming plots to support themselves. After sovereignty transfer, estate enterprises and government officials sought to restore production or reorganize the use of the land. Cultivators could regard their occupation as a legitimate claim arising from wartime hardship and revolutionary experience, while estate managers invoked legal title and the need to produce exports. Conflicts over these different understandings of property became matters of national policy. Tanjung Morawa in 1953 revealed how a local dispute over land could affect a government in Jakarta.

Rural households were not composed exclusively of farmers working land they owned. Some combined cultivation with wage labor, petty commerce, fishing, craft production, or seasonal migration. A household's ability to withstand a poor harvest depended on access to savings, land, credit, kinship networks, and alternative employment. This variety helps explain why economic conditions were experienced differently even within the same village. A rise in the price of rice could benefit a farmer with a marketable surplus while imposing hardship on a laborer who needed to purchase food. National economic policy therefore had distributional consequences that cannot be understood from aggregate production figures alone.

Urban growth reflected the expansion of ministries, universities, ports, manufacturing, and informal commerce. Migration was often supported by family or regional networks that helped newcomers find lodging and employment. The growth of cities increased demand for water, sanitation, housing, transport, and schools, while municipal resources were limited. Urban neighborhoods included people with very different relationships to the national economy: salaried civil servants, dockworkers, market vendors, industrial employees, students, and people without stable paid employment. Independence expanded public employment and political activity, but access to these opportunities depended heavily on education and personal circumstances.

Women participated in these transformations as workers, voters, organizers, and members of families. Organizations such as Gerwani and other women's associations pursued differing programs involving education, family law, welfare, labor, and political participation. Their activities were not interchangeable: some were associated with particular parties, some with religious institutions, and others with professional or social organizations. The expansion of women's public participation did not automatically eliminate inequality within households or workplaces. By examining such institutions, historians can understand independence as a lived social transformation rather than exclusively a constitutional change. The challenge of the early Republic was to make legal citizenship meaningful for people whose material resources and opportunities remained profoundly unequal.

Independence therefore brought uneven results. Cities, and Java more than other regions, were not representative of the whole country, and experiences varied by class, gender, region, and occupation. Many people gained schooling and political voice; others saw little change in land, income, or security.

Everyday Life and Social History

Independence Through the Experiences of Indonesian Households

Consider four types of household in the 1950s, as patterns historians identify rather than individual cases. A Javanese rice-farming family with little land might rely on tenancy, wage labor at harvest, and small trade, and might regard independence chiefly in terms of whether rents, debts, and local officials became less burdensome. A plantation worker's household in Sumatra lived within the company's housing and wage structure and might look to a union for improvements. A civil servant's household in Jakarta, with a fixed salary, could be hit hard by inflation even as the head of household held a prestigious post.

A fourth household might include a former fighter, expecting recognition and work after the revolution; whether that expectation was met depended on military demobilization, local networks, and available jobs. Across these households, children's schooling, women's income-earning, and access to medical care shaped daily life, and the experience of the new state was often mediated by a local official, teacher, or party or union organizer. Section 149.16

Foreign Policy, International Recognition, and the Bandung Conference

International recognition was part of state-building. Indonesia joined the United Nations in September 1950 and entered other international bodies, and its leaders hoped that international standing would strengthen its claims, including to Western New Guinea. Relations with the Netherlands remained difficult. The Netherlands-Indonesian Union created at the Round Table was gradually emptied of meaning and Indonesia formally ended it in 1956.

Mohammad Hatta had articulated the guiding approach in 1948 as a policy of independence and activism, often described as 'rowing between two reefs': Indonesia should decide its own course in the emerging Cold War rather than align with either bloc, while participating actively in international affairs. In practice, governments interpreted this differently. The Sukiman cabinet's aid arrangement with the United States provoked a political crisis because critics saw it as a step toward alignment; later cabinets stressed neutrality while maintaining trade and aid relations with both Western and communist states.

Relations with Asian neighbors and with newly independent states were increasingly important. Indonesia worked with India, Burma, Ceylon, and Pakistan, and in April 1955 hosted the Asian-African Conference in Bandung, attended by delegations from twenty-nine countries. Prime Minister Ali Sastroamidjojo was a central host, and Sukarno delivered an opening address. The conference condemned colonialism, affirmed principles including sovereignty and peaceful coexistence, and promoted economic and cultural cooperation among the participants, who held sharply different political positions.

Bandung was a landmark of anticolonial internationalism and of Asian-African solidarity, and it enhanced Indonesia's prestige. It should be understood in its own historical context, not as the formal founding of the Non-Aligned Movement, which was established later, in 1961, with a different membership and organization. Participants at Bandung included states aligned with the United States and with China.

The term bebas aktif captured two complementary dimensions of foreign policy. Independence of judgment meant that the Republic would not automatically accept the strategic priorities of a foreign bloc. Active engagement meant that Indonesia would not remain indifferent to wars, colonial rule, and international negotiations. The policy did not require identical relations with all states or equal amounts of trade and assistance from each. Instead, it gave Indonesian leaders a language in which to defend their freedom of action. Disagreement over what counted as unacceptable alignment remained a recurring subject of domestic political competition.

The Republic was admitted to the United Nations on 28 September 1950. Membership provided an institutional setting in which Indonesian diplomats could raise questions of colonialism and territory. Western New Guinea was especially important. The 1949 agreement had left the issue for subsequent negotiations, but the Netherlands and Indonesia continued to disagree over its future. Indonesian leaders treated the issue as unfinished decolonization, while Dutch authorities advanced different arguments concerning the territory and its inhabitants. Attempts to secure a diplomatic settlement through the 1950s proved unsuccessful. The dispute helps explain the termination of the Netherlands-Indonesian Union and the deterioration in economic relations, without making the later military and diplomatic campaign the focus of this chapter.

Bandung represented a broader conception of diplomacy. The five sponsoring governments had prepared the conference through earlier consultations. From 18 to 24 April 1955, representatives from twenty-nine Asian and African countries discussed colonialism, international cooperation, economic development, and peaceful coexistence. Participants did not constitute an ideologically unified bloc. Some were allied to Western powers, some maintained close relations with communist governments, and others were attempting to preserve freedom of action. The conference's achievements lay partly in its ability to establish shared principles among states that disagreed on important security questions. Those principles were expressed in a final communiqué and in the ten principles of Bandung, which included respect for sovereignty and territorial integrity, noninterference, and peaceful settlement of disputes.

The conference also illustrates the relationship between domestic institutional development and international prestige. Indonesia had to provide administrative coordination, facilities, translation, transport, and diplomatic support for the meeting. Hosting it demonstrated a level of government capacity that the Republic had acquired in a relatively short period. International recognition could reinforce the confidence of citizens and leaders in the national state, even while the same government faced difficulties at home. Bandung was therefore not merely a symbolic gathering. It was a practical manifestation of the Republic's determination to act internationally on its own terms. Its influence on later nonalignment was important, but the formal movement established at Belgrade in 1961 belonged to a subsequent phase.

International legitimacy and domestic state-building were linked. Diplomatic success reinforced the government's authority and national pride, while the failure to secure Western New Guinea fueled nationalist frustration and contributed to the radicalization of policy in the late 1950s. An independent and active foreign policy did not mean disengagement; it meant selecting partners and positions according to Indonesian interests.

The Armed Forces and Civilian Political Authority

The Indonesian armed forces were born in the revolution, formed from Japanese-sponsored auxiliary forces, local militias, and Republican units, and they regarded themselves as having won independence. After 1949 they absorbed former Dutch colonial army personnel and other armed groups, producing an organization of diverse origins, training, and loyalties. Demobilization and 'rationalization' threatened many officers and soldiers with loss of position, which contributed to tension with civilian governments.

Professionalization competed with revolutionary self-image. Some officers, including those trained in the Dutch system, favored a technically competent, politically restrained army; others, drawn from revolutionary units, believed the army had a claim to a voice in national affairs. The army organized itself territorially, with regional commanders holding authority over defined areas, a structure that gave commanders considerable autonomy and ties to local political and economic life. Abdul Haris Nasution, as army chief of staff, was central to debates about the army's role and to efforts to define its doctrine.

The confrontation of 17 October 1952 was the most dramatic early clash. Army officers, dissatisfied with parliamentary interference in military affairs and with proposed reorganization, supported demonstrations in Jakarta and aimed artillery at the presidential palace, calling for the dissolution of parliament. Sukarno refused to dissolve it. The affair divided the army, led to the suspension of its leadership, and showed both the army's capacity to intervene and its internal disunity. Nasution was reinstated as chief of staff in 1955.

The military's involvement in administration and the economy grew over time, especially after martial law was declared in 1957, which gave commanders broad powers over regions. Civilians in cabinets, parties, and parliament sought to control promotions and budgets, and the army sought autonomy and a say in policy.

The army's postrevolutionary organization presented both a personnel problem and a political problem. Fighters who had joined local armed groups during the revolution did not all possess the same training, and their expectations about military employment varied. Professional military leaders wanted a force with standardized command, discipline, and administrative procedures. Other officers worried that reorganization would eliminate their units or reduce the influence of the communities that had supported them. The central government also faced the financial burden of maintaining a large security establishment while trying to fund reconstruction. Military reorganization therefore involved questions of public finance, regional representation, and the political meaning of revolutionary service.

The territorial organization of the army gave commanders a particularly important role in local affairs. Their units operated across a country in which civilian transport, police, and communications were often limited. Commanders could become intermediaries between local populations and Jakarta and could develop relationships with businesspeople, local officials, and regional political leaders. These relationships sometimes helped the government exercise authority; at other times they gave officers the means to challenge central decisions. The existence of territorial commands did not make every commander an opponent of civilian government, but it made the distribution of power within the army important to the distribution of power within the country.

The October 1952 crisis arose from disputes over military reorganization, command, and parliamentary oversight. Nasution and other officers objected to what they regarded as political interference in military administration, while opponents within parliament and within the army contested the leadership's proposals. The pressure placed on Sukarno to dissolve parliament was a challenge to the constitutional order. His refusal showed that military influence had limits, and the subsequent removal of Nasution from his position demonstrated the divisions within the officer corps. His return as army chief in 1955 did not simply restore the situation that had existed before the confrontation; military politics continued to develop in the intervening years.

The declaration of martial law in March 1957 marked a major change in the relationship between security and civilian administration. Emergency powers gave military authorities a wider role in government, and regional rebellion reinforced their claim that military coordination was necessary for national survival. In late 1958 Nasution articulated an approach often called the Middle Way, rejecting both an army confined entirely to barracks and a straightforward military dictatorship while claiming a broader role in national life. This formulation was an important precursor to later developments, not a fully established version of the New Order's institutional doctrine. By the end of the decade, civilian political institutions remained important, but the army had acquired an expanded political and economic position that would shape the next constitutional period.

The army was not politically homogeneous. Officers disagreed about relations with parties, about regional demands, and about Sukarno. Disagreements within the officer corps should be distinguished from conflicts between military and civilian authority, and the later New Order military system, with its extensive political role, cannot be read back into the 1950s, when civilian parliamentary institutions still held formal authority.

Darul Islam and Competing Claims to Political Authority

Darul Islam ('abode of Islam') began in West Java during the revolution under S. M. Kartosuwiryo, who rejected the Republic's agreements with the Dutch and in 1949 proclaimed an Islamic state, the Islamic State of Indonesia (Negara Islam Indonesia). After the Republic returned to West Java and the sovereignty settlement, Kartosuwiryo's forces continued to fight, and conflict persisted for years in the countryside, affecting villagers who were pressed for food and support by both sides.

Related movements arose elsewhere, with their own origins. In Aceh, Daud Beureueh, a prominent religious leader, joined the rebellion in 1953, partly in response to the loss of Aceh's separate provincial status when it was merged into North Sumatra and partly because of religious and political aims. In South Sulawesi, Kahar Muzakkar, a former guerrilla commander, rebelled after disputes over the integration of his fighters into the army and later linked his movement with Darul Islam. These movements shared an Islamic vocabulary but differed in leadership, grievances, and goals, and were not a single coordinated national insurgency.

The government responded with military campaigns, local militias, and, in some cases, negotiation. Operations imposed heavy burdens on civilians, including displacement, food shortages, and violence from several parties. In Aceh negotiations in 1959 produced special regional status and agreements with much of the rebel leadership, although Daud Beureueh continued resisting; the West Java and South Sulawesi movements continued into the 1960s, when their leaders were captured or killed, developments beyond this chapter's scope.

The movements must be distinguished from constitutional Islamic politics. Masyumi, NU, and other Muslim organizations competed in elections, joined cabinets, and argued for Islam’s role in the constitution through parliamentary procedures; their constituencies and leadership cannot be equated with armed insurgents. Islamic identity did not necessarily produce support for rebellion, and many Muslims opposed Darul Islam.

The movements associated with Darul Islam emerged out of distinct local histories. In West Java, Kartosuwiryo's rejection of the Republic's political arrangements during the revolution developed into a separate claim to statehood. The proclamation of the Islamic State of Indonesia in August 1949 preceded the Dutch transfer of sovereignty. After the revolution, the movement continued to challenge the Republic's authority in areas where local insecurity, economic hardship, and disrupted administration complicated government control. A competing state project claimed authority over territory and people, not merely the right to express disagreement with national policies.

In Aceh, the context was different. The region had contributed to the survival of the Republic during the revolution and possessed strong networks of Muslim religious leadership. Its incorporation into North Sumatra following the postindependence reorganization was interpreted by some regional leaders as a loss of political status and recognition. Daud Beureueh's rebellion in 1953 drew on these grievances as well as demands concerning the role of Islam. The restoration of Aceh as a separate province in 1956 and the special-status concessions of 1959 illustrate that the government could respond to regional discontent through changes in administration and negotiation, as well as military action. The latter concessions covered religion, education, and customary law. They helped secure agreements with much of the rebellion's leadership but did not immediately end Daud Beureueh's resistance.

Kahar Muzakkar's movement in South Sulawesi emerged in part from the problem of integrating former guerrillas into the national armed forces. Disputes over recognition and command created a conflict that later became associated with the Darul Islam project. This history demonstrates why a religious political label does not supply a complete explanation for an insurgency. Similar ideological language could be adopted by movements whose origins were different. Local grievances, military careers, social relationships, and competing models of government all affected how rebellion developed.

The effects of these conflicts extended beyond the leaders and armed organizations. Communities in contested districts faced insecurity, pressure to provide resources, and disruption of farming, travel, and commerce. People might cooperate with one side to preserve their safety without supporting its political objectives. Historians must therefore distinguish evidence of political commitment from behavior produced by coercion or necessity. Government military records may emphasize security objectives, while regional histories and oral testimony can reveal the consequences for residents. The Republic's effort to establish authority involved both the assertion of a common constitutional order and decisions about when regional accommodation could be more effective than coercion. The different outcomes of the Darul Islam conflicts make the importance of those decisions especially clear.

The conflicts show that the Republic's claim to sovereignty was challenged within its own territory, and that disputes about religion, regional status, and military integration could produce armed resistance. They also helped the army justify expanded security powers.

Regional Discontent, Permesta, and the PRRI Rebellion

By the mid-1950s many regional leaders felt that the central government neglected their interests. Export-producing regions, notably in Sumatra and Sulawesi, complained that Jakarta took foreign-exchange earnings and gave little development in return. Administrative centralization, the dominance of Java in national politics, and the removal of local military commanders added to discontent. Military dissatisfaction was part of the picture, since regional commanders had long-established local ties and disliked transfers and interference from the army's central command.

In late 1956 and 1957 several regional military commanders formed councils that took over local administration. The Banteng Council in Central Sumatra, led by Colonel Ahmad Husein, assumed power in December 1956, followed by councils in other parts of Sumatra. In Sulawesi, in March 1957, regional leaders, including Lieutenant Colonel Ventje Sumual, proclaimed the Universal Struggle Charter (Permesta), seeking autonomy and a larger share of revenue. Hatta's resignation as vice president in December 1956 and the formation of the Djuanda cabinet under martial law occurred in this context.

Negotiations failed to resolve the crisis. In February 1958 dissident leaders in Padang, including civilian politicians associated with Masyumi and PSI such as Sjafruddin Prawiranegara, Mohammad Natsir, and Burhanuddin Harahap, joined with the military officers and proclaimed the Revolutionary Government of the Republic of Indonesia (PRRI). The central government dispatched forces, retook the main towns in Sumatra and northern Sulawesi in 1958, and the rebels turned to guerrilla warfare.

Foreign involvement affected the conflict. The United States, concerned about communist influence, gave covert assistance to the rebels, and the downing of an American pilot in 1958 exposed this support; the episode strained relations while the central government turned to other sources of arms. The conflict also strengthened the army, which gained prestige and responsibilities, and weakened the Masyumi and PSI, whose leaders were implicated in the rebellion.

The central-regional conflict involved the fiscal structure of the new state. Export-producing areas supplied much of the foreign exchange on which national import policy depended, while allocation of those earnings was largely directed through central institutions. Regional leaders argued that insufficient resources returned to the places where exports were produced. Some commanders and merchants developed trading arrangements outside officially controlled channels, allowing regions to finance their own activities while weakening central supervision. The dispute thus concerned both the distribution of public investment and the authority to manage economic relations. Grievances were reinforced by dissatisfaction with appointments and transfers within military commands.

The regional councils that emerged in late 1956 challenged the government's authority in different ways. In Central Sumatra the Banteng Council took over regional administration; other councils and commanders advanced their own demands in other parts of Sumatra. In eastern Indonesia, Permesta was proclaimed in Makassar on 2 March 1957, setting out a program of reform and regional autonomy. Its center of activity subsequently shifted toward North Sulawesi. The movement did not originate as a simple declaration of independence from Indonesia. Its participants argued that the central state had failed to fulfill the objectives of independence and needed institutional reform. Nevertheless, the assumption of authority by regional military leaders raised the question of whether such reforms could be pursued outside existing constitutional procedures.

The PRRI was proclaimed on 15 February 1958, with Sjafruddin Prawiranegara heading a rival government. It claimed to restore legitimate republican government rather than create a separate Sumatran nation. Permesta and the PRRI became associated in armed opposition to Jakarta, but their leadership and regional bases remained distinct. The central government's response reasserted control over major population centers during 1958, although resistance persisted into the following years. The outcome strengthened the position of the army's central command and narrowed the political options of civilian leaders involved in the opposition.

The Cold War complicated what had originated primarily as an Indonesian political and regional dispute. The Eisenhower administration feared communist influence in Indonesia and covertly supported the regional rebels. The capture of American pilot Allen Lawrence Pope on 18 May 1958 made the involvement harder to conceal. Declassified American diplomatic records and subsequent historical research illuminate both the intervention and the later movement toward engagement with the Indonesian central government. Foreign involvement did not create the regional grievances, however, and the rebels were not merely instruments of an external power. The proper historical explanation connects local disputes with international intervention while preserving the independent agency of Indonesian participants.

The political cost of the rebellion was substantial even after the central government recovered the principal towns. It strengthened advocates of emergency authority, damaged parties associated with the dissidents, and made negotiation over regional autonomy more difficult. The crisis therefore belongs at the intersection of economic, constitutional, military, and diplomatic history.

Opposition to the central government did not necessarily mean opposition to Indonesia. Many participants framed their action as a movement to correct the Republic, not to leave it, and the PRRI claimed to be a legitimate rival government rather than a secession. Regional reform demands, autonomy movements, military opposition, and separatism were different things, though they overlapped. The crisis confirmed the weakness of parliamentary institutions and shifted the balance toward the army and Sukarno, a shift examined in Chapter 180.

The Achievements and Unresolved Problems of the Early Republic

The first decade of independence was a period of institutional experimentation. Indonesia moved from a federal structure created under Dutch pressure and international negotiation to a unitary republic, and adopted a parliamentary constitution that guaranteed rights and competitive politics. It built ministries, courts, schools, universities, a national language system, and a central bank. It held national elections in 1955 that were widely regarded as orderly and competitive, and it hosted the Bandung Conference, which raised Indonesia's international standing.

The period also left serious unresolved problems. Constitutional disagreement over the basis of the state prevented adoption of a permanent constitution. Coalition cabinets were short-lived and dependent on bargaining. The economy remained reliant on a few exports, and nationalization increased public ownership without ensuring managerial capacity. Regional grievances over revenue and power produced rebellions, and Darul Islam showed that religious and local claims could produce armed opposition. The army grew into a political actor, divided internally yet increasingly assertive.

The system's weaknesses did not make its collapse inevitable. Parliamentary institutions continued to function, and the elections created a legitimacy that no earlier government had held. But the combination of fragmentation, regional revolt, economic strain, military ambition, and Sukarno's dissatisfaction with party politics made alternatives increasingly attractive to leaders who wanted a stronger executive.

By the late 1950s Sukarno was advocating a political system of 'guided' democracy and a broader executive role, and the army favored a stronger state. In 1959 Sukarno decreed a return to the 1945 constitution, ending the parliamentary period and the Constituent Assembly's work. The Guided Democracy that followed is the subject of Chapter 180.

The achievements of the early Republic can be assessed on several different levels. The territorial transformation of 1950 produced a unitary constitutional framework, but this did not mean that authority was immediately accepted everywhere. National ministries and administrative institutions gave the state a continuing existence independent of any single cabinet. Education and language policy supported the creation of a national public, but differences in access to services remained considerable. Central banking and economic planning expanded the state's ability to formulate policy, without eliminating dependence on overseas markets. These distinctions help explain why an institution can be a genuine historical achievement even when the problems it was designed to address persist.

The democratic achievements deserve similar care. The 1955 elections demonstrated that a newly independent archipelago could organize national political competition on a large scale. Parties gave citizens ways to participate in debates about religion, land, labor, development, and the state's international position. The Constituent Assembly provided a forum for serious constitutional deliberation. Neither the elections nor the Assembly, however, could settle disputes in which actors disagreed about the legitimacy of existing procedures or could draw on coercive power outside representative institutions. A parliamentary government required not only agreement among parties but also a relationship with military and regional authorities that made civilian decisions effective.

The constitutional crisis took place alongside economic and security emergencies rather than in isolation. Sukarno's proposals for a different system of democracy in 1957 gained influence as party coalitions struggled and regional opposition grew. Martial law enlarged the political position of the armed forces. The inability to agree on a permanent constitution made the return to the earlier constitutional text attractive to those seeking a stronger presidency, while other political actors defended the elected Assembly's mandate. On 5 July 1959 Sukarno issued the decree restoring the 1945 Constitution and dissolving the Constituent Assembly. The decree ended the provisional constitutional framework; Djuanda's cabinet was replaced on 10 July. Chapter 180 examines the political system that developed from this transition in greater detail.

Historians should avoid assessing the whole decade solely through knowledge of its endpoint. The fact that parliamentary rule ended does not prove that every earlier constitutional decision was mistaken. Federalism, decentralization, coalition bargaining, and the search for a permanent constitution were competing attempts to solve real political problems. Alternative outcomes were possible, and the conditions under which those alternatives became less viable need explanation. Conversely, admiration for the electoral achievements should not obscure the difficulties encountered by citizens facing economic inequality, coercion, or conflict. The significance of this decade lies in the coexistence of durable state-building and unresolved disputes about who should exercise power. It established the national institutional setting within which subsequent Indonesian politics would develop.

The early Republic was therefore neither a complete institutional failure nor an unqualified success. It created durable national institutions, a common language, and a legitimate electoral precedent, while leaving the distribution of political authority among parliament, president, army, and regions unresolved.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Revolutionary Independence to the Consolidated Nation-State

What Changed

  • A revolutionary movement became a state with ministries, courts, schools, and elected institutions
  • The political system moved from federal to unitary, and from revolutionary leadership to competitive party politics
  • The idea of national territory broadened to include the waters between the islands

What Remained Continuous

  • Java remained the demographic and political center, and the outer islands the export earners
  • Colonial-era economic dependence and legal inheritance persisted in modified form
  • Sukarno's prestige and the army's revolutionary self-image continued to shape politics

When and how: Change occurred across the decade, with 1950 (unitary state), 1955 (elections), and 1957-1959 (crisis and transition) as turning points.

Who experienced change most directly: Officials, politicians, and urban students experienced institutional change most directly; many villagers saw more continuity in daily life.

Why the pattern matters: The pattern shows that building a nation-state is a long process and that the achievements and failures of the 1950s were intertwined.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the achievement of independence did not resolve the problem of building a state. The Round Table settlement of 1949 conferred sovereignty on a country that lacked a unified administration, a stable economy, and a settled constitutional order. The first decade of independence was spent constructing these institutions under heavy constraints. The federal government and the revolutionary Republic had inherited different administrations, financial arrangements, and military institutions. The new state needed to reconcile them while establishing effective authority in regions with widely differing resources. Revolutionary legitimacy supplied an important foundation, but the capacity to administer law, deliver public services, and collect revenue required sustained institutional development.

The federal system created in 1949 lasted only months. It combined the revolutionary Republic with Dutch-sponsored states and territories of different kinds, some with genuine local support and some dependent on Dutch backing. Nationalist pressure, security crises, and negotiation produced a unitary republic in August 1950. The result was achieved through integration, but the question of how to accommodate regional diversity remained. The Natsir Integral Motion and agreement of May 1950 supplied a parliamentary and constitutional mechanism for integration. The South Moluccan episode showed that this process was not accepted everywhere. The constitutional question of federalism was distinct from the continuing practical question of decentralization within a unitary state.

The Provisional Constitution of 1950 established parliamentary government with a cabinet responsible to the legislature and a president with a largely representative role. It allowed rapid organization of the state and left foundational questions to a Constituent Assembly. Its provisional character brought both flexibility and instability, and it set Sukarno's great informal authority against his limited formal power. The system placed ministerial responsibility at the center of government and anticipated a permanent constitution. Its rights provisions were significant, but their practical application depended on legal remedies and civilian institutions. The Constituent Assembly possessed a separate mandate from the DPR.

The state was built by adapting colonial institutions. Ministries, courts, tax offices, and regional administration were retained and redirected toward national purposes, while Indonesianization replaced Dutch officials faster than trained replacements became available. Administrative capacity improved but remained uneven, and appointments were increasingly shaped by political affiliation. Regional government reform sought to address demands for local authority. Training officials and establishing dependable administrative procedures took much longer than promulgating laws. The development of a national civil service helped preserve institutional continuity even when cabinets changed.

Political life was competitive and pluralistic. The PNI, Masyumi, NU, and PKI, with smaller parties, represented different social and religious constituencies. Coalition cabinets rose and fell, reflecting party fragmentation, civil-military tension, regional pressures, and economic constraints, though they should not be treated as proof that parliamentary institutions could not function. Political parties operated through social organizations as well as parliament. Their links to religious associations, unions, educational institutions and regional networks made participation meaningful for many citizens. Those same relationships contributed to conflicts over ministries, employment, and public resources.

The 1955 elections were the democratic high point of the decade. They gave the legislature and the Constituent Assembly a popular mandate and were widely seen as orderly and competitive. They did not produce a majority party or resolve the debate over whether the state should rest on Pancasila or on Islam, a dispute that was more complex than a simple religious-secular divide. The separate legislative and constitutional elections demonstrated that effective election administration could be achieved despite difficult geography. Their results produced no majority party, making coalition government necessary. A mandate to elect representatives did not, by itself, guarantee constitutional consensus or civilian control of the armed forces.

Integration of the archipelago involved geography, citizenship, language, and sovereignty. Regional diversity in economic resources, administrative experience, and political culture created strains that did not make integration impossible. Citizenship law responded to colonial legal categories and to the situation of Chinese Indonesians and others, while Bahasa Indonesia, schooling, and broadcasting fostered a national public. The Djuanda Declaration asserted an archipelagic concept of territory later recognized in international law. The Djuanda Declaration was especially important because it made the relationship between islands and the surrounding seas a question of national legal organization. Its archipelagic claim required further legislation and international negotiations. Citizenship, language, and regional institutions were similarly part of building an integrated national community without eliminating its diversity.

Economic independence lagged behind political independence. The export-oriented economy, foreign ownership, and debt obligations constrained policy. Economic nationalism, including the Benteng program, central banking, and the takeovers of 1957 and legal nationalization in 1958, pursued Indonesian ownership, with mixed results. Outer-island resentment over revenue grew, while ordinary Indonesians experienced independence unevenly according to region, class, and gender. The Benteng Program and the establishment of Bank Indonesia addressed different obstacles to economic sovereignty. The former sought to enlarge Indonesian entrepreneurial participation, while the latter created a national monetary institution. The takeovers of Dutch businesses in 1957 and the nationalization law of 1958 expanded state ownership, but raised difficult questions about management, finance, and institutional accountability.

The armed forces, formed in the revolution, became a significant political actor. Their internal divisions were revealed in the confrontation of 1952, and their role expanded with martial law in 1957. Darul Islam and the PRRI and Permesta movements challenged central authority for different reasons and in different ways, and they show that regional autonomy, religious politics, military grievance, and separatism were distinct, if overlapping, forces. The Darul Islam movements had different regional origins, while PRRI and Permesta arose from demands concerning fiscal distribution, regional autonomy, military organization, and national politics. The central response included coercion and, in the Aceh case, negotiated accommodation. Foreign intervention complicated regional conflict without explaining away its Indonesian origins.

Internationally, Indonesia pursued an independent and active foreign policy, entered the United Nations, and hosted the Bandung Conference, an important moment in Asian-African solidarity. Diplomatic success supported state legitimacy, while the unresolved status of Western New Guinea fueled nationalist frustration. International membership and the Bandung Conference demonstrated how Indonesia could act as an independent diplomatic participant rather than simply accept external strategic priorities. Its independent and active foreign policy was not equivalent to disengagement. The Republic nevertheless struggled to secure a settlement of the Western New Guinea question.

By 1959 the parliamentary period was ending. The early Republic had created durable institutions and an electoral precedent but left unresolved the distribution of authority among parliament, president, army, and regions. The transition to Guided Democracy was shaped by these conditions, but was not inevitable, and it is examined in Chapter 180. The decree of 5 July 1959 ended the provisional constitutional framework and dissolved the Constituent Assembly. This outcome reflected political choices made amid mounting pressures, not a predetermined consequence of independence or cultural diversity. The achievements and weaknesses of the earlier Republic remain essential for explaining the political transformations that followed.

Chapter Reflection

The first decade of Indonesian independence shows the difference between decolonization and state-building. Independence transferred political authority, but a durable nation required institutions capable of administering territory, accommodating diversity, managing economic resources, and resolving disagreement peacefully. The distinction is useful for comparative Southeast Asian history because many newly independent governments inherited institutions whose original purposes differed from the purposes of representative national government.

The early Republic demonstrated considerable democratic and administrative achievement. It built a unitary state from a federal compromise, held competitive national elections, extended the use of a national language already championed by nationalists, and gave a new meaning to the sea as part of national territory. These were real accomplishments, achieved with limited experience and resources. The achievement was collective: officials, teachers, voters, religious leaders, workers and regional communities all helped make national institutions operate, often with limited resources.

It also confronted persistent structural constraints. An export-oriented economy, uneven geography, a divided armed force, and unresolved disagreements over the basis of the state limited what parliamentary governments could accomplish. Regional and religious challenges tested the Republic's claim to authority. The effects of these constraints were shaped by decisions about public spending, political participation and military authority. They were not simply the unavoidable results of archipelagic geography.

These unresolved problems helped create the conditions in which Sukarno and the army sought a different political system. The later emergence of Guided Democracy was not inevitable; alternatives existed, and the choices of 1957-1959 were shaped by contingency as well as structure. Understanding the early Republic on its own terms is the necessary first step to understanding what followed in Chapter 180. The transition is therefore best studied as a change in the distribution of political authority rather than as a simple replacement of one set of officeholders with another.

The chapter also shows that Southeast Asian societies were historical actors in decolonization, not recipients of a finished independence. Indonesian leaders, officials, soldiers, and citizens adapted inherited institutions, argued over the shape of the nation, and made choices whose consequences are still debated. The early Republic provides an example of the creative and contested institutional work that followed anticolonial mobilization.
