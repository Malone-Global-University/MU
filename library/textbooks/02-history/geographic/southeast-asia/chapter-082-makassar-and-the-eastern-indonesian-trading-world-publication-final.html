Makassar and the Eastern Indonesian Trading World

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

In the mid-seventeenth century, a port on the southwestern coast of Sulawesi welcomed Malay Muslims, Portuguese Catholics, English and Danish factors, Chinese traders, and merchants from Java, Borneo, and the Lesser Sunda Islands, while the Dutch East India Company tried to close the spice trade to all of them. Its rulers were neither simple free traders nor simple tyrants. They governed a commercial city and a rice-producing hinterland, and they ruled neighboring peoples by force and alliance. Their defeat in 1667–1669 was the work of an alliance of Southeast Asian rivals as much as of Dutch guns.

The port was Makassar, and the political partnership behind it was that of Gowa and Talloq, two neighboring kingdoms of South Sulawesi. In the first half of the seventeenth century, Makassar became one of the principal ports of the eastern archipelago. Cloves from Maluku, nutmeg and mace from Banda, sandalwood from the Lesser Sunda Islands, rice from South Sulawesi, and Indian cloth carried by merchants from the west all passed through its harbor. Its rulers had accepted Islam in 1605, and for several decades they presided over a court in which Arabic learning, Malay trading law, European mathematics, and local Makassarese political tradition coexisted.

Makassar's prominence rested on a combination of factors. Gowa possessed agricultural land, manpower, and military organization. Talloq, at the mouth of its river, supplied commercial and administrative leadership. Both held the harbors on which long-distance shipping depended. Around them lay other South Sulawesi kingdoms, including Bone, Wajo, Soppeng, and Luwu, which were politically distinct, often resentful of Gowa's power, and bound to it by obligations that varied over time. Gowa and Talloq were therefore at once a commercial entrepôt and a regional power, and the second role was as important to the story of the first as the first was to the second.

This chapter follows that history from the sixteenth-century expansion of Gowa through the conversion of its rulers, the growth of Makassar as an international port, and the cosmopolitan court of the 1630s and 1640s, to the war of 1666–1669. That war is often told as a contest between Dutch monopoly and Makassarese commercial freedom. The evidence supports a more complicated account. Makassar's openness to many merchants was real and attracted traders excluded elsewhere. But its rulers also taxed, conscripted labor, and enslaved, and they subordinated neighboring kingdoms. The Company's expedition under Cornelis Speelman succeeded because it joined forces with people who had their own grievances against Gowa, most prominently the Bugis nobleman Arung Palakka of Bone.

The chapter draws on several kinds of evidence. Court chronicles from Gowa and Talloq, edited and translated by William Cummings, preserve a Makassarese view of royal genealogy, expansion, and legitimate kingship. The Makassar annals, kept from the 1630s, record dated events in a different register. European sources include Company correspondence, diaries, treaties, and drawings, such as the bird's-eye view of Makassar made by Johannes Vingboons around 1665. Leonard Andaya's work on Arung Palakka and the war, Christian Pelras's work on the Bugis, and the work of Heather Sutherland and Ryan Holroyd on Makassar's trade provide the principal modern interpretations. Each source type reflects the interests of its makers, and the chapter asks students to read them with that in mind.

This chapter connects to its neighbors in the textbook as follows. Chapters 77 through 79 treated the creation of the Dutch East India Company, the conquest of Banda, and Batavia; this chapter assumes that background and does not repeat it. Chapter 81 examined the Malay successor world and the Bugis role in Johor-Riau in the early eighteenth century; here the South Sulawesi diaspora is examined only as an aftermath of the war. Chapter 83 turns to Mataram and the contest for Java, where the consequences of the Makassar War would also be felt.

Finally, the chapter asks what Makassar's history reveals about maritime power in early modern Southeast Asia. Control of a port could rest on openness to outsiders and on coercion of neighbors at once. Commercial success could generate political enemies. A European company's victory could depend on indigenous allies whose interests it did not share. And the defeat of a state did not end the commercial skills of its people, who carried their networks across the archipelago.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the geography of South Sulawesi and explain how its rice plains, rivers, anchorages, and position on maritime routes shaped the rise of Gowa and Talloq.
  2. Explain the institutional partnership of Gowa and Talloq, distinguishing the two realms and their offices rather than treating them as one centralized government.
  3. Distinguish a ruler's conversion, the adoption of Islam as the court's religion, military submission of neighboring realms, and the longer process of religious change in local communities.
  4. Analyze Makassar's role as an entrepôt, separating goods produced in South Sulawesi from goods redistributed from elsewhere.
  5. Compare the roles of Malay, Portuguese, Chinese, English, Indian, and other merchant communities and the conditions that drew them to Makassar.
  6. Reconstruct the social basis of Makassar's prosperity in farming, fishing, shipbuilding, textile work, households, and dependent and enslaved labor.
  7. Assess the sources of tension between Gowa and the kingdoms of Bone, Wajo, and Soppeng, and explain why these tensions mattered to the outcome of the Makassar War.
  8. Evaluate why Makassar's open-port policy brought it into conflict with the VOC's monopoly ambitions without treating the port as wholly unrestricted.
  9. Trace the course of the war of 1666–1669, distinguishing the Treaty of Bungaya of 1667 from the fall of Somba Opu in 1669.
  10. Interpret the roles of Sultan Hasanuddin, Arung Palakka, and Cornelis Speelman, avoiding reduction of the war to Dutch versus Bugis versus Makassarese blocs.
  11. Explain the colonial reorganization of South Sulawesi, the displacement of its peoples, and the continuity of its maritime commerce after 1669.
  12. Evaluate competing interpretations of the war as a commercial monopoly war, a conflict among Southeast Asian states, a struggle over sovereignty, or a combination.

Historical Context

By the late sixteenth century, the Indonesian archipelago was linked by trade that long predated European arrival, as earlier chapters have shown. Cloves grew only in the Maluku islands, nutmeg and mace only in Banda, and these spices moved west through ports on Java, Sumatra, and the Malay Peninsula. Chapter 69 treated the Portuguese capture of Melaka in 1511, which dispersed Malay and Asian merchants to other ports, and Chapter 71 treated the sultanates of Ternate and Tidore. Chapters 77 through 79 followed the Dutch East India Company (the VOC) as it sought to control the spice trade by force, by contract, and by fortification, conquering Banda, founding Batavia in 1619, and attempting to confine clove purchases to its own agents.

South Sulawesi lay outside the spice-producing zone but close to it, and it had resources the spice zone lacked. Its plains produced rice in surplus, and its coast faced the Makassar Strait, which connected Java and Borneo to the east. By the sixteenth century a number of small kingdoms existed on the peninsula, some Makassarese-speaking in the southwest, others Bugis-speaking in the center and east. Their political organization rested on aristocratic lineages, ranked status, and obligations of followers to lords. Chronicles, genealogies, and traditions of the lontaraq type recorded these arrangements. Bugis and Makassarese were related but distinct languages, and the identities attached to them did not form fixed political blocs. Marriage alliances, shifting loyalties, and rivalries cut across them.

Two cautions guide the chapter. First, the "Makassar" of the seventeenth century was a port and a political center, not a nation. The ruling houses of Gowa and Talloq governed a Makassarese-speaking core and claimed influence over many others, but the polity included people of diverse origins, and many of its subjects did not choose its rule. Second, a "Dutch monopoly" was an aspiration, often resisted and unevenly enforced. Chapters 78 and 79 showed the violence with which the Company pursued it where it had power, but its reach elsewhere was limited by distance, shipping, and the willingness of local rulers to sell to others. Makassar was one of the places where the limits of Company power were most visible, and this is why it became a Company target.

Division I · Section 82.1

South Sulawesi and the Geography of the Eastern Maritime World

The southern peninsula of Sulawesi extends toward the Flores Sea, with the Makassar Strait to its west and the Gulf of Bone to its east. From the peninsula's coast, seaborne routes ran north along the Strait toward Borneo, west to Java, south and east through the Lesser Sunda Islands to Bima, Sumbawa, and Timor, and east to Buton, Ambon, Seram, Banda, and the clove islands of Maluku. Monsoon winds governed the timing of voyages, as Chapter 4 explained, but wind alone did not fix routes: ports, provisioning, political protection, and the availability of cargo mattered as much. A location on the southwestern coast was therefore well placed to serve as a meeting point for shipping from the west and the spice-producing east, though location alone did not make a port.

The southwestern coastal plain was also productive land. The Jeneberang River, which reaches the sea near Makassar, and the neighboring Talloq river, with others to the north, supplied water to wet-rice fields. Surpluses of rice were available to feed a population larger than that of most agricultural settlements, to supply ships' provisions, and to be sold in regions that did not grow enough grain. Fishing, salt, and coastal shipbuilding added to local resources. The Jeneberang's mouth and nearby anchorages offered sheltered harbors, though the shoreline shifted, and historians must be cautious about reading the modern coastline back into the seventeenth century.

The peninsula was not politically unified. Gowa, Talloq, and several smaller Makassarese-speaking polities lay in the southwest. Bone, the leading Bugis kingdom, lay to the east, along the Gulf of Bone; Wajo and Soppeng lay inland to the north and east, and Luwu, often regarded in tradition as an old and prestigious kingdom, lay at the head of the Gulf. These realms were organized around aristocratic houses, with ranked status, retinues, and agricultural estates. Their relations included marriage, treaty, rivalry, and war. Some, like Wajo, had distinctive institutions in which merchants and shipowners played a visible role. The idea of a unified "South Sulawesi state" does not describe the seventeenth-century peninsula, and the chapter uses "South Sulawesi" as a geographic label rather than a political one.

The combination of agricultural capacity and maritime position gave the region its importance, but historians avoid determinism. Many coasts have rice and a strait; few produced Makassar. What made the difference was the way the ruling houses of Gowa and Talloq used the opportunities: through military organization, alliances, administrative arrangements, and policies toward merchants, as the following sections show. Geography supplied conditions, and political choices turned them into a port and a state.

A useful way to understand the region is to follow a sack of rice and a basket of cloves in opposite directions. Grain harvested in the southwestern plain could be carried by small craft to a coastal market, purchased for an oceangoing vessel, and transported toward islands where volcanic soils, labor requirements, or military disturbances limited food supplies. Cloves grown much farther east might pass through several carriers before reaching Makassar, where a merchant could exchange them for textiles or silver and arrange onward passage to ports outside VOC control. Neither journey required one ruler to command the entire route. It required successive agreements at landing places, protection from seizure, information about prices, and enough food and water to keep crews moving.

The contrast between western and eastern coasts of the peninsula also mattered. The Makassar Strait side offered approaches toward Borneo and Java, while the Gulf of Bone opened toward Bugis settlements and different channels to the eastern seas. Mountain ranges and difficult inland routes did not prevent communication, but they made access to waterways and lowland corridors valuable. Political influence could spread unevenly: a coastal court might command an estuary and the fields immediately behind it while holding more distant communities through alliances, tribute, or occasional campaigns. Historical atlases often simplify those relationships into broad areas of color. A more accurate classroom map would layer probable ports, river systems, cultivated plains, contested frontiers, and specific recorded campaigns.

Modern Makassar and South Sulawesi provide points of orientation, not direct replicas of seventeenth-century geography. Sediment, shoreline modification, and later urban development altered river mouths and settlements. The principal fortifications and anchorages must therefore be read through archaeological observations and period views as well as through maps made centuries afterward. Geography helps explain why Gowa and Talloq invested in coastal defenses, but it cannot tell us which marriages held a partnership together or which merchants trusted a particular ruler. Those are historical questions requiring chronicles, annals, company correspondence, and comparative evidence.

Division I · Section 82.2

The Expansion of Gowa and the Political World before 1600

Knowledge of Gowa before the seventeenth century comes mostly from the court chronicles of Gowa and Talloq, which were compiled after the events they describe and are organized around genealogies of rulers. Archaeological work and Portuguese references supplement them for the sixteenth century. For earlier periods, historians must use chronicle dates as approximate indicators of sequence rather than precise chronology. What the sources support is a pattern: Gowa began as one of several agricultural lordships in the southwestern plain and, over perhaps a century, became the leading power on that coast.

Chronicle tradition associates the early phase of this growth with Tumapa'risi Kallonna, whose reign is placed in the early sixteenth century. The traditions credit him with defeating rivals nearer the coast, securing the access of Gowa to the sea, and establishing a political relationship with Talloq that ended earlier hostility. They also associate his reign with the fortification of Somba Opu, which became Gowa's principal coastal stronghold. These accounts present the founding of institutions in a manner typical of royal chronicles, in which a single ruler is credited with changes that in fact took generations. Historians therefore treat his reign as a symbol of the transition and not as a documented set of acts.

The reign of Tunipalangga, placed from the mid-1540s to the mid-1560s, is better characterized in the chronicles and appears as a period of intensive expansion. The texts describe campaigns against neighboring communities, including coastal ones, and measures of military and administrative organization. They also credit him with commercial measures, including regularizing weights and measures and appointing officials concerned with harbors and foreign merchants, and with encouraging the settlement of trading communities. Because the chronicles tend to attribute innovations to named rulers, the details deserve caution. The general conclusion that the middle of the sixteenth century saw Gowa organize its military and commercial affairs more systematically is nevertheless widely accepted.

Several elements supported this expansion. Agricultural land in the plain fed warriors and laborers, and the obligations of subordinate lords supplied troops on campaign. Fortifications, including brick or earthen walls that could be defended with firearms, made conquest of the coast harder for rivals and made Gowa's own conquests more durable. Alliances with Talloq and other neighbors, secured by marriage, protected the flanks. And control of the coastal approaches gave the rulers access to revenue from shipping and the goods that visiting merchants brought. These elements reinforced one another, but none made the outcome inevitable; the chronicles themselves record setbacks and rivalries.

Students should distinguish the growth of state power from the flourishing of Makassar as an international port. By the time of Tunipalangga's successors, Gowa was a strong regional kingdom with a coastal base, but Portuguese and Malay merchants visited in modest numbers and the port was one among several in eastern waters. The great expansion of trade belonged to the decades after 1600, as new circumstances described in later sections, including Company pressure on competing ports, drew traders to Makassar. Earlier state-building created the capacity to take advantage of that opportunity, and it was not itself the commercial boom.

The Gowa chronicles offer a more concrete political sequence than a simple story of expansion from an inland kingdom to a port. Cummings identifies conflict in the 1530s between Gowa and a coalition involving Talloq, Maros, and Polombangkeng. Gowa's success improved its access to agricultural manpower and to the commercial approaches controlled by Talloq. A partnership that later authors described as natural was thus also an outcome of competition. It was maintained because the senior houses found shared government and the division of responsibilities more useful than a permanent attempt by one to extinguish the other. The distinction matters: the celebrated alliance was made and remade by political decisions, not inherited as an unchanging constitutional formula.

A later episode, conventionally placed around 1561, reveals the commercial dimension of this rise. The Gowa chronicle describes Malay merchants approaching Tunipalangga and negotiating the conditions under which they might reside in the realm. Rather than treating the arrival of foreigners as an accidental by-product of geography, the narrative attributes a deliberate policy of accommodation to the ruler. The account should not be accepted as a verbatim transcript of negotiations: it is a retrospectively written royal narrative. Yet it illuminates a broader mechanism by which ports grew, namely the granting of a place to dwell, arrangements for protection, and expectations about lawful conduct under a ruler's authority.

Weapons and specialists were part of the same process. The chronicles credit sixteenth-century rulers with encouraging ironworkers, builders, shipwrights, ropemakers, and makers of military equipment. These trades connected rice-producing territory to fortified coastlines. Artillery was useful only if cannon could be maintained, ammunition supplied, and crews trained; ships were effective only if carpenters, rope makers, navigators, and laborers could keep them in service. The court's ability to command specialist skills reinforced its military standing, while the commercial profits of a harbor helped acquire materials not available locally.

Growth was neither uninterrupted nor universally welcomed. The short, turbulent reign of Tunipasuluq in the early 1590s, followed by his deposition, demonstrated that ambitious royal demands could alienate nobles and merchants. Royal chronicles often cast an unpopular predecessor as a lesson in bad government, and historians should consider how such judgments justified his successors. Even so, the episode points to a structural limit on centralization: control over resources and people depended on cooperation from those capable of resisting a court's orders.

Division I · Section 82.3

The Gowa–Talloq Partnership: Kingship, Court Government, and Political Authority

Gowa and Talloq were separate kingdoms with separate ruling houses and territories. Yet by the later sixteenth century they operated as a partnership in which the rulers shared foreign policy, military campaigns, and the management of the port. The relationship is expressed in a traditional formula usually rendered "two rulers, one people." The formula should be read as an ideal of unity at the top and not as proof that the two realms were administered identically. Each retained its own nobility, lands, councils, and customary law, and the balance of influence between them varied by generation.

The ruler of Gowa held the title sombaya, "the one to whom homage is paid," and was often called by the title karaeng, a general term for a lord. The ruler of Talloq was also a karaeng. A central office tied the partnership together: the tumabicara-butta, "the one who speaks for the realm," often rendered as chief minister or spokesperson. In the period of Gowa's greatest power, the office was held by leaders of Talloq's ruling house, who exercised substantial influence over diplomacy, trade, and the reception of foreign envoys. The arrangement meant that Talloq's rulers had a major role in Gowa's foreign relations, while Gowa's rulers held the larger territory and the more prestigious title.

Below the rulers stood councils of nobles who held hereditary positions, often associated with particular territories. The chronicles describe a council of senior nobles that took part in royal succession and could influence the choice among eligible heirs. Princes and princesses of the two houses married to bind alliances, and high-ranking marriages with ruling families of subordinate realms extended influence outward. The court also appointed military commanders and officials with responsibility for specific tasks such as harbor affairs, taxation, and relations with foreign communities. Subordinate communities owed tribute and service, but the extent to which the center could enforce these obligations varied with distance and with the strength of each local lord.

The partnership was not free of tension. Chronicle narratives record disputes over succession and conflicts between the houses. Yet its durability, which lasted through the conversion to Islam, the growth of Makassar, and the war, indicates that both houses benefited. For Gowa, Talloq provided commercial skill, administrative continuity, and a harbor; for Talloq, the arrangement offered protection and a share in a larger enterprise. Historians have been careful not to treat the two realms as a single centralized state with uniform government, since the evidence points to negotiated authority within and between aristocratic houses.

The political language of the partnership obscures important questions about what government actually did. Decisions about war, reception of envoys, and protection of merchants could be discussed by a relatively small elite, but orders had to travel through local authorities who controlled land, households, and fighting men. A royal summons did not automatically produce an army. Nobles weighed their obligations against harvests, rival claims, and local threats; rulers reinforced obedience by distributing honors, arranging marriages, and threatening military penalties. This was a structured political world, not simply a collection of personal friendships, yet it differed from a modern bureaucracy staffed by salaried officials.

The office of tumabicara-butta is particularly instructive because its conventional English rendering as 'prime minister' can suggest powers too fixed or too uniform. In practice, its holders acted within a hierarchy of aristocratic houses, with authority shaped by court relationships, prestige, experience, and military success. A Talloq ruler who helped manage Gowa's diplomacy did not thereby erase Talloq's separate royal household. The partnership could coordinate foreign relations while leaving different communities with distinctive customary obligations. This makes it important to ask both who spoke for the coalition and who actually supplied the personnel and food needed to carry out a decision.

Succession made the arrangement vulnerable. A new ruler might be a minor, or a claimant might have influential relatives in another branch of the royal family. Marriage could reduce tensions, but it could also connect a succession dispute to powerful neighboring houses. Court chronicles remembered genealogies in detail because descent established eligibility and rank. Those genealogies were also political arguments, intended to explain why some people could rule and others owed allegiance. Comparing the Gowa and Talloq narratives allows students to ask whose ancestry receives attention and whose service disappears behind the language of royal achievement.

The partnership's longevity helped make Makassar a credible commercial center. Traders needed to know that agreements would survive a voyage lasting many months, that an official's promise had backing from influential nobles, and that disputes among court factions would not routinely threaten cargo. Political stability was therefore a commercial asset. It did not eliminate arbitrary decisions or the threat of seizure, but a court capable of coordinating several powerholders could offer merchants more predictable terms than a divided port. The very interdependence of Gowa and Talloq that complicated government also provided a wider base of political support.

Historical Vocabulary
Karaeng, Sombaya, and Tumabicara-butta

Definition. Karaeng is a Makassarese title of nobility meaning "lord," borne by rulers and many high nobles. Sombaya ("the one to whom homage is rendered") designated the supreme ruler of Gowa. Tumabicara-butta, roughly "the one who speaks for the land," designated the principal minister of the partnership, a post held in this period by leading members of the Talloq house.

Historical context. These were titles in an aristocratic system in which rank was inherited, differentiated by lineage, and often tied to specific territories and ritual duties.

Why it matters. The titles show that political power was shared between two ruling houses and that the offices were more than ceremonial: the tumabicara-butta often directed diplomacy and trade.

Potential misunderstanding. English glosses such as "king," "prime minister," or "chancellor" make the offices sound like those of a European monarchy. They were not; they were positions within a hierarchy of ranked houses and negotiated obligations.

Division I · Section 82.4

Islamization, Sultan Alauddin, and the Transformation of South Sulawesi, 1605–1611

Muslim merchants were present in Makassar before the court accepted Islam. Malay traders from the Peninsula, Sumatra, and Borneo, and Javanese and other merchants, had long visited and settled, and their communities had mosques or prayer places and legal practices of their own. Gowa's rulers tolerated and sometimes protected them, since they brought trade and revenue. Their presence meant that the court knew Islam before it embraced it, and it also meant that the rulers' decision was a political and religious choice made in a setting in which several religious communities, including Portuguese Catholics, already coexisted.

The decisive moment came in 1605, when Karaeng Matoaya, the Talloq leader who served as principal minister, and Sultan Alauddin, who had come to the throne of Gowa as a child in 1593, accepted Islam. Tradition and later chronicles associate the event with Muslim teachers who came from Sumatra, often identified as Minangkabau scholars, who instructed the rulers. Two years later, in 1607, formal communal Friday prayers were established, marking the public institutionalization of Islam at court. The rulers adopted the title of sultan, took Muslim names, and patronized teachers and mosques. The conversion also connected the court to the wider Muslim world, giving it a basis for diplomacy with Muslim rulers and merchants across the archipelago.

The motives for conversion are not documented in detail, and historians infer them from context. Religious conviction is not excluded, but the rulers also had political reasons: Islam linked Makassar to the networks of Muslim trade, supplied a vocabulary of legitimate kingship, and could be used to justify campaigns against neighboring realms. Some neighbors, notably Luwu, are reported by tradition to have accepted Islam earlier, and the reported order of conversions is itself debated. What is clear is that conversion in Gowa and Talloq was initiated at the top and that political and religious considerations were intertwined.

The next phase involved the campaigns known as the Islamic Wars. Between roughly 1608 and 1611, Gowa's forces moved against neighboring realms, demanding that they accept Islam and also acknowledge Gowa's political superiority. Soppeng, Wajo, and Bone submitted in turn. The accounts describe this as a religious summons, but the outcomes were political: rulers acknowledged subordination, agreed to obligations, and in several cases accepted Islam in form. These campaigns are an important context for later events, since they left the neighboring realms bound to Gowa by compulsion and with memories of defeat.

Students should keep four things distinct. The conversion of two rulers in 1605 was an individual religious and political act. The establishment of Islam as the religion of the court in 1607 was an institutional step. The military submission of neighboring realms in 1608–1611 was a political settlement that included religious demands. And the transformation of belief and practice in villages and households was a much longer process that continued for generations, varying by community, with older ritual practices continuing alongside new ones in many places. The decisions of rulers did not mean that every subject became a practicing Muslim, and the sources cannot tell us how quickly local religious life changed.

Religious change did not proceed in a single direction from an Islamic court to a uniformly Muslim countryside. Teachers, local ritual specialists, aristocratic families, and village communities faced different choices about practice and allegiance. Islamic rites could become markers of official status while older customs concerning ancestry, land, and ceremonial obligation remained influential. A ruler's decision to sponsor mosques and scholars altered the opportunities for learning and adjudication, but the speed at which ordinary households adopted new practices varied. Historians reconstruct this uneven process through chronicles and later traditions rather than from a continuous census of religious behavior.

The campaigns commonly called the Islamic Wars also require a political reading. When Gowa called on neighboring courts to embrace Islam, it did so from a position of military strength and with expectations about political submission. Neighboring leaders did not respond as isolated individuals considering religious propositions; they governed kingdoms with their own treaties, noble factions, and memories of earlier war. Acceptance of Islam under pressure could preserve some local institutions while acknowledging Gowa's superiority. Resistance could express attachment to established ritual, opposition to domination, or both. Treating the campaigns as either pure missionary activity or purely opportunistic conquest erases the combination visible in the sources.

The incorporation of Soppeng, Wajo, and Bone altered the balance of power in ways that lasted well beyond 1611. Submission created expectations about military support, tribute, and the public standing of rulers. Those expectations could be challenged as circumstances changed. The religious vocabulary of a shared Muslim political world did not remove competition over independence, seniority, or labor obligations. Indeed, the later opposition of Bone reveals how an order established through victory could remain contested for decades.

Conversion also offered the Makassarese court new diplomatic resources. Muslim merchant networks connected Makassar with the Malay Peninsula, western Indonesia, and Indian Ocean ports where written agreements, scholars, and religious prestige circulated together with commodities. Yet the court's Muslim identity did not require the exclusion of Portuguese Catholics or other non-Muslim merchants. The rulers' willingness to accommodate different trading communities was a practical feature of a port whose prosperity depended on multiple routes, and it remained compatible with distinctly Islamic royal patronage.

Division II · Section 82.5

Somba Opu, Fortifications, and the Development of Makassar

"Makassar" in the seventeenth century referred to a cluster of settlements along the coast rather than to a single walled town. The principal fortress was Somba Opu, the royal stronghold of Gowa near the mouth of the Jeneberang River, with walls, bastions, and artillery. To the north lay the estuary of the Talloq river and Talloq's own fortified center. Other forts, including the one at Ujung Pandang, strengthened a coastal line that ran for some distance. The defensive system was linked by earthworks and by the rivers, and its purpose was to protect harbors, shipping, and the residences of the rulers against attack from sea and land.

Within and around the forts were royal compounds, residences of nobles, markets, warehouses, and quarters of foreign merchants. European visitors described a bustling waterfront with many vessels, and mosques and other places of worship for the several communities. Foreign merchants lived in designated areas but were not confined to them, and the Company's and English factories operated as trading houses. Houses were largely of timber, raised on posts in the local fashion, with some brick or stone buildings for rulers and fortifications. Contemporary descriptions of the size of the population vary widely, and the chapter does not offer a figure: estimates depend on visitors' impressions and on assumptions about the surrounding settlements.

The city grew along with the territorial and commercial expansion of Gowa and Talloq. The earlier fortification of Somba Opu, the construction of additional forts, and the appointment of officials for harbor administration gave a framework. As the volume of shipping increased after 1600, merchants needed warehouses, credit, interpreters, and legal protection, and the court provided them in exchange for fees and customs duties. The rulers also used the port as a source of weapons and supplies, since foreign traders brought firearms and cannon and local metalworkers produced and repaired them.

Comparison with other ports clarifies what was distinctive. Melaka under its sultans, and later under Portuguese rule, was a fortified entrepôt with a strong harbor administration. Johor and Banten both received merchants and spices, and Banten's pepper trade made it a rival of Batavia. Batavia itself was a Company-controlled city whose Asian residents lived under Company law. Makassar differed from these in important respects: it was ruled by indigenous kings who were also leaders of a regional power; it was not a Company possession; and it was supplied by a rice-growing hinterland close at hand. The comparison should not be pressed too far, since each city had distinct institutions and histories.

The Vingboons drawing of about 1665 is the best-known visual source for the city. It shows the royal fort, buildings, vessels, and landscape, and it is useful for understanding the arrangement of the fortifications. But it is the work of a European artist, probably drawing on reports and surveys, produced for Dutch readers at a time when the Company was preparing for conflict. It emphasizes structures of military interest and says little about the people who lived and worked there.

A port complex should be understood through its working spaces, not just its walls. Vessels entering Makassar needed an anchorage, access to fresh water and rice, people willing to unload goods, and intermediaries capable of arranging prices and payments. A trader who had spent weeks crossing difficult seas might prefer to wait for the next monsoon rather than risk an immediate return. Such waiting created demand for lodging, storage, provisions, repairs, translation, and credit. The waterfront consequently connected the households of sailors and port workers with the palaces of rulers and the establishments of foreign merchants. Economic life occupied many locations rather than one marketplace inside the fortress.

Fortifications were valuable in both diplomatic and military terms. A fortified royal center signaled that Gowa could protect important people and resist an assault. Yet walls could also be a point of vulnerability: an army defending Somba Opu needed grain, water, replacement weapons, and safe communications with the surrounding plain. Other coastal forts and river approaches mattered precisely because no single stronghold could secure all movement along the waterfront. The later campaign against Makassar exploited this dispersal of defenses and the possibility of attacking them from the water and from the land.

The surviving images of the port allow students to pose precise questions but not to reconstruct a complete street plan. A European bird's-eye drawing may emphasize forts, ships, conspicuous buildings, and shorelines familiar to maritime observers. It normally says far less about rental arrangements, fishing grounds, neighborhood divisions, or people moving between houses and markets. An image can also represent a city as its maker imagined it from several vantage points. Archaeological work, descriptions of waterfront life, and the changing coastline should be considered alongside drawings rather than treated as decorative confirmation.

Makassar's comparison with Batavia is therefore instructive but limited. The VOC built a company headquarters with a formal council and corporate recordkeeping, while Gowa–Talloq operated through royal households, nobles, and port officials. Both depended on Asian workers and merchants, military force, and food supplies. Their physical similarity as fortified ports should not conceal different legal claims and political priorities. The struggle between them was partly a contest over who could set the terms of movement, residence, and trade through the eastern archipelago.

Division II · Section 82.6

Spices, Rice, Textiles, and the Eastern Indonesian Trade

Makassar's economic role was that of an intermediary. Cloves came from Maluku and nutmeg and mace from Banda, and when the Company limited purchases at those sources, producers and carriers looked for other buyers. Makassar offered a market for such goods and an onward route to merchants from Java, Borneo, the Malay world, and beyond. Indian textiles, especially cotton cloth from the Coromandel coast and Gujarat, came from the west and served as a principal medium of exchange in eastern ports, where people wanted cloth more than coin. Sandalwood from Timor and neighboring islands, beeswax, tortoiseshell, trepang and other marine products, and metals including iron and copper also moved through the port.

Students should distinguish what South Sulawesi produced from what it redistributed. Rice was the great local product, grown on the plain and exported to regions that grew too little. Textiles of local manufacture, salt, fish, and ships were also produced locally. Spices, sandalwood, and Indian cloth were not products of the region but were brought from elsewhere. Makassar's wealth depended on both domestic production and the carrying trade, and the two supported each other: rice supplied the provisions that ships needed, and the profits of reexport paid for imported goods that rulers redistributed to followers.

The port's connections reached many places. Ships went to Ambon and Seram and Banda, to Bima and Sumbawa, to Timor for sandalwood, to Borneo for forest products and gold, and to Java, where Makassarese and Bugis merchants were active. Inter-island shipping depended on locally owned vessels, many of them built in South Sulawesi or nearby, with crews recruited among coastal peoples, including seafaring communities such as the Bajau. Cargoes were often redistributed, and a vessel might carry goods to a port, sell part, buy others, and continue. Credit was extended among merchants and between merchants and rulers, with repayment in goods, and personal ties and reputation underlay many transactions. Provisioning mattered, since a ship that could not buy rice, water, and repair services could not continue its voyage.

The spice trade is best approached as a chain of transactions rather than as a map of commodities moving automatically between production centers and consumers. A clove grower in Maluku might sell to a local intermediary who needed rice or cloth. The intermediary might load a small vessel for a nearby island, where cargo was combined with other products and transferred to a larger carrier. Once a merchant reached Makassar, the transaction could involve price bargaining, credit, delivery against a promise, or exchange for textiles needed on a future voyage. Some cargo ultimately reached Java or the Indian Ocean, but portions could circulate within the eastern archipelago. Each transfer offered opportunities for profit, taxation, delay, and seizure.

Rice mattered at every stage. Ships required provisions for crews; coastal populations depended on food imported from productive districts; and military campaigns could disrupt harvests and routes. Gowa's rice-growing hinterland gave Makassar an advantage over some spice-producing islands whose marketable products could not by themselves feed their populations. But the court did not grow every sack of grain or move every boat. Farmers produced it, local traders assembled it, and carriers brought it to the waterfront. Royal revenue depended on these independent actors continuing to find the port useful.

Different cargoes moved under different commercial conditions. The VOC's strongest restrictions concerned high-value spices produced in geographically concentrated regions, particularly cloves and the Banda nutmeg complex. Sandalwood, wax, sea products, textiles, and locally grown grain were less susceptible to the same type of source monopoly. Merchants could respond to restrictions by changing carriers, mixing cargoes, using intermediary ports, or emphasizing products outside a company's principal interests. Such adjustments explain why the conquest of an important port could redirect exchange without destroying the wider economy that sustained it.

Prices themselves were political information. A merchant who learned that cloves fetched more elsewhere might decide to avoid a company buyer despite the risks. A ruler who promised access to competing buyers could attract ships and customs receipts. Conversely, restrictive levies or uncertainty about seizures might drive cargo to a neighboring harbor. It would be misleading to assume a stable, uniform price across these seas or to reconstruct numerical profit margins without the relevant records. What can be established is the repeated interaction among protection, coercion, information, and opportunities to bargain that made Makassar's trading policy strategically significant.

Historical Vocabulary
Entrepôt, Monopoly, and Inter-Island Trade

Definition. An entrepôt is a port whose prosperity depends on receiving, storing, and redistributing goods that come from elsewhere. A monopoly, in the VOC's usage, was an attempt to confine the purchase or sale of particular goods, especially spices, to a single buyer. Inter-island trade refers to the shipping that moved goods among the islands of the archipelago, mostly in vessels owned by Southeast Asian merchants.

Historical context. Makassar acted as an entrepôt for spices and cloth, while the VOC sought a monopoly of spices at the sources and in transit. The two systems collided because Makassar offered an outlet that the monopoly aimed to close.

Why it matters. The terms help to distinguish a port's own products from the goods it redistributed and to explain why a monopoly could be enforced at a production site more easily than in a distant port.

Potential misunderstanding. A monopoly in practice was rarely complete. "Free port" and "monopoly port" describe policies and aims, not the actual extent of control or the absence of taxes and rules.

Division II · Section 82.7

Malay, Portuguese, Chinese, English, and Other Merchant Communities

Makassar accommodated several merchant networks at once. Malay Muslim traders, many with ties to Johor, Pahang, Patani, Banjarmasin, and Sumatran ports, formed one of the largest and most influential communities, and Malay was widely used as a language of commerce and diplomacy. Their presence also made the court familiar with Malay legal and commercial practice. Portuguese and Portuguese-Asian merchants, many of them Catholics, had been present from the sixteenth century and kept a church and priests, and they traded between Makassar, Macao, Timor, and other places. English East India Company representatives established a factory in the early seventeenth century, with the 1613 date appearing in contemporary records, and Danish traders appeared for a time. Indian merchants, including Gujarati and Coromandel traders, brought textiles, and Chinese merchants settled and visited as well.

Chinese trade requires care. Chinese traders were present, and research by Ryan Holroyd and others has shown that their connections with Makassar changed over time, including some direct voyages and also commerce conducted through intermediary ports such as those on Java or the Malay world. The extent of direct junk trade at different periods should not be overstated, and historians distinguish Chinese residents living in Makassar from voyages that came from China itself. Both existed, and their proportions shifted with conditions in the China seas.

Several developments increased the number of traders. When the VOC captured Portuguese Melaka in 1641, Portuguese and Malay merchants who had depended on that port sought alternatives, and many went to Makassar. The Company's tightening of control over Maluku and its restrictions at Banten and Batavia made Makassar attractive to merchants who wished to buy spices and cloth without Company interference. And the rulers of Gowa and Talloq offered conditions that appealed: moderate customs duties, protection of persons and goods, allowance for each community's religious practice, and the use of interpreters and brokers. Merchants gained a legal setting in which disputes could be heard, and rulers gained revenue and diplomatic connections.

The result was a cosmopolitan port, but it was not a place of equal standing. Foreign traders operated under the rulers' regulations, paid fees, and depended on their goodwill. Some enjoyed particular privileges, such as the Portuguese, whom the court at times favored as a counterweight to the Dutch. The rulers' own relatives and officials took part in trade, and political favor affected commercial opportunity. The tolerance offered to religious communities was practical and was grounded in the benefits it brought, and it did not mean that all communities had the same status.

The arrival of merchant communities did not simply create parallel foreign quarters with no contact among them. Families, brokers, sailors, religious institutions, and court officials often connected people who used different languages and commercial conventions. Malay served as a widespread medium of communication, while Portuguese and local languages also remained useful in particular settings. A buyer might negotiate through an interpreter or a trusted household, and a visiting captain's ability to obtain cargo depended on knowing people who could guarantee delivery. Trust was a form of commercial capital that rulers could foster but not manufacture by decree.

Makassar's religious diversity likewise had practical dimensions. Muslim merchants needed space for worship and community leadership; Catholics sought access to clergy and devotional life; Chinese traders used networks of kinship and credit that crossed political boundaries. The court's willingness to admit several communities did not imply legal equality or an absence of tension. Foreigners' ability to reside, build, trade, and leave depended on royal permissions and could change when their political sponsors fell from favor. The port remained attractive because it offered greater alternatives to many traders than the more exclusive system pursued in VOC-controlled spice markets.

Chinese connections must be divided into distinct routes and periods. Holroyd's work identifies Makassar's place in Chinese trade during 1613–1669, but an imported Chinese product does not prove the presence of a ship that sailed directly from China. Textiles, ceramics, metals, and other goods could arrive through Java, Borneo, or intermediary merchants. Distinguishing a Chinese resident, an Asian trader handling Chinese cargo, and a junk arriving directly from a Chinese port helps students avoid exaggerating what individual references reveal. The same distinction applies to European cargoes carried by Asian vessels.

By the 1640s Makassar drew traders displaced or inconvenienced by changes elsewhere, including the Dutch conquest of Melaka. That created commercial opportunities and diplomatic dilemmas. A Portuguese merchant in Makassar could offer valuable contacts and goods while simultaneously becoming a reason for VOC hostility toward the court. The rulers did not possess an abstract doctrine of tolerance detached from politics; their policy reflected bargaining over trade, autonomy, and external danger. Reading merchant correspondence alongside local court records shows how a bustling, multilingual harbor could be both commercially resilient and politically precarious.

Division II · Section 82.8

Farmers, Sailors, Artisans, Families, and Labor in the Makassarese World

The port's prosperity depended on people whom the sources mention only in passing. Rice farmers worked the plains, with planting and harvesting patterned on the rains, and they owed a share of their crops and labor to local lords, who in turn owed tribute and service upward. Fishers worked the coast and the shallows, and their catch and the salt produced by evaporation supplied local markets. Shipwrights built vessels, some for local owners and some for visitors, using timber from the hinterland and skills handed down in families. Weavers produced cloth, and metalworkers made tools, weapons, and fittings. Markets brought producers and buyers together, and women played prominent roles in them as sellers and traders, as they did across much of the archipelago.

Households were the basic unit of economic life. Marriage joined families and sometimes alliances, and kinship structured obligation. Nobles maintained large households with dependents, servants, and followers. Among commoners, families farmed, fished, or traded, and merchants' households could be extensive, with wives, children, relatives, and dependents who handled business in the owner's absence. Women of rank could hold property and, in some cases, political office, though evidence for the daily lives of ordinary women is limited. Children worked from an early age at tasks suited to their strength.

Dependent labor was widespread. Many people lived in forms of bondage that varied from hereditary servitude to debt dependency, and some were captured in war or purchased. Warfare produced captives, who might be put to work, ransomed, or sold. Makassar was a recognized market for enslaved people, who came from eastern islands and from regions subjected to raids or conquest, and some were exported to other ports, including Batavia. Terms such as "slave" and "dependent" do not map neatly onto the local categories, which differed in rights and duties, and historians use them with care. What is clear is that coercion was part of the economy and that the port's labor depended partly on it.

Court demands shaped the rural economy. Rulers needed rice for troops and visitors, labor for fortifications and ships, and men for campaigns, and they drew these from subordinate communities. In peacetime, labor might be called out to build or repair earthworks and forts; in war, men were conscripted. These demands could be heavy, and the later grievances of Bone, discussed below, arose in part from them. Makassar's commercial prosperity therefore rested on agricultural surpluses, on a social order in which rank determined obligation, and on the use of coerced labor, as well as on the activities of visiting traders.

Ordinary workers experienced the port through time as well as through rank. Farmers had to balance the planting and harvest cycle with demands for military service or construction labor. Boatbuilders and repair crews faced intense periods of activity when fleets prepared to depart or damaged vessels returned. Sailors worked according to monsoon opportunities and the availability of cargo rather than a fixed annual wage schedule known to modern industrial employment. Markets brought these rhythms together as grain, cloth, fish, timber, and manufactured goods moved through households and small-scale producers before reaching larger merchants.

Women participated in economic life in ways that court histories record incompletely. They could manage household production, organize sales, maintain kinship connections, and exercise influence in marriage arrangements linking ranked families. Aristocratic women were important to dynastic strategy, but this does not mean that their experiences can stand for those of enslaved women, fishing households, or farmers. A useful history must identify those differences without inventing a representative personal diary. Many of the clearest surviving records concern elites and foreign traders; reconstructing workers' lives therefore requires combining scattered remarks with cautious knowledge of the economic activities the port could not function without.

The boundaries between dependence and enslavement were neither uniform nor always apparent in European descriptions. Some people were held because of war capture, others because of debt or inherited obligations, and their capacities to marry, move, acquire property, or negotiate release could differ. Historians should not treat these relationships as benign simply because the legal categories varied, nor should they assume every dependent was chattel property under one unchanging rule. The political question is who could command another person's labor, under what justification, and with what consequences for households affected by a military expedition or a new demand from the court.

Forts and fleets absorbed enormous human effort. Defensive works required earth-moving and masonry; vessels required crews and provisioning; court ceremonies consumed surplus food and specialist labor. If a subordinate kingdom had to send people for construction, its own harvest and security could suffer. The resistance that developed in Bone demonstrates how the material burden of imperial projects could become a political grievance. That connection between household labor and high-level warfare is one reason everyday social history belongs at the center of the chapter rather than as an optional sidebar.

Everyday Life and Social History

Farmers, Sailors, Markets, and Households

Consider how a single year might look for a rice-farming household on the plain behind Makassar. Planting and harvest followed the rains; between them, family members might fish, repair tools, or take on labor owed to a local lord. A son might serve on a trading vessel to Bima or Timor, returning with cloth or sandalwood. A daughter or wife might sell produce in a market where foreign merchants also bought provisions. If war came, the household's men might be called out, and its crop might be requisitioned. These possibilities are reconstructed from general evidence about the region and from the demands attested in chronicles and European reports; individual household records do not survive.

Historians reconstruct such lives indirectly, from chronicle references to labor and tribute, European descriptions of markets and shipping, later ethnographic accounts, and archaeological work on settlements and trade goods. All of these sources were produced by elites or outsiders. Students should therefore ask whose experience each source records and whose it omits, and should be wary of generalizing from the port to the whole of the countryside, where conditions varied.

  1. How did the demands of court and war affect ordinary households?
  2. What does the presence of enslaved and dependent people suggest about the limits of "commercial openness"?
  3. What kinds of evidence would best illuminate the lives of women and sailors?

Division III · Section 82.9

Gowa's Regional Hegemony and Relations with Bone, Wajo, and Soppeng

After the campaigns of 1608–1611, Gowa and Talloq stood at the head of a loose hierarchy of South Sulawesi realms. The relationship of each subordinate kingdom to the center was set by agreement or by force and could differ in detail. Typically, a subordinate ruler acknowledged Gowa's precedence, agreed to provide troops and labor when called, sent tribute, and accepted limits on independent foreign relations. Gowa sometimes placed relatives or favorites in positions of influence or took hostages from ruling families. These relationships are described as hegemony, since Gowa did not abolish the neighboring realms or administer them directly. Each kept its own rulers, councils, and customs.

Bone, Wajo, and Soppeng each had distinct institutions and traditions. Bone, the largest of the Bugis kingdoms, was ruled by an arumpone with a council of nobles and was proud of its own tradition of kingship. Wajo was organized differently, with a prominent role for commoner leaders and merchants and a tradition of limits on its rulers' power, and its seafarers were active in trade. Soppeng, closely linked by tradition with Bone, had its own ruling house. The three realms sometimes cooperated and sometimes competed, and their relations with Gowa changed over the years. Treating them as a single Bugis bloc misses these differences.

Gowa's power generated both cooperation and resentment. Some elites gained from association: trade opportunities, marriage ties, and military protection were valuable, and some served Gowa as commanders or officials. Others resented the obligations. Demands for military service took men from their fields, and demands for labor on fortifications fell heavily on subordinate communities. Interference in succession and the removal or humiliation of rival nobles offended claims to rank and honor. The memories of the Islamic Wars and of defeat in battle persisted. Bone in particular nursed grievances that later found expression in rebellion.

The result was a political structure that was powerful but brittle. It could mobilize large forces and hold the peninsula in a degree of order, but its stability depended on the willingness of subordinates to remain in their positions or on Gowa's ability to compel them. When an external power appeared that could offer them an alternative, the same structure provided it with potential allies. This is the central connection between the regional politics of South Sulawesi and the Makassar War.

Regional hegemony was experienced differently depending on a kingdom's historical standing, resources, and relationship to Gowa. Some rulers might preserve recognizable internal institutions while providing military assistance or tribute. Others faced replacement of officials, confinement of elites, or more direct supervision after rebellion. The resulting political hierarchy was therefore not a uniform set of provinces. It consisted of obligations whose enforcement changed with local strength and with the ability of Gowa's rulers to send an army. A formal submission in one year did not prove enduring willingness to obey in the next.

The earlier alliance of Bone, Wajo, and Soppeng, sometimes called the Tallumpoccoe or Three Peaks alliance, illustrates attempts to counter Gowa's rise before the Islamic Wars. Its existence also warns against assuming that shared Bugis language automatically produced permanent political solidarity. Members had their own interests and could quarrel or negotiate separately with Gowa. By the mid-seventeenth century the conditions governing each kingdom had changed, and Wajo's eventual support for Gowa during the final conflict differed sharply from Bone's alliance with Arung Palakka. These positions cannot be explained through ethnicity alone.

Tribute and labor were inseparable from diplomacy. A subordinate ruler might retain prestige through court visits, marriage, or reciprocal gifts, even while supplying troops and grain under pressure. The senior court could represent this as rightful obedience and protection. Subordinates could experience it as confiscation, humiliation, or a burden on their subjects. A useful primary-source exercise would compare a Gowa chronicle's account of a successful campaign with a Bugis memory of the same political relationship. Even where one version is later, differences in emphasis reveal why the meaning of conquest remained contested.

Such tensions created the conditions for the VOC to find allies. The company could furnish ships and artillery, but it could not easily conquer and govern a large hinterland without local knowledge and combatants. For Bone leaders, the VOC offered a means to challenge Gowa's supremacy. For other rulers, a new alliance carried opportunities and risks. The regional setting therefore belongs before the discussion of Speelman's expedition; otherwise the war appears as a foreign initiative imposed on an otherwise unified society.

Multiple Perspectives

Living under Gowa's Hegemony

From the perspective of the Gowa–Talloq court: Subordinate realms were bound to the center by oaths, marriage, and conquest, and their service was due to the sombaya. Hegemony kept order, protected trade, and gave Gowa the forces to defend its coast against rivals, including the Company.
From the perspective of Bone's nobility: Gowa's dominance meant humiliation and lost independence. Obligations of service and labor, interference in affairs of rank, and the memory of defeat provided grounds for resentment and for seeking a way to restore Bone's standing.
From the perspective of Wajo's leaders and merchants: Wajo valued its autonomy and its commercial access, and its leaders weighed the benefits of accommodation with Gowa against the costs. Their position, at times allied with Gowa, shows that relations were not simply of oppressor and victim.
From the perspective of commoners called to labor or service: Whatever the politics of rulers, the practical consequence for farmers and followers was labor, requisition, and the risks of war. Their views are rarely recorded, and historians infer them from the demands made.

These perspectives did not carry equal power. Gowa's court could compel; commoners had the fewest options.

Division III · Section 82.10

Makassar, Maluku, Buton, Sumbawa, Timor, and the Eastern Archipelago

Makassar's reach extended well beyond the peninsula, but the nature of its connections varied by place. Students should distinguish four things: commercial access, in which merchants could buy and sell; political influence, in which a ruler's decisions were affected by the power or favor of Gowa; military intervention, in which Makassarese forces were sent; and effective administration, in which Gowa directly governed. Most of Makassar's eastern ties involved the first two, some the third, and few the fourth.

In Maluku, the sultanates of Ternate and Tidore and the clove-producing communities of Ambon and Seram had long commercial ties with Java and Melaka. As the Company sought to confine the clove trade, many clove growers and local rulers, who disliked the Company's prices and terms, continued to sell to others, and Makassar was an important destination for such sales. Makassarese traders also went to Banda and Ambon themselves. Gowa's rulers cultivated relations with Maluku's rulers and maintained political contacts, though the Company's fortifications and naval patrols, and the military enforcement of its contracts, made these ties difficult and dangerous to sustain.

To the south and east, Buton, a sultanate on an island off Sulawesi's southeast, had ties with both Makassar and the Company, and its position between them made it a prize that each tried to attract. Bima and other communities on Sumbawa traded with Makassar and had dynastic ties with Gowa's house, and Gowa at times exercised strong influence there. Timor supplied sandalwood, which merchants collected and carried to Makassar, where it was sold to Chinese and other buyers. Such trade included enslaved people, wax, marine products, and food, and it ran through channels that combined commerce with the pursuit of political allegiance.

Through these connections, Makassar linked the eastern archipelago to the wider Indian Ocean and South China Sea trade. Spices that reached Makassar went on to Java, the Malay world, India, and Southeast Asian markets and beyond, and Indian cloth, Chinese goods, and metals came back eastward. But Gowa did not govern every port it influenced. Many communities valued Makassar as a market and a source of protection, but retained their own rulers and their own policies, and their relations with Gowa could shift as the balance of power changed. The Company's later efforts to separate such communities from Makassar exploited that independence.

A maritime network is not the same thing as an empire. Makassarese vessels reaching Buton, Sumbawa, or Maluku did not automatically transform those places into Gowa territories. Merchants could acquire docking permissions, credit, and partners without a military governor. Conversely, rulers could demand tribute or recognize a dependent king in a place whose trade remained conducted by independent carriers. Students should ask, for each location, whether the evidence concerns exchange, diplomacy, armed intervention, tribute, or actual installation of officers. Those distinctions prevent the commercial reach of Makassar from being confused with direct political control.

In Maluku the VOC's efforts to limit clove cultivation and circulation gave Makassar a special role. Producers and local rulers who rejected exclusive company sales could use alternative carriers, sometimes moving goods through islands not continuously policed by VOC vessels. The opportunity was lucrative and risky. A voyage might have to avoid patrols, wait for news of a blockade, or secure support from a local ruler. Makassar was attractive because it offered possible buyers beyond the company, but that attractiveness made it the subject of renewed Dutch pressure. The maritime economy of the port was thus tied to conflicts in spice-producing islands hundreds of kilometers away.

Buton occupied a particularly important strategic position on the routes southeast of Sulawesi. Relations involving the rulers of Buton, Gowa, and the VOC combined diplomacy with military pressure, and the island became crucial during the 1666–1667 coalition campaign. Bima and Sumbawa linked southern Sulawesi with the Lesser Sunda networks; Timor provided access to sandalwood; and Borneo offered forest and other goods through ports with their own political arrangements. These were not peripheral places waiting to be drawn into Makassar's orbit. They were participants whose rulers and merchants could redirect trade or enlist allies as regional conditions changed.

One limitation of the surviving sources is that a company's complaint about unlawful 'smuggling' need not describe how an Asian merchant understood a voyage. A Makassarese trader might consider a purchased cargo legitimate under the agreement made with the seller and the protection of a local ruler. A VOC officer might describe that same purchase as an infringement of monopoly rights. The contradictory vocabulary is itself evidence of competing claims to maritime jurisdiction. This is why commercial records should be read as political documents, not simply neutral lists of commodities.

Division III · Section 82.11

Karaeng Matoaya, Karaeng Pattingalloang, and the Cosmopolitan Court

Karaeng Matoaya, who acted as principal minister during the long reign of Alauddin and who led Talloq, was one of the architects of the court's development. His role in the conversion of 1605 and in the campaigns that followed made him central to the early seventeenth-century state, and his diplomatic contacts with Muslim and non-Muslim merchants and rulers fostered the port's growth. Matoaya ruled Talloq from 1593 to 1623, continued to advise its court after relinquishing the throne, and died in 1636. His son Karaeng Kanjilo, also known as Tumammaliang ri Timoroq, ruled Talloq from 1623 to 1641. A younger son of Matoaya, Karaeng Pattingalloang, exercised important ministerial influence before succeeding as ruler of Talloq in 1641; he died in 1654.

Pattingalloang is best known for the breadth of his interests. Foreign visitors, including European missionaries and merchants, remarked on his command of several languages, his curiosity about European mathematics and astronomy, and his interest in maps and instruments. Sources report that he sought globes, telescopes, and books from Europe through merchants and envoys, though the details of what he obtained are less well documented than the general reputation. He received foreign envoys, conducted correspondence with European and Asian rulers, and presided over a court in which Portuguese, Malay, and other languages were used. His diplomacy aimed to keep Makassar commercially open and politically independent, and his knowledge of the wider world informed that aim.

The court's openness to international knowledge did not mean abandonment of local institutions. The rulers remained Muslim sultans, patronized teachers and mosques, and upheld Makassarese titles, genealogies, and customs, even as they received Catholic missionaries and European visitors. This combination reflects a wider pattern in Southeast Asian courts, which selected and adapted what they found useful from outside while maintaining their own legitimacy. Cosmopolitanism in this sense was a strategy of rule, and it served diplomatic and commercial purposes.

A distinctive written source of the period is the lontaraq bilang, the Makassar annals, which began to be kept in the 1630s. Unlike the chronicles, which are organized by ruler and genealogy, the annals record dated events: the arrival of embassies, the movement of fleets, the building of forts, deaths, battles, and notable occurrences of court and port. They are brief, and they are written from the vantage of the court, but they provide a chronological framework that no other local source supplies.

The chronology of Talloq's leadership should be stated precisely because it reveals a system in which influence and formal rulership did not coincide. Karaeng Matoaya held Talloq's throne from 1593 until 1623, remained influential after relinquishing it, and died in 1636. His son Karaeng Kanjilo, known in the reign lists as Tumammaliang ri Timoroq, ruled Talloq from 1623 to 1641. Karaeng Pattingalloang, a younger son of Matoaya and brother of Kanjilo, succeeded to Talloq's throne in 1641 and ruled until his death in 1654. Pattingalloang's diplomatic and ministerial importance developed before his accession as ruler. This distinction guards against the impression that Talloq passed immediately from Matoaya to Pattingalloang during the 1630s.

The celebrated interest of Pattingalloang in European learning should also be placed within the conditions of a working diplomatic court. Maps and globes helped visualize the routes and political geography that merchants described. Foreign books and mathematical instruments could confer prestige and assist a ruler's advisers in evaluating claims about navigation, states, and distances. Translators and multilingual intermediaries were indispensable in discussions where commercial terms, religious practices, and the technical capacities of ships were at stake. These interests did not make Makassar a European-style scientific institution, but they demonstrate purposeful curiosity about knowledge useful to political and maritime decision-making.

The annals allow this political world to be studied at a different scale. Cummings's edition supplies date-indexed records, including entries associated with 18 November 1667 and 24 June 1669, two milestones in the final war. The date index establishes where a student should look; the edition must still be consulted for the precise wording of each entry and for any textual variants. Comparing an annal's brief notation of a treaty or military loss with a long VOC report can show how different authors selected what deserved recording. A seemingly matter-of-fact date is not free from the choices of a chronicler or a later copyist.

A further complication is that court knowledge had social boundaries. The record tends to preserve the actions of rulers, embassies, and notables more reliably than those of women working at market or sailors injured during transport. The historian's task is not to discount court writing but to use it for what it can establish, test its chronology when possible, and identify the perspectives missing from it. In that sense Pattingalloang's cosmopolitan reputation and the continued keeping of the annals are complementary subjects: they show both the breadth of knowledge that reached the court and the limits of what its writers chose to preserve.

Division III · Section 82.12

Makassar and the VOC: Commercial Rivalry, Diplomacy, and the Road to War

The Company arrived in Makassar early in the seventeenth century and at first sought to trade there on the same terms as others. Its aims soon diverged from the port's practice. The Company wanted exclusive control of cloves, nutmeg, and mace, and it sought to close the routes by which spices reached rival buyers. Makassar, by contrast, welcomed any merchant who paid its duties, and its rulers regarded the freedom to receive visitors as part of their sovereignty. The two positions were not easily reconciled.

Relations in the first decades combined negotiation and friction. There were violent incidents in the 1610s and disputes over trading terms, and the Company, which had a base at Batavia from 1619, sent ships to intercept Makassarese vessels bound for the spice islands. In the 1630s, the Company imposed blockades and demanded that the rulers exclude its rivals and stop buying spices from the sources it claimed; agreements were made, but the rulers did not fully honor them, partly because they could not control traders and partly because they did not wish to. The pattern was one of treaties signed, then strained, and then renewed by pressure.

Other merchants complicated matters. Portuguese, English, Danish, and Asian traders had reasons to value Makassar as a place where the Company's writ did not run, and the court cultivated them as a counterweight. The Company, for its part, treated them as competitors for the spice trade and sought to push them out. After 1641 and the fall of Melaka, additional merchants arrived, increasing the Company's concern. In the 1650s the Company also consolidated its position in Maluku, culminating in measures against clove cultivation outside its control, and these steps made Makassar even more important as an alternative.

The 1650s and 1660 saw growing tension and armed encounters. Company ships harassed Makassarese vessels; Makassarese forces took action in the east, including pressure on Buton, a Company associate; and in 1660 the two sides fought and negotiated, with an agreement that settled little. The same year saw a rising in Bone against Gowa's demands, as the next sections show. By the middle of the 1660s, the Company's leaders in Batavia concluded that diplomacy would not end Makassar's role in the spice trade and that a larger expedition, supported by indigenous allies, was needed.

The Company's concerns should not obscure the limits of Makassar's openness. Its rulers taxed trade, controlled who could build and bear arms, required the allegiance of subordinate realms, and demanded labor. The port was open relative to the Company's monopoly, but it was governed, and its rulers pursued their own interests and not an ideal of free trade. Scholarship by Heather Sutherland and Ryan Holroyd has been important in showing both the openness and the constraints of the trading economy.

Relations between Makassar and the VOC were already violent before the famous war. In 1615, as company personnel withdrew from Makassar, a Dutch attempt to seize local hostages produced bloodshed. The ensuing dispute was not an abstract disagreement about the future of the spice market: it involved identifiable people, retaliation, and questions about whether foreign merchants could use force within the sultan's jurisdiction. In December 1616 a party from the Dutch ship Eendracht was attacked and sixteen Dutch crewmen were killed. The episodes belong to a sequence of action and reprisal, and should not be merged into one undated 'early incident' or cited as proof that permanent war was inevitable.

The central issue later became the jurisdiction claimed over cloves and nutmeg. The company argued from contracts and its strategic control over production centers that others should not purchase or transport the spices in ways that undermined its arrangements. The rulers of Makassar saw receiving merchants and permitting exchanges as attributes of royal power. A trader's right to buy from a producer could consequently be legitimate under one political understanding and prohibited under another. The significance of the disagreement was not just the price of a shipment; it was the authority to define the rules governing travel and exchange across the eastern seas.

Treaties provided a method of managing this disagreement but also created new grounds for conflict. The agreement of 26 June 1637 has received close study precisely because it shows the difficulties of reconciling diplomatic promises with divergent expectations. Negotiations in the 1650s, including the 1655 treaty, likewise reveal that both sides sometimes preferred compromise to the considerable costs of war. The agreements were not meaningless simply because disputes recurred; they helped define obligations, provide temporary security, and expose the points on which the parties would not readily yield. Treating VOC diplomacy as no more than camouflage for conquest would erase these periods of genuine bargaining.

By 1660 the crisis had become military and regional. VOC attacks damaged Makassar's defenses and compelled concessions, while Gowa's rulers remained determined to preserve influence in the east. At the same moment, compulsory labor and political resentment in Bone generated opposition from within the Sulawesi political order. The company could now imagine a campaign supported by dissatisfied regional rulers, and Bone's leaders could imagine obtaining the military assistance needed to break Gowa's dominance. The later coalition grew from the convergence of two conflicts whose causes were related but not identical.

Division IV · Section 82.13

Sultan Hasanuddin and the Defense of Makassarese Sovereignty

Sultan Hasanuddin ruled Gowa from 1653 to 1669. He came to the throne after the reign of Malikussaid and, in 1654, lost the counsel of Karaeng Pattingalloang, whose death removed an experienced diplomat from the court. The situation Hasanuddin inherited had three main difficulties. The Company was pressing harder on the spice trade and on Makassar's connections with the east. Gowa's hegemony over neighboring realms rested on obligations that many resented. And the port's commercial independence depended on being able to receive merchants whom the Company wished to exclude.

Hasanuddin's court responded by strengthening defenses and maintaining commercial relationships. Forts were improved, artillery was acquired and cast, and military forces were organized under commanders drawn from the ruling houses. The court sought to keep Portuguese, English, Danish, and Asian traders attached to the port, and it continued to claim influence in the eastern archipelago. In diplomatic exchanges with the Company, the issue was not only trade but also principle: whether the Company could dictate to the rulers of Makassar which merchants they might admit and where their subjects might sail. For Hasanuddin, to concede that would mean accepting a limit on sovereignty.

Historians therefore treat Hasanuddin as a ruler pursuing identifiable strategic interests: preserving the commercial position of his port, defending his realm, and maintaining the hegemony on which the realm's power rested. He was not simply a symbol of resistance or an obstacle to Dutch expansion. His policies also included measures against rivals within South Sulawesi and in the east, which had costs for those who experienced them. In later Indonesian memory he is remembered as a national hero, and Dutch writers of his time and later gave him a famous nickname that alluded to his fighting spirit. These later memories reflect the concerns of the periods that produced them, and they should be distinguished from the evidence of what he did.

Hasanuddin inherited more than an international commercial quarrel. On taking power in 1653 he confronted the difficulties of governing a large regional hierarchy in which the obligations imposed by earlier conquests remained contested. The death of Karaeng Pattingalloang in 1654 removed a figure with exceptional experience in diplomacy and commerce. Hasanuddin could consult other nobles and officials, but he had to manage relations with neighboring kingdoms, European companies, and the increasingly complex port economy without the same senior political adviser. The court's decisions therefore involved tradeoffs among military security, revenue, elite solidarity, and the risk that stronger demands on subjects would produce resistance.

Military preparations drew directly on the resources described earlier in the chapter. Coastal fortifications could be strengthened, artillery deployed, vessels gathered, and food accumulated for a siege. Yet preparations had a cost. The court needed brickmakers, workers carrying earth, crews, and people to provision garrisons. If those demands fell heavily on subordinated communities, they could weaken the political support on which defense depended. The problem was circular: fear of VOC attack increased the demand for labor; harsh methods of obtaining labor heightened resistance; resistance made an external alliance more dangerous.

Diplomacy offered choices but no simple escape. Expelling European rivals might have reduced immediate pressure from the VOC but could also have destroyed valuable commercial relationships and weakened Makassar's claim to control its own harbor. Allowing ships to sail toward Maluku preserved revenue and reputation while inviting company retaliation. Hasanuddin's court had to judge whether signed agreements would be enforced, whether allied rulers would remain loyal, and whether the VOC could sustain a larger expedition. These decisions were made under uncertainty rather than in the knowledge that Makassar would fall.

Historical portraits often remember Hasanuddin primarily for resistance. His strategic decisions are better understood within a contested state whose successes had created enemies. A sovereign ruler defending commercial access could simultaneously command forced labor and suppress neighboring political autonomy. Recognizing both dimensions does not diminish the significance of resistance to VOC hegemony; it explains why Arung Palakka and others could regard the same conflict as an opportunity for liberation from Gowa.

Division IV · Section 82.14

Bone, Arung Palakka, and the Internal Opposition to Gowa

The internal opposition to Gowa centered on Bone. After its submission in 1611, Bone had experienced further conflicts with Gowa, and Gowa's forces defeated Bone again in the 1640s. Members of Bone's nobility were taken into Gowa's service or held under its supervision as part of the settlement. Bone's grievances were thus a mix of political subordination, personal humiliation of its elite, and the burden of labor and service owed to its overlord.

Arung Palakka, a Bugis nobleman of Bone born in the 1630s, embodied these grievances. According to Leonard Andaya's reconstruction, he belonged to a family connected with Bone's highest rank, spent part of his youth in Gowa as a result of the earlier defeat, and developed lasting resentment toward Gowa's domination. In 1660, Gowa called on Bone's people to labor on its fortifications, a demand that was heavy and, to the nobility, degrading. Arung Palakka and other leaders rose against this demand and against Gowa's authority more generally. The rising was suppressed, and Arung Palakka fled, passing through Buton and eventually reaching the Company's headquarters at Batavia in the early 1660s, accompanied by followers.

At Batavia, Arung Palakka's goals met the Company's. He wished to restore Bone's independence and his own family's honor; the Company wished to weaken Gowa. His men served the Company in campaigns elsewhere in the archipelago, where they gained a reputation as capable fighters, and the relationship grew. It was an alliance of convenience between parties with overlapping but not identical objectives, as the aftermath would show.

The war was not a simple conflict of peoples. Political loyalties crossed ethnic and dynastic boundaries. Some Bugis and Makassarese nobles served Gowa, while others joined or leaned toward the coalition. Wajo, a Bugis realm, was at times Gowa's ally and resisted the coalition to the end. Some Makassarese nobles, dissatisfied with Hasanuddin's policies, were willing to defect. Soldiers from Ambon, Buton, and other places fought for the Company or its allies for their own reasons. The coalition's members had distinct objectives: the Company sought commercial dominance, Arung Palakka sought liberation and status for Bone and himself, and other allies sought revenge, security, or advantage. Historians such as Andaya have used these relationships to show that the Makassar War was as much a regional political struggle as a colonial one.

The fortification labor crisis of 1660 linked the political economy of the court directly to rebellion. Gowa's preparations called on Bone's people to work on defensive structures, and Bugis nobles were made responsible for ensuring the laborers' compliance. Requiring people who identified with a formerly powerful kingdom to work under coercive supervision could be experienced as a public insult as well as a material burden. The resulting rebellion therefore cannot be explained by military calculation alone. It involved grievances about status, jurisdiction, and the right of Bone's elites to command their own followers.

The chronology of Arung Palakka's exile is important. After the failure of the rising he and his followers fled to Buton in late 1660 or the beginning of 1661, depending on the account. This was not merely a place passed on a direct voyage to Batavia. Buton gave displaced opponents of Gowa an arena in which to seek refuge, maintain their followers, and search for future partners. Arung Palakka moved to Batavia in 1663 and established relations with the VOC leadership, who eventually saw his followers as useful soldiers in a wider campaign. Several years separated the uprising from his return to South Sulawesi with Speelman's expedition.

The alliance was shaped by an asymmetry that neither party could ignore. Arung Palakka needed the company's naval power and supplies to challenge Gowa's defenses; the VOC needed allies who could recruit soldiers, interpret regional loyalties, and maintain political authority inland. Each side could attempt to use the other. Their cooperation became effective because immediate objectives overlapped, not because one had permanently surrendered its independent political ambitions. The complexities of that arrangement help explain tensions after victory, when decisions about the rights of Bone and other kingdoms still had to be negotiated.

The moral language in accounts of Arung Palakka also requires careful handling. Bugis traditions emphasize dishonor suffered under Gowa and the restoration of dignity, while company records often assess him according to military usefulness and treaty compliance. These are distinct documentary perspectives. Neither should be accepted as a complete account of what ordinary Bone families expected from the war. A regional history can recognize the legitimacy of resistance to subordination while remaining attentive to the coercion that victors and their allies subsequently exercised over other communities.

Historical Vocabulary
Arung, Siri', and Political Allegiance

Definition. Arung is a Bugis title for a noble ruler or lord; arumpone is the title of the ruler of Bone. Siri' (in Bugis and Makassarese, with related forms) is a concept of honor, dignity, and shame bound up with personal and family standing. Political allegiance in this system was personal and hierarchical, expressed through oaths, service, and marriage.

Historical context. Aristocratic politics in South Sulawesi turned on rank and honor. A humiliation of a lord or his house could be understood as an injury demanding redress.

Why it matters. The idea of siri' helps explain how Bone's nobles experienced demands for labor and subordination, and why Arung Palakka's pursuit of restored honor carried political weight.

Potential misunderstanding. Siri' should not be treated as an unchanging "national character" explaining all behavior. Historical actors also acted for strategic, material, and political reasons, and the term's meaning varied with context.

Division IV · Section 82.15

Speelman's Expedition and the Coalition War, 1666–1667

The Company's expedition, commanded by Cornelis Speelman, departed Batavia in November 1666 and reached the waters off Makassar in December. It was a combined force: Company ships and soldiers, Bugis followers of Arung Palakka, and contingents from other parts of the archipelago, including Ambon and Buton. Estimates of the total number of men and ships vary in the sources, and the chapter does not give a single figure. What matters is that the force combined European naval artillery with indigenous troops familiar with local terrain and politics.

The objectives of the participants differed. The Company wanted to break Makassar's role in the spice trade, secure access for itself, and weaken a political rival. Arung Palakka wanted to free Bone, restore its standing, and break Gowa's dominance. Other allies had their own aims, including security against Gowa's pressure and shares in anticipated spoils. These differences were later important, because the settlement had to satisfy partners with interests that overlapped only partly.

The campaign combined naval and land operations. Ships bombarded coastal fortifications and attacked Makassarese vessels, while troops landed and moved along the coast, taking or besieging forts. The allies also operated in the hinterland, where Arung Palakka's forces and local risings in Bone and elsewhere undermined Gowa's control. Supply lines were a constant concern for both sides: rice, water, and munitions had to be obtained or protected, and disease affected the campaigning forces. Gowa's forces defended with artillery and large numbers of troops, but they faced attacks from the sea and the rear, and their ability to hold the peninsula's many fronts was stretched.

Political defections affected the military balance. Some nobles and communities that had been Gowa's subordinates shifted allegiance as the coalition advanced, and Gowa's capacity to call on allies and subordinates declined. The combination of Dutch naval artillery, regional military support, and defections weakened Gowa's resistance, though it did not eliminate it. By late 1667, Hasanuddin's court faced the choice between continued war on unfavorable terms and negotiation.

A chronological reconstruction reveals the coalition's complexity. Speelman's expedition departed Batavia in late November 1666 and reached Makassar's approaches in December, but it did not immediately settle the conflict through a direct assault on Somba Opu. After initial demonstrations and attacks, the fleet turned toward Buton, where Gowa had applied military pressure and where its enemies had strong incentives to cooperate with the company. The relief of Buton and the reorganization of Bugis forces strengthened the coalition's regional position before the principal operations against Makassar resumed. Students should therefore resist compressing an extended campaign into one European naval victory.

The campaign depended on logistics at sea and ashore. Warships could carry artillery and disrupt shipping, but fleets needed safe anchorages, fresh water, food, and repairs. Land forces required guides, local provisioning, and knowledge of routes between forts and agricultural districts. Arung Palakka's supporters could recruit in regions where a company commander had little independent authority. Meanwhile Gowa had to defend extensive coastal positions, maintain communications with subordinate districts, and prevent its labor supply from collapsing. Even an impressive fortress was vulnerable if the political network supplying it disintegrated.

After operations in the eastern islands, Speelman returned to the Makassar theater in mid-1667. Fighting intensified during the second half of the year, and the company and its allies employed pressure at multiple points rather than relying on the walls of one town alone. Attempts to encourage the defection of local lords worked alongside direct combat. A local ruler might calculate that Gowa could no longer protect his community or that an alliance with Bone offered a better future. Such calculations multiplied as the coalition gained ground. The resulting military disadvantage was political as well as tactical.

Campaign descriptions can give an exaggerated impression of precise knowledge because commanders recorded large numbers of vessels, troops, and casualties. Such figures were shaped by incomplete reports, the desire to justify decisions, and differences in what counted as a fighting man or auxiliary. The chapter should not present an aggregate strength unless its provenance and uncertainty are explained. A more robust conclusion is that Dutch ships and artillery operated in combination with substantial indigenous forces, while Makassar retained formidable defenses and supporters capable of sustaining resistance beyond the first treaty.

Division IV · Section 82.16

The Treaty of Bungaya, 1667, and the Fall of Somba Opu, 1669

On 18 November 1667, representatives of Gowa and Talloq signed the Treaty of Bungaya with the Company and its allies. The agreement, as reconstructed from the text printed in Leonard Andaya's study, imposed far-reaching obligations. It granted the Company privileges in trade; it required the expulsion or exclusion of rival European merchants, specifically naming the Portuguese and English while extending its prohibition to other European nations; it restricted the movements and trade of Makassarese and other Asian shipping, placing them under a system of permits; it required Gowa to give up fortifications and territory, including the fort at Ujung Pandang; and it ended Gowa's claims of overlordship over neighboring realms, including Bone. It also required payments and the giving of hostages. For details of each article, students should consult the text itself.

The treaty's meaning depends on the position of each party. For the Company, it gave a legal basis for a monopoly in the east and an advantage over rival Europeans. For Arung Palakka and Bone, it removed Gowa's overlordship and opened the way to a leading role for Bone. For Gowa, it meant the loss of its commercial and political position. Its rulers and military leaders were divided, and some saw the treaty as an unacceptable surrender.

The treaty did not create a lasting peace. Tensions over its implementation arose quickly. Gowa's leaders were reluctant to carry out provisions that undermined their position, merchants and shipping continued, and fighting resumed in 1668. The Company brought reinforcements and renewed the campaign, and over the following months its forces and its allies besieged the remaining strongholds. The final assault came at Somba Opu, which fell on 24 June 1669. The fortress was captured and then largely destroyed, ending Gowa's capacity to resist. According to the reign list published by William Cummings, Hasanuddin abdicated on 17 June 1669, a week before Somba Opu fell, and he died in 1670.

Students should distinguish carefully between the 1667 treaty and the military conclusion of 1669. The agreement imposed obligations on paper, but its signature did not end the war, and the Company's and its allies' power on the ground was established only after further fighting. Moreover, the treaty's provisions, such as the exclusion of rival merchants, were not enforced uniformly or immediately. The treaty is evidence of what the Company and its allies demanded, and it is not in itself evidence of what they achieved.

The treaty can be read as an effort to reorder relations among merchants, shipping routes, and rulers. In the published English text used by Leonard Andaya, Article 6 singles out Portuguese and English merchants for exclusion and extends the prohibition to other European nations. It does not need to name every European group individually for its reach to be broad. Article 7 addresses commercially significant imports, including textiles and Chinese goods, in a way intended to privilege company trading interests. Article 9 concerns travel and shipping through specified routes under a system of Dutch permits. Students should examine these requirements separately: excluding rivals from residence, regulating imports, and controlling voyages were distinct interventions with different enforcement problems.

The treaty also reorganized political relationships. Requirements concerning forts, compensation, and neighboring rulers affected Gowa's security and position within the peninsula. The fort at Ujung Pandang was to pass to the VOC under the 1667 settlement, providing a fortified base long before the final assault on Somba Opu. For Bone, the end of Gowa's claim to supremacy was a central result. For the company, obtaining a defended foothold and limiting rival trade increased both its negotiating power and its ability to supervise future compliance. For Hasanuddin's supporters, these provisions struck at the foundations of the kingdom's earlier achievements.

A treaty text records demands and negotiated wording, not automatic obedience. A rule excluding particular merchants depended on their departure or expulsion; a permit requirement depended on patrols and the willingness of shipowners to comply. Local officials could sometimes evade, reinterpret, or delay these measures. Political opponents of the settlement could retain forces and strongholds. It follows that the signing of Bungaya on 18 November 1667 and the renewed war must be understood as successive stages in an unresolved struggle rather than a conclusion followed by unexplained violence.

The final assault on Somba Opu took place on 24 June 1669. An important correction to common shorthand is that Cummings's reign list places Sultan Hasanuddin's abdication on 17 June 1669, one week before that fall, not afterward. His removal from active kingship and the collapse of the fort's defenses belonged to the same crisis but were separate events. The fortress was taken and devastated; its destruction signaled a profound loss of Gowa's military and symbolic power. Hasanuddin died in 1670. The chronology therefore has at least three distinct turning points: a treaty that imposed terms in 1667, an abdication in June 1669, and the fall of Somba Opu days later.

Colonial Reorganization, Migration, and Historical Legacy

Division V · Section 82.17

Fort Rotterdam, the VOC, and the Reorganization of South Sulawesi

The 1667 Treaty of Bungaya already required the transfer of the fort at Ujung Pandang to the Company. The VOC established its position there, renamed it Fort Rotterdam, and later expanded and rebuilt its defenses, while war continued elsewhere. The fort became the Company's headquarters in South Sulawesi and the center of its administration of Makassar, a role that continued for generations. Around it, a Company-controlled settlement developed, and the old royal center of Somba Opu was left in ruins.

The new arrangements involved the Company, Bone, Gowa, and other powers. Arung Palakka emerged as the dominant ally on the peninsula, formally becoming ruler of Bone in the early 1670s, and his position was supported by the Company. Gowa survived as a diminished kingdom, its rulers bound by the treaty and by the Company's supervision, and its political and commercial activities were restricted. Its ability to field armies and to sail freely was limited, and its claims over neighbors were ended.

The Company's control was concentrated on Makassar and key coastal positions and did not amount to direct rule over every community. In the interior and along much of the coast, local rulers, including Bone's, continued to govern. The Company relied heavily on its alliance with Arung Palakka for security and influence, and the relationship was a negotiated settlement between partners whose interests overlapped but did not coincide. The Company wished for commercial advantage and low costs; Arung Palakka wished to build Bone's power and his own. Over time this partnership shaped South Sulawesi's politics, with Bone becoming the leading regional power under Company protection.

The company's acquisition of Ujung Pandang must be dated to the treaty settlement rather than described as a reward first taken after June 1669. Bungaya required its cession in 1667, and the VOC established its position there while the wider conflict continued. The fort was renamed Fort Rotterdam; later reconstruction and strengthening turned the site into a more permanent center of company power. These were stages of acquisition, occupation, and rebuilding, not one instantaneous construction after Gowa's final defeat. The distinction matters because a secure coastal base allowed the company to sustain operations while resistance persisted elsewhere.

The replacement of one regional hierarchy with another did not simplify government into direct Dutch administration. The company controlled a fortified port and sought authority over particular shipping and commercial activities. Arung Palakka and Bone possessed their own ambitions, alliances, and means of governing communities inland. Gowa remained a kingdom under severe restrictions rather than disappearing as a political society. Wajo and other realms also had interests that could not be reduced to either VOC policy or Bone's wishes. The postwar order was a negotiated and frequently contested arrangement among surviving political institutions.

For officials at Fort Rotterdam, commercial administration was inseparable from security. A harbor permit or a rule about textiles affected not only the customs a merchant paid but the routes a ship could legally follow and the political partners a trader could approach. In practice, limited personnel, long distances, and the number of available coastal landing places made enforcement difficult. The company relied on local brokers, soldiers, interpreters, and allied rulers to maintain its position. Its capacity to punish a vessel near Makassar did not amount to an ability to watch every bay between Borneo and Timor.

For the populations of South Sulawesi, the postwar settlement created new inequalities and opportunities. Some nobles gained favor by joining Bone or the company; others lost estates, followers, or access to customary sources of revenue. Merchants had to reassess where to trade and how to obtain permission. Farmers and sailors continued their work under changing demands for supplies and service. These consequences are more revealing than a single statement that Dutch colonial government began in 1669, because they show exactly where the reorganization reached and where older institutions persisted.

Division V · Section 82.18

Defeat, Displacement, and the Bugis–Makassarese Diaspora

War and reorganization displaced many people. Nobles who refused to accept the new order, merchants who disliked the Company's restrictions, sailors, soldiers, religious figures, and followers of defeated leaders looked for places where they could live under conditions they found acceptable. Some went to nearby islands, while others traveled much farther. Not all left by choice; captives and enslaved people were also moved by the war.

Resistance continued after Somba Opu. Wajo, which had remained allied to Gowa, held out until 1670, when its capital Tosora fell to Company and allied forces. This defeated Wajo's principal organized opposition, although resistance and regional disputes continued elsewhere in South Sulawesi in places. Some Makassarese leaders, including a son of Hasanuddin, left for Java, where they became involved in conflicts discussed in Chapter 83.

Bugis and Makassarese communities moved to Borneo, the Malay Peninsula, Sumbawa, Java, and other parts of the archipelago. Some joined existing coastal communities as soldiers or traders; others established new settlements. The migration was not wholly new, since earlier traders from South Sulawesi had already traveled widely, but defeat increased its scale and shaped its character. Displaced nobles brought followings and military experience, and merchants carried capital and connections. Displacement thus became a source of new commercial and political networks in the archipelago.

This chapter treats the diaspora only as an aftermath of the war. The later involvement of Bugis leaders in the politics of Johor-Riau, in 1718–1722, belongs to Chapter 81 and is not repeated here. The point to retain is that the Makassar War's consequences extended well beyond Sulawesi, as people trained in its commerce and warfare entered other political settings.

Migration after the Makassar War had several causes that must not be collapsed into a single mass departure. Some elite households left because political defeat endangered their rank or freedom; traders sought ports where commercial restrictions were less onerous; sailors and soldiers followed employers or kin; and ordinary families might leave to escape disruption or coercive demands. Departures occurred at different times and through different routes. Some migrants retained strong ties to South Sulawesi, while others established households and loyalties elsewhere. The resulting diaspora was a sequence of choices under pressure, not an ethnic exodus commanded by one leader.

The defeat of Wajo's principal organized opposition, culminating in the fall of Tosora in 1670, belongs to this immediate aftermath. It should not be described as the end of all resistance in South Sulawesi: local opposition, disputes over obligations, and later conflicts continued. For Wajo families and merchants, the loss of political security could make mobility attractive. Maritime skills and trading acquaintances were assets that could be carried to new ports even when land and court rank could not. The history of diaspora thus connects political defeat to the transfer of expertise, capital, and networks.

The movement of Bugis and Makassarese people also illustrates the resilience of relationships across the archipelago. Migrants entered existing settlements, served rulers and merchants, formed marriages, and sometimes used armed followings to negotiate status. Their destinations were not blank political spaces; local rulers made decisions about admission, land, commercial privileges, and military service. Consequently, diaspora networks could generate influence while also producing rivalries with established communities. Later developments in Johor-Riau, treated in Chapter 81, are one important outcome but not the whole story.

Evidence for the experiences of poorer migrants is especially incomplete. Chronicles and company reports more often identify named nobles, ships, campaigns, and commercial disputes than the fate of individuals leaving with families. The historian should avoid turning the better-documented journeys of elite exiles into a universal account of migration. It is nevertheless possible to explain the economic mechanism: people who carried skills in navigation, shipbuilding, brokerage, and military organization could help establish new routes, and their new connections often preserved regional exchange even when a major state lost its former political authority.

Division V · Section 82.19

Trade after Conquest: Commercial Recovery and the Limits of Monopoly

The conquest changed Makassar's commerce but did not end it. New trading restrictions, a permit system, and a Company-controlled harbor altered conditions. Many merchants left, especially Europeans and others who had come because the port was free of Company control, and the port's volume of trade was disrupted by the war and its aftermath. The destruction of Somba Opu and the loss of the court's patronage affected the networks that had centered on it.

Yet the Company could regulate some things more easily than others. It could control the purchase and shipment of strategic products, such as spices, where it held the sources and a naval advantage. It was less able to control all maritime exchange: small vessels, local routes, and goods of lesser value were hard to police, and merchants adapted. Asian networks continued to supply cloth, rice, and other goods, and Makassar remained a regional center for trade in the eastern archipelago. Chinese and other traders returned or arrived, and the city's role as a hub of exchange gradually recovered under the new conditions.

Heather Sutherland's research on Makassar's later history shows that the city's trading story did not end with the defeat of Gowa. Under the Company, Makassar continued as a port with a mixed population, and local and Asian merchants found ways to trade within and around the Company's rules. The Company's monopoly was therefore partial, and its enforcement depended on its capacity and on the cooperation of local partners. The pattern illustrates a broader point about monopolies in the early modern archipelago: they were most effective where production and shipping could be physically controlled and least effective where they depended on regulating many small traders.

The commercial consequences of the conquest unfolded in stages. In the first years the VOC sought to prevent Makassar from functioning as a rival entrepôt for the fine spices and to channel valuable imports through company-controlled networks. Merchants associated with the earlier commercial order could no longer assume that they would be welcomed on the same terms. Some departed; others adjusted their cargoes, partnerships, or legal status. The economic setback was severe, but the city's continuing population, food supply, and position on maritime routes meant that commerce did not simply vanish with Somba Opu.

Company officials faced a persistent problem of scale. A regulation could be posted at Fort Rotterdam and enforced against vessels entering the main port, but maritime trade also passed through countless smaller anchorages. Local captains might sail under different patrons, land at places beyond direct company supervision, or trade products outside the central spice monopoly. The company's control of the main harbor therefore affected the organization of exchange without providing absolute command of every transaction. Heather Sutherland's work emphasizes this distinction between political-military conquest and the more gradual, uneven process of commercial adaptation.

The range of commodities changed over time. Regional traffic in rice, forest products, metals, textiles, and marine products persisted. Chinese and other Asian merchants remained important intermediaries, even as the position of some previously prominent trading houses declined. Toward the eighteenth century, demand for tortoiseshell and later trepang offered new incentives for eastern Indonesian voyages. That later economy should not be projected unchanged onto the immediate aftermath of 1669: it developed from older commercial practices under new market conditions. It illustrates instead how a port whose earlier central product had been suppressed could build other connections.

Makassar's commercial recovery therefore does not mean the war had little effect. The loss of a court-centered international entrepôt, the disruption of merchants' assets, and the imposition of company restrictions were profound changes. Nor does recovery prove that the VOC's monopoly claims were imaginary: they had substantial consequences for where spices could be bought and sold. The analytical point is more precise. Coercive control over selected commodities and strategic ports could redirect commerce, but merchants and local communities continued to generate routes and exchanges that no single institution could fully govern.

Division V · Section 82.20

Makassar, Maritime Sovereignty, and the Transformation of Eastern Indonesia

Gowa and Talloq built their power by combining several resources. Agricultural land supplied food and manpower; Talloq's commercial and administrative leadership and the harbors gave them access to trade; military organization and fortification protected both; diplomacy with Asian and European merchants and rulers kept the port busy; and Islamic kingship provided legitimacy and links with a wider Muslim world. The same success that brought wealth and regional dominance generated opposition. Neighboring realms resented subordination, and the Company, whose monopoly aims Makassar's openness undermined, regarded the port as a threat.

The parties to the conflict had different aims. Gowa sought to preserve its commercial position, its sovereignty, and its hegemony. Bone, led by Arung Palakka, sought liberation from Gowa and a place of leadership. The Company sought control of the spice trade and the end of a rival port. Other allies and subjects of Gowa sought their own security or advantage. No single party's motives stand for the whole conflict, and interpretations that make one party's perspective the explanation distort the evidence.

The war's consequences were far-reaching. Eastern Indonesian politics were reorganized around the Company's position at Fort Rotterdam and Bone's ascendancy. The open-port model that had attracted many merchants was replaced by one of Company regulation, though not by a complete monopoly. Migrations carried South Sulawesi's seafaring skills and political connections across the archipelago. Yet the commercial energy of the region's peoples continued, in adapted forms.

The long history of Makassar challenges explanations that begin with the arrival of European ships. Gowa's expansion, the partnership with Talloq, the accommodation of Malay merchants, and South Sulawesi's rival political institutions all preceded the decisive VOC campaign. The company entered a region with an established political economy and sought to impose rules that conflicted with it. Understanding that earlier history is essential because Gowa's own success in acquiring manpower, revenue, and regional obedience created the resentments that its enemies later used. The Makassar War was shaped by the structures of an Asian state as much as by the strategy of a European corporation.

At the same time, the political accomplishments of the Makassarese court cannot be separated from the people who bore their costs. Agricultural producers supplied the grain on which port and army depended; specialist workers maintained ships and forts; dependent and enslaved people performed labor through unequal obligations; and subordinated kingdoms furnished troops and resources. These demands gave the court considerable capacity to act. They also limited its legitimacy among some subjects and neighbors. This duality explains why an economically successful port could stand at the center of a politically fragile regional hierarchy.

No single combatant can represent the whole war. Hasanuddin sought to preserve royal sovereignty and commercial access; the VOC pursued privileged markets, secure coastal positions, and treaty compliance; Arung Palakka and Bone sought to escape Gowa's supremacy and gain a new political standing; and other rulers chose alliances according to their circumstances. These aims intersected without merging. The coalition's success produced a new hierarchy whose members continued to bargain and sometimes resist each other. Reducing the conflict to an uncomplicated opposition between freedom and domination erases those competing projects.

The enduring result was neither total Dutch control nor the disappearance of South Sulawesi's maritime world. Gowa's military center was shattered and the company's position at Fort Rotterdam transformed coastal politics. Bone rose under Arung Palakka, Wajo and other kingdoms confronted changed regional conditions, and migrants carried skills and relationships into new settings. Over time, trade resumed through different commodities and channels. The wider conclusion is that early modern maritime sovereignty was powerful but contingent: rulers and corporations could win ports, impose treaties, and claim monopolies, but their achievements remained dependent on local labor, regional allies, mobile merchants, and shifting political consent.

Historical Debate

Commercial Conflict, Colonial Conquest, or Regional Political Transformation?

The question: What kind of conflict was the Makassar War? Historians have emphasized different aspects, and the answer affects how Makassar's defeat is understood.

Interpretation A — a commercial monopoly war: The war arose from the Company's determination to close the spice trade and Makassar's role in it. On this view, the Company's economic aims drove its decision to launch the expedition, and the outcome is understood chiefly in terms of trade.
Interpretation B — a struggle among Southeast Asian states: The war was substantially a conflict among South Sulawesi powers, in which the Company was a participant and a beneficiary but depended on indigenous allies with their own aims. This interpretation draws heavily on Andaya's work on Arung Palakka and Bone.
Interpretation C — a conflict over sovereignty and its transformation: The war decided whether Gowa could maintain its sovereignty and hegemony, including its right to receive merchants, and transformed the political order of the region. This view stresses political authority over either commerce or conquest alone.

Evidence: Company correspondence and treaties emphasize commerce and strategy; Bugis and Makassarese chronicles and the Andaya reconstruction emphasize regional politics and honor; the annals supply dated events. All three bodies of evidence reflect their makers' perspectives.

Points of disagreement: Disagreement concerns emphasis and causation more than established facts: how much weight to give to commercial motives, to regional rivalries, and to political sovereignty. Many historians treat the interpretations as complementary and not exclusive.

Why it matters: If the war is seen mainly as a commercial war, the Company's role is central; if as a regional struggle, Southeast Asian agency is central. The emphasis changes how the end of Makassar's independence is understood.

Student task: Using the evidence in this chapter, argue which interpretation best explains the outcome, or explain how they can be combined. Identify evidence that would help distinguish them.

The chapter's answer to its three questions can now be stated. Gowa and Talloq developed Makassar by combining rice-producing land, military power, commercial openness, and Islamic kingship. Their expansion, the resistance of neighbors, and the Company's monopoly ambitions combined to produce a war that Gowa lost, less because of Dutch arms alone than because a coalition formed around Bone's grievances and the Company's aims. And that defeat reshaped eastern Indonesian politics, commerce, and migration, without extinguishing the commercial dynamism of South Sulawesi's peoples. Chapter 83 turns to Mataram and the contest for Java, where the Company's power and the displaced peoples of Makassar also appear.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the rise of Gowa and Talloq, the development of Makassar as the major commercial center of the eastern archipelago, and the war of 1666–1669 that ended Gowa's regional power. Its governing argument is that Makassar's rise rested on a combination of Gowa's agricultural and military resources, Talloq's commercial leadership, connections with many merchant communities, and access to eastern trading networks, and that its defeat resulted not simply from Dutch arms but from a coalition built around the grievances of other South Sulawesi powers, particularly Bone.

South Sulawesi's geography gave it rice-producing plains, river mouths, and a position on routes to Java, Borneo, the Lesser Sunda Islands, and Maluku. Geography supplied conditions and not outcomes; the peninsula was divided among several kingdoms, and political choices turned its opportunities into a port and a state. Gowa expanded from the early sixteenth century, with the reign of Tunipalangga marking a period of intensive military and administrative growth, though chronicle dates and attributions are approximate.

Gowa and Talloq formed a partnership of two ruling houses, linked by shared policy and by the office of tumabicara-butta, without being merged into a single centralized government. The chronicles, edited by William Cummings, are the principal indigenous source for this system, with their genealogical emphasis and their purpose of legitimating rulers.

Islam became the religion of the court after the conversions of 1605 and the Friday prayers of 1607, and the campaigns of 1608–1611 brought Soppeng, Wajo, and Bone into subordinate relationships. The chapter has distinguished the conversion of rulers, the establishment of Islam at court, military submission, and the longer transformation of religious life in communities.

Makassar became an entrepôt for spices, Indian cloth, sandalwood, and other goods, supported by local rice and shipping. Malay, Portuguese, Chinese, English, Danish, and Indian merchants used the port, and the fall of Melaka in 1641 and Company restrictions elsewhere increased its attraction. Its openness was relative: rulers taxed, regulated, and conscripted, and the economy depended on farmers, sailors, artisans, and on dependent and enslaved labor.

Gowa's hegemony over Bone, Wajo, and Soppeng produced both cooperation and resentment. Under Matoaya and Pattingalloang, the court combined diplomacy, learning, and local institutions, while the annals began to record dated events. Meanwhile the Company's monopoly aims conflicted with Makassar's practice of receiving all merchants, producing blockades, agreements that did not hold, and armed encounters in the 1650s and 1660.

Sultan Hasanuddin defended Gowa's sovereignty and commercial position against this pressure. Arung Palakka of Bone, after the failed rising of 1660 and flight to Batavia, joined the Company in a coalition whose members had different aims. The expedition under Speelman, which departed in November 1666, combined Dutch naval artillery with regional forces and benefited from political defections.

The Treaty of Bungaya of 18 November 1667 imposed far-reaching restrictions, but it did not end the war. Fighting resumed, and Somba Opu fell on 24 June 1669, with Wajo's capital Tosora falling in 1670. The Company established itself at Fort Rotterdam, and Bone under Arung Palakka became the leading regional power, while the Company's authority remained concentrated on Makassar and key positions.

Defeat displaced nobles, merchants, and sailors, who spread across the archipelago and formed new networks. Commerce in Makassar continued under new restrictions, because the Company could control strategic goods more easily than all maritime exchange. Historians debate whether the war should be understood as a commercial monopoly war, a struggle among Southeast Asian states, a conflict over sovereignty, or a combination, and the evidence supports a combination.

Taken together, the chapter shows that Southeast Asian polities were active makers of early modern maritime history: they built power, accommodated and coerced, formed and broke alliances, and shaped the outcome of a conflict with a European company.

The port and cluster of coastal settlements associated with Gowa and Talloq, and the commercial center of the eastern archipelago in the seventeenth century.
Somba Opu
Gowa's principal coastal royal fortress, captured and largely destroyed on 24 June 1669.
Karaeng
A Makassarese title of nobility meaning "lord," borne by rulers and high nobles.
Sombaya
The title of Gowa's supreme ruler, meaning one to whom homage is paid.
Tumabicara-butta
The principal minister or spokesperson of the realm in the Gowa–Talloq partnership, held in this period by leaders of Talloq's house.
Arung and Arumpone
Bugis titles for a noble ruler and for the ruler of Bone respectively.
Siri'
A concept of honor and dignity in Bugis and Makassarese society, linked to personal and family standing and relevant to aristocratic politics.
Lontaraq
Traditional South Sulawesi written records, including genealogies and chronicles.
Lontaraq bilang
The Makassar annals, kept from the 1630s, recording dated events in brief entries.
Islamic Wars
Gowa's campaigns of about 1608–1611 against neighbors, combining a summons to accept Islam with demands for political submission.
Hegemony
Dominance over other realms, achieved through obligation, alliance, and force, without abolishing their rulers or administering them directly.
Entrepôt
A port whose prosperity depends on receiving, storing, and redistributing goods produced elsewhere.
Monopoly (VOC usage)
An attempt to confine the purchase or sale of certain goods, especially spices, to the Company, often enforced by force and contract.
Open port
A port that receives merchants of many origins under moderate duties; open relative to a monopoly, but still regulated by its rulers.
Inter-island trade
Shipping among the islands of the archipelago, conducted largely in vessels owned by Southeast Asian merchants.
Bugis
A people and language of central and eastern South Sulawesi, organized in several kingdoms including Bone, Wajo, and Soppeng, not a unified political bloc.
Makassarese
A people and language of the southwestern peninsula; the ruling houses of Gowa and Talloq were Makassarese, but their subjects and servants were of diverse origins.
Arung Palakka
Bugis nobleman of Bone who led opposition to Gowa and allied with the VOC; later ruler of Bone.
Treaty of Bungaya
The agreement of 18 November 1667 between Gowa–Talloq and the VOC and its allies, imposing commercial and political restrictions; it did not end the war.
Fort Rotterdam
The name given by the VOC to the former Ujung Pandang fort, which became its headquarters in South Sulawesi.
Pass (permit) system
A system under which shipping required a Company permit to sail to designated places.
Diaspora
A dispersed population maintaining ties across settlements; here, Bugis and Makassarese communities that spread across the archipelago.
Conversion
The adoption of a new religion by an individual or ruler; distinct from the institutional establishment of a religion and from the wider change of communal practice.
Chronicle
A narrative record of rulers and events, often genealogical and compiled after the events, serving purposes of legitimation.
Annals
A record of events set out by date, usually brief, kept close to the time of events.
Coalition warfare
Warfare conducted by allied parties with different aims, as in the 1666–1669 war.
Dependent labor
Labor by people in forms of bondage or obligation, including captives, enslaved people, and debtors, whose status varied locally.
Sovereignty
Supreme authority within a realm, including the right to decide whom to admit and how to trade; at issue in the conflict between Gowa and the VOC.
Periodization caution
The recognition that sixteenth-century dates drawn from later chronicles are approximate, and that seventeenth-century dates rest on dated annals and European records.

Chapter Reflection

Makassar's history matters because it shows how a Southeast Asian state could build a commercial and political system of its own and could be defeated by a coalition in which indigenous actors played decisive roles. It cautions against telling the story of European expansion as the simple triumph of a stronger power over weaker ones.

The chapter also complicates the opposition between monopoly and freedom. Makassar's openness attracted merchants that the Company wished to exclude, but it was a regulated openness, supported by taxation, conquest, and dependent labor. The Company's monopoly was ambitious and violent, but it was partial and depended on local partners.

The war's consequences reached beyond Sulawesi. The Company's base at Fort Rotterdam, Bone's ascendancy, and the dispersal of Bugis and Makassarese people shaped later politics across the archipelago, including developments examined in Chapters 81 and 83. Meanwhile continuities in farming, shipbuilding, religion, and seafaring show that conquest altered the order of power without ending the social and economic life beneath it.

For the textbook as a whole, Chapter 82 reinforces its central commitment: Southeast Asian societies were historical actors that selected, transformed, resisted, and shaped outside forces, and their histories cannot be reduced to the story of larger powers.
