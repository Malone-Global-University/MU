Banda and the Violence of Monopoly

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

On 8 May 1621, in the shadow of a Dutch fort on the island of Banda Neira, forty-four Bandanese leaders were put to death. Within months the Dutch East India Company had seized much of the producing territory of the world’s only significant source of nutmeg and mace. It had achieved that control not by out-bidding rivals but by destroying most of the society that grew the spice.

The Banda Islands are a tiny cluster of volcanic islands in the Banda Sea, in what is today the Indonesian province of Maluku. Their combined land area is small enough to be crossed in a few hours' walk on any single island. Yet for centuries before 1621 these islands supplied nearly all the nutmeg and mace consumed in Asia, the Middle East, and Europe. A seed and its red covering, produced by a single evergreen tree species growing in a narrow tropical zone, made the islands one of the most commercially valuable places on earth, and made their inhabitants participants in a trading world stretching from Java to the Mediterranean.

This chapter studies what happened when a corporation decided that this trade was too important to leave to the people who conducted it. The Dutch East India Company (the Verenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie, or VOC), whose founding and chartered powers are examined in Chapter 77, arrived in Banda in 1599 as one buyer among many. Within two decades it was building forts, demanding exclusive purchasing rights, fighting Bandanese communities and their English rivals, and debating how to guarantee a monopoly. In 1621 the Governor-General, Jan Pieterszoon Coen, led an expeditionary fleet to Banda Besar (also called Lontor). The result was a campaign of conquest, execution, starvation, expulsion, and enslavement that the Dutch National Archives and many historians today describe as genocide.

The governing argument of the chapter is that the VOC's commercial monopoly in nutmeg depended on the seizure of political authority, land, and labor. It did not rest merely on superior financial organization or maritime technology. The company could buy nutmeg on Banda for as long as the Bandanese chose to sell, and as long as Javanese, Malay, Chinese, and English buyers could also reach the islands, the company could not control either price or supply. A monopoly of purchase therefore required, in the eyes of the company's most aggressive officers, a monopoly of the places where nutmeg grew and of the people who tended it. The chapter distinguishes carefully between these two forms of control, because that distinction organizes the entire story.

The Bandanese are the central subjects of this chapter, not merely its victims. They had their own political institutions, their own commercial judgment, and their own understanding of treaties and alliances. They negotiated, resisted, fled, hid, bargained with the English, and rebuilt their communities in exile. Those who were enslaved in 1621 and afterward carried agricultural and commercial knowledge that the colonial system came to depend upon. Their descendants, and the descendants of the refugees, remain part of the history that this chapter attempts to tell.

The chapter also grapples with evidence that is overwhelmingly one-sided. Most surviving written records of the conquest were produced by the company that carried it out. They include instructions, resolutions, daily journals, letters, and interrogation records. Such sources tell us a great deal about what company officials intended, decided, and chose to record, and very little directly about what Bandanese men and women thought or suffered. Historians therefore combine company archives with archaeology, English and other European accounts, oral tradition, and the study of refugee communities. Throughout, this chapter identifies which claims rest on which kinds of evidence.

One of those claims has been revised very recently. For generations, histories assumed that Dutch conquest transformed nutmeg cultivation into a more intensive and rational enterprise. Research published online on 30 September 2026 by S. J. Miske in the Journal of Southeast Asian Studies challenges that assumption. It argues that cultivation methods showed considerable continuity across the conquest and that productivity stagnated or declined after 1621. If this reassessment holds, the violence of 1621 bought the company control of an existing economy rather than a more efficient one.

The chapter follows the VOC's escalation from commercial negotiation to armed conquest (Division II), the destruction of Bandanese society in 1621 (Division III), the construction of the plantation system (Division IV), and the continuing resistance, diaspora, and historiographical significance of these events (Division V). It prepares the ground for Chapter 79, which examines Batavia and the wider VOC colonial system, and it should be read alongside Chapter 71 on the Spice Islands of Ternate and Tidore, Chapter 77 on the founding of the Company, and Chapter 82 on Makassar.

  3. Analyze how Bandanese participation in Asian commercial networks shaped their response to Dutch demands for exclusive trade.

Historical Context

By 1599 the spice trade of eastern Indonesia was already ancient. Cloves from Ternate, Tidore, and neighboring Malukan islands, and nutmeg and mace from Banda, had reached South Asia, the Middle East, and the Mediterranean world for many centuries by overlapping chains of sea merchants. By the early sixteenth century the principal ports through which these spices flowed to wider markets were Melaka, the Javanese north coast, and the ports of Makassar and Brunei, as earlier chapters show. Chapter 64 examines Melaka, whose conquest by the Portuguese in 1511 (Chapter 69) dispersed rather than destroyed Asian commercial networks. Portuguese ships reached Banda in 1512, but the Portuguese preferred to buy Bandanese spice from merchants in Melaka and never established lasting control over Banda itself.

The second half of the sixteenth century saw an increasing European appetite for Asian spices and, in the Netherlands, a commercial revolt against both Spanish rule and Portuguese-dominated supply. Dutch merchants sent fleets directly to Southeast Asia in the 1590s. After a period of fierce competition among Dutch syndicates, the States General chartered the VOC in 1602 as a united company with extensive quasi-sovereign powers, including the right to make treaties, build forts, and wage war in Asia. Chapter 77 examines this corporate-state structure. For the present chapter, the essential point is that the VOC's directors, the Heeren XVII, wanted to extract exclusive supply contracts from Asian producers and to exclude European and Asian competitors. They were willing, in principle, to use force to do so.

Dutch ambitions met a regional world that was not passive. Banda, like Ternate and Tidore, was embedded in dense local politics. Bandanese leaders had choices: they could sell to Javanese ships, to Malay and Chinese merchants, to Portuguese and later English buyers, or to the Dutch. The Dutch needed Bandanese goodwill because they lacked the regional commercial infrastructure to buy spice on their own terms. Yet they also needed to compete against merchants who offered better prices or goods, and who sailed to Banda each year with rice and cloth. This tension between dependence and ambition drove the escalation described in the pages that follow.

Historians approach Banda through an uneven documentary base. Portuguese and Dutch observers left descriptions of the islands. Dutch company archives, preserved in the Nationaal Archief in The Hague, hold instructions, resolutions, journals, and correspondence. English company records describe the English presence on Run and Ai. Bandanese voices survive in more fragmentary ways: in testimony recorded by Dutch interrogators, in later oral tradition among descendant communities, and in archaeological evidence from the islands and from refugee settlements. The challenge of the chapter is to use the abundant company documents without adopting the company's own account of why it acted and who was responsible.

Two cautions apply throughout. First, the Banda Islands should not be treated as an inevitable victim of European power. The company's decisions were contingent, contested within the company itself, and driven by specific commercial and political circumstances, including rivalry with the English. Second, modern Indonesian national borders did not shape the world of 1599. The people of Banda were a maritime trading society with its own links to Maluku, Seram, the Kei Islands, Java, and beyond, and it is within those networks that their history is best understood.

Division 1 — Banda before Dutch Conquest

Division I · Section 78.1

The Banda Islands and the Geography of Nutmeg

The Banda Islands lie in the Banda Sea, south of Seram and far to the east of Java, in the region of eastern Indonesia that this textbook treats as part of Maritime Southeast Asia (Chapter 3). The group consists of about ten small islands, of which a handful matter most to this chapter. Banda Neira is a small island with the best-sheltered anchorage in the group, and it became the commercial and, later, administrative center. Banda Besar, known to Bandanese and to many historians as Lontor, is the largest island, a crescent-shaped ridge that curves around the harbor. Gunung Api ("fire mountain") is an active volcano rising directly out of the sea opposite Neira. Ai and Run lie to the west, several hours' sail from the central group, and Rosengain lies to the southeast. Together the islands cover a very small area, and the central harbor between Neira, Lontor, and Gunung Api offers a protected anchorage unusual in this region of open sea.

Geography shaped the economic importance of Banda in two ways. First, the islands' volcanic soils, humid tropical climate, and exposure to the monsoon winds support the nutmeg tree (Myristica fragrans), a long-lived evergreen that thrives in a narrow tropical range. In the early seventeenth century Banda was effectively the only commercially significant source of nutmeg and mace in the world. Second, the small area of the islands meant that the entire supply was concentrated in a few places that a single naval force could, in principle, dominate. This concentration was the physical precondition for any monopoly: no merchant could seriously attempt to corner the supply of a commodity grown across a continent, but one could imagine cornering a commodity grown on a handful of islands that a fleet could blockade.

The same geography created vulnerabilities. Banda is small, steep, and agriculturally specialized. Volcanic hillsides produced excellent nutmeg groves but did not readily yield the rice and other staples on which a dense population depended, and the Bandanese relied on imported food, as Section 78.3 explains. Banda is also seismically and volcanically active, so that earthquakes, ash falls, and the hazards of Gunung Api were permanent features of settlement, and tropical storms and seasonal winds regulated the arrival of ships. Navigation to Banda followed monsoon patterns (Chapter 4), so that merchants arrived and departed in seasonal rhythms, a pattern that shaped the timing of trade and, later, of Dutch military operations.

It is important to distinguish nutmeg geography from clove geography. Cloves, examined in Chapter 71, were produced on a number of Malukan islands including Ternate, Tidore, Makian, Motir, and Bacan, and later Ambon, so that clove production was spread across a larger area and a number of competing sultanates. Banda had no sultan. Its politics were organized very differently, as the next section shows, and the VOC encountered there not a court with which to bargain but a collection of communities, each led by locally prominent men. This difference between a dispersed clove economy governed by sultanates and a concentrated nutmeg economy governed by community leaders helps explain why the Dutch approach to Banda took the particular, violent form that it did.

The archipelago's small size should not be mistaken for geographical simplicity. A ship seeking the central harbor had to account for narrow approaches, submerged hazards, seasonal currents, and the availability of safe anchorage. A garrison that held Neira did not thereby control every landing beach on Banda Besar or the approaches to Run. The same water that enabled trade and escape allowed a better-equipped naval power to cut off supply. This contrast—sea routes as lifelines for the islanders and as potential instruments of blockade—helps explain why Banda's geography mattered to both resistance and coercion. Geographic accounts must also distinguish the distribution of nutmeg trees from the boundaries of villages and political authority. A productive grove could be linked to a household, kin group, or settlement whose social world extended across the harbor. An island map that depicts only forts and plantations therefore obscures the preconquest landscape of exchange and obligation. When students use later Dutch maps, they should ask whether plotted boundaries describe terrain that already existed or the land divisions imposed after conquest. The visual precision of a seventeenth-century chart is not proof that colonial administrators understood, or respected, the social geography they overlaid.

Division I · Section 78.2

Bandanese Society before the VOC

Reconstructing Bandanese society before 1599 is difficult, because early written descriptions come mostly from outside observers, including Portuguese, Dutch, and Asian visitors, and because the 1621 conquest destroyed much of the society that those observers described. Historians use these accounts cautiously, alongside later oral tradition, linguistic evidence, and archaeological work. A body of research by historians and anthropologists, including John Villiers's study of the sixteenth-century islands and later work on Bandanese land tenure and ritual, forms the basis for what follows.

The Bandanese lived in settlements, usually near the coast or in defensible hillside positions, each with its own leadership, ritual specialists, and landholding groups. There was no single monarch or centralized government of the Banda Islands as a whole; contemporary descriptions nevertheless mention three or four local kings whose practical authority was limited compared with that of the orang kaya. Instead, authority was exercised by locally influential men whom outside observers called orang kaya, and by community decision-making in which such leaders consulted others. Settlements and island groupings were connected by kinship, shared rituals, trade, and alliance, and also divided by rivalries. Accounts of Banda describe recurring tensions between communities and alliance groupings, including between settlements on Neira and on Lontor, and such rivalries were an important feature of the political landscape that European newcomers sought to use. They should not be read, however, as proof of weakness: Bandanese politics involved bargaining among relatively autonomous communities, a pattern common elsewhere in maritime Southeast Asia.

Religion in Banda was diverse and should not be reduced to a single label. By the sixteenth century Islam had reached the islands through Javanese and Malay merchants, and many coastal Bandanese communities had adopted it, though in forms shaped by local practice and alongside older ritual relationships to land, ancestors, and sacred places. Other communities retained non-Islamic traditions. The tie between people and the land was spiritually as well as economically significant, and the groves, springs, and places associated with ancestors formed part of a sacred geography. This matters for understanding the conquest, since the VOC's seizure of land was also an assault on social and religious relationships anchored in particular places.

Bandanese society was also internally differentiated. Leading families controlled valuable nutmeg groves and commercial relationships with visiting merchants. Others worked groves, harvested, processed, and carried spice. There were enslaved or dependent people in Banda, as in much of maritime Southeast Asia, where bondage and dependency took many forms and were different from the plantation chattel slavery that the VOC later created (Chapter 91 compares these systems). The Banda Islands also hosted resident and seasonal foreign merchants, including Javanese, Malay, and Chinese traders, some of whom settled for months or years, so that the "Bandanese" population was fluid, cosmopolitan, and not easily counted.

Historical Vocabulary
Orang kaya

Definition. A Malay phrase meaning, literally, "rich person" or "wealthy man," used in much of the Malay-speaking world and in eastern Indonesia for locally influential leaders. European sources applied it to the men who represented Bandanese communities in negotiation.

Historical context. In Banda the orang kaya were leaders of settlements or of kin and landholding groups, and they acted as spokesmen and commercial brokers in dealings with merchants and with Europeans. Their authority depended on wealth, family standing, reputation, and the consent of those they represented, and it overlapped with the roles of ritual and community elders. They negotiated prices, made agreements, and assembled the community when decisions were required.

Why it matters. The Dutch repeatedly treated the orang kaya as if they were rulers who could bind the whole of Banda to an exclusive trading contract. When some leaders signed agreements and other communities refused to be bound, the Dutch interpreted this as bad faith. In 1621 the company arrested, interrogated, and executed leaders in an effort to eliminate the political structure through which Bandanese communities had acted.

Potential misunderstanding. The English term "chiefs" or "nobles" can mislead. The orang kaya were not a single council governing the islands; their authority coexisted with local royal titles, community assemblies, harbor officers, and other forms of status. Their authority was locally situated, negotiated, and contingent; a signature by one spokesman cannot be assumed to have bound every community.

The surviving early descriptions also complicate the idea that commercial authority was exclusively male or simply hereditary. Accounts used by S. J. Miske describe larger public meetings, apparently held at Ortatan, where leaders brought issues before assembled community members and might have to reconsider decisions that did not receive approval. These accounts are filtered through Dutch recorders and should not be turned into a romantic image of universal democracy; they nevertheless make the notion of unlimited chiefly power untenable. Sources also mention the ulilima and ulisiva alignments, linking different settlements. Such divisions could produce rivalry and cooperation, and their organization belonged to a wider Malukan political vocabulary. Distinct local royal titles, port officers, imams, and wealthy merchants existed alongside the orang kaya, even though no monarch possessed island-wide control. Women appear in some accounts as cultivators, processors, and participants in local marketplaces; elite men's dominance of negotiations with Europeans should not be confused with control over every aspect of exchange. This layered political and social system matters because foreign signatories could not automatically convert a negotiation with selected leaders into binding consent from all settlements. A useful question is not whether Bandanese institutions resembled a European monarchy, but how decisions were made, challenged, and enforced within a network of communities.

Division I · Section 78.3

Asian Merchants and the Precolonial Spice Economy

The Bandanese nutmeg economy was not a closed local system awaiting a European buyer. For centuries before the arrival of the Dutch, Bandanese leaders sold nutmeg and mace to merchants who sailed to the islands from Java, the Malay world, and farther afield. Javanese traders from north-coast ports were among the most important, and Malay merchants, many linked to Melaka and later to its successor ports (Chapters 69, 70, and 81), also visited regularly. Chinese traders came to Banda, and merchants from Gujarat and other South Asian ports were connected to the trade by way of Melaka, from where spice flowed west to India, the Middle East, and Europe. By the sixteenth century the Portuguese preferred to buy Bandanese spice through Melaka rather than to sail to Banda themselves, and they never established a permanent base there.

What did Bandanese communities need from this trade? The answer reveals the foundations of their commercial autonomy and their vulnerability. Banda grew little rice, and its inhabitants relied heavily on imports, especially rice, supplemented by sago and fish. They also imported cloth, which served both as everyday clothing and as a medium of prestige and exchange, along with metalwares, ceramics, and other goods. Visiting merchants therefore brought essential foodstuffs and valued manufactured goods in return for spice. This meant that Bandanese specialization in nutmeg was a rational choice within a trading system that reliably supplied what they did not produce. It also meant that anyone who disrupted the arrival of rice and cloth could place enormous pressure on Bandanese communities.

Scholars such as John Villiers have shown that the trade was competitive. Bandanese leaders could and did compare offers from different buyers, bargain over prices and over the goods exchanged for spice, and switch trading partners. Because several merchant groups competed for limited supplies, the Bandanese held a considerable degree of bargaining power. The Dutch discovered that they could not simply replace this network. They did not command the rice supply on which Banda depended, nor the textiles that Bandanese valued, and their ships arrived from far away at irregular intervals. If the Dutch were to obtain an exclusive supply, they would need either to out-compete Asian merchants on price and goods, which proved difficult, or to prevent those merchants from reaching Banda. The second path required naval blockade, fortifications, and ultimately conquest.

This background helps correct two common misunderstandings. The first is that Banda was a remote backwater to which Europeans brought world trade. In reality Banda was linked by established commercial relationships to the principal trading ports of Asia. The second is that Bandanese leaders were naive about commerce. They were seasoned traders who understood exchange, credit, and alliance, and who judged European offers against Asian alternatives. When they resisted exclusive contracts they were acting on commercial logic, not ignorance.

Everyday Life and Social History

Food, Family, Trade, and Ordinary Life before Conquest

Ordinary life in Banda before 1621 revolved around the nutmeg groves and the sea. Households tended trees, collected fallen fruit, split the fruit to separate the seed from its red covering, and dried both products for sale. Mace, the covering, and nutmeg, the seed, were processed with different techniques and carried differing values, so that skilled handling affected the quality and price of what visiting merchants purchased. Work was shaped by season and by the rhythm of the ships that came to buy.

Because rice had to be imported, food was never wholly separated from trade. Households supplemented imported rice with sago and with fish from the surrounding seas, and fishing, canoe building, and short-distance sailing were important skills. Textiles were valued as clothing and as wealth. The arrival of merchants brought not only goods but news, credit, marriage and kinship connections, and the presence of foreign residents in Bandanese settlements. Women and men had different tasks in households and groves, although the evidence for gendered divisions of labor before 1621 is thin and later colonial descriptions cannot simply be projected backward.

What historians know about everyday life comes largely from outsiders and from traces in later oral tradition, and this means that the voices of ordinary Bandanese women, farmers, boatbuilders, and the enslaved are largely recovered indirectly. The conquest destroyed many of the settlements, groves, and social networks in which this ordinary life took place, and one of the tasks of Division V is to examine how some elements of it survived in exile.

The imported necessities also gave trade a social importance not captured by a simple price series. A shipment of rice could affect the capacity of households to remain in their villages through a difficult season; bolts of cloth could serve as payments, gifts, or stores of value as well as clothing. Merchant arrivals could reopen debt relationships, carry messages among islands, and renew political ties. The fact that these relationships were commercially useful did not make every transaction free of inequality: Bandanese households had different access to groves, boats, and influential intermediaries. Nonetheless, the availability of competing buyers was a meaningful protection against a single purchaser dictating all terms. This explains why a naval restriction on imports could strike at food security before it secured any additional spice for the Dutch. The company could control its ships and command its personnel, but initially lacked the network of trusted local suppliers and regular provisioning relationships that Asian merchants had developed. Its strategy thus moved toward replacing commercial competition with coercive regulation. One should avoid making the Bandanese choice of trading partners look like a direct equivalent of modern free trade: these were relationships embedded in diplomacy and community politics. Their significance lies in the alternatives they offered and in the social costs of suppressing them.

Division I · Section 78.4

Nutmeg, Mace, Cultivation, and the Economics of Monopoly

Nutmeg and mace come from the same fruit. The nutmeg tree produces a fleshy fruit which, when ripe, splits open to reveal a hard-shelled seed wrapped in a bright red, lacy covering. The seed, once its shell is removed and it is dried, is nutmeg; the covering is mace. Both are used as flavorings and, in the early modern period, were also valued for their supposed medicinal properties, so that demand in Asia, the Middle East, and Europe was driven by culinary taste, by medical theory, and by the status of spice. The tree takes years to begin bearing and then produces for decades, so that a grove represents long-term accumulated labor and knowledge, and it cannot be replaced quickly if destroyed.

Cultivation in Banda before 1621 was based on gathering from groves of planted and semi-wild trees managed by households and kin groups, with practices that included the use of shade trees to protect young seedlings, the management of the forest canopy, and the careful harvesting and drying of the product. The knowledge required was cumulative and local, and it included understanding of soils, winds, pests, and tree maturity. Much of this knowledge was held by those who had worked the groves all their lives. This point becomes decisive later, because a company that seized the groves also needed the people who knew how to tend them.

The economics of nutmeg made it especially attractive to monopoly-seeking buyers. The supply was geographically concentrated and could not be expanded quickly, since new trees needed years to mature. Demand in Europe was high enough that the difference between the purchase price in Banda and the sale price in Amsterdam was very large, which meant that anyone who could control supply could capture a substantial profit. A single buyer who could prevent rivals from purchasing could set the price paid to producers and limit the quantity reaching European markets so as to hold up the price there. Such a monopsony (a single buyer) on the producer side combined with a monopoly (a single seller) on the market side was the ideal that the VOC pursued.

This chapter organizes its argument around a distinction between two kinds of control. The first is control of purchase and distribution: the ability to dictate who buys the spice, at what price, and who carries it to market. This can in principle be achieved by contract, by naval blockade, by alliance, or by the threat of force. The second is control of cultivation and labor: ownership or direct command of the land, the trees, and the people who produce the crop. The VOC began with the first and moved toward the second, because it found that the first was unenforceable so long as the Bandanese retained both the land and the freedom to sell to others. Understanding why the company crossed from one form of control to the other, and what that crossing cost, is the purpose of the remaining sections.

A distinction among the orchard, the forest, and the commercial ledger is essential. European readers of early reports sometimes imagined that spice trees could be counted and harvested like a regular cereal crop, but nutmeg trees mature over years and fruits ripen at different times. The existence of many trees says little about how many workers are available at harvest, what proportion of produce can be dried without spoilage, or which ships can carry it away. Quantities reported by merchants may refer to potential harvest, purchases, exports, or a particular year's deliveries rather than to identical measurements of production. This distinction becomes crucial in comparing periods before and after 1621. A company might increase the recorded quantity reaching its own warehouse merely by shutting rival traders out, while the physical harvest stayed constant or declined. Similarly, holding supplies back from sale could raise prices without reflecting improved production. The economic history of Banda therefore cannot be inferred from VOC receipts alone. To evaluate efficiency one needs evidence about trees, acreage, labor requirements, losses, storage, and output—and must recognize that many such measurements survive unevenly. This careful use of economic categories allows students to see monopoly as a redistribution of bargaining power rather than as a synonym for higher output.

Division 2 — From Commercial Negotiation to Armed Conquest

Division II · Section 78.5

The First Dutch Encounters and Trading Agreements, 1599–1605

Dutch ships first reached the Banda Islands in 1599 as part of the early Dutch expeditions to Asia that preceded the creation of the VOC. At this stage the Dutch were newcomers trying to buy spice, and the Bandanese received them as they received other merchants. Dutch ships offered goods and prices, took on cargoes, and sailed away. The early encounter suggests nothing like conquest: it shows commercial interest on one side and commercial opportunity on the other, with each side assessing the other. Several Dutch voyages followed, and by the early 1600s the Dutch were regular visitors.

The formation of the VOC in 1602 changed the character of Dutch dealings. Where earlier Dutch visitors had been competing syndicates, the Company was a single body with a charter authorizing it to make treaties and to use force, and its directors expected it to secure exclusive access to the spice trade. In Banda this ambition found expression in agreements made with Bandanese leaders in 1602 and 1605. Dutch representatives presented these as contracts giving the Company rights to purchase Bandanese spice, and, in the Dutch reading, rights superior to those of other buyers. The agreements also involved promises of protection and, in some versions, military assistance against Bandanese enemies and against the Portuguese and Spanish.

Here the central conflict over meaning emerged. For the Bandanese leaders who took part, agreements of this kind were most plausibly alliances and trading arrangements of a type familiar from Southeast Asian diplomacy: reciprocal commitments, renewable and dependent on continued performance, in which the exchange of gifts, goods, and military help created relationships that carried obligations on both sides. Such agreements did not normally convey permanent exclusive rights or surrender the freedom to trade with others. For the Dutch, by contrast, written contracts were legal instruments that bound the signatories and their communities in perpetuity and gave the Company an exclusive claim that could be enforced against both Bandanese and rival merchants. When Bandanese communities continued to sell to English, Javanese, and other buyers, Dutch officials accused them of breaking agreements, while Bandanese leaders insisted that they had not understood the contracts as giving up their commercial freedom.

Historians should be careful with this disagreement. The surviving texts of the agreements, their translation, and the circumstances in which they were signed are matters that require attention to the archival record, and different readings exist. What can be said with confidence is that the Dutch and the Bandanese did not share a clear understanding of what the agreements meant, that the Dutch used them as the basis for claims of exclusive rights, and that Bandanese leaders, who were experienced diplomats, did not simply fail to grasp the content. They were negotiating within an Asian diplomatic tradition in which commitments were relational and revisable, and they were facing a counterpart that increasingly treated commitments as permanent legal obligations backed by force. Also important is the fact that no single leader could bind all communities. The Dutch were therefore demanding something that Bandanese political structure could not deliver, since no authority in Banda could promise that every community would sell only to the Company.

The sequence of agreements should be reconstructed carefully rather than described as a single uniform surrender of trading rights. The 1602 arrangement is often discussed as promising the Dutch preferential or exclusive purchasing in exchange for protection, but the actual transaction occurred before the VOC had built a durable apparatus on the islands. Subsequent negotiations in 1605 involved particular leaders and settlements; Lontor did not simply enter every agreement on the same terms as Neira or its neighbors. Treaties translated between different political expectations could become points of conflict precisely because the Dutch treated the signature of a spokesman as enforceable against people who had not necessarily authorized him to bind them. Military aid also imposed reciprocal expectations that might lapse if the promised aid failed to materialize. Rather than assigning a single motive to all Bandanese sellers, students should consider households that needed provisions, leaders fearful of rival communities, traders maintaining connections with Java, and others who questioned the terms. The documentary issue is not whether a Dutch contract exists but what its scope was, whether consent was coerced, what could be translated accurately, and which communities considered themselves parties. Dutch assertions of treaty breach are historical evidence for the company's legal reasoning; they are not by themselves proof that the Bandanese understood or accepted a permanent loss of commercial choice.

Division II · Section 78.6

Pieter Willemszoon Verhoeff and the Crisis of 1609

In 1609 the VOC sent a large expedition to Banda under Admiral Pieter Willemszoon Verhoeff, with instructions to secure the Company's commercial position and, if necessary, to establish a fortified base. The expedition is the first clear sign that the Company was prepared to move from commerce to territorial presence. Verhoeff arrived with a substantial fleet and with the intention of obtaining an agreement for exclusive purchase and permission to build a fort on Neira. Bandanese leaders were divided. Some accepted the Dutch presence, hoping for protection and trade; others refused to permit a permanent European fortress on their island, recognizing what a fort would mean for their autonomy.

The Dutch proceeded to construct a fort on Banda Neira, which they named Fort Nassau, on the site of an earlier Portuguese fortification. Whether the Bandanese leaders had consented to this construction, and under what understanding, is disputed in the sources. Dutch accounts describe permission or at least acquiescence; Bandanese leaders later claimed that they had been forced or misled. Relations deteriorated while the fort was being built. In May 1609 Verhoeff and a number of his men were killed in a confrontation with Bandanese on Neira. The precise sequence of events, including whether the meeting where the Dutch were attacked was a planned ambush or an escalation of a dispute, cannot be established securely from the contemporary accounts, which come largely from the Dutch side.

The subsequent Dutch portrayal of the killing is itself an important historical subject. The Company presented Verhoeff's death as treacherous murder by Bandanese who had broken their word, and cited it as justification for harsher measures. This framing proved durable, and it appeared in later official and popular histories as a cause of later conquest. A critical reading separates what the evidence supports from this later use. The evidence supports the conclusion that a violent clash occurred, that the Dutch commander and a significant number of his men were killed, and that the Company responded with reprisals and a hardened attitude. It does not support the conclusion that the Bandanese as a whole were guilty of treachery, or that this single event made conquest inevitable. A Dutch commander had imposed a fortress on a community that had not clearly consented, and the violence that followed was the product of a relationship already tilted toward coercion.

Division II · Section 78.7

The Conquest of Pulau Ai and the Escalation of Violence, 1615–1616

After 1609 the Company maintained Fort Nassau on Neira, but its control of the islands remained limited. Bandanese communities continued to sell to other buyers, and English merchants had begun to appear on Ai and Run. The Company's response during these years combined diplomacy, blockade, and renewed use of force. Ai, the island lying west of the central group, became the first major target of a Dutch campaign to take territory outright.

In 1615 the Dutch launched an assault on Ai that failed. Bandanese defenders, familiar with the island's terrain and fortified positions, repelled the attack and inflicted losses on the attackers. The defeat is a reminder that Dutch military superiority in this period was not overwhelming and was not assured. The Bandanese fought effectively on land they knew, and the Dutch had to rely on ships, firearms, and reinforcement to compensate for the difficulties of the terrain. In 1616 the Dutch returned and captured Ai. The victory was achieved through a combination of naval power, firearms, siege tactics, and the exhaustion of defenders, and it was followed by the displacement of many of the island's inhabitants.

Ai thus became an early testing ground. Here the Company began to treat conquered territory as something to be occupied and held rather than simply visited and traded with. The existing commercial arrangements, under which Bandanese households sold to several buyers, were replaced by Dutch control of the island and its groves. The pattern previewed what would be attempted on a far larger scale in 1621: the seizure of land, the removal or subjugation of the population, and the substitution of company command for Bandanese commercial autonomy. It is also essential to note that Ai was not an isolated episode. It established precedent within the Company's own thinking, so that officials could point to Ai as evidence that conquest was feasible and effective.

The conquest of Ai also illustrates the interplay of European and Asian military resources. The Company's forces included European soldiers and sailors but were supplemented, as in later campaigns, by Asian auxiliaries. The use of such troops reflected both the Company's shortage of European manpower in Asia and its practice of recruiting soldiers across the region, a subject examined further in Chapters 79 and 91. Its fleet could blockade, bombard, and land troops, but it could not easily penetrate dense, steep terrain without heavy losses, and this limitation shaped later Dutch tactics of siege and starvation.

The conquest of Ai was particularly revealing about the limits of conquest as a productive strategy. After Dutch forces took the island, many inhabitants had been killed, fled, or lost the ability to control their groves. An occupying force could confiscate trees, yet it could not simply command them to bear more fruit or replace the knowledge of experienced growers. The Dutch National Archives describes the importation of captives from Siau to work there, alongside resistance by those forced into labor and difficulty cultivating an unfamiliar crop. Thus Ai supplied a military precedent but also an early warning that removing inhabitants could undermine the labor and knowledge on which the desired commodity depended. Geographic isolation further complicated the operation: taking a landing place did not automatically secure all boats or prevent news reaching other islands. Reports of people fleeing by sea must be read with attention to maritime conditions, since panic, overcrowding, and rough waters were themselves deadly. The experience of Ai made the reorganization of land conceivable to the Dutch, but it also demonstrated why colonial settlement depended on coerced labor and continuing supplies from outside. Its significance lies in both dimensions—the precedent invoked for the subsequent conquest of Lontor and the operational failures that contemporary advocates of force often underestimated.

Division II · Section 78.8

Pulau Run, the English East India Company, and the Struggle over Monopoly

The English East India Company, chartered in 1600 and thus older than the VOC, had interests in the spice trade and sought access to Banda. English ships visited the islands, and by 1616 English agents had established positions on Run and Ai. Their presence mattered enormously. English merchants offered the Bandanese an alternative buyer, and thus a way to resist exclusive Dutch demands. They also offered cloth and other goods, and in some cases military encouragement. For Bandanese leaders, the English were a counterweight, a means of preserving the commercial pluralism that sustained their autonomy.

The three parties had distinct interests. The Bandanese wanted to continue selling to multiple buyers at competitive prices, to retain control of their land, and to avoid permanent foreign garrisons. They welcomed English trade as a means to those ends, and Bandanese leaders on Run entered arrangements with English agents which the English later interpreted as a transfer of sovereignty to the English Crown. Whether Bandanese leaders understood these arrangements in that sense, or as another alliance of the familiar relational kind, is open to the same question that arose over the Dutch treaties. The English Company wanted a share of the spice trade and a base in the East Indies but lacked the resources to protect Banda against Dutch force, and its small garrison on Run, led for a time by Nathaniel Courthope, held out under Dutch pressure for several years. The VOC wanted exclusive control and saw the English as interlopers who encouraged Bandanese defiance.

Anglo-Dutch rivalry encouraged coercive Dutch policies by raising the stakes. Every Bandanese sale to the English undermined the monopoly the Company sought, and every English fortification threatened to make the islands permanently contested. Dutch officials argued that Bandanese resistance was being sustained by English support and that only conquest would end it. At the same time the two companies' governments in Europe were negotiating over the East Indies trade, with formal arrangements in 1619 aimed at cooperation that the Company and its English counterpart found difficult to apply on the ground. The distinction between European diplomatic claims and Bandanese political rights is crucial. When Dutch and English representatives argued over who held rights in Banda, the Bandanese, whose islands they were, were largely excluded from the discussion. European agreements between companies could not confer rights that Bandanese communities had not granted, yet both companies treated them as if they could.

The ultimate resolution of the competing European claims lies well beyond this chapter. It is enough to note, as retrospective context, that the English Company's position on Run eventually collapsed, and that in the Anglo-Dutch settlement of 1667 England formally relinquished its claims to Run in exchange for Dutch recognition of English control of other territory, including the settlement later known as New York. That later exchange illustrates how thoroughly Banda's fate was treated as a matter between European powers. For the Bandanese, the more immediate consequence of English involvement was that it prolonged resistance, intensified Dutch fear of encirclement, and strengthened the arguments of those within the Company who held that only the destruction of Bandanese independence would secure the monopoly.

Division 3 — The Conquest and Destruction of Bandanese Society in 1621

Division III · Section 78.9

Jan Pieterszoon Coen and the Decision to Conquer Banda

The decision to conquer Banda was not made in a single moment, and it was not made by a single man. Between roughly 1612 and 1621 company officials in Asia and directors in the Netherlands debated how to secure a nutmeg monopoly. Their correspondence reveals a spectrum of positions. Some officers favored continued negotiation, supplemented by blockade and fortification. Others argued that Bandanese leaders would never reliably honor exclusive contracts and that the Company needed to take control of the islands themselves. Within this second group were senior officials such as Jacques l'Hermite, who advocated subjugation and, in some formulations, the displacement or replacement of Bandanese communities. Jan Pieterszoon Coen, who rose to become Governor-General at Batavia in 1619, was the most forceful and influential advocate of this approach, and he had the position to carry it out.

Several factors converged. First, the Company's directors in the Netherlands were under pressure from shareholders to produce returns, and the high price of nutmeg in Europe offered a strong incentive to secure the supply. Second, the experience of the previous decade suggested that treaties alone did not deliver exclusivity: Bandanese leaders continued to sell to other buyers. Third, the English presence on Run and Ai offered a standing alternative. Fourth, the success on Ai in 1616 suggested that conquest was practicable. And fifth, Coen and his allies were convinced that the only secure monopoly was one that eliminated the possibility of competition by placing production under company control.

It is essential to distinguish three levels of responsibility. Individual officials such as l'Hermite and Coen advocated particular policies in their letters and reports, and their arguments shaped the debate. Company directors in the Netherlands authorized, with varying degrees of enthusiasm and information, a strategy of forceful monopoly, and they approved the use of arms in general terms, though they did not plan the specific form that the 1621 operation took. Operational decisions were made in Asia, and after Coen became governor-general in 1619, he had the authority to organize and lead the expedition. The directors' general authorization, the advocacy of senior officers, and Coen's execution together produced the outcome. The chapter does not diminish Coen's personal role, but it avoids reducing the conquest to one man's will: a corporate structure produced the incentives, authorized the means, and tolerated the results.

The central question is why the Company came to regard independent Bandanese commerce as incompatible with its monopoly objectives. The answer lies in the logic of the monopoly itself. A monopsony in nutmeg required that the Bandanese sell to the Company alone. But the Bandanese had both the ability and the incentive to sell elsewhere, since competing buyers paid more or offered goods the Company did not. Preventing this required either permanently policing every Bandanese transaction, which was impossible given the islands' geography and the Company's limited resources, or removing the Bandanese ability to choose, by taking the land and the labor. Once the Company's officials framed the problem this way, the survival of independent Bandanese communities appeared not as a partner to be negotiated with but as an obstacle to be removed.

Cause and Effect

From Commercial Monopoly Demands to Military Conquest

Long-term causes

European demand for spice and the very high markup between Banda and European markets; the concentration of nutmeg production on a few small islands; the chartered company's quasi-sovereign powers and shareholder pressure for returns; and the existing Asian commercial networks that gave the Bandanese alternatives to Dutch buyers.

Short-term causes

Disputes over the meaning of the 1602 and 1605 agreements; Bandanese sales to English and Asian merchants; the violence of 1609 and its later use as justification; the precedent of Ai in 1616; and the rise of aggressive advocates, including Coen, to senior command.

Immediate trigger and context

Persistent failure to enforce exclusive purchase; Anglo-Dutch rivalry; and Coen's authority as governor-general after 1619 to organize and lead an expedition, together with the company's accusations that Bandanese leaders had broken their obligations.

Event and effects

The invasion of Banda Besar in 1621, the execution of leaders, the killing, starvation, expulsion, and enslavement of the population, and the confiscation of land. Longer-term effects include the perken system, slave plantation labor, refugee communities, and a company monopoly maintained for generations.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the conquest. Commercial incentives created pressure, but the specific choice to destroy rather than accommodate Bandanese society was a political and moral decision, taken by identifiable officials who rejected alternatives. Historians also disagree about how far the final form of the operation was planned in the Netherlands or developed in Asia.

The documentary chronology makes clear that calls for extreme measures preceded the invasion by years. Jacques l'Hermite's proposals in 1612 and Coen's subsequent reasoning treated continued independent commerce as incompatible with lasting exclusivity. By 1615 the directors had endorsed plans for subjugation and population replacement in general terms, even though the precise course of the 1621 campaign was determined later by officials in Asia. This matters for responsibility: the operation cannot be interpreted solely as a spontaneous reaction to one alleged conspiracy discovered during the conquest. The comparison with less destructive alternatives should also distinguish feasibility from virtue. A system of continued competing purchases would have sacrificed the company's proposed monopoly; diplomacy might have required accepting limited and revocable privileges; price competition would have demanded paying what sellers judged acceptable. Company officers selected measures that secured exclusive power rather than alternatives that preserved local independence. Yet commercial calculation did not dictate a unique military outcome; the willingness to employ systematic removal and enslavement was a choice made in a specific institutional culture. Explaining that culture does not justify it. The historical problem is to trace how financial goals became translated into coercive instructions, and how official decisions then became actions affecting entire communities.

Division III · Section 78.10

The Invasion of Banda Besar in 1621

Coen assembled the expedition at Batavia in the closing months of 1620 and the early weeks of 1621. The fleet that sailed was large by the standards of the Company's Asian operations. Commonly cited figures place it at about thirteen vessels and some sixteen hundred men, though numbers differ among sources and should be treated as approximate. The force included Dutch soldiers and sailors, Asian soldiers, and a contingent of Japanese mercenaries who had been recruited in the region and who would play a notorious role in the executions described in Section 78.11. Enslaved and conscripted laborers also accompanied the fleet. The expedition reached Banda in February 1621.

The target was Banda Besar (Lontor), the largest nutmeg island and the home of numerous settlements, many of them fortified. Dutch forces landed and attacked villages. Bandanese leaders had been negotiating, and in some cases offering concessions, as the fleet approached, and contemporary accounts describe a mixture of negotiation and fighting in the first stages of the campaign. Bandanese defenders resisted, using their knowledge of the hills and their fortified positions. In many places the Dutch took villages quickly, in part because the Bandanese could not match European firepower, and in part because the arrival of such a large force produced division, panic, and retreat. As Bandanese communities lost their lowland settlements, many withdrew into the interior and the mountains, from which they continued resistance.

The development of the campaign from an initial conquest of settlements into a prolonged operation against communities resisting from the interior is crucial. Dutch forces could seize villages and burn them, but they had greater difficulty reaching and defeating people entrenched in rugged terrain. The Dutch therefore adopted tactics that exploited their control of the coast and the harbor: they cut off food and water, burned crops and settlements, and waited. This strategy of siege and starvation is documented in the Company's own records, and Section 78.12 returns to it. Here the point is that the conquest was not a rapid set-piece battle but a sustained campaign of attrition whose effects fell heavily on civilians, including women, children, and the elderly who had fled with the fighting men.

Military events must be kept analytically separate from the later killing, enslavement, and expulsion of civilians. The initial landings, battles, and sieges were acts of war that, by the standards of the period, were conducted by military forces against armed defenders. The subsequent policies of execution after interrogation, deliberate starvation of sheltering populations, forced deportation, and enslavement were decisions of a different kind: they were policies directed at people who were not necessarily combatants, undertaken to remove the population and to reorganize the islands. The distinction does not excuse the violence of the campaign itself, which was waged to impose a commercial monopoly. It does, however, allow students to see that 1621 involved both a military conquest and, within it, a program of population removal.

The armed force that arrived in 1621 was drawn from more than one recruiting and service system. Contemporary and later accounts combine Company soldiers and seamen with Asian auxiliaries, coerced workers, and Japanese troops, producing different totals depending on what a historian counts. S. J. Miske records nearly 1,200 company sailors and soldiers alongside more than one hundred Japanese and Southeast Asian mercenaries, while other reconstructions give larger totals when additional personnel and vessels are included. The disagreement is therefore partly a matter of categories, not simply of careless arithmetic. Once aboard the islands, the campaign required interpreters, food transport, occupation of anchorages, and control of routes between villages and interior refuge areas. An officer's claim to have captured a settlement may conceal how long people continued to resist outside it. Documents describing blocked access to food, shelter in mountainous fortifications, and damaged crops show that control of the coast was used against the population as a whole. A military chronology should consequently indicate not only when forts or villages changed hands but also when civilians were displaced, what supply routes became unavailable, and how the siege continued after an apparent battlefield victory. The violence of removal was not merely an accidental aftermath of the landing; it was bound up with how the occupation was consolidated.

Division III · Section 78.11

The Execution of the Orang Kaya and the Destruction of Political Leadership

After the initial conquest of villages on Banda Besar, Bandanese leaders came to Neira to negotiate with the Company. According to Company records, 47 orang kaya were detained, accused of having conspired against the Dutch, broken earlier agreements, and plotted with the English or with other enemies. They were interrogated, and torture was used. On 8 May 1621, 44 of these detainees were executed at Fort Nassau on Neira, in a public ceremony. Japanese soldiers in company service carried out the killings, which included beheading and the mutilation of bodies, and the heads of some of the leaders were displayed on stakes.

The legal and political character of the proceedings requires careful analysis. The Company staged the executions as the punishment of conspirators who had violated their agreements and threatened Dutch lives. Interrogations produced statements by the accused that the Company recorded as confessions to a plot. But the circumstances in which those statements were obtained, under detention, in a language and legal framework alien to the accused, and under torture, make it impossible to treat them as reliable evidence of an actual conspiracy. Historians do not accept coerced confessions as proof. What the records establish with confidence is that the Company arrested, tortured, and executed a large group of leaders, that it did so in a public and theatrical way designed to terrorize, and that it justified the action as a response to a conspiracy of which the only evidence was produced under duress.

The consequences were political as well as human. The orang kaya were the individuals through whom Bandanese communities had negotiated, made decisions, managed trade, and held land in trust for their groups. Killing 44 of them at once removed a large portion of the recognized leadership of the islands, along with their knowledge of landholding, genealogy, trade relationships, and ritual obligations. It also sent a message to surviving communities that resistance would be met with extreme violence. By destroying the political leadership the Company created a vacuum in which it could impose its own authority, and it removed those who might have negotiated the terms of a surrender or contested the Company's claims to the land.

Historians have emphasized that the executions cannot be separated from the broader policy of population removal. They were not an isolated punishment but part of a campaign that aimed to transform Bandanese society. The evidence that survives from the Company's side, including journals and decisions of its council, shows that senior officials understood the killings as instrumental to their aims. The sources do not tell us how Bandanese families experienced the loss, how the survivors reacted, or what the executed leaders themselves said, other than through the filtered and coerced record produced by their captors.

The company record describes forty-seven detained leaders and forty-four executions, but the difference between these numbers has a history: modern reconstructions report that two prisoners died during interrogation and another killed himself. A simple statement that three were 'found not guilty' would therefore be false. The archival descriptions also identify victims occupying different offices, including religious and harbor roles, reminding us that the accused were not interchangeable chiefs. The employment of Japanese soldiers in the killings is supported by Adam Clulow's work on Japanese military service within VOC campaigns. This detail should not displace responsibility from the officials who ordered the sentence: the Dutch authorities controlled the judicial process and directed the execution. Nor does the presence of an official interrogation mean that the accused received a fair trial. Coerced admissions cannot establish independent agreement among the defendants or the truth of the alleged plot. A careful narrative should emphasize the asymmetry of documentation: the officials recorded accusations and verdicts, while no comparable free testimony from the accused survives. Execution deprived survivors of people who represented their communities in negotiation, but one should avoid assuming that all indigenous political activity ceased overnight. Leadership, kinship, and survival strategies continued elsewhere, especially among those who escaped into neighboring maritime networks.

Division III · Section 78.12

Mass Killing, Starvation, Enslavement, and Depopulation

The conquest of 1621 produced a human catastrophe whose elements must be distinguished. Dutch forces burned settlements and groves. People were killed in the fighting and in reprisals. Others, who had withdrawn into the mountains of Banda Besar, were deliberately deprived of food and water, and many died of starvation, exposure, and disease; Company records document decisions about this strategy. Large numbers of survivors were taken captive and deported, many to Batavia, where they were enslaved or put to labor. Others escaped by sea in small boats to nearby islands, and some remained on Banda, either hiding, enslaved, or reabsorbed into the new plantation economy. The aggregate result was the destruction of most of the preconquest Bandanese society on the islands.

Quantifying these outcomes is difficult, and this section discusses the evidence carefully. Estimates of Banda’s pre-conquest population vary widely, including figures of 2,500–3,000 attributed to Tomé Pires around 1515, 10,000–12,000 attributed to Johann Verken, and the frequently cited 15,000 in the later Gijsels/Valentijn source tradition. None is a verified census, and they refer to different times and uncertain populations. It derives from European estimates of uneven quality, and it is uncertain how many of those counted were permanent residents as opposed to foreign merchants and seasonal visitors. Estimates of the numbers killed, deported, and enslaved also vary. Commonly cited figures place the number of Bandanese deported as slaves at several hundred, with some accounts giving around 800, but these figures come from company-era sources whose accuracy is uncertain. The number who escaped to other islands is not known precisely and should not be reduced to an unsupported exact figure. After the conquest, early Dutch observers recorded only a few hundred or, in some accounts, roughly a thousand Bandanese living on the islands, though the reliability and the categories of those counts are uncertain.

For analytical clarity, students should keep five categories separate. Deaths include people killed in combat and in reprisals, executed, or dead of starvation, exposure, and disease. Deportations include those removed from the islands by the Company. Enslavement overlaps with deportation but also includes people held as laborers on Banda itself. Local survival includes those who remained, in hiding or in bondage. Refugee flight includes people who escaped and founded communities elsewhere. Because the sources were not designed to count these categories separately, and because the categories overlap, it is not possible to assign confident numbers to each. What can be said is that a large majority of the preconquest population did not remain living as free Bandanese on their own land in the years that followed.

The characterization of these events as genocide rests on documented policy and its consequences, not on a single demographic figure. The Dutch National Archives itself uses the term for the events of 1621, and a number of historians do the same. The evidence includes the Company's recorded decisions to remove the inhabitants of Banda Besar, the deliberate use of starvation, the execution of the leadership, the mass deportation and enslavement of survivors, the destruction of settlements and groves, and the intention, documented in correspondence, to replace the population. Historians who use the term emphasize that the Company aimed to destroy the Bandanese as a distinct society on their land. Some caution is warranted about terminology: the legal definition of genocide dates from the twentieth century and cannot be applied retroactively as a matter of law, and scholars differ over how best to name early modern events of this kind. But the weight of evidence for intentional destruction of most of a community through killing, starvation, and forced removal is substantial, and it does not depend on whether the pre-conquest population was 13,000 or 15,000.

Banda in 1621: Who Experienced the Conquest, and How

Bandanese communities and leaders: Faced with a fleet that could not be matched, communities fought, negotiated, fled to the hills, or sought refuge by sea. For leaders, the question was how to preserve people and land; for villagers, survival, food, and the safety of families were immediate concerns. The destruction of groves and settlements threatened not only livelihoods but spiritual and kinship ties to the land.
VOC officials: Senior officials saw the conquest as the necessary means to a monopoly their directors expected. They described Bandanese resistance as treachery, wrote of removal and replacement as practical solutions, and measured success in secured trade. Some officers had doubts, but the policy proceeded under the authority of the governor-general.

Refugees: Those who escaped by sea left land, groves, and kin, and faced an uncertain reception in places where they had to build new lives. Their decisions to flee shaped the survival of Bandanese language and culture, and some maintained the hope of return.
Enslaved survivors: Those deported or held on Banda as laborers experienced separation from kin, loss of status, and forced labor. Their agricultural and commercial skills were exploited by the very people who had destroyed their communities.
Merchants and rival powers: English agents and Asian merchants lost access to Banda spice and watched as the Company established its monopoly. Their reactions ranged from protest to accommodation, and, in the English case, an eventual diplomatic settlement made without Bandanese participation.

A useful counterpoint to the familiar preconquest population estimate is the more precisely documented journey of deportees. Miske reports a group of 789 people sent toward Batavia, of whom 176 died at sea; those are distinct counts rather than an estimate of all those captured or killed. The same research describes people sheltering in the hills and reports thousands of graves encountered after Dutch forces entered fortified positions. These sources establish a disaster without yielding a complete mortality register. Indeed, attempts to reconstruct population from a single number risk overlooking children born or dying later, survivors who returned, persons fleeing without leaving records, and people held in bondage outside Banda. Conversely, it is unwise to subtract a later reported island population from an uncertain earlier estimate and label the entire difference deaths. For historical interpretation the decisive evidence includes intentional removal, blockade, starvation policy, and the destruction of institutions and communities—not an apparently exact percentage. The term genocide should be introduced through the documented acts and intent, and its modern legal and historiographical meanings should be distinguished. This method maintains moral clarity while respecting the limits of fragmentary seventeenth-century enumeration.

Division 4 — Plantation Reorganization, Slavery, and Colonial Production

Division IV · Section 78.13

The Confiscation and Redistribution of Bandanese Land

Conquest gave the Company physical control of the nutmeg groves, but control of groves was not the same as a functioning production system. The VOC claimed the land of Banda by right of conquest, treating the former inhabitants as having forfeited their rights through rebellion. It then began dividing the nutmeg-producing lands into designated agricultural plots known as perken (singular perk), to be held by individuals under conditions set by the Company. The conversion of an inherited landscape of groves, managed by kin groups with customary rights, into numbered plots allocated by an external authority was a profound transformation. It replaced a system in which land rights were embedded in lineage, ritual, and community with one in which they were granted, recorded, and revocable at the Company's pleasure.

The reorganization began on Ai, where the Company was able to move relatively quickly. An agreement concerning the division and allocation of nutmeg land on Ai, dated 5 May 1621, is among the earliest documented steps, and correspondence from Martinus Sonck, who governed Banda in these years, records subsequent adjustments. Reorganization on Banda Besar, a larger island where the conquest had been more extended and where many of the groves were in disrepair after the fighting, proceeded more slowly. Later accounts often mention around sixty-eight plots across the producing islands, but this is not a verified count for the early 1620s: the number, boundaries, and sizes changed over time. Miske reconstructs many initial plots as roughly 1.8–2.8 hectares, rather than about one hectare.

The chapter must stress that this reorganization was a process extending through the 1620s, not an administrative transformation completed in 1621. Surveying land, recruiting people willing to hold plots, finding labor, rehabilitating damaged groves, and organizing supplies of food all took years. Early arrangements were improvised, disputes over boundaries and entitlements were frequent, and the Company repeatedly adjusted its rules. The image of an efficient, planned colonial plantation system created overnight is a retrospective fiction. In practice, the Company was improvising within a landscape it had devastated, and with a labor force it was still assembling.

It is also important to distinguish different kinds of rights. The Company claimed ultimate ownership by conquest. The holders of plots, the perkeniers, possessed them under leasehold-like conditions, with rights that could be inherited or sold in some periods but which were subject to Company regulation, and with obligations to deliver the crop to the Company. The former inhabitants, whose land had been seized, were not compensated and had no recognized rights, a fact that became central to later Bandanese memory. Some early land rights went to groups other than European colonists, such as the Mardijkers discussed in the next section. The architecture of these rights demonstrates that the monopoly was built on dispossession: the legal and economic structure presupposed that the original owners had been removed.

Historical Vocabulary
Perk and Perkenier

Definition. A perk (plural perken) was a designated plot of nutmeg-producing land, established by the VOC after 1621. A perkenier was the person to whom the Company granted the right to operate a plot. The word comes from Dutch usage for a bounded plot of land.

Historical context. Perkeniers were typically Europeans and other individuals favored by the Company, many of them former Company employees. They undertook to maintain the groves, to organize labor, and to deliver the harvest to the Company at prices the Company set, in exchange for the right to hold and, in time, sometimes to inherit the plot and to draw income from it. The system is often called the perkeniersstelsel.

Why it matters. The perk system combined private landholding with company control of purchase, so that the Company could claim a monopoly without directly managing production. It allowed the VOC to shift the burden of supervision and capital onto private holders while retaining the commercial advantage.

Potential misunderstanding. Perkeniers were not independent farmers or free-market planters. They operated within a system of regulated deliveries and fixed prices. Nor should the system be assumed to have been efficient or stable; the Company repeatedly complained of mismanagement and illicit sales.

Division IV · Section 78.14

Perkeniers, Colonial Landholding, and VOC Commercial Control

The perk system was designed to give the Company what it most wanted: control over the sale of nutmeg and mace, without having to manage every grove itself. In exchange for the right to a plot, perkeniers were obliged to deliver the crop to the Company. The Company in turn set the prices it paid, which were a small fraction of the price the spice would fetch in Europe, and supplied the perkeniers with provisions, especially rice, cloth, and other necessities that Banda could not produce. This arrangement recreated, in a new form, the old Bandanese dependence on imported food, now with the Company as the sole supplier and therefore with extraordinary leverage over the settlers and their enslaved workers.

The relationship between land rights, deliveries, prices, debt, and provisioning shaped the entire system. Because perkeniers received fixed prices for their crop and had to buy supplies from or through the Company, they could fall into debt, which gave the Company further control. At the same time perkeniers had incentives to evade the system. They could sell spice illicitly to other buyers, mix poorer-quality nutmeg into deliveries, and misreport harvests. Company records complain repeatedly of such practices, and the Company responded with inspections, regulations, and penalties. The monopoly, in other words, was never as complete as its architects imagined; it was a continuing contest between company officials and the settlers whom it had empowered.

Those who held plots were not all European leaseholders. Among the early recipients of land rights were the Mardijkers, a community of formerly enslaved Asian Christians, who in the Company's Asian settlements occupied an intermediate legal and social position between slave and free. Their inclusion shows how the Company drew on the social categories of its Asian colonial world, including those developed at Batavia (Chapter 79), to populate Banda. It also underlines the plural origins of the settler community: the new landholding class included Europeans, Mardijkers, and others, and was never simply Dutch.

What the system accomplished was a shift in the form of control. The Company did not need to manage cultivation directly, since perkeniers supervised labor and tended groves; it needed only to monitor deliveries, to police the islands against smuggling, and to control the supply of provisions. This arrangement allowed a corporation with limited manpower in Asia to maintain a monopoly with relatively few officials. At the same time it created a settler class with its own interests, which sometimes aligned with the Company's and sometimes did not, and it embedded a slave-labor system, examined in the next section, at the center of the nutmeg economy.

The detailed financial obligations of early planters reveal why acquiring a plot did not necessarily produce wealth. Company rules could combine a share of harvest, assessments associated with the number of enslaved people, obligatory sales, and fixed purchasing prices. These arrangements left the VOC powerful as landholder, rule-maker, supplier, and the principal authorized purchaser, while planters carried the costs of feeding and supervising workers and maintaining productive trees. Early correspondence, as reconstructed by Miske, repeatedly describes debts and dissatisfaction rather than a prosperous and autonomous settler society. This is why the word 'private' must be handled carefully: a leaseholder was not free to select any buyer or set the price as an independent agricultural entrepreneur might expect. At the same time the company did not exercise perfect control over every transaction, and officials worried about waste, negligence, illicit trade, and loss of workers. The social composition of the perkeniers also changed over time. The early involvement of Mardijkers, former VOC personnel, and other people should not be erased by projecting the later nineteenth-century image of a homogeneous plantation elite backward. The new economy was a negotiated and often conflict-ridden hierarchy inside colonial power, built upon the violent removal of earlier land rights.

Division IV · Section 78.15

Enslaved Laborers and the Social Organization of Plantation Life

The perk system rested on enslaved labor. Because the conquest had removed or killed most of the free Bandanese population, perkeniers needed workers, and the Company supplied them. Some of the enslaved were Bandanese survivors who had been captured, or who had been deported and later returned to the islands as laborers. Many more were brought from elsewhere in Asia, purchased or seized by Company agents and by private traders, and transported to Banda. Over time the enslaved population was drawn from a wide range of regions in South and Southeast Asia, and the nutmeg estates became, in the assessment of scholars such as Phillip Winn, one of the very few cases in Southeast Asian history of a plantation economy based on slave labor on European-owned estates, a pattern more familiar in the Atlantic world. Anthony Reid's influential comparative work had suggested that such agricultural slavery was rare in Southeast Asia, and Banda stands as a notable exception.

The enslaved on Banda were of different legal categories. Some were owned privately by perkeniers; others were owned by the Company and allotted to estates, a distinction which created tensions over discipline, responsibility, and supervision. Perkeniers sometimes exchanged older or weaker private slaves for stronger company-allotted workers, and sometimes directed Company-allotted workers to tasks, such as growing food or serving in the household, other than nutmeg production. The Company complained of such practices, and the competition over control of labor was a continuing feature of the system.

Work on the estates followed the rhythms of the nutmeg tree. Nutmeg is a tree crop and does not require the intensive annual planting and harvesting of sugar or rice, so that the demands on labor were seasonal and in some respects less continuous than on other plantation crops, though harvest, processing, and maintenance were laborious. Tasks included gathering fallen fruit, splitting the fruit to separate seed and mace, drying and sorting the product, tending the shade trees and young plants, clearing undergrowth, and producing food for the estate. Enslaved workers also performed domestic labor in the settlers' households, built and maintained structures, and worked as boatmen and carriers. Gender shaped the work: enslaved women labored in households and in processing, and their reproductive and family roles affected the composition of the enslaved population, though the evidence for details is uneven.

Family life under slavery was precarious. Families could be separated by sale, and the legal status of children followed that of their mothers in most cases. Discipline, which included corporal punishment, was applied by owners and by Company officials, and the small size of the islands meant that escape was difficult, though people tried. Resistance took various forms: flight by canoe to other islands, work slowdowns, theft of spice for sale to outsiders, and the preservation of language, ritual, and community in the face of coercion. Archaeological and historical research on the plantations also shows that enslaved communities created new foodways and cultural forms by combining practices from many origins, and Bandanese today use the word campur, "mixed," to describe the creolized culture of the islands.

The role of indigenous knowledge is important. The newly enslaved Bandanese were not merely sources of muscle. They were the people who knew how to manage nutmeg groves, how to read the signs of tree health, how to harvest and process the crop to maintain quality, and how to deal with the island's soils and winds. The settlers, many of them former soldiers and officials with no background in nutmeg cultivation, lacked these skills, and the Company's own records acknowledge the dependence. Scholars argue that the colonial system could not have functioned without Bandanese expertise, which was extracted through coercion. The irony, and the cruelty, is that the destruction of Bandanese society was followed by reliance on surviving Bandanese knowledge.

The enslaved population was not a single undifferentiated group. One census discussed in Miske's research records substantial numbers of Bandanese survivors and laborers taken from the Malabar coast and elsewhere, alongside a much smaller free European population. The figures depend on the administrative categories used, but they make clear that the new labor system linked Banda to a wider Indian Ocean market in coerced people. Workers could have different languages, religions, household ties, experience in cultivation, and opportunities to escape. The company's procurement of labor was itself a problem because workers died, resisted, fled, or required training. Forced workers were not simply agricultural implements: they learned and transmitted techniques, negotiated daily tasks where possible, created relationships, and sometimes escaped. Documentary fragments suggest repeated attempts at flight in the 1620s and 1630s, a reminder that laborers were agents even in a coercive system. A careful social history does not invent their conversations or assign motives to individuals whose testimony is unavailable. Instead, it analyzes what plantation administrators repeatedly complained about, what rules they made to suppress movement, and what those complaints reveal indirectly about workers' actions. This approach recovers some elements of lived experience without pretending that company accounts give direct access to captive voices.

Division IV · Section 78.16

Nutmeg Ecology, Agricultural Practice, and the Limits of Colonial Efficiency

Nutmeg cultivation depends on ecological conditions that no conquest could change. The trees need shade when young, protection from strong winds, well-drained volcanic soils, and years of growth before they produce. Traditional Bandanese practice used larger forest trees, notably the kenari (Canarium) tree, to provide shade and shelter, and managed groves as a kind of agroforestry in which nutmeg grew within a tended forest canopy. Harvesting relied on collecting ripe fruit by hand and with long poles, and processing involved drying seed and mace carefully to preserve quality. Because Banda produced little rice, food had to come from outside or from sago and fish, and a plantation economy that depended on enslaved workers had to feed them, which made the islands dependent on imported provisions.

A central question for historians is whether Dutch rule changed cultivation methods. The conventional view held that the Company introduced a more intensive and rational system: that it organized groves into regular plots, imposed discipline, and thereby increased output. This view has been influential in both Dutch colonial historiography and later economic history. Recent research challenges it. S. J. Miske’s study, published online on 30 September 2026 in the Journal of Southeast Asian Studies, argues that there was a significant degree of continuity in cultivation methods between the precolonial and early colonial periods, and that productivity stagnated or declined after 1621. If that is right, the dramatic reorganization of land and labor changed who owned the groves and who worked them, and how the crop was sold, without fundamentally altering how nutmeg was grown or making the groves more productive.

Several reasons help explain why greater control did not mean greater efficiency. The conquest itself destroyed groves, settlements, and the knowledgeable population. Replacing experienced growers with enslaved workers from elsewhere, and with settlers who lacked expertise, was unlikely to improve cultivation. The Company's pricing system gave perkeniers little incentive to maximize output, since fixed low prices limited the returns on extra effort, and the opportunity to sell illicitly at higher prices encouraged diversion rather than improvement. And because the Company's goal was to maintain a profitable monopoly, not to maximize volume, it had reasons to limit production to keep prices high in European markets. These factors suggest that the system was designed to secure revenue through control, not to raise yields.

The distinction between control and efficiency is the key analytical point of this section. A system can strengthen the ability of an owner to dictate prices and exclude rivals while doing nothing to improve productivity. The VOC's perk system did the former, and the evidence now suggests that it may have failed to do the latter. This is not a defense of the pre-conquest economy as an idyll, nor a claim that all questions are settled; it is a call to separate claims about power from claims about agricultural improvement.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Nutmeg Cultivation before and after Conquest

What Changed

  • Land: customary kin-based holdings were replaced by numbered plots allocated by the Company
  • Labor: a free Bandanese workforce was largely replaced by enslaved people, many brought from elsewhere
  • Sale: an open market with several buyers became a Company monopoly at fixed prices
  • Population and settlement: dispersed village communities gave way to estates, a fort, and a colonial administrative center

What Stayed the Same

  • The ecology of the tree and its needs for shade, shelter, and volcanic soil
  • Core cultivation and processing methods, according to recent research, sustained in part by surviving Bandanese knowledge
  • Dependence on imported food, now supplied through the Company rather than Asian merchants
  • The concentration of production in a few small islands

When and how: Change was abrupt in ownership and population in 1621 but gradual in organization through the 1620s and after. Who experienced what: Bandanese survivors experienced the sharpest rupture, from landowner to enslaved laborer or refugee, while the trees themselves, and the techniques needed to tend them, showed greater continuity. Why it matters: The pattern shows that the most visible transformation was political and social, and that technical continuity can persist beneath dramatic changes in power.

Several early colonial efforts to replace mature trees illustrate the resistance of a living ecosystem to administrative command. Nutmeg seedlings need shade and shelter; planting without the surrounding canopy could cause failure even when an official counted the number of seedlings placed in the ground. Reports from the late 1620s discussed unsuccessful plantings and the need for greater protection, while mature trees could not be rapidly replaced because they took years to yield fruit. Consequently, the company's plan to divide groves into neatly regulated parcels did not amount to the remaking of the forests into open European-style orchards. This is one of the reasons a modern scholar must distinguish a map's orderly geometry from an actual cultivated landscape. Equally, output evidence is difficult to reconcile: preconquest accounts give widely differing crop estimates, and later export records do not perfectly equal annual harvests. Miske's 2026 reassessment uses these limitations explicitly while finding no support for a substantial initial productivity gain. A convincing account should present decline as a plausible interpretation and stagnation as another, not pretend that surviving ledgers measure everything with certainty. The key finding is narrower and stronger: conquest and slavery transferred commercial authority, while evidence for a technical revolution in cultivation is lacking.

Division 5 — Resistance, Survival, and the Historical Meaning of Banda

Division V · Section 78.17

Run, Renewed Resistance, and the Violence of 1622

The conquest of Banda Besar in 1621 did not end Bandanese resistance, nor did it end Company violence. Run, the outlying island where English influence had been strongest, remained contested. Bandanese on Run and the surrounding islands had not submitted, and Company officials feared that resistance elsewhere could revive and that surviving communities might continue to trade with the English or with Asian merchants. The Company continued to treat the islands as hostile territory.

In 1622 Company officials accused Bandanese on Run of conspiracy against Dutch authority. Interrogations followed, again under conditions of coercion, and on 12 September 1622, according to later historical research, approximately 160 free adult men were executed. The figure is reported by historians Tristan Mostert and Wim Manuhutu from VOC material and should be treated as an attributed reconstruction rather than a new census. The events show the pattern of 1621 repeating itself: accusations of conspiracy, forced interrogation, public execution, and the removal of local leadership.

Fear of renewed resistance shaped the early colonial regime. Company officials, aware of how thin their control was, responded to suspected conspiracies with collective punishment, preferring to eliminate potential leaders before they could organize. This reflected both genuine insecurity, since the Company had few troops and much territory to hold, and the logic of the original conquest, in which the survival of independent Bandanese leadership was treated as incompatible with the monopoly. The result was an atmosphere of terror that discouraged open resistance but did not eliminate it.

The violence of 1622 is therefore a necessary part of the story. It shows that 1621 was a decisive turning point but not the end of violence, and it corrects any impression that the conquest concluded in a single campaign. It also underscores that Bandanese resistance continued, in forms that included armed defiance, flight, and the maintenance of contacts with the English and with Asian traders. The system that the Company built, in other words, was erected on a foundation of continued coercion, and not on a settled peace.

The reported executions on Run in 1622 require careful attribution because the island's history is entangled with both Dutch punishment and English claims. Later scholarship by Tristan Mostert and Wim Manuhutu describes an episode on 12 September 1622 in which approximately 160 free adult men were killed after accusations of plotting, a more precise date than the draft's general reference to September. The description is not an independently counted census of all deaths on Run, and it should not obscure those who escaped or were subjected to different forms of coercion. The charges also belong to an established pattern: alleged treachery was often presented as the reason for collective punishment after a conquest already intended to extinguish independent trading relationships. The events must therefore be read together with 1621 rather than as an isolated disturbance. Run's location outside the central harbor had made it important for English trading access, but it did not protect inhabitants indefinitely from a naval power willing to impose its terms by force. Continued violence after Coen's main campaign shows that monopoly required surveillance, punishment, and repeated military commitment. The distinction between securing a claim on a map and governing actual people remains visible even in this small island setting.

Division V · Section 78.18

Bandanese Refugees, Diaspora Communities, and Cultural Survival

Many Bandanese escaped the conquest. Some fled by sea in small boats to nearby islands. Refugee communities took shape in eastern Seram and the small islands near it, in the Kei Islands, in Makassar, and elsewhere in the eastern archipelago. The Kei Islands are particularly important: settlements there, including villages whose names recall Banda, are traditionally remembered as founded by Bandanese refugees, and their inhabitants maintain a historical consciousness of descent from Banda. Makassar, whose openness to merchants of many origins is examined in Chapter 82, also received Bandanese, who joined a cosmopolitan trading community that remained outside Dutch control until the Makassar War.

Exile did not mean abandonment of Banda. Refugee communities cultivated political alliances with rulers in their new homes, sought support against the Dutch, and in some cases tried to regain access to the nutmeg groves or to carry on trade. Their descendants preserved elements of Bandanese language, ritual, and genealogical memory, and some maintained a tradition of coming from Banda and being entitled to it. These communities link the story of Banda to the wider history of Maluku, Seram, and the Kei Islands, and they demonstrate that the conquest produced not only destruction but dispersal and renewal.

Survival also occurred on Banda itself. Some Bandanese remained, hidden, enslaved, or incorporated into the new society, and their knowledge, family ties, and customs persisted in altered forms. The population of the islands that emerged over the following generations was mixed, drawing on survivors, enslaved people brought from elsewhere, Mardijkers, European settlers and their descendants, and later migrants. Present-day Bandanese society reflects this layered history, and the creolized culture of the islands is itself a product of the conquest and its aftermath.

Writing the history of refugees and survivors requires different evidence from the history of the conquest. Company records mention flight and note the loss of population, but they say little about where people went or what became of them. Historians therefore combine the colonial documents with archaeological evidence from the islands and the plantations, linguistic evidence, and carefully contextualized oral traditions in refugee communities. Oral traditions can preserve memories of origin, migration, and loss that no written record contains, but they also reshape the past in the service of present identity and must be read critically, as one source among several. Used cautiously, they restore to the history of Banda the people whom the Company's archive left on the margins.

Historical survival can be traced through languages, settlement memories, ritual ties, and claims of ancestry, but each kind of evidence poses a different question. A later community's recollection of flight may preserve a history of displacement while leaving the precise date or number of voyagers uncertain. Archaeology can identify changing settlement patterns and imported ceramics without identifying every migrant's origin. Family histories may join people displaced in different episodes, including 1609, 1616, and 1621, into one remembered catastrophe. The Kei Islands are especially important in this regard, but it would be misleading to assert that every refugee village there was founded in the same year or by the same group. The Dutch records themselves acknowledge that escape occurred and that some former residents settled beyond colonial control; those admissions can be tested against oral history without assuming the two bodies of evidence answer identical questions. Diaspora should therefore be approached as a continuing set of relationships among communities rather than a one-time departure. It also requires attention to people who stayed on Banda, sometimes under bondage, and participated in the development of later mixed communities. Their continued existence complicates any narrative that portrays genocide as a literal and complete disappearance of a people.

Division V · Section 78.19

Legal Justifications, Colonial Violence, and the Limits of VOC Sources

The Company justified its actions in Banda through several overlapping arguments. It claimed that Bandanese leaders had violated the agreements of 1602 and 1605 by selling to other buyers. It portrayed Bandanese resistance as rebellion against lawful authority, and treated Verhoeff's death in 1609 as evidence of treachery. It accused Bandanese leaders of conspiring with the English and of plotting to kill Company personnel. And Company officials drew on racial and religious characterizations that portrayed the Bandanese as faithless and as unworthy of the consideration due to a European party to a contract. These arguments served to transform an act of commercial aggression into a response to wrongdoing.

Each argument depends on assumptions that deserve scrutiny. The claim of treaty violation assumes that the agreements meant what the Dutch said they meant, a reading that the Bandanese contested and that depended on a European conception of contract. The claim of rebellion assumes that the Company possessed lawful sovereignty over Banda, which the Bandanese had not conceded. The conspiracy accusations rested on testimony obtained under coercion. And the characterizations of Bandanese as treacherous reflected the Company's interest in justifying its own conduct. Historians do not treat such assertions as neutral descriptions, but as arguments made by an interested party, and they weigh them against other evidence.

Contemporary criticism existed. Some Dutch observers, within the Company and in the Netherlands, objected to the scale of the violence and to the treatment of the Bandanese, and the conquest was debated by contemporaries. The extent and influence of such criticism are matters of historical judgment, but its existence shows that the policy was contested at the time and that officials could have chosen differently. Later writers, especially in the Dutch tradition, sometimes defended Coen's conduct as a necessary part of building Dutch commerce and empire, and Coen came to be commemorated in the Netherlands as a founder of Dutch Asian power. That commemoration has itself been challenged by modern historians and by public debate, a reminder that the historical meaning of Banda is still contested.

This section teaches a source-critical method. Students should distinguish administrative decisions, which record what the Company decided; eyewitness testimony, which records what individuals saw, filtered through their purposes; official propaganda, which presents a favorable version; legal arguments, which seek to justify conduct under accepted norms; and modern historical interpretation, which weighs all of these. Each category has its uses and its limits, and the historian's task is to understand what each can and cannot show. In the case of Banda, the most reliable evidence for what the Company intended is often its own internal documents, written for other officials rather than for public defense, and the least reliable guide to what happened is its later justification.

Equally, students should note the silences of the record. Bandanese perspectives appear mostly through the distorting lens of interrogation and through later oral tradition. The experience of the enslaved appears in administrative records of ownership, allotment, and punishment, rarely in their own words. Any account of Banda must therefore be honest about what is known, what is inferred, and what is lost.

Legal arguments about Banda depended on who could make rules, who could interpret them, and what counted as legitimate consent. The VOC was empowered by the Dutch Republic to make treaties and wage war, but that charter was not itself an agreement with Bandanese communities. A Dutch officer who asserted that resistance to a fort constituted rebellion was already assuming an authority whose foundation was contested. Similarly, a proclamation that the company's monopoly was lawful tells us about Dutch justification but not what a Bandanese household had promised. The internal documents are particularly valuable because they can show intentions and the timing of decisions with less public ornament than later commemorative accounts. They remain administrative artifacts, however, and are not disinterested: writers shaped reports for directors, justified expenses, and defended military choices. Historians should compare a resolution with its implementation, check reported dates across copies, and avoid treating an accusation recorded under torture as corroborated fact. Modern research does not remove these difficulties; it makes them more visible by identifying the archive's silences, including the near-absence of free Bandanese testimony. Students should learn that critical reading requires both taking a document seriously and refusing to adopt its categories unexamined.

Division V · Section 78.20

Banda, Genocide, and the Political Economy of Monopoly

The story of Banda shows how the pursuit of commercial monopoly could become a system of conquest, dispossession, and coerced production. The VOC began as one buyer among many and sought exclusivity by treaty. When treaties proved unenforceable, it fortified, blockaded, and finally conquered. In doing so it transformed Bandanese political sovereignty, which was destroyed with the execution of the orang kaya and the occupation of the islands; landownership, which passed from kin groups to Company-allocated plots; labor systems, which shifted from free and dependent Bandanese labor to plantation slavery; population distribution, which changed from dense local settlement to a small, mixed population and a refugee diaspora; and economic relationships, which changed from an open Asian market to a Company monopoly.

The chapter has argued that destruction and dispossession could generate commercial control without necessarily increasing agricultural efficiency. The company's power over the nutmeg trade grew because it had removed competitors and seized the means of production, not because it had found a better way to grow nutmeg. The apparent success of the monopoly, measured in the stability of Company revenue over the following generations, hid a system whose productivity may have stagnated and whose human cost was immense.

Historical Debate

Did the VOC's New Production System Improve Efficiency, or Principally Redistribute Control through Coercion?

The question: After 1621 the Company reorganized nutmeg production into perken worked by enslaved laborers. Did this reorganization make production more efficient and productive, as many earlier historians argued, or did it chiefly transfer control of an existing economy to the Company by coercive means?

Interpretation A — the system intensified and rationalized production: Many historians have argued that the VOC replaced a dispersed, customary system with a more organized and disciplined one, introducing regular plots, supervision, and a clearer chain of accountability, and that this made nutmeg production more intensive and rational. On this view the violence of conquest, however terrible, produced a more efficient economic system. Some interpretations in this vein frame Banda as an early example of a company directly shaping agricultural production, with elements of agrarian capitalism.
Interpretation B — continuity in cultivation and a redistribution of control: Research by S. J. Miske, published online on 30 September 2026 in the Journal of Southeast Asian Studies argues that cultivation methods showed considerable continuity across the conquest, that productivity stagnated or declined, and that the principal change was in who owned the land, who supplied the labor, and who controlled the sale. On this view the Company did not invent a more efficient agriculture; it captured an existing one by killing, expelling, and enslaving its practitioners, and then relied on surviving Bandanese knowledge.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on Company records of land allocation, deliveries, prices, and complaints, on descriptions of cultivation practice, and on comparisons with the period before 1621. They differ in how they read evidence about yields and methods and in how much weight they give to continuity in technique relative to change in ownership and labor.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and, partly, evidence: how to measure productivity from fragmentary figures, how to compare pre- and post-conquest practice when pre-conquest sources are sparse, and how to define efficiency (output per hectare, per worker, or revenue to the Company). It also concerns terminology, since a system can be "rational" from the standpoint of control while being unproductive agriculturally.

Why it matters: If the system improved production, the violence might be portrayed, wrongly or rightly, as the cost of economic progress. If it did not, the violence bought control and little else, which weakens any claim that conquest served broader economic improvement. The answer also shapes how historians understand corporate colonialism, forced commodity production, and the relationship between coercion and capitalism.

Student task: Define "efficiency" in three different ways and explain how each definition would change the evaluation of the perk system. Then identify what evidence, for example yield records, descriptions of cultivation practice, or labor data, would most help to decide between the two interpretations.

Banda belongs in the broader history of European corporate empire, colonial slavery, and forced commodity production. The VOC was a corporation with quasi-sovereign powers, and in Banda it exercised those powers directly over land and people. The perk system, with its enslaved workforce, anticipated features of later plantation economies in the Indies and elsewhere, and its logic, in which the control of a commodity required the control of those who produced it, recurs in later colonial history, including the Cultivation System examined in Chapter 102. It also links to the Company's other monopoly campaigns in the Malukan clove islands (Chapter 71) and to its efforts against rival ports such as Makassar (Chapter 82).

Finally, Banda must be remembered as the history of a people, not only of a commodity. The Bandanese resisted, escaped, rebuilt, and preserved their culture across generations, and their descendants continue to do so. The next chapter turns to Batavia, the city from which Coen directed the conquest of Banda and to which many deported Bandanese were sent. Chapter 79 examines Batavia as the administrative center, port, migrant city, and slave society of the VOC colonial system, and thus shows how the monopoly established by violence in Banda was embedded in a wider apparatus of colonial government.

Banda also teaches a more general lesson about measurement and moral judgment. A successful commercial monopoly can make a company wealthy through restrictions on competitors and control of prices without making workers more productive, communities safer, or food systems more resilient. The financial benefit to shareholders is not equivalent to an economic benefit to everyone involved. Moreover, the destruction of political institutions and forced conversion of landholding into leased estates imposed consequences that cannot be summarized by sales receipts. In the longer regional history, the Company's dependence on coerced labor and imported food linked Banda to other Asian ports and to Batavia's governing institutions. That connection belongs in detail to Chapter 79, just as the wider imperial competition involving Makassar belongs to Chapter 82. The distinct contribution of Chapter 78 is to show the causal chain in Banda itself: a corporate demand for exclusive purchase, the narrowing of local trading choices, armed subjugation, population removal, and the creation of a system in which the VOC governed who could hold land and where crops could be sold. The Bandanese history of resistance and survival runs through every link of that chain, and it prevents the chapter from concluding merely with the company's apparent victory.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the Dutch East India Company transformed an ambition to monopolize nutmeg and mace into the conquest of the Banda Islands, and how that conquest destroyed much of Bandanese society and created a new colonial production system. Its governing argument is that the VOC's monopoly depended on the seizure of political authority, land, and labor, not merely on financial organization or maritime technology.

Banda before the Dutch was a small, geographically concentrated, ecologically specialized, and commercially integrated society. The volcanic islands around the sheltered harbor of Neira, together with Lontor, Ai, and Run, supplied nearly all the world's nutmeg and mace. Their inhabitants lived in autonomous communities led by locally influential orang kaya, with no central government, and they traded with Javanese, Malay, Chinese, and other merchants, importing rice and cloth in exchange for spice. This commercial pluralism gave the Bandanese bargaining power and also defined their vulnerability, since they depended on imported food.

The Dutch arrived in 1599 as one buyer among many, and the creation of the VOC in 1602 transformed their ambitions. Agreements made in 1602 and 1605 became the basis for Dutch claims to exclusive rights, while Bandanese leaders, acting within Southeast Asian traditions of relational alliance, did not understand themselves to have surrendered commercial freedom. No Bandanese leader could in any case bind every community. This mismatch of expectations, and the Company's insistence on enforceable exclusivity, set the course of escalation.

The escalation unfolded through fortification and violence. The building of Fort Nassau and the death of Verhoeff in 1609 hardened Dutch attitudes and supplied a durable justification. The failed assault on Ai in 1615 and the successful conquest in 1616 established a precedent for territorial occupation. English presence on Run and Ai gave the Bandanese an alternative buyer and strengthened the Dutch conviction that independence was intolerable. Throughout, the Company's instructions and strategy authorized coercion before any decisive Bandanese provocation.

The decision to conquer Banda Besar in 1621 was the product of structural incentives and human choices. Shareholder pressure, high European prices, concentrated supply, and the failure of treaties created conditions in which aggressive officials, notably Jan Pieterszoon Coen, who became governor-general in 1619, could argue that only the removal of independent Bandanese communities would secure the monopoly. Responsibility was shared among advocates such as Jacques l'Hermite, directors who authorized force in general terms, and the officers who executed the policy.

The campaign of 1621 combined conventional military conquest with a program of population removal. Dutch forces took villages, and those who resisted from the hills were starved. On 8 May 1621, 44 detained orang kaya were executed after interrogations that cannot be treated as reliable evidence of conspiracy. Settlements were burned, many people died, many were deported and enslaved, and others fled. Estimates of the preconquest population (commonly 13,000 to 15,000) and of the dead, deported, and surviving are uncertain, and this chapter has insisted on keeping those categories distinct. The Dutch National Archives and many historians describe these events as genocide, a judgment resting on documented policy and consequence rather than on a single number.

After conquest the Company confiscated land and divided it into perken, a process that began on Ai in May 1621 and extended through the 1620s. Perkeniers, including Europeans and Mardijkers, held plots under Company regulation and delivered spice at set prices, while the Company controlled provisioning. The labor system rested on enslaved people, Bandanese survivors and others brought from across Asia, whose agricultural knowledge sustained the groves. Enslaved workers resisted through flight, theft, and cultural preservation, and their creolized culture remains part of Banda's identity.

The question of efficiency has been reopened by recent scholarship. Research published in the Journal of Southeast Asian Studies in 2026 argues that cultivation methods showed considerable continuity across 1621 and that productivity stagnated or declined. If so, the Company's achievement was control, not agricultural improvement: the monopoly was built on dispossession of an existing economy rather than on better farming. The chapter has treated this reassessment as significant but not as the last word.

Violence did not end with the conquest. On Run in 1622 approximately 160 adult men were reportedly executed after further accusations of conspiracy. Survivors dispersed to Seram, the Kei Islands, Makassar, and elsewhere, forming communities that preserved language, memory, and claims to Banda, while others remained on the islands in bondage or hiding. The Company defended its conduct through arguments about treaty violation, rebellion, and conspiracy that must be read as the claims of an interested party, though some contemporaries criticized the scale of the violence.

Banda thus illustrates how corporate empire, forced commodity production, and slavery could combine, and it prepares the study of Batavia and the wider VOC colonial system in Chapter 79. Its historical significance lies in what it shows about the relationship between commercial monopoly and territorial power, and in the survival and agency of the people against whom that power was exercised.

Constructing a Source Dossier on the Conquest of Banda

This project asks you to build a dossier that compares how different kinds of evidence describe the events of 1621 and their aftermath.

  1. Select sources. Using the Dutch National Archives' Banda teaching materials, locate the VOC documents cited in this chapter, and identify at least two other kinds of evidence, such as an English account, archaeological reports, or published studies of Bandanese oral tradition.
  2. Analyze authorship and purpose. For each source, record who created it, when, for whom, and why.
  3. Compare accounts. Identify points where sources agree, disagree, or are silent, especially on numbers, responsibility, and Bandanese experience.
  4. Assess the population evidence. Summarize the range of estimates for preconquest population, deaths, deportations, and survivors, and explain the limits of each.
  5. Write your findings. In a 4–6 page essay, present your dossier's conclusions, use cautious language where evidence is uncertain, and explain what additional evidence would help.

Library resources to use: Nationaal Archief teaching materials on Banda, reference collections on the VOC, and scholarship on Bandanese history, slavery, and landholding available through the MGU History Library.

Chapter Reflection

Banda matters because it shows, in unusually concentrated form, what a commercial monopoly could demand when backed by the powers of a chartered corporation. A few small islands, a single valuable crop, and a company with the authority to build forts and wage war together produced a transformation that began in contracts and ended in conquest. The chapter has argued that this was neither inevitable nor merely accidental. It was the outcome of incentives, decisions, and the rejection of alternatives.

The chapter also reveals how much of Southeast Asian history in this period depended on the agency of local societies. The Bandanese negotiated, resisted, escaped, and survived, and the Company's own system came to rely on their knowledge. The history of the conquest cannot be told only from the viewpoint of Batavia or Amsterdam, and the sources that survive from those places must be read with constant attention to what they leave out.

Banda shaped later developments. The perk system anticipated forms of slave plantation labor and forced commodity production, and it demonstrated to the Company that monopoly could be built on territorial control. It prefigured later Dutch campaigns elsewhere in the archipelago, examined in Chapters 82, 83, and 103. It also left a continuing legacy in diaspora communities, in the creolized culture of the islands, and in public debate over how colonial violence should be remembered.

For this textbook, Banda is a reminder that the early modern commercial world was not simply a story of exchange. It was also a story of coercion, dispossession, and survival. Chapter 79 turns to Batavia, where the VOC built the administrative and social apparatus of its Asian empire, and the reader should carry forward the central lesson of this chapter: that commercial power and political violence were often inseparable, and that the people subjected to that power remained historical actors.
