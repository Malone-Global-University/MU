Samudera Pasai and the Muslim States of Sumatra

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

In northern Sumatra, carved marble grave markers record the deaths of kings who ruled some of the earliest Muslim courts that historians can document in Southeast Asia. Yet the most famous of those stones may have been made generations after the death it commemorates, and that single fact shows how carefully the history of these courts must be read.

In the lowlands of what is now North Aceh, on the plain between the Strait of Malacca and the forested ranges of the interior, stand cemeteries whose grave markers have drawn the attention of scholars for more than a century. Some are of local stone. Others are of imported marble, carved with Arabic inscriptions that praise rulers, quote scripture, and record the dates of death according to the Islamic calendar. Together they form the most concentrated body of early Muslim funerary evidence in maritime Southeast Asia. They are the principal physical traces of the polity that modern historians call Samudra-Pasai, and they sit within a wider landscape of neighboring centers: Perlak, Lamuri, Pedir, and other communities along the northern and northeastern coasts of the island.

This chapter studies how those communities became sites of Muslim political authority, and how their rulers used religion, commerce, diplomacy, and ceremony to create and sustain power. Chapter 62 explained the transregional networks of merchants, scholars, ports, and commercial institutions that linked Southeast Asia to wider Islamic worlds. Chapter 63 asks a different question: what happened when those networks took root in particular places? Which families ruled, how were they remembered, what institutions supported them, and how did their courts relate to merchants, scholars, rivals, and the inhabitants of the countryside?

Answering those questions is difficult because the evidence is unusual. For the century after about 1250, historians rely on a handful of outside observers, including Marco Polo in 1292 and the Moroccan traveler Ibn Battuta in the 1340s; on Chinese records; on Arabic inscriptions; on small gold coins; on a Javanese court poem; and on a Malay dynastic chronicle, the Hikayat Raja-raja Pasai, whose surviving manuscript is far later than the events it describes. No archive of Pasai's own administration survives. The result is a history built from fragments, and one of the central lessons of this chapter is how to tell what each fragment can and cannot prove.

The most instructive case is the tombstone of the ruler al-Malik al-Salih, whose death is recorded as having occurred in 1297. Research on the form, material, and style of the stone has led to the argument that it was manufactured much later than that date, so that it may be a retrospective memorial rather than a contemporary monument. A date on an inscription tells us what the inscription says happened. It does not, by itself, tell us when the object was made, who commissioned it, or why. Throughout the chapter, students will meet this distinction between the date an object records and the date an object was produced, together with parallel distinctions between early evidence and actual origins, and between royal memory and documented history.

The chapter also resists two tempting simplifications. The first treats Samudra-Pasai as the point at which Islam "arrived" in Southeast Asia, as though conversion were a single event with a single place and date. The evidence supports something more complicated: Muslim merchants were present in many ports, rulers adopted Islamic titles and practices at different times, and the earliest surviving evidence for a Muslim court is not the same thing as the earliest conversion. The second simplification treats Pasai as a mere trading station, or as a stepping stone toward the later sultanate of Aceh. Pasai was an active political society with its own dynasty, ceremonies, diplomacy, rivalries, and scholars, and the later power of Aceh, examined in Chapter 80, requires its own explanation.

The chapter proceeds in five divisions. Division I establishes geography and evidence. Division II examines the formation of Samudra-Pasai, the adoption of Islamic kingship, the first documented rulers, and court government. Division III turns to commerce, currency, social life, funerary inscriptions, and Islamic learning. Division IV places Pasai within regional politics and examines the dynastic chronicle that preserves its memory. Division V considers continuity and change, the problem of conversion, the situation of northern Sumatra around 1500, and the legacy of these courts. The chapter's chronological center is approximately 1250 to 1500, with earlier commercial background and only limited discussion of the Acehnese developments that followed.

Finally, the chapter connects to the larger concerns of this textbook. The rulers of northern Sumatra were not passive recipients of an external faith. They selected titles, commissioned or received imported monuments, welcomed some scholars and merchants, fought or negotiated with neighbors, and shaped the story told about their own origins. Their history is a clear example of the textbook's central argument that Southeast Asian societies were historical actors, not merely a crossroads.

Learning Objectives

  2. Describe the geography of northern Sumatra and explain how coast, rivers, hinterland, and the Strait of Malacca shaped the location and character of its ports.
  3. Trace the emergence of Samudra-Pasai between roughly 1250 and 1350, distinguishing securely documented developments from later tradition.
  4. Distinguish between the date recorded in an inscription and the date the inscribed object was produced, using the tombstone of al-Malik al-Salih as a case study.
  5. Explain how rulers adopted Islamic titles, patronage, and ceremony as part of a broader strategy of political legitimacy.
  6. Analyze the economic foundations of the Muslim courts, including port commerce, pepper, customs revenue, and gold coinage, while recognizing the limits of numismatic evidence.
  7. Reconstruct aspects of everyday life in port communities, including merchants, households, women, labor, and dependence, using cautious inference.
  8. Compare Samudra-Pasai with Perlak, Lamuri, Pedir, and other northern Sumatran centers without assuming unified or fixed territorial kingdoms.
  9. Evaluate the Hikayat Raja-raja Pasai as both historical evidence and a political-literary construction.
  10. Assess competing interpretations of when and how northern Sumatra became Muslim, including the difference between earliest evidence and historical origins.

Historical Context

The northern tip of Sumatra lies at the junction of two seas. To the west is the Andaman Sea and, beyond it, the Bay of Bengal. To the east is the Strait of Malacca, the principal maritime passage between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea. Ships crossing the Bay of Bengal with the monsoon winds made their first landfalls in this region, and long before the thirteenth century its harbors were familiar to merchants from South Asia. Evidence from the eleventh and thirteenth centuries, including a Tamil inscription of the Chola ruler Rajendra and the Chinese compilation of Zhao Rugua, shows that outside observers knew of a polity in the Lamuri region and that it was connected to wider commercial networks. Chapters 18 and 28 examined the earlier maritime trade and the commercial world of Srivijaya, which for centuries organized much of the Strait's commerce from southeastern Sumatra.

By the thirteenth century that earlier order was changing. Chapter 47 described the transformation of Srivijaya, the effects of Chola raiding, and the shifting of commercial and political weight among competing centers. Java was being reshaped by the rise and fall of Singhasari and by the creation of Majapahit (Chapters 48 and 49), and the Mongol-Yuan court in China was dispatching envoys and military expeditions across Asian waters (Chapter 46). In this environment, northern Sumatran ports gained new significance. They stood nearest to Bengal, the Coromandel Coast, Gujarat, and the Islamic markets beyond, and they could supply and service ships waiting for favorable winds.

Chapter 62 explained how Muslim merchants, scholars, Sufi networks, marriage alliances, and commercial institutions linked Southeast Asia with Indian Ocean Islamic worlds. That chapter stressed the network. This chapter examines a particular place within it. The distinction matters because networks do not automatically create states. A port may host Muslim traders for generations without its ruler becoming Muslim, and a ruler may adopt Islamic titles without the countryside following. The questions of political authority, institutions, and social change therefore require their own investigation.

Finally, historians must be careful about names. "Samudra" and "Pasai" appear in different sources, and sometimes in compound form, and the sources do not allow us to assume that they always meant the same settlement, the same territory, or a single stable administrative unit. "Samudera" is the spelling used in the textbook's chapter outline and in many Indonesian-language works; "Samudra-Pasai" is the form used here for the political and archaeological complex, with the individual names retained when a source distinguishes them. As with all pre-modern Southeast Asian polities (see Chapter 1), these were centers of authority whose reach varied by period and by neighbor, not territories bounded by fixed lines.

Section 63.1 · Division I — Northern Sumatra: Geography, Sources, and the Emergence of Muslim Political Centers

Reconstructing the Early Muslim States of Northern Sumatra

The early Muslim courts of northern Sumatra are known only through evidence that was created for other purposes and then preserved by accident, by religious devotion, or by later political need. No chancery records, tax registers, or legal archives from Samudra-Pasai have survived. Historians must therefore assemble a picture from several different kinds of material, each of which illuminates a different part of the past and distorts it in a different way.

The first category is material and epigraphic evidence. Grave markers inscribed in Arabic record names, titles, praise formulas, scriptural quotations, and dates in the Islamic calendar. Because they were meant to commemorate the dead, they preserve royal names and genealogy that no other source supplies. The most important modern study of this corpus, Claude Guillot and Ludvik Kalus's Les monuments funéraires et l'histoire du sultanat de Pasai (2008), documents the inscriptions and uses them to reconstruct royal succession and aspects of political and social history. A second material category is coinage: small gold coins with Arabic legends that were issued in the name of Pasai's rulers. Archaeological survey, though limited, adds information about cemeteries, settlements, and imported goods.

The second category is outside testimony. Marco Polo, who stayed on the coast in 1292, and Ibn Battuta, who visited Samudra in the 1340s, each described what they saw and heard. Chinese dynastic records and the accounts of Ming-era travelers such as Ma Huan record diplomatic contacts and describe the region from the perspective of the imperial court. Tomé Pires, writing in the early sixteenth century after the Portuguese seizure of Melaka, offers a Portuguese view of northern Sumatra just as its political order was changing. A Javanese court poem, the Nagarakretagama of 1365, lists Samudra and Perlak among places that its Majapahit-centered author associated with the Javanese court. None of these writers was a local official, and each had his own purposes, interests, and limits of knowledge.

The third category is local literary tradition. The Hikayat Raja-raja Pasai is a Malay chronicle that narrates the conversion of the founder of the dynasty and the early history of the kingdom. Its likely medieval composition must be separated from the date of its surviving manuscript, which is much later, and its stories were shaped by the desire to explain and legitimate dynastic rule. The chronicle is therefore a valuable witness to how Malay audiences understood the origins of Muslim kingship, but it is not an independent eyewitness record of every event it describes.

Careful historians distinguish four layers within this evidence. There is contemporary documentation, such as Polo's observations or an inscription cut at the time of a death. There is later tradition, such as chronicle narratives of conversion. There is retrospective commemoration, such as a monument made generations after the person it honors. And there is modern scholarly reconstruction, which combines these layers and sometimes revises them. The history of Samudra-Pasai is best written by comparing layers rather than by treating any one of them as self-sufficient.

Because the evidence was produced at different times, an apparent sequence can be an artifact of survival. A later funerary commission may commemorate a much earlier ruler, while a traveler may describe people whom local texts do not mention. Evidence of a port's commercial importance may survive without its legal records; evidence for an individual ruler may survive without a continuous genealogy. A rigorous reconstruction therefore assigns confidence to each claim separately rather than letting the vividness of one source determine the certainty of the whole narrative.

Historical Source and Method Study
What Each Kind of Evidence Can and Cannot Show

Inscribed gravestones. They can show names, titles, religious formulas, and the date of death as recorded. They cannot, on their own, show when the stone was produced, who ordered it, or whether the person ruled the territory later tradition assigns to them.

Coins. They can show political self-presentation, language, and monetary practice. Because Pasai's coins generally lack explicit dates and rulers' names recur, they do not automatically settle chronology.

Travelers' accounts. They provide dated observation, but their authors were outsiders who saw limited places for limited periods and often relied on information from others.

Chinese and Javanese court texts. They show how the region was perceived and categorized from imperial or royal centers, including claims of tribute or dependence that may overstate actual control.

The Hikayat. It preserves memory and legitimating narrative, and it must be compared with independent evidence before being used as a record of events.

Section 63.2

The Geography of Northern Sumatra and Its Maritime Hinterlands

Northern Sumatra forms the northwestern gateway of Maritime Southeast Asia. The island's long spine of volcanic mountains, the Barisan range, runs along its western side, leaving a broad belt of lowland and swamp along the eastern coast that faces the Strait of Malacca. In the far north the coastal plain narrows and the land bends west, so that the region looks outward across the Andaman Sea as well as across the Strait. Short, fast rivers cross the coastal plains of what are today the Aceh districts, and they create alluvial lowlands suitable for rice, gardens, and fishing. Beyond the plains lie forested uplands whose products, including aromatic resins, timber, and gold from further inland, entered coastal trade.

Within this environment, historic settlements clustered where rivers met the sea. A river mouth provided an anchorage, a route into the interior, and fresh water. A port town located a short distance upstream could be sheltered from open-sea storms while remaining accessible to small vessels, and an offshore anchorage could handle larger ships. Many of the sites associated with Samudra-Pasai lie along the Pasai River and neighboring waterways of the northeastern coast, and archaeologists and historians continue to debate the relationship between particular cemeteries, riverside settlements, and the places named in the sources. Because rivers shift course and coastlines change, the historical map of these ports cannot be reconstructed from the present landscape alone.

The region's position in the monsoon system was fundamental, though not deterministic. Ships traveling between the Bay of Bengal and the Strait depended on seasonal winds, and navigators often needed to wait for favorable conditions. Northern Sumatra's harbors were natural stopping points where goods could be exchanged, ships provisioned and repaired, and news and passengers transferred. Marco Polo's stay of several months in 1292, which he attributed to the lack of favorable winds, reflects exactly this pattern. At the same time, geography alone does not explain why a particular ruling family prospered. Ports at similar locations could rise or stagnate depending on their rulers' ability to attract merchants, provide security, and negotiate with neighbors.

The coastal communities were also tied to inland and upland neighbors. Farmers cultivated rice and other crops where soils and water allowed; fishing and shipping provided livelihoods; and upland communities, many of them not Muslim in the period under study, supplied forest and agricultural products. Ports exchanged imported cloth, metalware, and ceramics for these goods. The relationship was one of exchange, but also sometimes of coercion and warfare, as Section 63.8 will show. When this chapter speaks of Samudra, Pasai, Perlak, Lamuri, or Pedir, it means centers of political authority connected to coastal ports and their hinterlands, not states bounded by modern provincial lines.

Section 63.3

Marco Polo, Perlak, and Samara in 1292

In 1292 the Venetian traveler Marco Polo, escorting a Mongol princess from China toward Persia by sea, spent about five months on the northern coast of Sumatra while waiting for the monsoon winds to change. His narrative, composed after his return to Europe, is the earliest datable outside description of the region's religious situation. It lists a sequence of kingdoms on the island, including Ferlec, Basma, Samara, Dagroian, and Lambri, and it comments on the religion and customs of each.

Scholars generally identify Ferlec with Perlak, Samara with Samudra, and Lambri with Lamuri. Basma and Dagroian are less secure, though Basma has often been connected with Pasai. These identifications rest on phonetic similarity, the order in which Polo lists the places, and the later history of the names. They are probable rather than certain, and they remind us that medieval European spellings of Southeast Asian names are approximations of what a traveler heard from interpreters and fellow passengers.

Polo's most significant observation concerns Ferlec. He reports that the inhabitants of the town had been converted to Islam by Muslim merchants who came there to trade, while the people of the surrounding mountains remained idol-worshippers. For Samara, he describes a kingdom with its own ruler, and he says nothing that would indicate a Muslim court there. The account therefore supports three conclusions with reasonable confidence. By 1292 Muslim merchants were active on the northern Sumatran coast. At least one port community, Perlak, had a Muslim population. And religious change was uneven, differing between town and countryside and from one polity to the next.

The account also carries limits that students must not overlook. Polo was an outsider who relied on interpreters and on information that reached him secondhand; he spent his time in a limited number of places; and the text, as transmitted in many manuscripts, shows variation from copy to copy. His silence about a Muslim ruler at Samara does not prove that there was none, and his report of Perlak's conversion does not date the origin of Islam in Sumatra, since conversion of a port community plainly predates his visit. What the account provides is a datable snapshot, not a beginning.

Primary Source Window I

Marco Polo's Account of Northern Sumatra, 1292

Creator: Marco Polo, Venetian merchant-traveler, with the text shaped by the writer Rustichello da Pisa and by later copyists · Date: visit in 1292; narrative composed c. 1298–1300 and transmitted in many manuscripts · Type: Travel narrative; secondhand and firsthand observation, transmitted through multiple manuscript traditions

Context: Polo's party left the Chinese coast by sea in the early 1290s and was delayed by winds on the Sumatran coast. Modern translations, such as those of Henry Yule and Henri Cordier and of Ronald Latham, differ in wording and in how they render place names.

Editorial paraphrase (not a verbatim excerpt):

In the account, the town of Ferlec is inhabited by people who follow the religion of Muhammad because Muslim merchants frequenting the port had converted them, whereas the peoples of the mountains eat and live differently and worship idols. In the account of Samara, the kingdom is described as having its own king and as lacking the Muslim population attributed to Ferlec.

Analysis: The passage shows that a port town in northern Sumatra had become Muslim by 1292 through the influence of visiting merchants, and that the surrounding highlands were not Muslim. It also illustrates the mixed religious landscape of the coast.

Limitations: The source does not identify the date of conversion, the identity or origin of the merchants, the role of the local ruler, or the details of local belief and practice. It cannot show whether Perlak was ruled by Muslims.

Perspective: The text represents a European merchant's view as relayed through his collaborator and copyists. The voices of Perlak's residents, converts, merchants, and rulers are absent.

Section 63.4

Archaeology, Burial Grounds, and the Physical Landscape of Early Pasai

If the texts of Pasai are fragmentary, its physical remains are in some respects richer, but they are unevenly studied. The most visible remains are cemeteries. Clusters of graves in the lowlands of North Aceh, including sites around Geudong, contain markers of several types: simple upright stones, carved stones of local material, and distinctive marble monuments with elaborate inscriptions. The marble monuments belong to a group commonly called Cambay-type or Gujarat-type, after the Gujarati port region from which similar stones were exported. Their form, carved decoration, and calligraphy resemble stones produced for Muslim patrons in South Asia and elsewhere in the Indian Ocean.

Pioneering work in the early twentieth century, notably by J. P. Moquette, identified the Gujarat connection of the marble and used it to argue for an Indian origin of Southeast Asian Islam. More recent research has refined this picture by examining how the stones were made, shipped, and used. The imported marble indicates that Pasai's elites could obtain luxury goods from a distant manufacturing center, and that Pasai belonged to a sphere of Indian Ocean funerary fashion. It does not, by itself, show where Pasai's Muslims came from or how they converted.

Settlement archaeology is thinner. The palaces, markets, mosques, and warehouses of Samudra and Pasai have not been located with the same confidence as the cemeteries, and fieldwork has been constrained by modern conditions in the region, by changing river courses, and by later building. Reported finds of imported ceramics and other goods point to commercial connections, but historians should avoid reading a list of imports as a precise map of trade, since survival, excavation, and publication are all selective. A cemetery tells us about the elite and pious dead of a community, not about the thousands who lived and worked there.

Archaeology also provides a corrective to textual sources. The chronicles and foreign accounts emphasize rulers and religious conversion, whereas the physical record raises questions about sequence, production, and replacement. Funerary monuments might be reused, moved, replaced, or commissioned long after a burial, a possibility central to the case of al-Malik al-Salih. The landscape of early Pasai is thus a layered one, in which early burials, later monuments, and modern commemoration can be hard to separate without careful analysis of the objects themselves.

Material classification must precede historical interpretation. In the Pasai corpus, locally fashioned batu Aceh markers, imported Gujarat-type stone and uncertain fragments cannot be treated as interchangeable signs of one original building campaign. The production of an imposing memorial generations after a death may itself represent a political event: descendants or later patrons could elevate a founder's prestige through newly fashionable visual forms. Yet an inferred political purpose is not evidence for a specific commissioning ruler unless another source identifies one.

Context: is the marker in its original position, and is there evidence of later reuse or replacement? Absence: who is not commemorated, and what does that omission imply?

Section 63.5 · Division II — The Formation of Samudra-Pasai and Its Royal Institutions

The Emergence of Samudra-Pasai, c. 1250–1350

The century after about 1250 is the period in which an identifiable Muslim ruling center in northern Sumatra first becomes visible. It is a period of exceptionally uneven evidence, and the account that follows deliberately separates what the sources document from what later tradition asserts.

The commercial and political background lay in the preceding centuries. Northern Sumatran communities, notably Lamuri, were already part of long-distance trade before the thirteenth century. The loosening of Srivijaya's hold over the Strait (Chapter 47) opened room for other port polities, and the Mongol-Yuan court's maritime diplomacy created new incentives for local rulers to send envoys and to be recognized. Chinese records from the 1280s onward note contacts with places identified with Sumatran ports, including Samudra, although the identification of names and the interpretation of "tribute" require caution. By 1292 Marco Polo could list several named kingdoms along the northern coast and note Muslim merchants and converts among them.

The most famous memorial for an early Muslim royal house in Samudra records the death of al-Malik al-Salih in 696 AH (1297 CE), but modern stylistic research suggests that the surviving markers were made much later. The date therefore belongs to a retrospective inscription, not demonstrably to an object carved in 1297. A separate monument recording the death of Muhammad al-Malik al-Zahir in 726 AH (1326 CE) provides another epigraphic anchor, whose object history must also be assessed. By the 1340s the court is independently visible in the account of Ibn Battuta, who describes a ruler committed to Shafi‘i legal scholarship, court ceremonies and military action against non-Muslim neighbors. The Javanese Deśawarṇana (Nāgarakṛtāgama) of 1365 names Samudra and Perlak in its own Java-centered political geography. These witnesses establish different things: a later account of an earlier royal death, funerary commemoration, a visitor’s account of a functioning court, and the perception of a distant court.

What cannot be established is the earlier history of the dynasty before the late thirteenth century. The chronicle tradition places the foundation of Samudra with Merah Silu, who becomes al-Malik al-Salih, and attributes the foundation of Pasai to his successor. Those traditions may preserve real memory, but they were shaped later, and they cannot be dated independently of the epigraphic evidence. The reasonable conclusion is that a Muslim ruling house was established in the Samudra area by the late thirteenth century, that it consolidated its position over the following decades, and that Pasai became prominent as a port and political center by the mid-fourteenth century. The exact sequence by which the two names came to designate a single political complex remains uncertain.

The emergence of Samudra-Pasai was not the creation of a bounded kingdom. It was the strengthening of a ruling family whose authority was strongest around its riverine port and extended outward through alliance, tribute, marriage, naval power, and periodic coercion. Neighbors such as Perlak and Lamuri remained distinct centers, and the relationship among them was shaped by negotiation and rivalry rather than by a single dynastic map.

State formation likely involved accumulating control over services rather than simply declaring sovereignty. A coastal ruling house could win merchants' confidence by resolving disputes, protecting approaches to a river anchorage, maintaining provisioning relationships and transmitting political authority through kin and allied communities. Those capacities could grow unevenly. A visible Muslim ruler at Samudra did not require every neighboring harbor to accept the same authority or every inland household to share the ruler's religious practice. The most persuasive chronology is thus one of increasingly recognizable institutions, while the date of their first appearance remains open.

Section 63.6

Conversion, Kingship, and the Adoption of Islamic Political Authority

A ruler's adoption of Islam was never only a private religious decision. It altered the language of kingship, the institutions around the court, and the ruler's relations with other Muslim rulers and merchants. In northern Sumatra, as elsewhere, local rulers who already commanded followers, ports, and shipping adopted Islamic forms in ways that reinforced rather than replaced existing authority.

The evidence for the adoption of Islamic kingship is visible in titles. The earliest royal names recorded on inscriptions use Arabic honorifics, such as al-Malik al-Salih ("the righteous king") and al-Malik al-Zahir ("the victorious king"). Titles of this kind were used by many Muslim rulers across the Islamic world, and their adoption suggests that Pasai's rulers presented themselves in a recognizable Islamic idiom. Historians should not read this as proof of formal ties to any distant caliphate or sultanate. It is better understood as participation in a shared language of legitimacy that merchants, scholars, and rulers across the Indian Ocean could recognize.

The chronicle tradition explains the change as a miraculous conversion of Merah Silu, who in a dream received the Prophet's instruction and awoke able to recite the creed. Whatever the historical basis of the story, its function is clear: it makes the founder's authority religiously validated from the outset and links his house directly to the Prophet and to the holy cities. The story also shows the work that conversion narratives do. They answer a question every new Muslim dynasty faced, namely why its rule was legitimate, and they do so by placing divine sanction at the dynasty's origin.

Practical considerations supported the religious ones. A Muslim ruler could more easily attract Muslim merchants, who valued ports where their law, worship, and credit relationships were respected. He could host scholars and jurists who brought prestige and legal expertise. He could claim a place among the Muslim rulers of the Indian Ocean and obtain luxury goods, such as imported marble for monuments. And a ruler who campaigned against non-Muslim neighbors could frame warfare as religious duty, a point reflected in Ibn Battuta's description. Yet these advantages were not automatic, and the new religion also created obligations and constraints. Rulers had to balance Islamic law and learned opinion against the local customs and older loyalties of their subjects.

Centuries of Indian Ocean commerce had brought Muslim merchants to northern Sumatran ports, and the weakening of earlier dominant powers in the Strait left room for new commercial courts.

Rulers needed revenue, legitimacy, and alliances in a competitive environment of neighboring port polities, Chinese and Javanese diplomacy, and visiting merchants.

Immediate effects

Long-term consequences

Historical complexity: Commercial advantage, religious conviction, scholarly prestige, and political rivalry operated together, and the surviving evidence does not allow historians to rank them with confidence. Reducing conversion to either sincere piety or mere calculation misrepresents a process that involved many people and motives.

Section 63.7

Al-Malik al-Salih, Al-Malik al-Zahir, and Early Dynastic Succession

The earliest rulers of Samudra-Pasai are reconstructed from inscriptions, commemorative monuments and chronicle genealogy. The surviving gravestones attributed to al-Malik al-Salih record a death in 696 AH (1297 CE) and place him at the beginning of later dynastic memory. Their likely much later manufacture makes them evidence that a later community commemorated an earlier ruler, rather than direct physical proof of a monument commissioned in 1297. The Hikayat Raja-raja Pasai identifies the founder with Merah Silu. That identification is influential and historically plausible, but it cannot be confirmed by a contemporary declaration identifying the two names.

The successor tradition leads to rulers bearing the title al-Malik al-Zahir, which appears more than once. A funerary inscription records the death of Muhammad al-Malik al-Zahir in 726 AH (1326 CE). The chronicle describes a son of the founder under a comparable royal name. Ibn Battuta, however, reached Samudra nearly two decades later and met a sultan described by the title al-Malik al-Zahir; this need not be the man commemorated in 1326. Al-Malik al-Zahir is a royal honorific, or laqab, rather than a unique personal identifier. Reconstructing succession therefore requires comparison of names, dates, kinship claims, numismatic legends and independent travel or diplomatic testimony, rather than the mechanical joining of identical titles.

Dynastic succession was usually within a single royal house, but the sources suggest that it was not always smooth. Chronicle traditions recount disputes among royal sons and between rulers and powerful courtiers, and Ming records of the early fifteenth century describe a contest between a legitimate king and a usurper that involved the intervention of a Chinese fleet (see Section 63.16). Succession in such courts commonly depended on the ruler's relatives, military supporters, and the officials and merchants who benefited from his patronage, and royal women could be important links in alliance and inheritance. The tomb of Nahrasiyah, who died in 1428 according to its inscription, shows that royal women of the fifteenth century were commemorated with exceptional splendor.

For the early period, therefore, historians can reconstruct the broad outlines of a dynasty with some confidence but should treat the details of genealogy with caution. The epigraphic corpus provides dated anchor points, the chronicle supplies a narrative framework, and external observers supply glimpses. Where these do not match, as with the reigns of rulers sharing titles or with the sequence of early fourteenth-century succession, the honest conclusion is that the evidence is insufficient to produce a fully secure king list.

A king list compiled from these fragments should show its evidentiary seams. The 1326 inscription can preserve a named royal death while leaving gaps before the next independent witness; a coin can repeat a title whose bearer cannot be dated securely; a later chronicle can connect two rulers in a narrative whose chronology is uncertain. Apparent contradictions may reflect repeated honorifics, editorial transmission or genuinely competing dynastic claims. Students should not assume that every difference demands the invention of another ruler or that every identical name proves personal identity.

Material Culture and Numismatic Study

Pasai's Gold Coins and the Problem of Dating

Samudra-Pasai is notable in the early Southeast Asian record for issuing its own small gold coins bearing Arabic legends. The coins name rulers and use the formulas of Islamic coinage, which makes them valuable evidence for the court's political self-presentation and for the existence of a local monetary practice. They have been found in Sumatra and in some other regional contexts, which suggests circulation, though the evidence for their wider spread requires careful assessment.

Studies of the coins stress two difficulties. First, the coins generally lack explicit dates. Second, the rulers' names and titles recur across generations, so a coin that names a ruler with a common name cannot be assigned to a specific reign without additional argument. Coins therefore tell us about political identity and monetary form, but they do not automatically resolve dynastic chronology.

Section 63.8

Court Government, Political Hierarchy, and Royal Authority

The institutions of Samudra-Pasai's government must be reconstructed from fragments, and the reconstruction should not be mistaken for a complete organizational chart. Ibn Battuta's account is the richest contemporary source. He describes a court in which the ruler held audience, received visitors, and presided at religious observances, and in which officials and learned men held recognized positions. He names a high official whose title, amir, shows the use of Arabic-Persian administrative terminology, and he describes a legal and scholarly circle that included a qadi and other learned figures. Later Malay texts, including the Hikayat, mention a range of court titles and offices, but their terminology may reflect the political culture of later courts such as Melaka rather than the administrative practices of the fourteenth century.

At the center was the ruler, whose authority rested on lineage, religious legitimacy, wealth, and the ability to attract and reward followers. Around him were relatives, whose cooperation or rivalry shaped succession; ministers and military commanders; religious officials and scholars; and the leading merchants and harbor officials who managed trade. The court was also a household, with royal wives, servants, and dependents whose roles are difficult to see in the surviving sources but whose presence is attested by royal women's monuments and by chronicle narrative.

Royal revenue came principally from the port. Rulers could levy customs duties and fees on ships and cargo, charge for services such as storage and brokerage, receive gifts from visiting merchants and envoys, and benefit from monopolies or preferential access to valuable goods. In addition, they could claim a share of agricultural and forest produce from their dependents and could obtain labor through obligation or capture. The Samudra sultan's campaigns against non-Muslim neighbors, according to Ibn Battuta, involved the seizure of captives and the exaction of tribute, a pattern consistent with a political economy in which control over people was as important as control over land. These campaigns also had a religious dimension, since the ruler framed them as warfare against unbelievers.

The reach of royal authority was uneven. In the immediate vicinity of the port the ruler's power was strongest. Along the river valleys and in nearby settlements it depended on alliances with local leaders, and in the highlands it was weaker still. Relations with neighboring ports, such as Perlak and Lamuri, were governed by diplomacy, marriage, and sometimes war. For all these reasons, scholars prefer to describe Samudra-Pasai as a river-and-port polity with overlapping spheres of authority rather than as a territorial state with defined borders, an approach consistent with the discussion of mandala-style political organization in Chapter 1.

The scarcity of administrative records also affects how historians describe offices. Calling someone a harbor master, revenue collector or judge may be useful when a source actually identifies the function, but it becomes misleading if one projects the elaborate bureaucracies of later Malay courts backward without evidence. Pasai's rulers probably depended on intermediaries who understood shipping and local communities, yet their titles, salaries and formal chains of command cannot be reconstructed from analogy alone. Political authority may have consisted as much in personal relationships and negotiated privileges as in stable territorial administration.

Section 63.9 · Division III — Commerce, Society, Religion, and Intellectual Life

Port Commerce, Currency, and the Economic Foundations of the Sultanate

The wealth of Samudra-Pasai rested on its role as a port at the northern entrance of the Strait of Malacca. Ships from the Bay of Bengal, the Coromandel and Malabar coasts, and Gujarat arrived with textiles, metalwork, and other goods, and ships from the east brought Chinese ceramics, silks, and other products. The harbor offered a market, supplies, and a place to await the monsoon. Rulers, harbor officials, and brokers profited from each stage of the exchange.

Pepper was particularly important to the region’s commercial reputation, as descriptions from the later medieval and early Portuguese periods indicate. Claims about exactly when production increased, however, should not be presented as a measured agricultural trend: no annual series of output, acreage or customs duties survives. Forest products, including aromatic resins, entered the harbors through coastal and inland exchange; other goods passed through the ports rather than necessarily being produced nearby. Pasai participated in several commercial circuits linking the northern Strait to Sumatra’s interior, the Bay of Bengal and the eastern archipelago. The changing relative weight of these exchanges is difficult to reconstruct, and reported product lists cannot be treated as quantitative statistics.

Commerce also depended on services. Pilots, shipwrights, and sailors manned and maintained vessels; warehouse keepers stored goods; brokers matched buyers and sellers; interpreters bridged languages; and merchants extended credit and shared information within their communities. Muslim merchants valued a port where Islamic law protected contracts and where co-religionists could pray, trade, and marry. Non-Muslim merchants, including Chinese and others, also traded in the port, and the existence of a Muslim court did not exclude them. Tribute and gift exchange with distant courts, including the Ming, brought additional prestige and goods.

The wealth that commerce produced was not evenly distributed. Rulers and leading merchants benefited most, while farmers, fishers, porters, and dependent laborers received a smaller share. Trade also made the court vulnerable, since a decline in shipping, a rival port's rise, or a political crisis could reduce revenue quickly. The economic basis of Pasai's power was therefore strong but not secure, and it depended on continuing political management of merchants and neighbors.

Coins offer firmer evidence of certain monetary conventions than general accounts of trade. Specialist numismatic studies examine small gold pieces with Arabic names or titles, alongside evidence for tin fractional currency. The nomenclature itself has a historiography: terms such as mas and dirham were used differently in sources and later collecting traditions, so a modern label should not be treated as a straightforward medieval denomination. Because most examples have no written minting year, recurring names and die variations complicate ruler-by-ruler attribution. Nor do coins prove that wages, food purchases and inland debts were always paid in gold. Barter, credit and other media of exchange could coexist with royal coinage.

Historical Vocabulary
Dirham, Mas, and Pasai Gold

Definition. The dirham was originally a silver coin of the early Islamic world, and the term came to be used in many regions for coins and for units of weight. In Southeast Asian Malay usage, mas refers to gold, and the small gold coins of Pasai are often described in modern scholarship by reference to both terms.

Historical context. Pasai's gold coins carried Arabic inscriptions naming rulers and using Islamic formulas. By issuing them, the court asserted authority and participated in a monetary tradition recognized across much of the Islamic world.

Why it matters. The coins show that Pasai had an organized economy and political identity. They do not, by themselves, tell us how widely they circulated or how much of ordinary trade was conducted in coin as opposed to barter, weighed bullion, or other media.

Potential misunderstanding. The name "dirham" should not be taken to mean that Pasai's coins conformed to the weight or metal of silver dirhams elsewhere. Terms migrated across regions while denoting different objects.

Section 63.10

Merchants, Families, Women, Labor, and Everyday Life

The people who lived in and around Samudra and Pasai included many more than the kings and scholars named in inscriptions. A port town of the fourteenth century was a place of rice fields and gardens on its outskirts, of houses of wood and thatch (the standard building materials of the region), of landing places and warehouses, and of markets where languages mingled. Residents included local Malay-speaking families, merchants and visitors from South Asia and the Middle East, Chinese traders, sailors, laborers, and servants. Many were Muslim, though neither the whole population nor the whole region can be assumed to have been.

Households were the basic units of production and sociability. A merchant's household might include relatives, apprentices, enslaved or dependent workers, and agents who traveled with cargo. Marriage connected newcomers to local families. Muslim merchants who settled in port towns commonly married local women, a pattern that gave them kinship ties within the community and gave local families links to trade networks. Such marriages also affected religion, since children were typically raised within the community and sometimes joined the father's faith, though evidence for individual cases is scarce.

Women appear unevenly in the sources. The monument of Nahrasiyah demonstrates that royal women could command spectacular commemoration, and the chronicle tradition gives royal wives and daughters roles in dynastic narrative. Women also worked in markets, agriculture, and textile production, and they participated in the kinship networks on which trade and alliance depended. Historians should be cautious about drawing detailed conclusions, however, since the surviving evidence concerns mostly the elite and was produced largely by men.

Labor was organized through a mix of voluntary work, obligation, and dependency. Fishing and agriculture supported the town's food supply; shipwrights, sailors, and porters supported commerce; and enslaved people and debt-bondspeople worked in households and in the maritime economy. Raiding and warfare produced captives, as Ibn Battuta's account implies. Slavery and bondage were widespread in the early modern Southeast Asian world (see Chapter 91), and the earlier period was no exception, but the evidence for scale and conditions in Pasai is limited.

Religious life varied by social position. At court, Islam was expressed through ceremony, patronage, law, and learned discussion. Among merchants, it shaped contracts, worship, and charity. In the countryside, beliefs and practices may have changed more slowly and in different ways, and older spirit traditions and earlier religious practices could persist or combine with Islamic ones. A history that mentions only the court's piety would therefore miss the likelihood of a more complex and uneven religious life.

The evidence for women's experiences is especially asymmetrical. Nahrasiyah's monumental burial establishes a highly visible form of elite remembrance but does not by itself reveal whether she governed independently, controlled particular lands or represented the legal position of ordinary women. Merchant households may have relied on women's property, kin relationships and labor, but specific arrangements in thirteenth- or fourteenth-century Pasai require direct proof that is largely missing. The best social history states what is documented at court and what may be inferred cautiously from the wider world of port communities.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living and Working in a Northern Sumatran Port

A resident of fourteenth-century Pasai lived in a world shaped by wind, water, and trade. The arrival of ships marked the busy season; the monsoon's change brought departures and quiet. Work might mean tending gardens, fishing, loading cargo, building boats, weaving, cooking, or serving in a merchant's household. Language and religion shaped daily routines: those who prayed and observed Islamic law would have followed its rhythms, while neighbors who did not would have followed other customs.

Because the available sources were written or commissioned by elites and visitors, we know little in detail about ordinary diets, clothing, or family life. Historians therefore reason cautiously from archaeology, from later descriptions, and from comparison with better-documented ports, and they label such inferences as inferences. The absence of ordinary people from the sources is itself a historical fact: it reflects who had the means to write, carve, and commission.

Section 63.11

Royal Tombstones, Arabic Epigraphy, and Historical Chronology

The royal funerary monuments of northern Sumatra are central physical sources for the dynastic history, but they are not uniform in date, material or provenance. Their Arabic inscriptions identify names and titles, express religious praise, and sometimes give Hijri death dates. Some elaborate Pasai memorials, notably that associated with Nahrasiyah, belong to wider Indian Ocean traditions of finely carved imported Gujarati or Cambay-type marble. The markers of al-Malik al-Salih should not be placed in the same material category. Lambourn’s comparative analysis identifies them as later, locally produced examples of a batu Aceh funerary tradition rather than imported thirteenth-century marble. The diversity of the surviving corpus is itself evidence of changing elite patronage and production networks.

The central problem is chronology. An inscription can record an earlier death without having been carved at the time of that death. A monument might be commissioned during a funeral, decades later by descendants, or generations later when a dynasty sought to honor its founders; an old marker might also have been replaced. Elizabeth Lambourn’s typological and comparative study argues that the surviving grave markers of al-Malik al-Salih, notwithstanding the 1297 death date they record, were probably produced around the 1470s–1520s. The suggestion that an older memorial was replaced is plausible but not independently demonstrated. Thus the recorded date, the date of carving, and the degree of historical confidence in the biographical claim are three separate questions. This does not disprove the ruler’s existence; it changes the evidentiary status of the object.

This argument has major implications. If the best-known evidence for the earliest Muslim ruler is later than it appears, then the date of the earliest Muslim court has to be established through other evidence, such as Polo's 1292 observations and the independent accounts of the 1340s. At the same time the argument does not erase al-Malik al-Salih, since the tradition, the title, and the dynasty's later commemoration all point to a real founder. What it does is change the status of the evidence: from direct testimony to memory, and from a fixed date to a historical problem. The 1326 inscription of al-Malik al-Zahir raises its own questions, since the title recurs and the relationship of the commemorated ruler to the king Ibn Battuta met must be established independently.

Epigraphy also reveals social and political values. The praise formulas show how rulers wished to be remembered: as just, powerful, and pious. The use of Arabic, and sometimes of verses and formulas current across the Muslim world, links Pasai's elite to a broad cultural community. The monument of Nahrasiyah, with its elaborate carving and extended text, demonstrates how fifteenth-century royal commemoration had become a sophisticated art. Funerary evidence thus tells us much about elite self-image and about the connections that supplied the stones, while telling us less about everyday life or the daily workings of government.

A dated epitaph and a dated archaeological object answer different questions. If an inscription on a late monument accurately reproduces an older date, it may preserve valuable dynastic information; if a memorial's text was composed at the time of replacement, its praise formulas may reflect later political ideals. Even the identification of the stone's workshop has to be established through material, cutting technique, lettering and comparison with objects of known provenance. These observations make the study of gravestones an inquiry into historical transmission, not simply a matter of copying numbers from Arabic script.

Section 63.12

Scholars, Jurists, Sufi Traditions, and the Muslim Court

Rulers who adopted Islam needed more than titles. They needed people who could teach the faith, interpret its law, lead prayer, settle disputes, and give the court a reputation for learning. Samudra-Pasai developed such a circle, and Ibn Battuta's account provides the best contemporary glimpse of it. He found a ruler devoted to the Shafi‘i school of Islamic law, the legal tradition that later became dominant in much of maritime Southeast Asia, and a court that engaged in religious discussion and welcomed learned visitors. His narrative names scholars of Persian origin among those around the sultan, an indication that the Samudra court had links to the wider Muslim intellectual world and could attract men trained in the Persianate centers of the Islamic world.

Islamic learning in Pasai was practical as well as devotional. Jurists could advise on contracts, inheritance, marriage, and commercial disputes; teachers could instruct the children of merchants and officials; and scholars could write or copy texts. Sufi orders and devotional traditions, characteristic of the Indian Ocean Muslim world in these centuries, provided another channel of religious life, though the evidence for specific Sufi institutions in fourteenth-century Pasai is limited. Later tradition, including Malay literary works, remembered Pasai as a place of learning, and the later Malay chronicle Sejarah Melayu recounts that Melaka consulted Pasai's scholars on religious questions. That account is later tradition, not contemporary documentation, but it reflects a reputation that Pasai evidently came to possess.

Historians must separate this evidence from stories about missionaries and saints. The chronicle tradition credits a sheikh from the Arabian world with guiding the founder's conversion, and local memory preserves names of holy men. Such stories express the community's understanding of its religious origins, and they may reflect real teachers and networks, but they cannot be treated as independently confirmed biography. A more cautious conclusion is that the court's Islamic learning was sustained through repeated contact with scholars and merchants from the Indian Ocean world, not by a single founding mission.

The relationship between scholars and rulers was reciprocal. Rulers patronized religious teachers because their learning conferred legitimacy and administrative skill; scholars benefited from patronage, protection, and the opportunity to teach and judge. This partnership helped embed Islamic law and religious ceremony in court life. It did not, however, guarantee that all of society followed legal norms or that scholarly Islam described popular belief. The history of learning in Pasai therefore belongs with the history of power, not apart from it.

Section 63.13 · Division IV — Regional Rivalries, Diplomacy, and Historical Memory

Mapping Samudra-Pasai's Commercial and Diplomatic Connections

Samudra-Pasai's connections extended in several directions, and each kind of connection followed its own logic. Commercial routes carried goods, diplomatic routes carried envoys and gifts, and scholarly routes carried people and books. These routes sometimes overlapped but were not identical, and it would be misleading to infer one from another.

Westward, Pasai was tied to the Bay of Bengal and to the ports of South Asia, including Bengal, the Coromandel Coast, Malabar, and Gujarat. Textiles flowed from these regions, merchants and scholars traveled from them, and the marble of royal tombs came from Gujarat. Beyond these ports lay the Persian Gulf, the Red Sea, and the Mediterranean world described in Chapter 62. Eastward, the Strait of Malacca linked Pasai to the Malay Peninsula and to the commercial centers that were to become Melaka. Across the sea to the northeast lay the South China Sea and the markets of China, where Yuan and then Ming courts received envoys and traded.

Within the archipelago, Pasai dealt with other Sumatran centers and with Java. The Nagarakretagama, composed in 1365 at the Majapahit court, lists Samudra and Perlak among the Sumatran places it associates with Majapahit's sphere. Majapahit's court poets and officials presented Java as a center of wide-ranging influence, but such claims reflect the poem's own perspective, and they should not be read as evidence of effective administration over northern Sumatra. The Pasai chronicle, for its part, preserves a tradition of conflict with Java, and later Malay narratives remember Majapahit as a major power in the Strait (see Chapters 50 and 52). Both sources point to contact and rivalry without allowing a precise reconstruction of events.

Diplomacy had its own conventions. Chinese records frame foreign relations as tribute: rulers sent envoys with gifts and received titles and trade privileges in return. For ports like Pasai, such missions offered recognition, access to the Chinese market, and sometimes support against rivals. Relations with Muslim courts elsewhere, including those of South Asia, rested on shared religious identity, commerce, and the movement of scholars. Each connection supplied different resources: goods, prestige, learning, or alliance.

The routes were connected, but the political relationships were not interchangeable. A merchant's voyage from Gujarat, a scholarly journey across the Bay of Bengal and an envoy's visit to a Chinese court had different objectives, expenses and claims to authority. Maritime maps can show plausible corridors while concealing delays, intermediaries and temporary detours. Samudra's appearance in diplomatic documents therefore needs to be compared, not equated, with its prominence in trade or in the circulation of religious learning.

Section 63.14

Perlak, Lamuri, Pedir, and the Northern Sumatran Political World

Samudra-Pasai was one center among several in northern Sumatra, and its history cannot be separated from its neighbors. Perlak, to the east of Samudra, appears in Polo's account as a town whose people had been converted by Muslim merchants, and later local tradition claims an even earlier Muslim dynasty there. Those traditions are difficult to evaluate and are not confirmed by contemporary evidence, so the safest statement is that Perlak had Muslim inhabitants by 1292 and remained a recognized center in the following decades.

Lamuri, near the northern tip of the island, has the longest documented history of the group, with references in the eleventh-century Chola inscription and in thirteenth-century Chinese accounts, and Polo lists it as Lambri. The region of Aceh Besar, which later became the core of the Acehnese state, lay in or near this zone. Pedir (Pidie), on the northern coast, became prominent as a port, and the early sixteenth-century account of Tomé Pires describes it as an important and wealthy center engaged in the pepper trade. Other places, such as Daya and Biar, appear in Portuguese sources as smaller polities.

The relations among these centers involved competition and cooperation. Ports competed for shipping and for access to the pepper-producing districts. Rulers made marriage alliances that connected royal houses, and they sometimes claimed precedence or overlordship over neighbors. The sources, however, do not support the picture of a single northern Sumatran kingdom under Pasai. Instead they reveal a shifting constellation of centers, some stronger at one moment and weaker at another, with their own dynasties and relationships to hinterland communities. Historians avoid assuming that all these centers were simultaneously independent kingdoms or that any one held uncontested supremacy.

Another dimension of regional politics was religious. Not every coastal center became Muslim at the same time or in the same way, and hinterland communities often remained outside the new religious order. The contrast between town and mountain in Polo's account is a reminder that Islamization involved boundaries, and that rulers' relations with non-Muslim neighbors could be hostile, commercial, or both.

The varied visibility of neighboring ports makes regional comparison difficult. Lamuri appears in early foreign testimony, Perlak in Marco Polo's observations of a partly Muslim coast, and Pedir prominently in later commercial writing. Such uneven records do not necessarily mean that one harbor was flourishing while another had disappeared. Nor was every political relationship reducible to conquest: rulers could negotiate access to markets, share kinship ties, compete for inland supplies and accept ceremonial precedence without permanent submission. Their fortunes changed as merchants and local communities made decisions of their own.

Section 63.15

The Hikayat Raja-raja Pasai: Conversion, Dynastic Memory, and Historical Tradition

The Hikayat Raja-raja Pasai is the earliest known Malay dynastic chronicle and the principal local literary source for the history of Samudra-Pasai. It narrates how a young man named Merah Silu, found in the region of Samudra, becomes ruler, converts to Islam through a dream encounter with the Prophet, and takes the name al-Malik al-Salih. It recounts his sons, the foundation of the city of Pasai by one of them, the reigns of succeeding kings, episodes of conflict and intrigue, and an attack on Pasai from Java. It ends with the weakening of the kingdom.

Dating the chronicle requires separating its narrated events, its original composition and the surviving manuscripts. The principal witnesses are Raffles Malay 67, a manuscript copied for the Raffles collection in the early nineteenth century, and British Library Or. 14350, containing an incomplete version of Hikayat Raja Pasai within a Jawi manuscript associated with a 1797 Semarang copy. A. H. Hill published a romanized text and English translation of Raffles Malay 67 in 1960; Russell Jones edited the work in 1987 and subsequently published revised work and a translation titled The Pasai Chronicle in 2013. Differences between the witnesses and their respective contexts matter for textual criticism. Neither surviving copy belongs to the thirteenth century, and arguments for a much earlier composition require literary and historical analysis rather than inference from manuscript dates alone.

The work is best read as political and religious literature. Its account of conversion places divine sanction at the beginning of the dynasty and links the founder to Mecca and the Prophet through a visit by a holy man and through the dream. This is a story about legitimacy, not a verified account of how a ruler adopted Islam. The chronicle also shows ideas about kingship: the ruler's justice, his piety, his relationship to ministers and scholars, and the dangers of dynastic quarrel. Episodes of royal cruelty, rivalry among princes, and misfortune provide a moral frame, and it is difficult to separate historical memory from literary and didactic purpose.

Historians therefore compare the chronicle with independent evidence. The existence of a founder who became al-Malik al-Salih is consistent with the inscriptions, and the location of the early court in the Samudra area is consistent with Polo and Ibn Battuta. The chronicle's genealogy, however, cannot always be reconciled with inscriptional dates and recurring titles. Its claims about the sequence of rulers, the precise timing of conversion, and the details of foreign relations require corroboration. Used with care, it is invaluable evidence for how a Malay audience remembered Muslim kingship, and it influenced later Malay historical writing, but it is not a contemporary eyewitness record.

The two surviving manuscript witnesses also illuminate the history of the chronicle after Pasai's political prominence had waned. The incomplete British Library manuscript places the work in a later Javanese copying milieu; Raffles Malay 67 belongs to another episode of transmission and collection. Neither can be assumed to reproduce a lost fourteenth-century archetype without alteration. Comparison of shared episodes, gaps and differences may disclose a text's circulation among Malay-speaking communities, while still leaving uncertain exactly when its conversion story assumed its surviving form. The chronicle's long afterlife is itself evidence of how dynastic authority was remembered beyond the court that first inspired it.

Section 63.16

Samudra-Pasai and the Fifteenth-Century Asian Political Order

The fifteenth century brought new pressures and opportunities. The Ming dynasty, after its establishment in 1368, revived and expanded maritime diplomacy, sending embassies across Southeast Asia and, in the early fifteenth century, dispatching the large fleets associated with the admiral Zheng He. Samudra, often written as Su-men-da-la in Chinese sources, appears among the places that sent envoys and received Chinese missions, and Chinese texts record diplomatic exchange beginning in the later fourteenth century. The fleets of Zheng He called in the region, and the account of Ma Huan, an interpreter who traveled with some of the voyages, describes Samudra as a Muslim country whose people were engaged in trade and whose pepper and other goods were exported.

Chinese court sources describe a dynastic conflict in Samudra around 1414–1415. Their account names a ruler rendered Zainuliabiding (commonly identified as Zain al-Abidin) and a challenger Su-gan-la, often rendered Sekandar or Iskandar. In the Ming version, the challenger attacked or threatened the ruler, resisted Zheng He’s forces after receiving no recognition, and fled toward Lamuri, where he was captured with members of his family. He was transported to China in 1415. Geoff Wade emphasizes that the exact local dynamics remain obscure: Ming sources framed the conflict as a confrontation with a rebel and credited their fleet with restoring order, but the label “usurper” expresses a court-centered judgment rather than neutral proof of legitimacy. What the episode establishes is that an internal Sumatran contest could become entangled with outside naval intervention and imperial diplomacy.

Meanwhile the political and commercial center of the Strait was shifting. Melaka, on the Malay Peninsula, was rising as a major port in the fifteenth century (Chapter 64), and its rulers attracted merchants with favorable terms, Chinese recognition, and a convenient location. Melaka's growth meant competition for Pasai and gradually drew some of the commercial primacy that Pasai had enjoyed. The relationship was not purely rivalry, since Melaka's court became Muslim and drew on the scholarly reputation and political model of northern Sumatra, and Malay tradition recalls links of marriage and learning. Java's fortunes also changed as Majapahit fragmented (Chapter 52), reducing one source of external pressure and opening opportunities for Muslim ports on the Javanese north coast.

Pasai thus operated in a world of shifting centers. Its rulers continued to commission monuments, send envoys, and host merchants, and the tomb of Nahrasiyah (1428) attests to ongoing prosperity. Yet by the end of the century its position was less dominant than in the fourteenth. Whether this relative decline resulted chiefly from Melaka's rise, internal succession disputes, shifting trade routes, or a combination remains a matter of interpretation, and it should not be told as the story of an inevitable fall.

The 1414–1415 conflict is important for more than its dramatic capture. The Ming account translated local succession into the imperial vocabulary of loyal rulers, tribute and rebellion; it thereby presented Chinese intervention as the defense of an acceptable political order. From the standpoint of Sumatran rulers, foreign recognition could be a resource to seek, resist or contest. The episode does not show that China administered Pasai as a province, nor that every faction agreed on the Ming designation of legitimacy. It demonstrates instead how maritime diplomacy could alter the outcome of a local struggle without dissolving local political agency.

Section 63.17 · Division V — Political Transformation, Historiography, and the Legacy of Pasai

Continuity and Change in Northern Sumatran Muslim Society

Across the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, northern Sumatran Muslim society changed in important ways while preserving some enduring features. The most visible continuity was the pattern of royal patronage. Rulers continued to present themselves through Arabic titles, funerary monuments, ceremony, and support for scholars. Royal commemoration became, if anything, more elaborate, as shown by the carved tomb of Nahrasiyah in the fifteenth century. Elite households, linked by marriage to merchants and to the court, maintained their role in trade and politics, and the port remained the economic core.

Scholarship also showed continuity. The Shafi‘i legal tradition remained a mark of the court's religious identity, and Pasai maintained a reputation for learning that later Malay literature recalled. Commerce continued to depend on the same basic conditions: monsoon-driven shipping, the services of the harbor, and the court's ability to attract merchants. Meanwhile ordinary life, centered on farming, fishing, and local exchange, probably changed more slowly than the politics of the court.

Change is visible in the political environment. The fourteenth-century court dealt with Yuan and Javanese powers; by the fifteenth century, Ming diplomacy, Melaka’s rise, and rivalry among northern Sumatran centers affected its position. Chinese records and the dynastic chronicle preserve different kinds of testimony about contestation and succession. Pepper became a notable export in later accounts, though no continuous evidence measures its cultivation from decade to decade. Pasai’s relative prominence was challenged as other harbors attracted traffic, without proving that all its trade or institutions entered simultaneous decline.

Continuity in institutions need not mean continuity in the same personnel. A royal household might preserve Arabic funerary titles and patronage of jurists while individual lineages gained or lost prominence; visiting merchants could maintain routes even as specific partnerships ended. The cemetery record makes elite commemoration visible over time, but its uneven survival cannot measure how many people in town or countryside followed Islamic rites. The persistence of religious language at court is better documented than the changing practice of each occupational or rural community.

A further distinction concerns the fate of political authority compared with the fate of a place. Competition from Melaka or Pedir could reduce Pasai's relative bargaining power without emptying its harbor, eliminating its scholars or cancelling the commercial knowledge accumulated by its residents. Likewise, the reuse or replacement of an older royal memorial might signal an investment in history during changing political circumstances, rather than uninterrupted power of the founder's descendants. These different trajectories explain why historians should assess trade, dynasty, religious learning and local settlement separately instead of using one curve of 'rise' and 'decline' for all of them.

Section 63.18

When and How Did Pasai Become Muslim? Evidence and Competing Interpretations

The question of when and how northern Sumatra became Muslim has been debated for more than a century. Part of the difficulty is that the question contains several distinct problems. One concerns the presence of Muslims, a second concerns the conversion of local people, a third concerns the adoption of Islam by rulers, and a fourth concerns the establishment of Islamic institutions. These do not necessarily occur at the same time, and the evidence for each differs.

Scholars have also separated the earliest surviving evidence from actual origins. The earliest surviving evidence for a Muslim royal court in northern Sumatra comes from the late thirteenth century, and the case of al-Malik al-Salih's tombstone shows how even this evidence may be later than it appears. But the existence of a Muslim town at Perlak by 1292 shows that conversion had begun earlier. The date of origin is therefore earlier than the date of the first surviving evidence, by an interval that cannot be measured.

The merchant-centered explanation starts with the obvious fact that Muslims traded in northern Sumatran ports before the surviving royal inscriptions. Its strength is the dated testimony of foreign travelers and the geographic fit with Bay of Bengal exchanges. Its limit is causal: commerce alone cannot show why one ruler adopted Islamic titles while another did not. The royal-patronage explanation begins with funerary formulas, courtly scholarship and the political advantages of religious affiliation, but it risks turning decisions by rulers into explanations for the faith of all their subjects. Neither model can bypass the uncertain dating of al-Malik al-Salih's surviving markers.

A third approach emphasizes sustained family life, religious teaching and gradual local adoption. It can account for changes across generations more plausibly than a single conversion scene, yet the day-to-day evidence from Pasai remains exceptionally sparse. Later stories about holy men and Sufi teachers testify to the significance that later audiences gave such figures; without corroboration they cannot supply securely dated biographies of the earliest missionaries. The strongest interpretation therefore compares stages—resident Muslim groups, royal institutionalization, and change among wider populations—while recognizing that the stages might overlap or proceed in different sequences at adjacent ports.

Historical Debate

Merchants, Missionaries, or Rulers? Explaining the Rise of Muslim Courts in Northern Sumatra

The question: By what processes did northern Sumatran communities and rulers become Muslim, and from what regions did the decisive influences come?

Interpretation A — Trade-centered explanations: Many scholars emphasize the role of Muslim merchants, whose presence in port towns led over time to conversion of local communities and then rulers. Polo's account of Perlak, in which merchants convert the townspeople, is often cited. Early twentieth-century scholars, notably Snouck Hurgronje and Moquette, connected the process to South Asia, particularly Gujarat, partly through the evidence of imported marble tombstones.
Interpretation B — Scholars, Sufis, and missionaries: Other interpretations stress the role of learned men and Sufi teachers who spread Islamic teaching and gave it local roots. This view draws on chronicle traditions, though these are later and legendary, and on the presence of scholars at Samudra attested by Ibn Battuta.
Interpretation C — Rulers and local agency: A third approach emphasizes political decisions by local rulers who adopted Islam for reasons of legitimacy, alliance, and commercial advantage, and it highlights the selective way local societies shaped what they adopted. It underlines that the origin of the religion in a place does not determine how locals used it.

Evidence: The debate draws on Polo, Ibn Battuta, inscriptions, coins, the chronicle, and comparative evidence, and it has been complicated by the argument that some key funerary evidence is later than its recorded date, which weakens simple reliance on the earliest stones to map origins.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns chronology (how early conversion began), causation (which actors mattered most), geography (which regions supplied influences), and interpretation (how to read legendary traditions). Many scholars today favor combinations of these explanations rather than a single one.

Why it matters: How historians answer shapes the role they assign to Southeast Asian agents, to Indian Ocean networks, and to rulers' choices, and it affects how the beginnings of Islam in the Malay world are narrated.

Student task: Select one interpretation and identify the evidence that best supports it and the evidence that challenges it. Then explain what additional evidence could help decide among the interpretations.

Section 63.19

Northern Sumatra around 1500 and the Foundations of Later Acehnese Power

Around 1500, northern Sumatra consisted of several Muslim and partly Muslim political centers. Tomé Pires, writing in the years after the Portuguese conquest of Melaka in 1511 (Chapter 69), describes the region's kingdoms and ports, including Pasai, Pedir, and smaller polities such as Lamuri, Daya, and Biar, and notes the importance of pepper. His account suggests that Pasai remained a significant Muslim center but faced internal division and the pressures of changing commerce, while Pedir was prosperous. This is an outside Portuguese perspective, and it was written when Portuguese power was beginning to reshape the Strait (Chapter 70).

Within a few decades Pasai came under Portuguese pressure and then fell within the expanding authority of Aceh, whose ruler consolidated the Aceh Besar region and extended control over neighboring northern Sumatran ports in the 1520s. The detailed story of that expansion, and of Aceh's later sixteenth- and seventeenth-century power, belongs to Chapters 70 and 80. For the present chapter the question is what the earlier Muslim courts left behind.

Their legacies were real but should be stated carefully. Northern Sumatra had an established tradition of Muslim kingship, with its titles, ceremonies, and dynastic memory. It had a reputation for scholarship and a community of religious specialists. It had commercial institutions and knowledge of the pepper trade and of harbor administration. And it had a population accustomed to living under Muslim rulers and in contact with the wider Muslim world. These were resources available to later rulers.

They did not make later Acehnese power inevitable. The Acehnese state arose from specific political circumstances: the consolidation of a ruling house in Aceh Besar, the effects of the Portuguese presence at Melaka on Muslim commerce, military and diplomatic choices, and the participation of merchants and scholars in new networks. Historians avoid reading Pasai's history as a prelude to Aceh, because that would obscure both the independent achievements of the earlier courts and the contingent decisions of the later ones.

The period around 1500 is best viewed from several vantage points. Portuguese writers soon described northern Sumatran harbors as competitors and suppliers, not as the remnants of one uniformly exhausted kingdom. Muslim merchants, scholars and households could transfer affiliations among ports as circumstances changed, while rulers sought revenue, protection and dynastic advantage. Modern historians know more about the next century because foreign documentation becomes richer; that improvement in surviving sources should not itself be mistaken for an abrupt transformation in local capacities. A more limited conclusion is that older institutions coexisted with intensified commercial and political competition on the eve of major sixteenth-century change.

Section 63.20

Samudra-Pasai and the Early Muslim States of Sumatra: A Historical Synthesis

The history of Samudra-Pasai and its neighbors demonstrates that the formation of Muslim states in Maritime Southeast Asia was a political process as well as a religious one. Local rulers took advantage of commercial networks, adopted a recognizable language of Islamic kingship, patronized scholars, minted coins, commissioned or inherited monuments, and negotiated with neighbors and distant courts. Their courts were not passive trading posts but active participants in the political life of the Strait.

The history also demonstrates the importance of source criticism. The date inscribed on the tombstone of al-Malik al-Salih may record a real death in 1297, but the stone may have been produced much later; the title al-Malik al-Zahir may belong to more than one ruler; undated coins cannot fix chronology by themselves; and the Hikayat Raja-raja Pasai preserves dynastic memory rather than eyewitness record. These problems do not make the history unknowable. They require historians to be explicit about layers of evidence and about the difference between early documentation and origins.

Historical Synthesis

Five Findings about Early Muslim Northern Sumatra

  • Politics: A Muslim ruling house was established in the Samudra area by the late thirteenth century, and its authority extended through port control, alliance, and coercion rather than fixed borders.
  • Religion: Islamic titles, scholarship, and ceremony reinforced royal legitimacy, while religious change in the countryside was slower and uneven.
  • Economy: Customs revenue, pepper and other exports, harbor services, and a local gold coinage sustained the court.
  • Evidence: Inscriptions, coins, travelers' accounts, Chinese and Javanese texts, and a Malay chronicle must be read together, with attention to dates of manufacture and composition.
  • Legacy: Pasai contributed institutions, memory, and reputation to the later Malay-Muslim world without making Acehnese power inevitable.

Why the synthesis matters: It shows northern Sumatra as a creative political society and demonstrates how historians turn fragmentary evidence into careful interpretation.

Within the larger textbook, this chapter connects the transregional networks of Chapter 62 with the later histories of Melaka (Chapter 64), the wider process of Islamization (Chapter 65), and the northern maritime states (Chapter 66). It shows that Islam's early history in Maritime Southeast Asia was not a single arrival but a sequence of local political and social transformations, and it prepares the ground for understanding how later courts built on, rivaled, and reinterpreted the earlier ones.

No single document answers all the central questions. Marco Polo provides an account of the religious differentiation of port and upland in 1292, but not a complete royal genealogy. A later gravestone gives a recorded death date for al-Malik al-Salih, but its carving probably belongs to another political generation. Ibn Battuta depicts an active learned court in the 1340s, but says little about many households beyond it. Undated coins represent claims to sovereignty without supplying regnal years, and the Hikayat organizes dynastic memory in the form of morally charged literature. Read together and with their limits exposed, these sources permit a meaningful but provisional reconstruction.

Pasai's historical significance lies in the interaction of these different developments: maritime exchange created opportunities, rulers and their allies fashioned institutions, scholars maintained connections across seas, and local populations experienced religious and political change at varied speeds. The evidence for later conflict with neighboring centers and for Ming intervention warns against imagining an isolated or stable court. The development of Aceh in a subsequent era drew on parts of this northern Sumatran world but was not written into its earlier history. To describe Samudra-Pasai accurately is to recover a set of consequential local choices within an interconnected region, without turning those choices into a predestined national or religious story.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined how northern Sumatra's coastal communities developed some of the earliest documented Muslim courts in Maritime Southeast Asia, and how those courts used religion, commerce, diplomacy, and institutions to establish and maintain authority. Where Chapter 62 described the networks linking Southeast Asia to Islamic worlds, Chapter 63 studied the states and societies that took shape within them.

The evidence is fragmentary and varied: Arabic inscriptions, undated gold coins, the reports of Marco Polo and Ibn Battuta, Chinese and Javanese court texts, Portuguese description, limited archaeology, and the Malay Hikayat Raja-raja Pasai. Each source type has distinctive strengths and limits, and historians must compare them rather than rely on any one. The chapter's central methodological lesson is that dates recorded in inscriptions must be distinguished from the dates on which objects were made, as the case of al-Malik al-Salih's tombstone shows.

Northern Sumatra's geography shaped its history without determining it. River mouths and anchorages at the junction of the Andaman Sea and the Strait of Malacca made the region a natural place of landfall, provisioning, and exchange for ships using the monsoon. Rulers who could attract merchants and manage neighbors prospered, while their authority extended outward unevenly from the port into hinterlands whose peoples were often not Muslim.

Marco Polo's 1292 account provides a datable snapshot: Muslim merchants had converted the townspeople of Perlak, while hill communities remained non-Muslim and Samara had its own ruler. This shows that conversion was already underway, that it was uneven, and that the earliest surviving evidence is not the same as the earliest conversion.

Samudra-Pasai emerged as an identifiable Muslim ruling center between roughly 1250 and 1350. Its rulers adopted Arabic honorific titles such as al-Malik al-Salih and al-Malik al-Zahir, patronized scholars, minted gold coins, and were commemorated with Arabic funerary inscriptions. Recurring titles and undated coins complicate the reconstruction of a king list, and the chronicle's genealogy cannot always be reconciled with inscriptions.

The economic foundations of the court lay in port commerce: customs and fees, harbor services, pepper and other exports, and gifts and tribute exchange. These resources supported royal households, ceremonial display, and military action, including campaigns against non-Muslim neighbors that, according to Ibn Battuta, produced captives and tribute. Wealth was unevenly distributed, and the economy depended on political management of merchants and rivals.

Social life in the port involved merchant households, intermarriage with local families, royal and non-royal women, and labor that included enslaved and dependent people. Islamic learning, associated with Shafi‘i law, visiting scholars, and devotional traditions, supported legitimacy and administration. Stories of missionaries and saints preserve memory but cannot be treated as confirmed biography.

Samudra-Pasai interacted with the Bay of Bengal, Java, China, and the Strait. Neighbors such as Perlak, Lamuri, and Pedir were separate centers, and the region is best understood as a shifting constellation rather than a unified kingdom. In the fifteenth century Ming diplomacy, Zheng He's expeditions, and the rise of Melaka altered the balance of commerce and politics, and Pasai's primacy was increasingly contested.

Scholars explain conversion through merchants, scholars and Sufis, and rulers' choices, and many favor combinations of these. The debate shows how interpretation depends on evidence and on assumptions about agency. Around 1500 northern Sumatra comprised several Muslim polities, and the legacies of Pasai in kingship, scholarship, and commercial knowledge were resources for later rulers, though they did not make Acehnese power inevitable.

The chapter thus presents Samudra-Pasai as an active political society whose history reveals the formation of Muslim states in Maritime Southeast Asia as a process of local choice, negotiation, and adaptation. It prepares the way for the study of Melaka in Chapter 64, the wider processes of Islamization in Chapter 65, and the northern maritime networks in Chapter 66.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Samudra-Pasai
The Muslim political and commercial complex of northern Sumatra's northeastern coast, known from inscriptions, coins, foreign accounts, and a Malay chronicle; the names Samudra and Pasai are not always interchangeable in the sources.
Perlak
A northern Sumatran port center described by Marco Polo in 1292 as having a Muslim town population converted by merchants.
Lamuri
A northern Sumatran polity near the island's northern tip, known from Chola, Chinese, and European sources, in a region later associated with Aceh Besar.
Pedir (Pidie)
A northern coastal port center described by Portuguese sources of the early sixteenth century as prosperous and engaged in the pepper trade.
Al-Malik al-Salih
The earliest documented Muslim ruler of Samudra, whose death is recorded in 696 AH (1297 CE) and who tradition identifies with the chronicle's Merah Silu.
Al-Malik al-Zahir
A royal honorific borne by successive Pasai rulers; it cannot identify one individual without corroborating evidence.
Laqab
An Arabic honorific title adopted by rulers and other prominent men, distinct from a personal name and often shared by several individuals.
Hijri (AH) date
A date reckoned from the Islamic era beginning in 622 CE; inscriptions of Pasai record death dates in this system.
Epigraphy
The study of inscriptions; in this chapter, chiefly Arabic funerary texts on grave markers.
Numismatics
The study of coins and monetary practice; Pasai's undated gold coins illustrate the limits of numismatic dating.
Cambay-type marble
A category of carved marble grave markers resembling stones produced in the Gujarat region and exported across the Indian Ocean, found in northern Sumatran cemeteries.
Retrospective memorial
A monument produced substantially later than the death or event it commemorates, preserving a dynasty's memory rather than contemporary testimony.
Manufacture date
The time at which an object was actually made, which may differ from any date inscribed upon it.
Shafi‘i school
A Sunni school of Islamic law prominent in the Samudra court and later in much of maritime Southeast Asia.
Qadi
An Islamic judge, one of the learned officials associated with Muslim courts.
Sayyid
A title for men claiming descent from the Prophet, borne by some scholars and notables in the Muslim world.
Sufism
The devotional and mystical traditions of Islam, which provided teachers and communities across the Indian Ocean world.
Hikayat
A Malay prose narrative, including dynastic chronicles such as the Hikayat Raja-raja Pasai.
Hikayat Raja-raja Pasai
A Malay dynastic chronicle narrating the conversion of Merah Silu and Pasai's early rulers; composed later than the events it describes and surviving in a much later manuscript.
Merah Silu
The founder of the dynasty in chronicle tradition, who converts to Islam and takes the name al-Malik al-Salih.
Entrepôt
A port whose wealth depends chiefly on its role as a hub of exchange.
Customs revenue
Income from duties and fees levied on ships and cargo, a principal source of royal wealth in port polities.
Anchorage
A sheltered place where ships can moor, essential to the location of ports.
Hinterland
The inland and upland area connected to a port by exchange, tribute, and conflict.
Tributary diplomacy
A Chinese framework of foreign relations in which rulers sent envoys with gifts and received recognition and trading privileges.
Nagarakretagama
A Javanese court poem of 1365 listing places, including Samudra and Perlak, within the Majapahit court's geographic horizon.
Islamization
The uneven, multi-layered process by which Islam became part of the religious and political life of societies, distinct from the presence of Muslim merchants or the conversion of a ruler.
Mandala
A modern scholarly model of overlapping, center-oriented political authority without fixed borders, useful for understanding port polities such as Pasai.
Source criticism
The practice of evaluating the origin, date, purpose, and limits of evidence before using it.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a state is made in the interaction of networks, institutions, and local choices. Samudra-Pasai's rulers did not simply receive Islam from merchants and missionaries. They turned it into a language of kingship, a framework for law and learning, and a basis for diplomacy, and in doing so they shaped how later Malay courts understood Muslim rule.

The chapter also teaches a way of reading the past. A carved date, a coin legend, a traveler's remark, and a dynastic story are each real evidence, yet none speaks for itself. The case of the tombstone, whose recorded date and probable date of manufacture may be separated by generations, is a reminder that the most authoritative-looking evidence may preserve memory rather than testimony, and that memory is itself a historical fact worth studying.

The history of northern Sumatra connects forward and backward in the textbook. Backward, it builds on the commercial networks of Chapter 62 and the transformation of Srivijaya in Chapter 47. Forward, it prepares for Melaka, for the wider course of Islamization, and for the northern maritime networks of Brunei and Sulu, and it supplies part of the background to the later Acehnese state without determining it.

Finally, the chapter reinforces the textbook's central argument. The rulers, merchants, scholars, craftspeople, and farmers of northern Sumatra were historical actors who selected, adapted, and transformed outside influences within their own political and social worlds. Understanding them as such, and understanding the limits of the evidence that survives about them, is essential to writing a history of Southeast Asia that is both accurate and humane.
