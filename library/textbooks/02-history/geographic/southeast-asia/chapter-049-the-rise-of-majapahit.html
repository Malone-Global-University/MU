The Rise of Majapahit

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Majapahit did not begin as the great archipelagic power remembered in later Javanese literature. It began in 1293 as a small, improvised court founded by a defeated dynastic claimant, supported by villagers, kinsmen, and regional allies, and made possible by the arrival and failure of a Mongol-Yuan fleet.

In 1294, a ruler who styled himself Kertarajasa Jayawardhana issued a royal charter that recorded his gratitude to a village called Kudadu. The village had helped him, the text explains, during a moment when he was a fugitive, pursued after a military defeat and without a throne. The charter survives, and it is one of the few documents from the foundation years of Majapahit that was produced at the time rather than composed generations later. It reveals a king who remembered, and wished others to remember, that his authority had been rescued by ordinary local people at a moment of acute danger. It also shows a monarch using a legal instrument to turn gratitude into a lasting political relationship.

The crisis that produced that charter unfolded across 1292 and 1293. In 1292 the kingdom of Singhasari, the most ambitious East Javanese state of the thirteenth century, was destroyed when Jayakatwang, a ruler connected to the older traditions of Kediri, attacked and killed King Kertanagara, a story introduced in Chapter 48. Within months, a large Yuan expeditionary force sent by Kublai Khan arrived in Java to punish Kertanagara, only to find him dead and the political order he had defied already overturned. Out of this collision of a local succession struggle and an imperial punitive expedition came a new political center in the Brantas basin, ruled by a man whom contemporary inscriptions call Narāryya Saṅgrāmawijaya before his consecration and remembered as Raden Wijaya and identified with Harṣawijaya in some later literature, although that identification is disputed.

The central historical question of this chapter is how Raden Wijaya transformed the political crisis of 1292–1293 into the foundation of Majapahit, and what institutions and alliances allowed the new kingdom to survive its turbulent first years. The question requires attention to several kinds of history at once. It involves dynastic politics and marriage, because Wijaya's claim rested on kinship with the rulers of Singhasari. It involves military and diplomatic history, because Wijaya cooperated with the Yuan commanders and then turned on them. It involves institutional history, because a king must reward supporters, appoint officials, and secure revenue before a court can endure. And it involves social and economic history, because the new settlement depended on rice land, river transport, labor, and the goodwill of village communities.

A distinction governs the whole chapter. The founding of a new ruling center in 1293 and the later construction of Majapahit's extensive regional political network are separate historical developments. The kingdom established by Wijaya was small, recently created, and vulnerable. The far-reaching influence associated with Gajah Mada and Hayam Wuruk, examined beginning in Chapter 50, was assembled over most of a century. Treating the first as though it already contained the second is a form of teleology, the assumption that the later outcome was present at the start, which this textbook resists throughout. Students should learn to ask what a given source or tradition can actually say about 1293, as opposed to what later writers wished to say about the origins of their own kingdom.

The evidence for these events is unusually varied and unusually difficult. Contemporary copper-plate and stone charters in Old Javanese record royal grants. The official Yuan history, compiled by Ming dynasty scholars from earlier records, gives a Chinese imperial account of the expedition. Javanese literary works, especially the Pararaton, the Deśawarṇana (commonly known as the Nagarakretagama), and several later kidung poems, narrate the founding in vivid detail but were composed decades or centuries afterward for audiences that already revered the dynasty. Section 49.1 explains why none of these can stand alone, and why the foundation of Majapahit must be reconstructed by testing sources against one another.

This chapter closes Volume III. The kingdoms studied in that volume, from Angkor and Pagan to Đại Việt, Champa, Srivijaya, and Singhasari, were the classical states of mainland and maritime Southeast Asia. Majapahit's first generation is where that story passes into the next volume's concerns: the religious, commercial, and political transformations of the period from about 1200 to 1500. Chapter 50 takes up Gajah Mada and Majapahit at its height, Chapter 51 the society, religion, and commerce of Majapahit Java, and Chapter 52 the kingdom's fragmentation. The present chapter supplies the foundations those chapters assume: how the court began, who made it possible, and what it inherited.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  2. Explain why Jayakatwang's overthrow of Singhasari rested on genuine dynastic claims rather than simple illegitimate rebellion.
  3. Analyze Raden Wijaya's descent, marriage ties, and the debate over the names Narāryya Saṅgrāmawijaya and Harṣawijaya.
  4. Trace the sequence of the Yuan expedition of 1293, from Kublai Khan's decision through the defeat of Jayakatwang and the Yuan withdrawal.
  5. Assess why Wijaya and the Yuan commanders each had reasons to cooperate, and why that cooperation ended.
  6. Describe the establishment of the Majapahit settlement at Tarik and the royal consecration of Kertarajasa Jayawardhana, distinguishing documented facts from foundation tradition.
  7. Interpret the Kudadu and Sukamerta inscriptions as evidence of how royal grants, sīma status, and loyalty formed the early kingdom's political system.
  8. Evaluate the early court's officials, ritual networks, and agricultural and commercial foundations without projecting Hayam Wuruk-era institutions backward.
  9. Compare competing reconstructions of the early rebellions of Ranggalawe, Sora, and Nambi and explain why their chronology is contested.

Historical Context

East Java in the thirteenth century was a region of fertile volcanic soil, productive wet-rice farming, and navigable rivers, above all the Brantas, which flows from the highlands near Malang north and east to the Madura Strait and the coast near modern Surabaya. Kings in this region had long drawn their strength from control of rice-producing communities and from access to river ports through which goods flowed to the Java Sea. This agrarian and riverine foundation, rather than any simple commercial emphasis, shaped the political competition examined in this chapter. Chapters 30 through 32 traced the earlier shift of royal power from Central to East Java, and Chapter 48 treated the later East Javanese state of Singhasari.

Political tradition in East Java was divided between two memories. One centered on Kediri, the kingdom whose rulers, based around Daha in the upper Brantas plain, had presided over a prosperous court culture in the twelfth century. The other centered on Tumapel, the district near modern Malang from which Singhasari emerged. According to the Javanese literary tradition, a commoner-turned-ruler named Ken Angrok overthrew the last Kediri king in the early thirteenth century and founded the line later called the Rajasa dynasty. Whatever the historical details, the Kediri defeat left a legacy of grievance and of surviving noble families whose claims to rule did not vanish. This is the background to Jayakatwang's later revolt and to the claim, examined in Section 49.2, that he was no mere rebel.

Singhasari reached its peak under Kertanagara, who pursued an assertive foreign policy that included an expedition toward the Malay world and religious innovations that fused Śaiva and Buddhist practice. These policies earned him admirers and enemies, and modern historians continue to debate how far they strained the kingdom's resources. What matters for the present chapter is that Kertanagara's court was a network of kinsmen, officials, and regional lords whose loyalty was personal and renewable rather than automatic. When the king died, that network did not simply transfer its allegiance to a single successor, and a surviving member of the dynasty had to rebuild it.

Beyond Java, the thirteenth century was shaped by the expansion of the Mongol-Yuan state under Kublai Khan. Chapter 46 compared Yuan campaigns and diplomatic pressure in Đại Việt, Champa, Pagan, and Java. Yuan policy combined demands for submission and tribute with naval and land expeditions, and its envoys visited Southeast Asian courts repeatedly. The Yuan court regarded Java's refusal to submit as an offense requiring punishment. The result was an unusual episode in Javanese history: a Chinese-organized army, carried by sea across a considerable distance, landed in the middle of a Javanese succession crisis and became, for a few months, a participant in it.

49.1 — Reconstructing Majapahit's Foundation: Inscriptions, Chinese Records, and Later Javanese Literature

The foundation of Majapahit is documented by three kinds of evidence that were created for different purposes and that do not always agree. Understanding the differences is the first task of this chapter, because the story of 1292–1293 changes in important ways depending on which source is trusted for which question.

The first kind is the contemporary royal charter. Two are central. The Kudadu inscription of 1294, a grant issued by the newly crowned Kertarajasa Jayawardhana, records favors given to the village of Kudadu in return for help during Wijaya's flight. The Sukamerta charter, dated 29 October 1296, records a grant connected with faithful service and the confirmation of a village's special status. Both were issued within a few years of the events they mention, chiefly in Old Javanese; the Sukamerta text also contains Sanskrit linguistic material. They are the closest thing to firsthand evidence from the Majapahit court. But they were not histories. They were legal instruments, composed to justify rewards and to establish rights, and they mention the founding crisis only to the extent that it explained a grant.

The second kind is the Chinese imperial record. The Yuan shi, the official history of the Yuan dynasty, was compiled in the early Ming period from earlier court documents. Its account of the Java expedition appears in a chapter on Java and in the biographies of the commanders Shi Bi, Gao Xing, and Yiheimishi. These texts are the main evidence for the military course of the campaign and for the Yuan court's later assessment of it. Their strengths are an external viewpoint, a concern with dates and operations, and records produced by bureaucrats with access to reports. Their limits are equally clear. They describe events from the standpoint of an empire that had suffered a setback, they transcribe Javanese names and titles in Chinese characters that are hard to match with confidence, and their figures for troops and casualties reflect official reporting rather than independent counting.

The third kind is later Javanese literature. The Deśawarṇana, usually called the Nagarakretagama, was composed in 1365 by the court poet Prapañca during the reign of Hayam Wuruk and presents the founder's career as part of a glorified dynastic history. The Pararaton, a prose chronicle known as the Book of Kings, survives in manuscripts that are much later than the events it describes and mixes dynastic history with legend. Several kidung, narrative poems preserved largely in later, including Balinese, traditions, tell dramatic stories of Wijaya's flight, his stratagems, and the quarrels among his companions. These works preserve memories and local detail found nowhere else. They also reflect the interests of courts that venerated Wijaya as an ancestor, and they are shaped by literary conventions in which cleverness and destiny are favored over administrative detail.

The practical consequence is that no single source supplies a reliable narrative. The charters give anchored but narrow evidence, the Yuan shi gives an external and dated military account, and the Javanese literature supplies the human story at the cost of reliability. Historians therefore work by triangulation. Where a charter and a chronicle agree, as in the importance of Kudadu, confidence is high. Where only a chronicle describes an episode, as with the clearing of the forest at Tarik, the account is best treated as tradition. Where Chinese and Javanese sources conflict, as in details of Wijaya's reversal against the Yuan, the disagreement itself is evidence about what each tradition wished to emphasize.

Source Method
Javanese Inscriptions, Chronicles, and Yuan Records

Contemporary inscriptions (Kudadu, 1294; Sukamerta, 1296) are the strongest evidence for what the court did and claimed in its first years, but they speak narrowly, in legal language, and from the king's perspective.

The Yuan shi gives an external, dated account of the military campaign and of its political aftermath in China, but it was compiled later from official reports, and its numbers must be treated as official claims rather than measured facts.

The Pararaton, the Deśawarṇana, and the kidung supply names, motives, and episodes, but they were produced decades or centuries later by writers honoring the dynasty. Their narratives of cleverness and destiny are literary as well as historical.

Working rule: prefer contemporary evidence for what it covers, use Chinese records for the shape of the campaign, and treat later Javanese literature as valuable tradition to be tested rather than as a transcript of events.

Section 49.2

49.2 — The Destruction of Singhasari in 1292 and the Political Claims of Jayakatwang

The events of Jayakatwang's revolt were told in Chapter 48. What matters here is the political situation they created, and above all the question of how Jayakatwang's actions looked to the East Javanese elite who lived through them. The later Majapahit tradition, which shaped most surviving narratives, cast him as a treacherous usurper. A more careful reading shows a ruler with substantial claims.

Jayakatwang is associated in the sources with Gelang-gelang, an area of the interior, and with the older ruling traditions of Kediri. The Javanese literary tradition describes him as a descendant of the Kediri kings overthrown in the early thirteenth century and as connected to Kertanagara through ties of kinship and marriage. Whatever the precise genealogy, Kediri had been the seat of a prestigious court, and the families descended from it had not forgotten that. The long competition between Kediri and its neighbors, in which Javanese tradition also mentions Janggala, left a reservoir of legitimacy that an ambitious claimant could draw on. Reading Jayakatwang as a Kediri claimant rather than as a rebel against a legitimate monarchy explains why his takeover was accepted, at least for a time, by many of the region's people.

The later narratives describe the attack as a two-pronged operation. In the usual account, a force was sent north to draw off Singhasari's defenders while another struck the capital from another direction, and Kertanagara was killed. These details come from the Pararaton and the kidung, and they should be treated as tradition. The Pararaton also suggests that the king was caught engaged in ritual practice, a detail that may reflect later criticism of Kertanagara's religious experiments rather than reliable reporting. What can be said with confidence is that Singhasari collapsed in 1292, that Kertanagara died, and that Jayakatwang took power and ruled from Daha.

For those who survived, the situation was dangerous and uncertain. Singhasari's officials, kinsmen, and army commanders had to decide whether to submit to Jayakatwang, flee, or resist. Some, according to tradition, accepted his rule. Others, including a member of the Singhasari family named Wijaya, sought refuge. Jayakatwang's regime was brief, but it was not a mere interlude. It had a court, followers, and an expectation of lasting rule, and the Yuan intervention, examined below, ended it before that expectation could be tested.

49.3 — Raden Wijaya: Dynastic Descent, Marriage Alliances, and Claims to the Throne

Raden Wijaya is the name by which Javanese literary tradition remembers the founder of Majapahit. The title Raden reflects later usage, while the Kudadu and Sukamerta inscriptions identify the future king before his consecration as Narāryya Saṅgrāmawijaya. Later narrative literature, notably the Kidung Harṣawijaya, associates the founder with Harṣawijaya. This is not simply a contrast between a documented man and an invented poetic name. The earlier Mūla-Malurung charter actually names a high-ranking Śrī Harṣawijaya, and the name occurs again in the fragmentary Balawi material of 1305. Hadi Sidomulyo argues that this historical Harṣawijaya was a different member of the dynasty, plausibly the founder’s father Dyah Lembu Tal, and that later storytelling conflated father and son. His identification is a reasoned reconstruction, not an uncontested genealogical fact.

The stronger documentary point concerns the founder’s lineage. The 1294 Kudadu record calls him a grandson of Narasinghamurti, a ruler prominent in Singhasari’s extended Rajasa family; the Deśawarṇana describes Dyah Lembu Tal as his parent. The Mūla-Malurung inscription supplies additional evidence about the region’s branching lineages, but it does not turn every later family story into fact. Wijaya therefore had a credible position among Singhasari’s relatives without demonstrable appointment as Kertanagara’s formally designated crown prince. This distinction explains why marriage, alliances, and victory all mattered to his accession.

Marriage strengthened that claim. According to the Sukamerta charter and later literature, Wijaya married daughters of Kertanagara. These marriages bound him to the household of the last Singhasari king and gave him standing as a son-in-law and kinsman, in a society where marriage ties created political obligations and where descent through women could carry legitimacy. They also meant that any children born to the new king would inherit blood from both lines of the older dynasty, a point developed in Section 49.13.

A caution is essential here. Later sources sometimes describe Wijaya as having been formally chosen or recognized as Kertanagara's heir. The surviving evidence does not establish this. What it shows is a kinsman with a plausible claim, strong marriage ties, and the opportunity created by the catastrophe. Whether he was seen by contemporaries as the natural leader of the surviving Singhasari faction, or became one through his actions in 1292–1293, is a question the evidence leaves open. Treating Wijaya as the designated successor would project a later legitimizing narrative back onto a moment when his position was uncertain.

Historical Debate

Who Was Wijaya, and What Was His Claim?

The question: How should historians reconcile the founder's name and descent as given in contemporary inscriptions with the different names and genealogies preserved in later chronicles and poems?

Interpretation A — inscriptional differentiation: The contemporary Kudadu and Sukamerta records identify the founder as Narāryya Saṅgrāmawijaya; the Mūla-Malurung record identifies an earlier Śrī Harṣawijaya. Sidomulyo reads these as separate people and proposes that the older Harṣawijaya may have been Dyah Lembu Tal. The hypothesis has explanatory power but is not a universally settled identification.
Interpretation B — the literary traditions preserve useful memory: Other readers argue that the chronicles may preserve genuine family memory, including lines of descent from the Rajasa founder, that the charters did not need to record, and that the later names and genealogies cannot simply be discarded.

Evidence: The documentary comparison involves the Kudadu and Sukamerta charters, the earlier Mūla-Malurung charter, the Balawi fragments, the Deśawarṇana and later narratives. The disagreement is about the identification of named individuals as well as the weight assigned to literary evidence.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns terminology and genealogy, not whether Wijaya was a member of the Singhasari dynastic family, which all sources affirm.

Why it matters: If the inscriptional line is followed, Wijaya's claim looks like that of a kinsman strengthened by marriage rather than that of a designated successor, and the foundation of Majapahit looks more contingent.

Student task: Explain what kinds of evidence would support the view that a chronicle's genealogy preserves genuine family memory, and what would count against it.

Section 49.4

49.4 — Defeat, Flight to Kudadu, Refuge in Madura, and the Support of Wiraraja

After Jayakatwang's forces overran Singhasari, Wijaya and his followers were defeated and fled. The Kudadu inscription of 1294 provides unusually strong evidence for this episode. It records that during his flight he was helped by the leadership of the village of Kudadu, and that he later rewarded the village for it. The text thus fixes two points that later narratives embellish: that Wijaya was a fugitive, and that village communities gave him aid when the court could not.

The inscription should be read in two ways. It is a record of what happened, and it is also an instrument designed to justify reward. A king acknowledging that villagers had saved him was making a public statement about his legitimacy and about the proper return for loyalty, a lesson any observer could draw. The text therefore preserves a real event but presents it in a way that served the new ruler's purposes. This does not make it unreliable, but it means that the gratitude it expresses was also a political act.

From Kudadu, the Javanese narratives describe Wijaya's journey to Madura, the island across the strait to the northeast. There he found a patron in Wiraraja, a figure associated with Sumenep. According to tradition, Wiraraja had earlier been sent away from the Singhasari court by Kertanagara and so had reasons for ambivalence toward the old regime. The later accounts credit him with securing refuge for Wijaya, arranging political relationships, and counseling a course in which Wijaya would seem to submit to Jayakatwang while preparing for a return. In some versions, this led to Wijaya being granted land in the forested area of Tarik, which became the site of the future settlement. These details come from the Pararaton and the kidung and cannot be confirmed from contemporary sources.

Even allowing for embellishment, the outline is plausible and historically important. Wijaya's recovery depended on regional support: village leaders who sheltered him, a Madurese ruler who gave him refuge, and a network of followers who stayed with him. The importance of Wiraraja is reinforced by the later prominence of his family in the new court, which suggests that the tradition of an early alliance preserves a real political bargain. Wijaya's survival in 1292 was not primarily a story of individual cleverness. It was a story of relationships, in which a defeated claimant found others willing to bet on his return.

Context: The inscription was issued shortly after Wijaya's consecration, as part of his effort to reward those who had aided him and to publicize that obligation. It is among the earliest contemporary documents from the Majapahit court.

Editorial paraphrase (not a collated translation or verbatim excerpt):

The charter recounts that, after a military defeat, the future king was pursued and sought safety, that the leadership of Kudadu gave him assistance at a moment of danger, and that, having become king, he granted the village favored status in recognition of that help.

Analysis: The source shows that Wijaya's flight was a real event, that village leadership played a part in his survival, and that early Majapahit royal policy included reciprocal rewards for personal loyalty. It also shows how a king could use a legal document to record gratitude in a form that had practical, lasting consequences for the beneficiaries.

Limitations: The text is brief and was written to justify a grant, so it omits events that did not bear on the reward. It does not allow us to locate Kudadu precisely, and it cannot confirm the later literary accounts of Madura, Wiraraja's advice, or the submission to Jayakatwang.

Perspective: The king's. The villagers' own understanding of the episode, their risks, and their motives are not recorded.

Division II — The Yuan Expedition and the Defeat of Jayakatwang · Section 49.5

49.5 — Kublai Khan's Decision to Intervene: Yuan Diplomacy and the Punitive Expedition

The Yuan court had been sending demands for submission to Southeast Asian rulers for years, and Java was among the states approached. Chinese records report that Kertanagara resisted these demands. The incident that became the stated cause of the expedition occurred in 1289, when the envoy Meng Qi was sent to Java and was mistreated. Chinese sources report that his face was marked or mutilated and that he was sent back. For the Yuan court, an insult to an envoy was an insult to the emperor, and it demanded a response.

The decision to dispatch a military force must be understood in the context of Yuan imperial policy, which Chapter 46 examined comparatively. By the early 1290s the Yuan state had already campaigned against Đại Việt, Champa, and Pagan and had attempted overseas invasions elsewhere. Those undertakings had produced mixed results, and the cost of projecting force across water was well known. The Java expedition drew on this experience but also faced its own difficulties: a very long sea voyage, dependence on monsoon winds, uncertain intelligence about Javanese politics, and the need to supply an army far from China.

The chosen commanders were Shi Bi, Gao Xing, and Yiheimishi, whose careers are recorded in the Yuan shi. Shi Bi and Gao Xing were Chinese officers, and Yiheimishi, whose name is also rendered Yi Ke Mishi, was of Uyghur background and had experience of Yuan maritime diplomacy. Their mixed backgrounds reflect the diversity of the Yuan establishment. The fleet’s departure from Quanzhou belongs to the final lunar month of the Yuan regnal year conventionally labeled 1292; in reconstructed Gregorian reckoning, the sailing is placed around 22 January 1293 (Hung and colleagues, 2022). A date given only as “late 1292” without specifying the calendar is misleading.

A crucial point follows from the timing. The expedition was prepared and launched on the basis of information that was already obsolete. By the time it sailed, Kertanagara, whose conduct had provoked it, was probably dead, and the Javanese political order had been overturned. This meant that the Yuan force sailed toward a situation its planners did not know. It would arrive to find no clear target for the punishment it had been sent to deliver, and it would have to improvise a political settlement on the spot. The chapter's concern is the consequences of that improvisation for Java, not a second full operational history of the campaign.

Section 49.6

49.6 — The Arrival of the Yuan Fleet in 1293: An Expedition Confronts a Changed Political Order

The Yuan shi places the expedition within a chain of maritime passages along the South China Sea and past the coast of Champa before it crossed toward the island channels north of Java. A reconstruction informed by the Karimata Strait evidence follows the Natuna–Tambelan area, Serutu and the adjoining Karimata waters, and Pulau Gelam before proceeding toward Karimunjawa and Tuban. Older literature sometimes assigned a disputed island-name to Belitung; archaeological and textual reconsideration counsels against treating that identification as established. These are reconstructed correspondences between medieval descriptions and modern geography, not a complete ship-by-ship itinerary recorded by an eyewitness.

The study by Hsiao-chun Hung and colleagues links a stone inscription discovered on Serutu with the fleet’s voyage and reconstructs an arrival at Karimunjawa followed by a landing at Tuban on the north coast of Java. Its precise modern-calendar dates and island identifications depend on scholarly conversion and interpretation, and should not be presented as independently confirmed for every vessel. Tuban is a coastal landing point; Hujung Galuh and the lower-Brantas/Kali Mas waterways belong to the subsequent operational geography and cannot simply be substituted for Tuban. The commanders now had to get men, messages, and supplies from coastal positions toward Java’s interior.

It is worth asking what the archaeological evidence actually establishes. An inscription or surviving artifact at an island site can document the presence or memory of people associated with the expedition, but it cannot by itself fix the course of every vessel, the full size of the fleet, or the allegiance of each coastal community. Matching Chinese-transcribed names to islands is a separate historical inference. Where textual narratives and material traces converge, the reconstruction becomes stronger; where they diverge, the honest map retains alternative routes instead of presenting a seemingly exact line across the sea.

This geographical distinction affected operations. A fleet might possess substantial strength at anchor yet be unable to deploy that strength efficiently on inland tracks and river channels. Tidal waters, shallow crossings, provisioning points, and local guides determined which detachments could move and how quickly. The arrival of ships did not mean that the Yuan court had acquired a functioning network of inland officials. Establishing a foothold and governing the Brantas basin were different tasks, and that gap between seaborne power and political control created opportunities for Wijaya and his allies.

This created a dilemma for the commanders. They could turn back, which would have meant returning to China without achievement after a long and expensive voyage. They could press on and compel Jayakatwang's submission, treating the new ruler as heir to Java's defiance. Or they could seek a local ally who would offer submission and help them achieve some result. The Chinese record shows that they chose a combination of the second and third, and that the ally they found was Wijaya.

The role of intermediaries was critical. The Yuan force depended on guides, interpreters, and local knowledge of rivers, harbors, and political relationships, and these were supplied in part by Javanese who chose to cooperate. Wijaya's followers were among them. The expedition's reliance on such help illustrates a theme of this chapter: the Yuan army was a powerful force, but in a landscape it did not know, it depended on local actors whose interests did not coincide with its own. The Javanese were not passive in this encounter. They selected which outsiders to assist, and on what terms.

Section 49.7

49.7 — Raden Wijaya's Negotiations with the Yuan Commanders

Wijaya's approach to the Yuan commanders was shaped by an asymmetry of interests that made cooperation attractive for both sides, despite incompatible ultimate aims. The Yuan commanders wanted submission and punishment of a defiant Java. Wijaya wanted the removal of Jayakatwang, the Kediri ruler who had defeated his dynasty. The immediate enemy was the same, and each side could use the other to deal with it.

According to the Yuan shi, Wijaya, whom the Chinese text transcribes in a form such as Tuhan Pijaya, presented himself as a local leader prepared to submit to the Yuan emperor and to assist the expedition against Jayakatwang. The Javanese literary tradition tells a related story of cleverness, in which Wijaya and his advisers recognized the Yuan force as an instrument and planned to use it. Neither account records the actual conversations, and the dialogue found in later narratives should be understood as literary reconstruction. What the sources support is the outcome: a working understanding in which Wijaya offered submission and local support, and the Yuan commanders agreed to attack Kediri alongside him.

Wijaya's position was strong in some ways and fragile in others. He could offer guides, local knowledge, and followers, and he could present himself as a claimant with a stake in the outcome. But he had no army comparable to the Yuan force, and he could not have been certain that the commanders would respect his interests once Jayakatwang was gone. The cooperation was therefore a calculated risk. Wijaya was entrusting a critical stage of his recovery to an outside force whose long-term intentions were unclear, and he was gambling that he could deal with those intentions afterward.

For the Yuan commanders, the arrangement offered a way to salvage the mission. A defeat of Jayakatwang, combined with the submission of a local ruler, could be reported as a victory and as the extension of imperial authority over Java. This reasoning has a logic that the later outcome obscures. The commanders were not naive. They had a plausible path to a political result, and they pursued it. That it failed does not make it foolish, a point developed in Section 49.10. Wijaya's advisers and the local intermediaries who helped broker the arrangement acted within the same environment of uncertainty, trying to turn an unexpected situation to their advantage.

Section 49.8

49.8 — The Campaign against Kediri: The Defeat of Jayakatwang and the End of His Rule

The combined campaign against Kediri took place in the first half of 1293. The Yuan shi describes an advance by river and land in several columns, with Javanese forces under Wijaya cooperating with the Chinese commanders. The geography of the Brantas basin shaped the operation. Rivers provided routes for moving troops and supplies and also obstacles to be crossed, and the Daha region lay inland from the coast, requiring the force to move from the sea toward the interior.

Chinese records report that Jayakatwang's forces met the invaders, that battles were fought in the vicinity of Daha, and that the Kediri side was defeated. The Chinese account stresses the discipline and firepower of the Yuan troops, and the Javanese tradition, in turn, tends to emphasize Wijaya's role. Both emphasize their own side because each was written to honor it. The figures given for enemy forces and casualties in the Chinese sources are especially difficult to verify, since they come from commanders reporting to a court that evaluated them by results.

The fate of Jayakatwang is reported differently in different traditions. The Chinese account describes his capture by the Yuan forces and his later execution, and the Chinese commanders took captives as evidence of victory. Javanese traditions do not all present the sequence in the same way, and some give different accounts of how and where he died. The disagreement is not trivial. The Chinese record serves an imperial reporting purpose, while Javanese memory concerns the dynastic outcome. Where the sources conflict and no further evidence exists, a historian can say only that Jayakatwang's rule ended in 1293 and that he did not survive its aftermath.

The campaign's result was to change Wijaya's position decisively and to leave it dangerous. The rival who had defeated Singhasari was gone, and no Kediri claimant remained to challenge Wijaya at once. But a large foreign expeditionary army now occupied a position within Java, with captives, supplies, and ships, and with its own view of what the campaign had achieved. The Yuan commanders expected to convert their victory into a settlement that would bring Java into the imperial system. Wijaya had other intentions, and the next stage of the story, examined in Division III, turns on that conflict.

49.9 — Wijaya's Reversal: The Attack on the Yuan Forces in 1293

The decisive turn of 1293 was Wijaya's change from ally to enemy of the Yuan commanders. The Yuan shi describes the episode in terms that suggest a deliberate plan. After the defeat of Jayakatwang, Wijaya asked to be allowed to return to his own territory to collect tribute and a princess to be presented to the emperor, a request that fitted the pattern of submission the Yuan court expected. Permission was granted, and Wijaya was allowed to depart with a small escort. Once away, he reorganized his supporters and turned on the Yuan forces.

The Chinese account reports that Yuan officers who accompanied Wijaya's party were attacked and killed, and that Javanese forces then assaulted the main body of Yuan troops, who were dispersed and unprepared. The Javanese literary tradition tells a similar story from the opposite viewpoint, as a successful stratagem that outwitted the foreign commanders. The two accounts agree in outline. They differ in the planning, the numbers involved, and the sequence of individual encounters, and the Chinese record's figures for Yuan losses should be treated as an official account rather than an independent count. The evidence supports a deliberate political reversal, but not the details of every encounter.

The emperor’s officers had interpreted Wijaya’s submission request within their own diplomatic framework: a local ruler who supplied hostages, tribute, or a princess might be registered as a compliant subordinate. Wijaya could use the same language without accepting the consequences the foreigners intended. This difference between a formal gesture and a durable political obligation matters more than the precise words of the appeal, which are transmitted through Yuan reporting. When the escort was attacked, it revealed not simply an error of personal judgment but the difficulty of discovering what another court understood its promises to mean.

It would be a mistake to read Wijaya's action as merely personal treachery. The issue at stake was whether Java would be governed by an indigenous claimant or subordinated to a foreign imperial court. Accepting Yuan suzerainty would have made Wijaya a dependent ruler whose authority rested on an external power, with obligations of tribute and perhaps the stationing of imperial agents. Wijaya's goal was an independent kingship. The cooperation of the first months was a means to eliminate a rival, and the reversal was a means to secure the result. Whether this was planned from the start or developed as events unfolded is not recoverable from the sources, and either is consistent with the evidence.

The reversal should also be distinguished from a modern national war of independence. There was no Indonesian nation-state in the thirteenth century, and “Java” itself contained rival courts and communities whose interests differed. Wijaya sought a kingship grounded in East Javanese dynastic legitimacy; some inhabitants may have viewed Yuan intervention or Jayakatwang’s regime differently. Later stories of a united struggle can obscure those divisions. The historical achievement was the removal of a dangerous expeditionary presence at a moment of opportunity, not a predetermined emergence of a modern territorial state.

The Yuan commanders had their own difficulties. Their force was far from home, split between operations, and burdened with captives and supplies. The reversal came at a moment when they had lowered their guard, believing the campaign's goals achieved. Gao Xing is reported in the Yuan biographies to have opposed the decision that allowed Wijaya to depart, a disagreement that shaped the aftermath, as the next section explains. The episode shows the vulnerability of an expeditionary army that depended on a local ally whose interests it had misjudged.

Section 49.10

49.10 — The Yuan Withdrawal: Military Losses, Logistical Constraints, and Political Consequences

After Wijaya's attack, the Yuan commanders chose to withdraw. Several pressures combined. The force had suffered losses, and the Chinese record reports casualties in the thousands, a figure that is an official claim rather than a verified count. Its position in Java was exposed, with hostile local forces and no secure political settlement. And the maritime logistics of the expedition imposed a deadline, since the monsoon winds that carried ships between Java and China favored particular seasons, and a delayed departure risked prolonged stranding. The withdrawal came in the middle of 1293, with the army carrying captives and spoils.

The Yuan shi biographies allow a more precise comparison of the commanders’ treatment. In juan 162, Shi Bi was sentenced to seventeen strokes and confiscation of one-third of his family’s property after the costly withdrawal; that property was later returned. The separate biography of Yiheimishi in juan 131 records confiscation of one-third of his family’s property, likewise later restored, and blames the release of Wijaya. That biographical passage does not independently specify the same seventeen strokes for Yiheimishi. A later chronicle describes corporal punishment for both, but its testimony must be attributed separately. Gao Xing, who had objected to letting Wijaya leave, received favorable treatment. These differences show how the imperial bureaucracy assigned responsibility after a campaign that had won battles but failed to secure political submission.

The distinction between these biographies is methodologically important. Later writers frequently combine several court records into one neat story of defeat and punishment, yet the original entries address different individuals and select different details. An absence of a stated flogging in one biography is not proof that it never happened; equally, the punishment of one commander should not be silently copied onto another. The record demonstrates a court weighing losses, conduct, and perceived errors of command, not a single transparent account of everyone’s experience.

The chapter should not conclude from this that the Yuan military was incapable of overseas warfare. The expedition achieved a major objective, the defeat of Jayakatwang, and it did so far from home. What it failed to achieve was its intended political settlement, the submission of a stable Javanese ruler to the Yuan emperor. That failure resulted from a specific error, the trust placed in Wijaya, and from the inherent difficulty of imposing a political order on a distant society with an army that depended on local goodwill. The Yuan court's later decisions about Southeast Asian expeditions were shaped by these experiences, and Kublai Khan died in 1294, ending the era of his campaigns.

For Java, the consequences were profound. The departure of the Yuan force left the island without a foreign overlord and without Jayakatwang, and it left Wijaya as the leading claimant to power in the Brantas basin. The expedition had destroyed the Kediri regime, and Wijaya had benefited without becoming a subordinate. Yet this was an advantage whose permanence was not assured. Wijaya's new position rested on a small coalition and an uncertain territorial base, and he now had to turn military success into a functioning kingdom.

Section 49.11

49.11 — Tarik, the Clearing of the Forest, and the Establishment of the Majapahit Settlement

The new political base was created in the Tarik region of the lower Brantas basin, and the Javanese tradition tells a vivid story of its founding. According to the Pararaton and the kidung, Wijaya obtained permission to clear a stretch of forest at Tarik and, with the help of supporters including men brought from Madura, turned it into a settlement. The tradition, in this form, belongs to a widespread type of foundation narrative in which a ruler converts wilderness into a center of settled authority.

The same tradition explains the name Majapahit. In the story, workers clearing the land found a maja tree whose fruit was bitter, and the settlement took its name from it, with pahit meaning bitter. This is a foundation tradition rather than a securely documented etymology. Names of places in Java often have such explanations, and scholars treat them with caution. The tale is useful as evidence of how later Javanese remembered the founding of their capital as an act of settlement and cultivation, but it cannot be used to date or describe the earliest years.

The location had practical advantages. Tarik lay on the lower Brantas, which offered access to the coast and to river routes inland, and the surrounding plain was suitable for rice cultivation. A settlement there was farther from the old Kediri and Singhasari centers than either had been from the other, which reduced the risk of immediate challenge from those traditions. It was also positioned to draw on the labor of the lowland villages and to connect to the ports through which goods and people moved. These advantages were not determinative, but they explain why a claimant seeking a new base would choose such a site.

The name Majapahit eventually became attached to an archaeological zone around Trowulan, in the same general region. Trowulan's extensive remains of brick structures, water works, and urban features belong largely to the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, when Majapahit was a far larger and more developed state. The foundation-era settlement of 1293 should be distinguished from this later capital. The earliest Majapahit was a modest new center, and the monumental city whose traces survive was the product of generations of growth. The link between the two is continuity of place and name, not equivalence of scale.

Section 49.12

49.12 — The Establishment of Majapahit in 1293 and the Royal Consecration of Kertarajasa Jayawardhana

Wijaya's assumption of royal authority was marked by a consecration in which he took the formal name Kertarajasa Jayawardhana. The 1294 Kudadu inscription confirms that he had taken the royal title by that date, which fixes a firm lower limit on the event. The more precise date of the ceremony is reconstructed principally from later tradition, which places it in November 1293, and modern accounts often cite 10 November. This should be understood as a conventional date derived from retrospective sources, not as one established by a contemporary foundation inscription.

The royal name itself carried meaning. It is generally interpreted as combining elements suggesting prosperity and an association with the Rajasa dynasty, together with a term connected with victory and increase. The precise sense of each element is an interpretation by modern scholars, but the overall effect is clear. The name presented the new king as heir to the line of Ken Angrok and Singhasari while also proclaiming a fresh beginning. In this the king's titulature expressed the political position he had to maintain: continuity with a prestigious dynasty and the independence of a new foundation.

The coalition that stood behind Kertarajasa was different from the one that had supported Kertanagara. It included members of the old Singhasari elite who had survived, regional allies such as Wiraraja and the men from Madura, village communities that had aided his flight, and the companions who had stayed with him through the crisis. The political center was different too, located in the Majapahit settlement rather than at Tumapel or Daha. The new king therefore combined dynastic continuity, through blood and marriage, with new foundations, through a different capital and a new set of political debts.

It is important to describe what this foundation did and did not mean in 1293. It meant that a king had been consecrated and that a court existed in the Brantas basin. It did not mean that all of East Java accepted his rule, that the kingdom had stable institutions, or that its authority extended beyond a modest region. The new king's first task was to turn a successful claim into effective government, and the sources for his reign show him doing so through grants, appointments, and marriage, the subjects of Division IV.

Historical Vocabulary
Kraton, Sīma, Rakryan, and Royal Consecration

Kraton (also keraton) is a Javanese term for a royal residence and, by extension, the court and its sphere of authority. It is the later form of a concept in which the ruler's dwelling was the symbolic and practical center of the kingdom. In the foundation period it refers to the king's compound and household, not to the large walled complexes of later centuries.

Sīma was a status granted by the king to a village or estate, typically exempting it from certain taxes and obligations and sometimes redirecting its revenue to a religious foundation or a named beneficiary. It was recorded in charters and confirmed in ceremonies. The grant was a means of rewarding service and of creating durable relationships between the ruler and local communities.

Rakryan was an honorific title borne by high officials and members of the elite in Old Javanese inscriptions. It appears in combination with other terms to indicate rank and function. Its meaning varies with context, and historians distinguish the titles documented in Wijaya's reign from the more elaborate hierarchy attested in later Majapahit sources.

Royal consecration (abhiṣeka) was the ritual by which a ruler was formally installed and received a regnal name. It marked the transition from claimant to king.

Potential misunderstanding: These terms come from later or varying usage, and they should not be read as describing a fixed bureaucracy in 1293.

49.13 — Royal Marriage, Kertanagara's Daughters, and Dynastic Continuity

Marriage was one of the principal instruments by which Kertarajasa secured his legitimacy. The Sukamerta charter and later literature, including the Nagarakretagama, record that he was married to daughters of Kertanagara. They are named in the sources as Tribhuwaneswari, Narendraduhita, Prajñāpāramitā, and Gayatri, the last of whom became known as Rajapatni. The numbers and the names differ somewhat between sources, but the central fact is clear: the new king married into the household of the last Singhasari ruler.

These marriages did several kinds of political work. They connected Kertarajasa to the dead king in the most intimate way available, making him both kinsman and son-in-law and allowing him to present his rule as a continuation of the Singhasari line rather than a rupture. They gave the daughters of Kertanagara, who might otherwise have become the focus of rival claims, a place in the new court. And they created the genealogical channels through which later rulers would trace authority. In a society where descent through both parents could carry legitimacy, the children of these unions held a particularly strong position.

The royal women should not be treated as ceremonial figures. They held rank in their own right, were the subjects of dedications and commemorations, and their status shaped the politics of succession. Gayatri Rajapatni, in particular, became a figure of lasting importance. Later literature presents her as a revered senior woman of the dynasty, and her descendants included rulers of the fourteenth century. Her prominence shows that women of the royal house were central to the transmission of dynastic authority and that their influence extended beyond the lifetimes of their husbands.

The king also had other consorts. The Pararaton tells of Dara Petak, also linked in later writing with Indreswari and the Malay world, and names her as Jayanegara’s mother. That tradition needs to be read against Kertarajasa’s own charters: the 1296 Sukamerta record places Jayanegara as the son accompanying the chief queen, Tribhuwaneswari, and the 1305 Balawi evidence associates him with her as well. The inscriptions are strong testimony to his publicly asserted dynastic parentage and standing, but debates continue over how this relates to the later biological-maternity story. Jayanegara succeeded his father in 1309. The daughters of Gayatri became ancestors of the later royal line, a different succession trajectory developed in Volume IV.

From Singhasari to Majapahit

What Changed

  • The ruling coalition shifted to include Madurese allies, village communities, and surviving Singhasari kinsmen

  • The new king ruled with a smaller territorial base than Kertanagara had claimed

  • Dynastic legitimacy through descent and marriage within the Rajasa line

  • Śaiva and Buddhist patronage as a source of royal authority

Timing and who felt it: The change was abrupt in 1292–1293 and gradual afterward. Those closest to the court felt the transformation most directly, while rural communities experienced greater continuity in agricultural life, though they too faced new obligations. Why it matters: The pattern shows that Majapahit was both an heir and a new creation.

Section 49.14

49.14 — Rewarding Loyalty: The Kudadu Inscription of 1294 and the Sukamerta Charter of 1296

A newly consecrated king who owed his throne to many people needed to repay service while building a durable government. Kertarajasa’s answer included royal grants, and the Kudadu (1294) and Sukamerta (1296) charters are two especially informative surviving examples. They are not the only documentary products of his reign: records such as Adan-Adan (1301) and Balawi (1305) belong to a wider surviving inscriptional landscape. The two examples considered here illuminate how the early court publicly rewarded loyalty and formalized rights.

The Kudadu inscription, as seen in Section 49.4, records privileges granted to the village of Kudadu in recognition of its aid during Wijaya's flight. The grant converted a personal debt into an institutional arrangement. Kudadu received a status that distinguished it from ordinary villages, and that status was recorded in a document that could be shown to officials and passed to later generations. The king's gratitude was thus transformed into a legal right.

The Sukamerta charter, dated 29 October 1296, is more specific than a generic confirmation of village privilege. It concerns the restoration of Sukamerta (also transcribed Sukhamerta/Sukhāmr̥ta) to sīma status for Sang Apañji Patipati Pu Kapat, whose loyalty to Wijaya during the crisis was memorialized by the court. The DHARMA edition’s plate 5r, lines 2–5, names the beneficiary and directs that the privilege be inherited by his descendants. Its account of Pu Kapat’s conduct continues on plate 5v. This was a specified hereditary concession, not an anonymous royal favor; the charter also records the ruler’s consorts and the standing of Jayanegara.

The detail of the named beneficiary changes the question students should ask. The royal grant did not reward “the village” in the abstract; it organized rights around a particular intermediary and his descendants, with effects on residents, revenue collectors, and other claimants. A restoration also implies an earlier legal status being recalled or reasserted, rather than a privilege created from nothing. Historians must not assume that every resident received the same benefits or that the beneficiary’s interests were identical with those of those who supplied labor and produce. The inscription is particularly articulate about the court’s obligations to a loyal servant and much quieter about possible local disputes.

The institutional logic of these grants is worth spelling out. In a kingdom without a large standing bureaucracy or a developed tax-collecting apparatus, the king's ability to reward followers depended on his control of rights over land, labor, and produce. By granting sīma, he transferred a portion of those rights to those he wished to favor, converting loyalty into a stable income or status. The recipients gained security and standing. The king gained supporters with a material interest in the survival of his rule. The arrangement also bound local communities to the court, since a village's privilege depended on the king's continued recognition.

The Sukamerta document survives on eleven copper plates, and its diplomatic formula combines an Old Javanese administrative context with Sanskrit elements in the titulature and praise. The National Museum’s descriptive catalogue classifies its principal language as Old Javanese, whereas the DHARMA critical edition identifies both Old Javanese and Sanskrit linguistic material. Those descriptions need not be treated as competing translations of the actual grant. They reflect different cataloguing levels. The concrete evidence for the privilege is the edited wording concerning Sukamerta, sīma, Pu Kapat, and the succession of his descendants; broader claims about religious status belong to modern historical interpretation unless a passage explicitly supports them.

These grants were not merely administrative. They were carried out in ceremonies in which officials, local leaders, and often religious figures took part, and the privileges were confirmed through ritual and recorded in durable materials. Kenneth R. Hall's studies of Majapahit political institutions emphasize that royal authority in this period operated through networks of ritualized exchange, in which grants, feasts, and honors created and renewed relationships between the king, officials, and local communities. Power was maintained through reciprocal obligation as much as through coercion. A grant to a village created a relationship that could be called upon in future crises, as the Kudadu case itself showed.

The charters also reveal that early Majapahit's political operating system was personal. Loyalty was repaid in specific, remembered acts, and the king's circle of obligated supporters was necessarily limited by what he could grant. This helps explain both the strength and the fragility of the early court. It was strong because those it had rewarded had reason to defend it. It was fragile because those who felt inadequately rewarded, a theme in the quarrels among the founders examined in Section 49.17, had few institutional channels for their grievances.

Section 49.15

49.15 — The Early Majapahit Court: Officials, Regional Authorities, and Political Organization

The structure of government under Kertarajasa was simpler than the elaborate hierarchy that Majapahit later developed. The king stood at the center of a court composed of kinsmen, senior officials, military leaders, and religious specialists. Beneath and around the court were regional leaders and local communities with their own authorities, whose relationship to the king depended on grants, kinship, and personal loyalty.

Inscriptions of the period and of earlier East Javanese kingdoms use the title rakryan for high officials, in combinations that indicate function. Titles of senior ministers, including those later grouped in a small set of principal ministers, are attested in Javanese inscriptions across several centuries, and the early Majapahit court inherited this vocabulary from its predecessors. It would be an error, however, to attribute to Wijaya's reign the full range of offices and the elaborate ranking found in the fourteenth-century sources. The Nagarakretagama describes a mature structure under Hayam Wuruk, and historians distinguish that picture from what the contemporary charters of Kertarajasa actually support.

Tradition names some of the individuals who held positions of importance, among them companions of the flight such as Nambi, who in the later literature became a chief minister, and members of Wiraraja's family. Their appointments, according to the literary accounts, were disputed, a theme taken up in Section 49.17. The religious sphere was also represented at court. Officials responsible for supervising Śaiva and Buddhist affairs are attested in Javanese sources, and the king's relationship with religious foundations was an element of his authority, as Section 49.18 discusses.

Regional authority operated through a layered system. The king ruled directly in his own area and relied on relatives, allies, and local leaders elsewhere. Those leaders acknowledged his supremacy through attendance, tribute, and ritual participation, while retaining much autonomy. This was a political order in which authority diminished with distance and depended on renewal, a pattern consistent with the way scholars have described other Southeast Asian polities using the mandala model discussed in Chapter 1, though it should be remembered that mandala is a modern analytical term.

Inscriptions show practical relationships among the king, officials, villages, and religious establishments. A grant required the participation of officials who witnessed and confirmed it, village leaders who received it, and sometimes religious figures who blessed it. These documents reveal a society in which political authority was exercised through recurring transactions among identifiable people, rather than through anonymous administration. Hall's research is especially useful for understanding these networks, because it treats the charters as evidence of the social relationships through which the king's power actually operated.

Archaeological Study

Trowulan and the Problem of Locating the Early Capital

The Trowulan area in East Java contains an extensive scatter of brick structures, gateways, ponds and water-management features, and artifacts, and it is widely regarded as the site of the later Majapahit capital. Documentation of the site began in the early nineteenth century, and systematic archaeological work followed in the twentieth.

  • What the remains establish: the scale, density, and sophistication of the later Majapahit capital, especially in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries
  • What they do not establish: that every surviving structure dates to Kertarajasa's first years. Dating individual monuments is often difficult, and many features belong to later phases
  • The central problem: the foundation-era settlement of 1293 was small and new, and its exact location and character are hard to separate from the later city built over and around it
  • Method: historians combine excavation, survey, and textual references from inscriptions and the Nagarakretagama, treating each as partial evidence

Section 49.16

49.16 — Rice Agriculture, River Transport, Trade, and the Economic Foundations of Majapahit

A court of the kind Kertarajasa founded required resources: food and labor for the royal household, goods with which to reward officials, and support for allies and retainers. These came from the agricultural and commercial economy of the Brantas basin, and the choice of a site in that region was not accidental. The fertile volcanic plains of East Java supported intensive wet-rice cultivation, and the villages that worked them were the foundation of the kingdom's wealth.

Rice cultivation in this region depended on water management, with irrigation channels, embankments, and local arrangements for sharing water. Much of this was organized at the village level and by local leaders, and the king's role was to protect and confirm these arrangements rather than to direct them. The grants described in Section 49.14 often concerned precisely this kind of local organization, by recognizing a village's rights and obligations. Production remained locally organized, which meant that the court's income depended on negotiated relationships with village communities rather than on direct control of the fields.

The Brantas and its branches provided the transport network. Rivers carried rice and other goods downstream toward the coast, and people and commodities moved upstream. The site of the new settlement placed it close to these routes. Markets in towns and along the rivers brought together farmers, artisans, and merchants, and ports near the river mouths connected the interior to maritime trade. The presence of foreign merchants and the movement of goods across the Java Sea are well attested for later centuries, and they began long before 1293. The maritime connection was a source of both revenue and political resources, though the full treatment of Majapahit's later commercial society belongs to Chapter 51.

The economy imposed constraints as well as opportunities. A court could grow only as fast as the surplus it could draw from villages, and every grant of sīma reduced the income it could collect directly. Rulers therefore balanced the need to reward supporters against the need to preserve revenue. The early kingdom's small scale reflects this reality. It had enough resources to support a court and a modest circle of followers, and not enough to dominate distant regions. The later expansion of Majapahit's reach required a long process of economic and political growth that cannot be assumed for 1293.

Everyday Life and Social History

Villagers, Agriculture, Merchants, and Transport

The founding of Majapahit is usually told as the story of princes, envoys, and commanders. It also depended on people who left no individual names in the record. Farmers prepared and irrigated rice fields and gave a share of the harvest to local leaders and to the court. Villagers, like those of Kudadu, decided in moments of danger whether to help a fugitive, and their decisions had lasting consequences. Laborers cleared land and built the settlement, according to the foundation tradition. Boatmen and carriers moved goods along the rivers, and traders linked villages, towns, and ports.

Armies in motion, as in 1293, were also an experience of ordinary communities. They required food, transport, and labor, and their passage could bring hardship. The surviving sources, written by or for rulers, mention these people mainly when their actions mattered to the court. Historians reconstruct their lives indirectly, from the grants that recognized their privileges, from the structure of village obligations, and from archaeology. The charters' attention to village status is itself evidence that villages were corporate actors with interests that a king had to respect.

49.17 — Rivalries among the Founders: Ranggalawe, Sora, Nambi, and Competing Political Traditions

A kingdom founded by a coalition inherits the coalition's quarrels. Javanese literary tradition preserves memories of tension among Wijaya's supporters, centered on a few names: Ranggalawe, associated with Wiraraja's family; Sora, a military companion; and Nambi, who in the tradition became a chief minister. The recurring theme is dissatisfaction over appointments, rewards, and regional authority, as men who had risked everything for Wijaya judged the new king's distribution of favors.

In the literary accounts, Ranggalawe resented the elevation of another man to a senior post and rebelled, and he was defeated. Sora, who is said to have been involved in the suppression of Ranggalawe, later came into conflict with the court himself, and Nambi, whose position was prominent, eventually met opposition and destruction. Behind these personal stories stood structural problems. The early court had limited means of satisfying all those who expected rewards, and regional lords such as those of Madura had their own interests and autonomy. Wiraraja's regional position, in particular, gave his family a base independent of the king's favor.

The available sources do not, however, offer an administrative ledger naming each disappointed supporter and the benefit denied to him. The later tales organize rivalry through memorable confrontations, speeches, and accusations, forms that suit political storytelling. An event can express a plausible institutional problem even when the speech assigned to its protagonist cannot be recovered. A careful reconstruction therefore separates the existence of factions and rival offices from the precise motivations and dialogue reported by a text composed later. That distinction is especially important where a dramatic narrative appears to supply more certainty than a dated charter does.

The chronology of these episodes is contested. The Pararaton and the kidung do not supply a uniformly reliable sequence, and different reconstructions place some of the rebellions within Wijaya's reign and others near or after his death. Some of the best-known episodes, notably Nambi's fall, are placed by many accounts in the reign of Jayanegara. The responsible approach is not to assume that every famous early rebellion occurred before 1309, and not to compress events from a later reign into the founder's to create a more dramatic narrative of a troubled beginning.

Evidence from the founding reign indicates that senior appointments and royal favor were matters of public record, but it does not verify the whole literary rebellion sequence. The Sukamerta charter names officeholders at the court and at Daha, allowing a snapshot of the allocation of posts in 1296. Such a snapshot can test whether a named person held a title at that moment; it cannot by itself prove the date of a quarrel told by a later kidung. The reward system made offices worth contesting, yet the particular circumstances of any revolt require their own evidence. Nambi’s later downfall, often placed under Jayanegara, is best treated as part of the continuity of this problem rather than pressed into a 1293–1309 narrative.

What can be said with confidence is limited but meaningful. The early court included men with strong claims on the king and regional power bases of their own, and the literary tradition consistently remembered the first generation of Majapahit as marked by factional conflict. That memory, even where its details are uncertain, is consistent with the structural fragility of a kingdom built on personal loyalty and limited resources. The disputes also show how thinly the sources document the early years, since the same events can be placed in different decades.

Historical Debate

Dating the Early Rebellions

The question: Which of the revolts associated with Ranggalawe, Sora, and Nambi occurred during Kertarajasa's reign (1293–1309), and which belong to the reign of his successor?

Reconstruction A — an early cluster: Some readings follow the literary chronology, placing the revolts of Ranggalawe and Sora in the first years of the new kingdom and presenting early Majapahit as a period of continuous internal struggle under the founder.
Reconstruction B — a more spread and later pattern: Others argue that the literary sources compress or rearrange events, that some episodes, especially Nambi's, belong to Jayanegara's reign, and that the founder's reign may have been less turbulent than the tradition suggests.

Evidence: The question rests mainly on the Pararaton and kidung, with some support from the Nagarakretagama and limited help from inscriptions. All are retrospective.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns chronology and the reliability of literary sources, rather than whether the individuals existed or the tensions were real.

Why it matters: The answer changes the picture of Kertarajasa's consolidation, either as a struggle against early revolt or as a reign of relative stability that gave way to crisis under his son.

Student task: Identify what kinds of new evidence, such as dated inscriptions, would help resolve the chronology, and explain why the literary sequence cannot settle it alone.

Section 49.18

49.18 — Śaiva-Buddhist Religion, Royal Legitimacy, and the Commemoration of the Rajasa Dynasty

Religion was integral to royal legitimacy in Java, and the new dynasty inherited the religious world of Singhasari, in which Śaiva and Buddhist traditions coexisted and interacted. Kertanagara had been associated with forms of worship that blended the two, and the Majapahit court continued to patronize both. Royal officials with responsibility for Śaiva and Buddhist affairs, religious foundations that received grants, and sacred sites tied to the dynasty all formed part of the machinery of authority. The relationship was reciprocal: the king supported religious institutions, and those institutions legitimated the king.

Legitimacy also depended on royal ancestors. The Deśawarṇana, completed in 1365, identifies two commemorative settings for Kertarajasa after his death in 1309: a Śaiva association at Simping (Sumberjati, near Blitar), where the ruler was remembered in the Harihara tradition, and a Buddhist/Jina association at Antahpura, within the royal precinct. These are later descriptions of posthumous ritual representation. They illuminate how succeeding generations linked the founder to divine and dynastic authority, but they do not prove that both finished commemorative establishments existed throughout his reign.

The dates of physical work sharpen this distinction. Hayam Wuruk is associated in fourteenth-century testimony with the restoration of Simping and renewed dedication of the founder’s image during the 1360s, decades after Kertarajasa’s death. The image and its architectural setting therefore speak at least partly for the ideals and resources of the later Majapahit court. A historian cannot take a monument known from the 1360s and assign its complete form to the 1290s simply because its commemorated subject was the earlier ruler. Political memory has a history of its own: successors had reasons to present their dynasty’s founder in forms suited to their particular ritual and political setting.

This distinction matters. The elaborate commemorative and ceremonial system described for Hayam Wuruk's reign was the product of decades of development, and it should not be read back into the earliest period without evidence. What contemporary sources support for Kertarajasa's reign is more modest: religious endowments through grants, royal support for religious foundations, and an evident concern with dynastic continuity. The charters' references to religious institutions and the king's family show that these concerns were present from the beginning, but the full ritual architecture came later.

The religious dimension also shaped relationships beyond the court. Grants to religious foundations created links between the king and communities of religious specialists, who often held land, maintained local shrines, and served as educators and record-keepers. Villages with sīma status that supported such foundations became part of a religious as well as a political network. The new dynasty's adaptation of Singhasari's traditions thus served practical ends, binding communities to the court through shared ritual and obligation, and giving the founder, whose own claim had been unconventional, a religious framework of continuity.

Section 49.19

49.19 — Kertarajasa's Death in 1309 and the Succession of Jayanegara

Kertarajasa Jayawardhana died in 1309, after a reign conventionally dated 1293–1309. The date comes from the Javanese literary tradition, and individual inscriptions and retrospective works must still be used carefully in reconstructing the events surrounding it. By any account, the reign had lasted about sixteen years, a long period for a founder in a turbulent environment, and it had established a court that survived the transition to a second ruler.

His successor was Jayanegara, recognized as Kertarajasa’s son. The Pararaton, a substantially later work, names Dara Petak as his mother. By contrast, the Sukamerta charter of 1296 presents him in close association with chief queen Tribhuwaneswari, and the Balawi evidence of 1305 similarly associates his lineage with her; the 1296 text places the young prince in the royal context of Daha. These sources do not simply tell the same genealogical story. The inscriptional identification is direct evidence for his officially presented status and maternal affiliation; the literary attribution may preserve another memory, but establishing biological maternity from these divergent presentations remains a separate problem. The chapter should not choose one without acknowledging the other.

Dynastic parentage was politically consequential. Association with Tribhuwaneswari, Kertanagara’s eldest daughter and Kertarajasa’s chief queen, linked Jayanegara publicly to the preceding royal house through both sides of the ruling family. An assertion in an official charter could thereby help make succession intelligible to officials and communities asked to recognize a prince in Daha. It would be unsafe, however, to reduce royal marriage to reproductive biology. Titles, precedence, adoption or acknowledgment, household rank, and courtly representation could shape how the line was presented. Our surviving evidence allows a firmer account of the prince’s publicly acknowledged position than of every private circumstance of his birth.

The political relationships left unresolved by the founder also remained. The regional lords, the families of the early companions, and the tensions over rewards that Section 49.17 described did not disappear at the king's death. The new ruler inherited a kingdom that had survived its first generation but had not resolved the problem of how to hold together a coalition of ambitious men. The later reign of Jayanegara, which lasted until 1328 and ended with his assassination, was troubled by revolts and by court intrigue. It lies outside the scope of this chapter, and Chapter 50 introduces the rise of Gajah Mada in that setting.

This documentary comparison also reveals the limits of retrospective narratives. The Pararaton tells a compelling family story, but the charter was produced while the king and his young successor were alive, at a time when officials had an interest in fixing legitimate status. Both can be partisan; they are not equally close to the events. The historian’s task is to specify which kind of question each is able to answer. The claim “Jayanegara succeeded his father in 1309” is much better supported than a claim that one narrative settles every aspect of his maternity. The distinction becomes important when the later politics of Gayatri’s descendants are discussed in Volume IV.

Kertarajasa's death thus marks the end of the foundation period. The state he left was not yet the powerful regional kingdom of later literature. It was a small, recently consolidated court with a functioning system of grants, a network of allies, and a dynastic connection to the older rulers of East Java. Whether it would last depended on whether his successors could do what he had done, turning loyalty and ritual into stable government.

Section 49.20

49.20 — The Rise of Majapahit: From Political Crisis to the Foundations of a Regional Power

The history of Majapahit's foundation weaves together five developments. The collapse of Singhasari in 1292 destroyed the East Javanese political order and left its elite divided. The survival of a dynastic claimant, Narāryya Saṅgrāmawijaya, depended on villagers, a Madurese patron, and loyal followers. The Yuan intervention of 1293 removed Jayakatwang and then failed when Wijaya turned against the invaders. The formation of a new political center in the Brantas basin gave the claimant a base. And the creation of a functioning royal government, through marriage, grants, officials, and ritual, turned a successful claim into a kingdom.

Majapahit inherited a great deal from earlier East Javanese states. It inherited a vocabulary of royal and official titles, the practice of granting sīma, a tradition of Śaiva and Buddhist patronage, the agricultural wealth of the Brantas basin, and a dynastic ideology centered on the Rajasa line. At the same time, it created new forms of authority through Wijaya's particular coalition, which included regional allies and village communities that had not been central to Singhasari. The kingdom was neither a simple continuation nor a clean break.

Students should understand that Majapahit became a powerful regional kingdom through a historical process and not immediately upon its foundation in 1293. The court of Kertarajasa was small, its authority limited, and its survival uncertain. The expansive network associated with Gajah Mada and Hayam Wuruk was a later achievement, built over decades by rulers, ministers, and military leaders who worked within the institutions the founder had established. To confuse the two is to turn the founding into a prophecy.

Historical Synthesis Study

Dynastic Continuity versus Political Innovation

Continuity: descent from the Rajasa line through Narasinghamurti; marriage to Kertanagara's daughters; the grant of sīma; Śaiva and Buddhist patronage; reliance on Brantas rice agriculture and river transport; personal loyalty as the basis of political bonds.

Innovation: a new capital in the lower Brantas basin; a coalition drawing on Madura and village communities; a foundation achieved by exploiting and then expelling a foreign army; a royal name proclaiming both heritage and renewal; a reign that managed, with difficulty, to survive its founder.

Synthesis: The evidence supports neither a story of pure continuity nor one of pure novelty. Wijaya succeeded because he could present himself as the legitimate heir of the old dynasty while building new relationships suited to a changed world.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how Majapahit began: as a small court founded in 1293 by a defeated dynastic claimant in the aftermath of Singhasari's destruction. Its central argument is that the foundation of a new ruling center in 1293 and the later construction of a wide regional network under Gajah Mada and Hayam Wuruk are separate developments, and that the first must be understood on its own terms.

The evidence for the foundation is of three kinds that disagree in instructive ways. Contemporary charters, notably the Kudadu inscription of 1294 and the Sukamerta charter of 1296, give narrow but anchored evidence. The Yuan shi gives an external, dated account of the military campaign. Later Javanese literature, including the Pararaton, the Nagarakretagama, and the kidung, supplies narrative and memory, shaped by the purposes of later courts. No single source suffices, and historians reconstruct the founding by testing them against one another.

Singhasari's collapse in 1292 came from Jayakatwang's revolt, and Jayakatwang had substantial claims rooted in Kediri's political traditions. He should not be treated simply as an illegitimate rebel. His brief regime left the survivors of Singhasari to decide whether to submit, flee, or resist. Among them was a kinsman of the dynasty, identified in the inscriptions as Narāryya Saṅgrāmawijaya and in later literature as Raden Wijaya (and identified with Harṣawijaya in some literary traditions, a disputed equivalence), whose claim rested on descent and on marriage to Kertanagara's daughters. The evidence does not show that he had been formally designated heir.

Wijaya's survival depended on others. Villagers at Kudadu sheltered him, a fact recorded in a charter that also served to justify his reward, and tradition adds that he found refuge and counsel in Madura through Wiraraja. These episodes show a recovery built on relationships rather than on individual cleverness alone.

The Yuan expedition, launched on outdated information to punish Kertanagara, arrived to find a changed order. Wijaya and the commanders each had reasons to cooperate against Jayakatwang, and together they defeated him. Wijaya then obtained permission to return to his own territory and turned against the Yuan forces, who withdrew. The Yuan court punished some commanders and treated Gao Xing differently. The episode reveals the limits of expeditionary power when it depends on local allies, without showing that Yuan forces were incapable of overseas warfare.

The new settlement at Tarik in the Brantas basin, with its foundation tradition of forest clearing and the bitter maja fruit, became the center of the kingdom. Wijaya was consecrated as Kertarajasa Jayawardhana, conventionally in November 1293, with the kingship confirmed by 1294. The settlement should be distinguished from the later, much larger capital whose remains lie around Trowulan.

Consolidation depended on marriage, grants, and a simple court. Marriages to Kertanagara's daughters connected the king to the old dynasty and produced the lines of later rulers. Royal grants of sīma, documented in the Kudadu and Sukamerta charters, converted loyalty into durable relationships, in what Kenneth R. Hall has described as networks of ritualized exchange. The court's officials, regional lords, and religious institutions operated within this personal system, and its economy rested on locally organized rice agriculture, river transport, and coastal connections.

The founders quarrelled over rewards, and Javanese tradition remembers rebellions by Ranggalawe, Sora, and Nambi. Their chronology is contested, and historians should not assume that all occurred before 1309. Kertarajasa died in 1309 and was succeeded by his son Jayanegara, leaving unresolved problems of succession and factional rivalry. Majapahit's rise was a historical process: the founding created a kingdom that inherited institutions and traditions from earlier East Javanese states while adding new forms of authority, and the powerful regional state of Chapter 50 was built on those foundations over the following decades.

An honorific title for high officials and elite persons in Old Javanese inscriptions.

Hall's term for the relationships of grant, feast, and honor through which royal power operated in Majapahit Java.

The error of explaining an earlier event as if it were aimed at a later outcome, such as treating 1293 as already containing Majapahit's later reach.

The method of testing sources of different kinds against one another to reconstruct events none can establish alone.

Chapter Reflection

The rise of Majapahit shows how a kingdom can emerge from catastrophe through contingency, relationships, and improvisation rather than inevitability. A destroyed dynasty, an imperial fleet launched on stale information, a village that sheltered a fugitive, and a regional lord who offered refuge all contributed to an outcome none of them could have foreseen. The chapter's lesson is that origins are shaped by many hands, and that later literature, however eloquent, simplifies the process.

The episode also illustrates the agency of Southeast Asian actors in encounters with outside powers. The Yuan expedition was a formidable undertaking, yet its commanders depended on local intermediaries and were outmaneuvered by an ally they had misjudged. Wijaya neither submitted to the empire nor ignored it. He used it, and then ended its role. Such selective engagement runs through Southeast Asian history and echoes the argument of Chapter 1 that the region's societies were never merely passive recipients of outside influence.

The foundation period also shaped what followed. The practices of grant, ritual exchange, and personal loyalty, the dynastic marriages, and the tensions among supporters and between royal lines formed the framework in which later rulers operated. The questions of succession and factional rivalry left unresolved in 1309 would reappear under Jayanegara and his successors, and they help explain why Majapahit's rise was neither smooth nor automatic.

With this chapter, Volume III ends. The classical kingdoms it surveyed, from Angkor to Pagan, from Đại Việt to Srivijaya, belonged to a world of sacred kingship, agrarian and maritime exchange, and shifting spheres of authority. Volume IV turns to the religious, commercial, and political transformations of the period from about 1200 to 1500, beginning with the kingdom whose foundation this chapter has examined, and with the question of how a small court in the Brantas basin became one of the most influential powers in maritime Southeast Asia.
