Chapter Opening

The Spanish conquest of the Philippines was neither a single military event nor the straightforward replacement of indigenous societies by a European state. It was a sequence of landings, negotiations, battles, alliances, hunger, and administrative improvisation, carried out between 1565 and the early 1580s, in which Spanish claims of sovereignty repeatedly outran the territory and the people the colonizers actually controlled.

On 27 April 1565, a small Spanish fleet anchored off Cebu in the central Visayas. The arrival fell on the anniversary of Ferdinand Magellan's death at nearby Mactan in 1521; there is no secure evidence that the expedition deliberately timed its landfall to that date. It was Magellan's encounter, not a settled colony, that had given the archipelago a place in Spanish imperial memory. The commander of the fleet, Miguel López de Legazpi, had left the Pacific coast of New Spain (Mexico) the previous November under royal orders. He was accompanied by soldiers, sailors, officials, and Augustinian friars, among them the navigator Andrés de Urdaneta. What Legazpi's expedition set out to do was not merely to visit the islands but to remain in them, and to find a way home across the Pacific that would make remaining possible.

The people who watched the fleet arrive were not strangers to long-distance contact. Cebu had been a trading port, and its leaders remembered the earlier foreign visit. Across the archipelago, chiefs known by titles such as datu, rajah, and lakan led communities organized through kinship, obligation, raiding, and exchange, a world reconstructed in Chapter 72. Their responses to the newcomers varied from calculated hospitality to flight to armed attack. That variety is the first of this chapter's central themes: the Spanish did not enter a political vacuum, and the colonial order that emerged was shaped by choices made on both sides of the encounter.

The second theme is the gap between claim and control. Spanish officials performed ceremonies of possession, named towns, and wrote to the crown as if islands and peoples had been brought under royal authority. Yet in the first decade of settlement the Spaniards struggled to feed their own garrison, were challenged by a Portuguese fleet, shifted their principal base from Cebu to Panay while retaining a foothold on Cebu, and depended heavily on indigenous provisioning, labor, ships, guides, and fighters. When they established themselves at Manila in 1571, they did so after a first attack on the settlement in 1570 and after fighting a battle whose details and participants remain debated. Even then, as the Limahong attack of 1574 showed, the new colony was vulnerable.

The third theme is that conquest quickly became institutional. Once Spanish authority was established at Cebu and Manila, officials began assigning tribute-collecting rights called encomiendas, organizing a municipal council, working through local chiefs, and sponsoring missionary activity. These arrangements transformed obligations and relationships within indigenous communities, often through coercion. Scholars have emphasized both the role of local elites as intermediaries and the destructive demographic and economic effects of conquest, and this chapter holds the two findings together rather than choosing between them.

The chapter draws on an unusually rich but heavily mediated body of evidence. Legazpi's letters, formal acts of possession, expedition narratives, royal orders, missionary correspondence, and later histories survive in Spanish archives and in the translated collection of Emma Helen Blair and James Alexander Robertson. Almost all of this material was written by Spaniards, for Spanish audiences, with purposes of reporting, justifying, requesting, or criticizing. Indigenous voices appear in it filtered through interpreters, officials, and friars. Part of the work of this chapter is to teach students how to read such sources critically.

Chapter 73 follows Chapter 72, which examined the indigenous political, commercial, and social worlds of the archipelago before 1565, and it precedes Chapter 74 on the Manila galleon trade, Chapter 75 on Christianity and colonial society, and Chapter 76 on the Moro-Spanish frontiers. It therefore concentrates on 1564 to 1582: the establishment of the first enduring Spanish centers, the military and diplomatic process through which they were secured, and the early institutions that began to convert conquest into government. It touches the later galleon economy, parish system, and southern wars only as consequences.

The chapter's governing distinction is among three things that are easily confused: conquest as an event, colonial domination as an evolving relationship, and effective administration as a geographically uneven process. Spain achieved the first at particular places and moments, began the second at Cebu and Manila, and had barely begun the third across most of the archipelago by 1582. Keeping those three apart is the key to understanding the Spanish conquest of the Philippines without either exaggerating or dismissing it.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the strategic, political, and logistical reasons for the Legazpi expedition and the importance of Andrés de Urdaneta's return voyage of 1565.
  2. Trace the sequence of Spanish settlement at Cebu, Panay, and Manila between 1565 and 1571.
  3. Analyze the roles of Rajah Tupas, Lakandula, Rajah Matanda, Rajah Sulayman, and other indigenous leaders as political actors with their own interests.
  4. Distinguish Goiti's 1570 attack and act of possession at Manila from Legazpi's 1571 establishment of Spanish Manila.
  5. Assess the participation of Visayan, Pampango, and other indigenous allies in Spanish campaigns without assuming they accepted permanent Spanish sovereignty.
  6. Describe how tribute, encomienda, municipal institutions, and early missions began to reorganize political and social relationships.
  7. Evaluate three primary sources, Legazpi's 1565 correspondence, the 1570 act of possession, and Salazar's 1582 letter, for perspective, purpose, and limitation.
  8. Interpret debates over Bangkusay, over the demographic effects of conquest, and over the role of indigenous intermediaries.
  9. Distinguish formal claims of sovereignty from effective Spanish administration across Luzon, the Visayas, Mindanao, Sulu, and interior regions.
  10. Contextualize the Limahong attack of 1574 within regional maritime and Chinese commercial networks.
  11. Connect the conquest to the later galleon economy, colonial Christianity, and the Moro-Spanish frontiers examined in Chapters 74 to 76.

Historical Context

The Philippine archipelago in the mid-sixteenth century consisted of hundreds of communities, mostly Austronesian-speaking, organized in what Spanish writers called barangay: kin-based settlements led by chiefs whose power rested on followers, ships, trade, raiding, and the ability to host feasts and honor obligations. Larger ports such as Cebu, Tondo, Maynila, and Butuan, and Muslim-influenced polities in Sulu, Mindanao, and Brunei's orbit, were integrated into commercial networks reaching China, mainland Southeast Asia, Borneo, and the Moluccas. These societies, examined in Chapter 72, possessed ranked social orders, forms of dependent labor, agricultural and maritime economies, and their own rivalries. They were politically fragmented, but fragmentation is not the same as disorder or weakness.

Spain's earlier contact with the archipelago was brief. Magellan's expedition reached the Visayas in 1521, and Magellan died at Mactan fighting forces loyal to a local chief, Lapulapu, after allying himself with the ruler of Cebu. Later Spanish expeditions from New Spain, most notably that of Ruy López de Villalobos in 1542 to 1543, named islands after the Spanish prince Philip but failed to establish a durable presence. Spain and Portugal had divided their spheres of claim in the Treaty of Tordesillas of 1494 and negotiated the Moluccan question in the Treaty of Zaragoza of 1529. How those agreements applied to the Philippines was uncertain, and Portuguese authorities in the Indian Ocean and the Moluccas regarded Spanish activity in the region with suspicion.

By the 1560s Philip II, who had been king since 1556, wanted a permanent Spanish foothold in Asian waters. Spanish interest combined claims to the Moluccan spice trade, hopes of reaching Chinese commerce, missionary ambitions, and the desire to justify earlier investments. The practical obstacle was navigational. Ships had repeatedly crossed the Pacific westward from New Spain, but no expedition had reliably returned eastward. Without a return route the colony could not report, reinforce, or exchange goods with its American base.

Two cautions govern the chapter. First, it should not retroactively treat the Spanish colony as a foregone conclusion: the survival of the settlement at Cebu was in doubt, and many outcomes were possible. Second, it should not use the modern Philippines or modern provincial boundaries as the framework for the 1560s and 1570s, when neither a Filipino national identity nor an archipelago-wide political structure existed. Spanish officials used the word "Philippines" for an area they claimed but did not know, and the local communities they met identified themselves through locality, kinship, language, and allegiance to a chief.

Historical Vocabulary
Encomienda

Definition. An encomienda was a royal grant that assigned to a Spanish colonist, the encomendero, the right to collect tribute from a specified indigenous community. In theory the grant came with obligations: the encomendero was to protect the people, support their instruction in Christianity, and defend the territory.

Historical context. The institution developed in the Caribbean and the Americas during the early sixteenth century and was introduced in the Philippines soon after the establishment of Spanish settlements. It rewarded conquistadors and soldiers with income rather than with money from the royal treasury.

Why it matters. Encomiendas gave individual Spaniards direct financial claims on indigenous households, and they defined much of the early relationship between colonizers and communities. Abuse was widespread enough that missionary and episcopal critics, including Bishop Salazar, protested it.

Potential misunderstanding. An encomienda was not ownership of land or of people. It was a right to collect tribute, subject to royal regulation. In practice, however, encomenderos often exceeded the legal limits.

Historical Vocabulary
Cabildo

Definition. A cabildo (also ayuntamiento) was a municipal council in Spanish law, composed of officials such as alcaldes (magistrates) and regidores (councilors), responsible for local government, justice, and supply.

Historical context. Spanish conquerors routinely founded cabildos in new towns because the corporate city gave their settlement legal status and a framework for dividing land and authority among settlers. At Manila the first municipal officers were appointed in June 1571.

Why it matters. The cabildo made Manila a recognized Spanish city and gave settlers a body through which to negotiate with the governor and the crown.

Potential misunderstanding. A city council did not govern the entire surrounding population. Indigenous communities near Manila continued to have their own leaders, and the cabildo's jurisdiction was initially small.

Division I — The Legazpi Expedition and the First Spanish Settlement Section 73.1

Reconstructing the Spanish Conquest of the Philippines

The documentary base for the conquest is richer than for almost any earlier episode in Philippine history, and also more one-sided. Legazpi wrote letters to Philip II and to the viceroy of New Spain; expedition members produced narratives and relations; notaries recorded formal acts; the crown issued instructions and replies; missionaries wrote reports and later chronicles; and a retrospective history by Antonio de Morga, published in Mexico in 1609, drew on these and on his own experience of the colony. Many of these texts were translated and published in the early volumes of Blair and Robertson's The Philippine Islands, 1493–1898 (1903 and later). Their translations are indispensable to English-language readers, but, like any edition, they reflect editorial choices, and students should recognize that they are translations of Spanish originals.

Each type of source answers a different question. Letters and relations to the crown report events as the writer wished the king to understand them, often stressing the writer's own services and requests for reinforcements. Legal acts, such as the act of possession discussed in Section 73.7, are written in a stylized language designed to give claims legal force. Missionary writings document encounters between friars and communities but also advance arguments about how colonization should proceed. Later histories such as Morga's are valuable for perspective and for details not preserved elsewhere, but they are retrospective and should not displace contemporary records for reconstructing events in 1565 to 1572.

A recurring problem is how conquest narratives characterize indigenous action. Spanish writers regularly described indigenous attacks as treachery or rebellion, and Spanish force as response or punishment. These terms presume that Spanish sovereignty already existed, which was the very claim at issue. A leader who refused tribute, abandoned a town, or sent ships against a landing party may have been acting as an independent ruler defending his people; Spanish documents often cannot tell us, because they were not written to record that point of view.

Indigenous testimony does exist, but it is mediated. Spanish officials interviewed chiefs, friars recorded complaints, and interpreters stood between speakers and writers. Chinese records, Portuguese reports, and archaeological evidence from settlements and trade ceramics provide independent angles, although for the 1565 to 1582 narrative they are sparse. Historians therefore reconstruct indigenous actions by reading Spanish sources against their grain, asking what a chief's behavior implies about his objectives, and comparing accounts for consistency.

Historical Source and Method Study

Reading a Conquest Narrative

When reading any Spanish report of the conquest, students should ask five questions of the passage.

  1. Who wrote it, for whom, and what did the writer want from the reader?
  2. Does the text report what the writer saw, what he was told, or what he later concluded?
  3. What words imply that Spanish authority already existed (for example "rebel," "pacify," or "treachery")?
  4. Which indigenous actors are named, which are anonymous, and who is absent?
  5. What independent evidence, if any, corroborates or contradicts the account?

Applying these questions prevents two opposite errors: accepting the Spanish account as neutral description, and dismissing it as worthless. It is neither.

Section 73.2

Philip II, New Spain, and the Expedition of 1564–1565

Philip II's instructions of the late 1550s ordered the viceroy of New Spain to prepare an expedition to the western Pacific. The king's stated aims included finding a route back across the Pacific, ascertaining what could be reached in the islands, and promoting Christianity. The viceroy chosen to organize the effort, and then the commander, Miguel López de Legazpi, a Basque official who had served in Mexico City, were selected partly for administrative reliability. Urdaneta, a veteran of the earlier Loaísa expedition, had retired as an Augustinian friar; his presence was understood to matter because earlier expeditions had failed to find the return passage.

The fleet assembled on the Pacific coast of New Spain in 1564. It consisted of several ships carrying soldiers, sailors, officials, supplies, and a group of Augustinian friars. Contemporary and later sources give differing figures for the numbers aboard, generally in the low hundreds to somewhat over four hundred, and students should treat any single figure with caution. The fleet departed in November 1564, crossed the Pacific, and reached the Marianas and then the central Philippines in early 1565. It was a project of the viceroyalty of New Spain, and New Spain, not Spain, supplied the men, ships, and money.

Legazpi spent the first weeks of 1565 moving among the central islands. Spanish sources describe encounters with local communities on Samar, Leyte, and surrounding islands, some friendly, some hostile, some involving hunger and attacks. At Bohol in March the Spaniards made an agreement with local leaders, including Datu Sikatuna, sealed in a ritual of friendship involving shared blood and drink. Later popular memory has turned this event into a famous image; the contemporary documents, however, are brief, and the meaning of the ritual for each party can be reconstructed only partially. Legazpi's subsequent decision to settle at Cebu was based partly on the information provided by local leaders about its size and trade.

Urdaneta's role was to navigate the return voyage. In June 1565 a ship commanded by Legazpi's grandson Felipe de Salcedo sailed from Cebu, with Urdaneta advising, and used a northward route to find the westerly winds of higher latitudes and the Pacific currents, reaching the coast of New Spain in early October 1565 after a long and difficult passage. The successful eastward voyages, and especially the documented route later associated with Urdaneta, helped make the colony viable. It did not yet create the Manila galleon trade, which developed from the 1570s as commercial interest grew and is the subject of Chapter 74. The 1565 return voyages made reinforcement and communication possible; commerce was a later development.

The expedition's financial and political organization also shaped its prospects. The viceroy and royal officers had to mobilize shipwrights, provisions, navigators, soldiers, and commercial credit on a coast distant from Mexico City. The voyage was thus part of the institutions of New Spain from the beginning; it was not a self-contained Spanish squadron arriving from Europe. A detached vessel, the San Lucas, commanded by Alonso de Arellano and piloted by Lope Martín, returned to Puerto de la Navidad on 9 August 1565, before the San Pedro reached Acapulco under Felipe de Salcedo with Urdaneta's navigational guidance on 8 October. The two voyages must be distinguished: chronological priority in reaching New Spain belongs to the San Lucas, while Urdaneta's carefully documented northern route became central to subsequent regular crossings. The circumstances of the San Lucas's separation from the fleet were contested.

Section 73.3

Cebu in 1565: Negotiation, Resistance, and Spanish Occupation

When Legazpi's fleet reached Cebu on 27 April 1565, the town was a commercial center under the leadership of Rajah Tupas, whom some sources describe as a relative of Rajah Humabon, the ruler who had received Magellan. Cebu's leaders knew what a European fleet could do and had probably heard of the violence that followed Magellan's visit, including his death at Mactan. Spanish documents report that the people of Cebu were alarmed, that some leaders abandoned the town, and that Tupas was reluctant to meet the Spaniards. Such reports show that Cebuano leaders had choices and weighed them.

Negotiation and fighting alternated in the following days. Spanish accounts describe exchanges of messages, an attempt by Legazpi to persuade Tupas and other chiefs to accept Spanish friendship and the king's overlordship, and an armed confrontation in which Spanish firearms and ship artillery were used against the town. The Spaniards occupied and burned part of the settlement, took possession of the site, and began to build a fortified position. Whether the Cebuanos' resistance was coordinated, how many communities participated, and what their goals were cannot be established from the Spanish record, which was written by the victors. The reasonable inference is that leaders balanced the risks of confrontation against the benefits of an alliance with a force that possessed ships and weapons.

In the weeks after the initial fighting, Tupas and other leaders reached an accommodation with Legazpi, which the Spaniards described as submission and a pact of friendship. For the Spaniards, these agreements established Spanish sovereignty; for Cebuano leaders, they may have represented a more limited arrangement that secured their communities and their positions in return for supplies and alliance. The distinction matters because the two sides may have understood the agreement differently. Over time, Spanish officials expected tribute and service, and local leaders encountered those expectations as demands that went beyond what they thought they had accepted.

Spanish settlement at Cebu was organized as a town and fort. Spanish writers also reported the discovery of an image of the Child Jesus, the Santo Niño, in Cebu, which later became central to local Catholic devotion; the accounts of that discovery are mediated by later tradition and by missionary interest, and historians approach the details cautiously. What is more certain is that the Spanish presence reshaped the town: the settlement attracted soldiers and officials, drew heavily on local food and labor, and reoriented some of Cebu's trade. A Spanish town on the site of an indigenous port was a combination of occupation and interaction, not a clean replacement.

The first weeks at Cebu also exposed the distance between the legal vocabulary of possession and the means of government. Legazpi could proclaim royal authority and place troops in a settlement, but could not command food deliveries from every neighboring community. The need to negotiate repeatedly with leaders who had survived the initial attack reveals the limits of the garrison's victory. In the early documents, a profession of friendship may be followed by warnings about shortages or hostility; that sequence is not inconsistent. It shows that the terms of association remained unsettled and that neither armed occupation nor ceremonies could substitute for sustained local relationships.

Students should consult the published edition for the full text and the editors' notes. Consult the published English edition.

Context: Legazpi wrote a month after reaching Cebu, while his settlement was precarious, supplies were short, and the ship that would carry news to New Spain had not yet sailed or arrived. He wrote to a king thousands of miles away who could not verify his claims.

Editorial paraphrase of this letter, not of its accompanying voyage relation (not a verbatim excerpt):

Legazpi reports that he crossed the Pacific, visited islands, and remained with few people at his settlement on Cebu. He says a vessel is being dispatched to New Spain to discover the return route. He sends a separate relation of the voyage, information on changes in indigenous relations, statements of possession, and pilot routes as accompanying materials; those details are not narrated in this short letter. He requests people, ammunition, and other supplies from New Spain and asks Philip II to examine the enclosures and support the enterprise.

Analysis: The letter explicitly documents how Legazpi presented a precarious settlement as a major imperial opportunity and sought additional resources. Its reference to separate enclosures is an invitation to compare documentary genres rather than assume that every event associated with the expedition appears in the letter itself.

Limitations: This short letter does not provide a detailed scene-by-scene account of negotiations with Tupas, nor a full description of local rulers or resources. For those subjects students must examine the separate relation and other documents, each with its own interests and silences.

Perspective: The perspective is that of a Spanish commander seeking royal support. The viewpoints of indigenous leaders, ordinary soldiers, and the Portuguese are absent.

Questions for students:

  1. Which events does this short letter actually report, and which does it refer to accompanying records?
  2. Who wrote it, and under what circumstances?
  3. What purposes might have shaped Legazpi's presentation of events and of indigenous rulers?
  4. What can historians infer about the settlement's condition from Legazpi's requests for people, ammunition, and supplies?
  5. What can historians not establish from this letter alone, and what other sources would help?
Section 73.4

Hunger, Portuguese Pressure, and the Move to Panay, 1565–1569

The first years of the Cebu settlement were marked by shortage. The expedition had arrived with limited provisions, and the Spaniards could not grow or buy enough food locally without the cooperation of the population. Cebuano communities had their own subsistence needs and, in some cases, had fled or been displaced by the fighting. Spanish reports describe hunger among the garrison, expeditions to nearby islands to buy or seize food, sickness, and desertion. The record shows that the survival of the colony depended on indigenous production and on the willingness of local leaders to supply it, a dependence that could be bought through gifts and alliances or taken by force.

The expedition's logistical weakness also reflected distance. The 1565 return voyage had opened a route to New Spain, but reinforcements and supplies took time to organize and to cross the Pacific, and the viceroyalty had many competing demands. Spanish officials at Cebu made decisions on local authority, sending out small parties and relying on local pilots and interpreters. Urdaneta himself did not return to the Philippines; he died in Mexico in 1568.

A different kind of pressure came from the Portuguese. Portuguese authorities in the Indian Ocean and the Moluccas considered the Spanish presence a violation of their claims, and in 1568 a Portuguese fleet appeared off Cebu. Documentary reports describe a confrontation in which the Portuguese demanded that the Spaniards leave and the Spaniards refused, citing royal orders. The two parties did not fight a sustained naval battle, but the Portuguese blockade and the threat of conflict increased the Spanish settlement's difficulties. This episode must be distinguished from indigenous resistance: it was an Iberian dispute about competing imperial claims, not a united regional response to Spanish colonization, and the Portuguese had no interest in defending local rulers.

In 1569 Legazpi moved the principal Spanish base from Cebu to the island of Panay, where food was more plentiful and the garrison could be fed. The move reflected hunger, the Portuguese threat, and the desire to be closer to sources of supply. It also began to shift Spanish attention northward. From Panay the Spaniards could reach Mindoro and Luzon, about which they were receiving reports of larger populations, more abundant food, and active trade. Cebu continued to have a Spanish presence, but it was no longer the colony's center. The retreat from the first settlement shows that the founding of a Spanish town was not the same as the establishment of an enduring colony.

Division II — Indigenous Alliances and the Spanish Advance toward Luzon Section 73.5

Indigenous Allies, Rivalries, and the Politics of Cooperation

Spanish campaigns in the Philippines relied heavily on indigenous participation. From Bohol and Cebu to Panay and then Luzon, Visayan leaders supplied ships, rowers, fighters, guides, interpreters, and food. In the Manila campaigns of 1570 and 1571, Spanish forces included several hundred Visayan auxiliaries, though the numbers reported in different sources vary. Without them, the small Spanish contingents could not have crossed the archipelago, fought on land, or fed themselves.

Why did leaders cooperate? The evidence suggests several reasons, which varied by leader and circumstance. Some chiefs saw the Spaniards as a means of defeating rivals; the archipelago was full of rivalries, and an ally with firearms and ships could tip a local balance. Some wished to obtain trade goods, gifts, or recognition. Some were coerced, or feared the consequences of refusal. Some cooperation was temporary accommodation, a calculation that it was better to supply the newcomers than to fight them. It is a mistake to read all of it as voluntary support for permanent Spanish rule, and equally a mistake to read it as simple collaboration against the interests of their own people. Chiefs were responsible for their followers' survival and welfare, and cooperation with a stronger force could be a form of protection.

Other leaders opposed the Spaniards or negotiated cautiously. Some communities fled inland or to other islands, a common form of resistance when direct confrontation seemed hopeless. Others refused to supply provisions or attacked small parties. Spanish documents tend to record the leaders who cooperated, because they were useful, and the ones who resisted, because they required a response. The leaders who simply avoided the Spaniards and left no trace in the record are the hardest to see.

The result was a patchwork of relations rather than a single story. Alliance with the Spaniards did not mean permanent subordination, and leaders who accepted Spanish friendship in one year could resist tribute demands in another. Spanish officials regularly misread alliance as submission. This gap between Spanish expectations and indigenous understandings was a source of repeated conflict throughout the 1570s.

The local boats described in expedition reports were themselves strategic resources. They carried messages between islands, moved provisions to anchored ships, and brought fighters into shallow waters where deep-draft European vessels were less useful. Guides supplied knowledge of reefs, currents, landing places, and the political affiliations of settlements. This assistance could not simply be manufactured by a newly arrived expedition. Indigenous maritime expertise made the Spanish force larger in practice than its European head count suggests, while continuing to be supplied by communities with their own interests. A report that lists hundreds of allied rowers is evidence of mobilization, not proof of their unanimous approval of the governor's political aims.

Section 73.6

Mindoro, Luzon, and the Search for a New Political Center

By 1569 and 1570, Spanish officials had good reasons to look north. The Visayas, and especially Cebu and Panay, had limited surplus food and a modest commerce. Luzon, by contrast, was reported to have large populations, extensive rice lands, and ports connected to Chinese merchants, who brought silk, porcelain, and other goods and left with gold, wax, and other local products. A Spanish base in Manila Bay promised access to that trade, to food, and to a strategic location on the South China Sea. Spanish interest in Chinese commerce was a strong motive; so too was the continuing need to feed the garrison.

The northward move began with reconnaissance. In 1570 a Spanish force under Martín de Goiti, the colony's maestre de campo, sailed from Panay with Juan de Salcedo and a contingent of Visayan auxiliaries. They passed Mindoro, where Spanish reports record encounters with local communities and with Muslim traders from the north, reflecting connections between Luzon and Borneo that Spanish officials noted with concern. They then proceeded to Manila Bay. The route took the expedition along the coast of Mindoro and through the waters south of Luzon, a journey that depended on local pilots and favorable winds.

Spanish officials were not simply discovering an unknown place. Manila Bay had a long-established commercial and political life: communities at the mouth of the Pasig River controlled access to the bay and the rivers leading inland, and their leaders had commercial relations with Chinese, Bornean, and other traders. The Spaniards learned about these arrangements from local informants and from their own observations. The decision to go to Manila was based on this knowledge, and the success or failure of the Spanish enterprise there would depend on how well the Spaniards could insert themselves into an existing system.

The choice of Manila illustrates a strategic trade-off. A river-mouth harbor gave access to food-growing districts and multiple inland waterways, yet a garrison placed there would enter a field of existing commercial claims. Chinese visiting ships, merchants linked to Brunei, and traders moving between the Pasig and Laguna de Bay had established relationships with local leaders. Spanish control of the anchorage therefore promised revenue but risked alienating the people whose exchanges made the anchorage valuable. The coast itself was not empty space that could be occupied without affecting existing rights to stores, wharves, houses, or landing places.

Mindoro was an important intermediary stage rather than a blank point on the route. Spanish expeditions encountered coastal communities engaged in regional exchange and learned that political and religious ties extended across the waters toward Manila and Borneo. Reports describing Muslim traders or leaders must be read as observations of particular networks, not as evidence that every settlement shared one religion or recognized one ruler. In this sense northward reconnaissance was also intelligence gathering: pilots and interpreters conveyed information about possible allies, centers of opposition, commodity flows, and channels through which the Spaniards might seek agreement.

Section 73.7

Martín de Goiti at Manila, 1570: Negotiation and Armed Confrontation

Goiti's expedition reached Manila Bay in May 1570. The Spanish sources agree that the Spaniards were first received with exchanges of messages and gifts, that local leaders, including the ruler of Maynila, discussed friendship, and that the Spaniards asked for peace, trade, and recognition of the Spanish king. The documents also record tension. The Spaniards were wary of the leaders' military strength, and the local rulers, who had known Muslim and Chinese traders but few Europeans, were aware of the force that had arrived with Goiti. The negotiation, which lasted several days, was conducted through interpreters.

Fighting then broke out. The Spanish version, discussed below, holds that the local leaders acted treacherously after agreeing to peace. Whatever the cause, the Spaniards attacked, aided by the Visayan auxiliaries, and the settlement of Maynila was burned. The Spaniards recorded that they found cannon and other weapons in the town, indicating that Manila's rulers were not unarmed. After the fighting, Goiti drew up a formal act of taking possession and returned to Panay, since he had too few men to hold the site.

It is important to separate this episode from the one that followed. The May 1570 destruction of Maynila was a raid with legal consequences, not the foundation of Spanish Manila. The Spaniards did not stay, and the local leaders remained in the area. When Legazpi returned in 1571, he found a recovering community and had to negotiate again, as Section 73.9 explains. Conflating the two events would suggest that Spanish control began in 1570, when in fact it began a year later and was far from complete.

The detailed expedition relation complicates the simplified story of peaceful negotiation abruptly broken by indigenous treachery. It describes offers of friendship, exchanges of gifts, soldiers demonstrating their weapons, anxiety over tribute, and reports transmitted by intermediaries about possible attack. Its author presents the Spaniards as restrained and local leaders as unreliable, but the same narrative reveals that the newcomers had armed men close to the settlement and sought recognition of a foreign monarch. From the position of a Manila ruler, that presence could threaten political independence even before any formal assessment of tribute. The documents allow historians to reconstruct a tense negotiation; they do not establish the private intentions of every participant.

The notarial act of 6 June made a different kind of assertion. It converted the outcome of an expedition into the documentary language of possession, witnesses, and royal title. It had value in the administrative world of Spanish imperial law, particularly when officials later reported achievements to the crown. But the absence of a resident Spanish government after Goiti returned to Panay demonstrates the limits of the ceremony. It is a particularly clear example of a written claim to dominion outrunning the actual means of enforcing it.

Section 73.8

Tondo, Maynila, and the Indigenous Political World of Manila Bay

The Spanish documents name several leaders at Manila Bay. Lakandula (often rendered Lakan Dula) is associated with Tondo, on the north bank of the Pasig River, and Rajah Matanda and Rajah Sulayman with Maynila, on the south bank. The Spanish sources sometimes describe Matanda as the older leader and Sulayman as a younger relative, and their titles reflect the Malay and Muslim influence that had reached coastal communities through trade with Brunei and other ports. Their exact relationships, the degree of their authority, and the extent to which Tondo and Maynila acted together remain open to interpretation. Students should avoid assuming that these settlements formed a single state.

These leaders did not respond uniformly to the Spanish arrival. Spanish accounts suggest that some were willing to treat with the newcomers, while others were more suspicious. Their interests differed: a ruler who hoped to expand his trade, one who feared a Spanish takeover of the river mouth, and one who was uncertain of his neighbors' loyalties might all behave differently. Spanish writers sometimes ascribed changes of attitude to the character of individual leaders, but the more likely explanation lies in shifting circumstances, including the threat of attack, the possibility of alliance, and relationships with communities up the Pasig and around the bay.

Manila Bay's rulers were connected to wider commercial and religious networks. Contact with Muslim traders and the presence of Islamic titles and practices suggest that some of the elite had at least partial exposure to Islam, a subject examined in Chapter 66. Students should not exaggerate this: the evidence is limited, and Islamic influence in Manila Bay was not comparable to its role in Sulu or Brunei. The communities also traded with Chinese merchants and with communities of the Pampanga delta, the Laguna de Bay region, and beyond. Spanish officials would have to decide how to deal with these networks when they settled at Manila.

Later narratives, including nationalist histories and popular tradition, have attached many political meanings to these figures. Lakandula is often presented as a patriot, Sulayman as a heroic resister, and Matanda as a collaborator. Such readings, though understandable, impose later categories on sixteenth-century politics and often outrun the evidence. The Spanish record does not support a clear division of the rulers into resisters and collaborators, and this chapter treats their choices as the decisions of leaders navigating a dangerous and uncertain situation.

The geography of the Pasig estuary helps explain why politics were plural. Tondo, Maynila, the settlements bordering the bay, and communities around Laguna de Bay could participate in overlapping trade without answering to a single chief. Boats moved between river channels, the lake, and coastal markets, giving several leaders opportunities to collect services, broker exchange, and cultivate outside partners. The Spaniards often looked for one ruler with whom to conclude an agreement, but their own accounts acknowledged that a leader who promised peace could not necessarily bind every village. This mismatch between negotiated personal authority and imagined territorial sovereignty became consequential as the colony expanded.

Division III — The Conquest of Manila and the Expansion of Spanish Claims Section 73.9

Legazpi's Return to Manila and the Campaign of 1571

Goiti's report of Manila's advantages persuaded Legazpi to act. In 1571 he assembled a larger force at Panay, including Spaniards and Visayan auxiliaries and a fleet of ships and local vessels, and sailed north, reinforced en route by units from the Visayas. Sources give differing numbers for the force, and students should treat any figure as an estimate. The expedition stopped at Mindoro and then proceeded to Manila Bay in May 1571. Legazpi came as governor and captain general, with royal authority to establish a settlement, and he intended to remain.

This was a different expedition from Goiti's raid. Legazpi arrived with the equipment and personnel for settlement, and his instructions were to found a Spanish town. He renewed negotiations with the rulers of Tondo and Maynila, who now had to decide whether to accept Spanish authority, resist, or find a compromise. The Spanish sources report that Matanda and Lakandula, among others, made agreements of friendship with Legazpi, accepted the king's overlordship in the form the Spaniards described, and exchanged gifts and pledges. Sulayman's response was more guarded. The agreements were likely to have meant different things to each party: Spanish officials regarded them as submission, while the rulers probably regarded them as arrangements that protected their communities and leadership.

The negotiations did not eliminate conflict. Elements among the region's inhabitants resisted, and Spanish forces fought engagements around the bay, as Section 73.10 describes. Legazpi's task was to combine diplomacy, military pressure, and ceremonial display in a manner that gave him a defensible position without alienating too many local leaders. He succeeded, in part because he had superior firepower and a large indigenous contingent, and in part because several local leaders concluded that cooperation was preferable to continued war.

The expedition's progress in 1571 was slowed by material conditions as well as political uncertainty. A near-contemporary Spanish account describes shortages on Panay, including damage from locusts, and says the governor took a combined force of Spaniards and indigenous boats toward Mindoro before entering Manila Bay. That account also reports that residents burned or abandoned parts of Maynila in anticipation of the expedition, making the renewed occupation inseparable from displacement. The narrator's remarks about the city's Indigenous leaders reveal the prejudices of a conqueror: their divided authority is treated as political inferiority even while the same account acknowledges that agreements with those leaders were necessary.

Section 73.10

Bangkusay, Indigenous Resistance, and the Battle for Manila Bay

In early June 1571, Spanish forces fought an engagement at Bangkusay, near Tondo, against a fleet of indigenous war vessels. The Spanish accounts describe an attack by forces coming from communities north of the bay, associated in the sources with the Pampanga delta region, against the Spanish position. The contemporary Spanish narrative places the expedition under Martín de Goiti, the maestre de campo, with Spanish troops and Visayan allies; it does not establish Juan de Salcedo as the engagement's commander. The Spanish narrator describes the indigenous boats as armed with small cannon and arrows, while emphasizing the effects of Spanish arquebuses and the pursuit by allied Visayans. After the battle, the defeated forces withdrew, and the Spaniards were able to consolidate their position in the bay.

The battle was significant, but the evidence is thin. We have a small number of Spanish accounts, written by participants or drawn from their reports, that disagree on details such as the numbers engaged and the movements of the fleets. The sources do not allow us to say with confidence who led the resistance or what the leaders intended. Later tradition identifies a leader called Tarik Sulayman, but the relevant contemporary Spanish narratives do not securely establish that name, and students should not identify him with Rajah Sulayman of Maynila. Equally, the evidence does not show that every community around Manila Bay acted together. The Spanish accounts depict rival choices: some local leaders and communities fought the Spaniards, others sided with them, and others stood aside.

The battle's consequences were considerable. The defeat weakened the most direct challenge to the Spanish presence and encouraged leaders who had been hesitating to come to terms. It also demonstrated that the Spaniards, with their allies, could defeat a substantial indigenous naval force in the bay. But it did not end resistance in Luzon, and it did not eliminate the autonomy of the leaders who accommodated Legazpi. The campaign was a step in the establishment of Spanish power, not its completion.

A near-contemporary Spanish relation describes the battle on 3 June 1571 as an expedition launched by the maestre de campo, Martín de Goiti, with Spanish soldiers and indigenous allies, against vessels associated with a settlement called Butas and nearby communities. According to the author, the opposing fleet used small cannon and arrows, and the Spaniards advanced with arquebusiers. The account attributes the pursuit of retreating fighters partly to the Pintados, or Visayan, auxiliaries. These details demonstrate the substantial indigenous contribution to the Spanish victory, while descriptions of enemy losses and cowardice remain claims by a victorious narrator rather than independently verified statistics.

The same source records the taking of prisoners and their distribution among Spanish soldiers after the fight. That reported consequence should not be omitted when the battle is used as a story of military consolidation. It shows that conquest incorporated seizure of people and distribution of rewards, not merely defense of a new city. The fight was also geographically limited: a flotilla's defeat did not establish uniform control of the river systems, lowland communities, and upland routes feeding Manila Bay. Later heroic names attached to the resistance must be evaluated against the narrower documentary record.

Section 73.11

The Establishment of Spanish Manila in 1571

After the fighting and negotiations of May and June 1571, Legazpi proceeded to found a Spanish city. The site was on the south bank of the Pasig River at the place of the burned Maynila settlement, which offered a defensible position at the river mouth, access to the bay, and proximity to the populations of Tondo and the surrounding area. The choice reflected both strategic logic and the pre-existing importance of the location: Manila was a commercial center before the Spaniards arrived, and the new city would take advantage of that role.

On 24 June 1571 Legazpi organized a municipal government, appointing magistrates and councilors to form a cabildo. This date is conventionally commemorated as the founding of Spanish Manila. The act gave the settlement a legal identity as a Spanish city, with officials responsible for local order, justice, and supply, and it provided a framework in which settlers could be assigned plots and obligations. Legazpi remained governor and captain general, with ultimate authority; the cabildo was a subordinate body. Fortifications were begun, and the settlers set about building houses and defenses.

A city's legal foundation is not the same as the construction of colonial institutions. In 1571 the Spanish population was small, perhaps a few hundred, and the city was a cluster of rough buildings. It coexisted with indigenous communities that retained their leaders and many of their practices. The relationship between the Spanish city and its neighbors had to be worked out over years: Tondo remained a large indigenous settlement, and Spanish officials depended on local leaders for labor, food, and information. The cabildo had jurisdiction over the Spanish settlement, not over the entire region.

Manila's later importance as the colonial capital and the terminus of the galleon trade was not a foregone conclusion in 1571. Officials considered other locations, and the colony's long-term viability remained uncertain. The settlement's advantages, its harbor, its river, its food supply, and its access to Chinese trade, became increasingly clear in the following years, as Chapter 74 will show.

The establishment of a cabildo was also a decision about whose political institutions would be recognized. Spanish settlers acquired magistrates and councilors operating within a legal framework they already knew, while neighboring indigenous residents retained chiefs and customs that colonial officers would gradually seek to regulate. At first the two systems were linked by interpreters, requisitions, personal agreements, and occasionally threats. The city needed a stable marketplace and food supply more urgently than it needed an abstract claim to all surrounding lands. The legal corporation could appoint officers, but its resolutions had to be implemented through relationships beyond the Spanish precinct.

Nor should the designation of Manila as a capital obscure the material fragility of its early buildings. The settlement was exposed to fire, coastal assault, and interruptions in rice delivery, and its defenses were improved over time rather than completed in 1571. The river mouth gave ships access but also gave potential attackers access to the same waters. The geographic advantages that attracted the governor therefore created obligations to defend, provision, and negotiate in a port that had already mattered to Asian merchants before the Spaniards arrived.

Section 73.12

The Expansion into Southern, Central, and Northern Luzon

With Manila established, Spanish commanders sent expeditions to extend their reach. Juan de Salcedo, a young and energetic officer, led several, and Goiti and others undertook others. In 1571 and the following years Spanish forces visited Cainta and the Laguna de Bay region, the Pampanga plain, and the coasts of southern Luzon, and in 1572 and later Salcedo's expeditions reached Pangasinan, Ilocos, and the Bicol region. The campaigns combined diplomacy, display of force, and fighting, and they were conducted with large contingents of indigenous auxiliaries.

Responses varied. In some places local leaders accepted Spanish demands for friendship and tribute after negotiations; in others they fought, and the Spaniards burned settlements or took prisoners; in still others the communities withdrew or avoided contact. Cainta, near Manila, is an example of a community where armed conflict occurred before submission. In the northern coastal areas, the Ilocos region, Spanish officials established a presence through alliances and the assignment of encomiendas, and a town was later founded at Vigan. In the Pampanga region, many communities became important allies of the Spaniards, supplying troops for later campaigns. Others in the mountains and interior remained outside Spanish control.

These expeditions rarely produced permanent administration. A column would arrive, receive or compel submission, assign an encomienda or record a promise of tribute, and move on. Without garrisons, missionaries, or officials in residence, the submission was often nominal. Local leaders could ignore Spanish demands until the next expedition, and the Spaniards lacked the manpower to enforce them. The expansion produced a map of claims and relationships that was much more extensive than the area under real Spanish control.

The result was an uneven pattern. Around Manila and in some coastal and lowland areas of central Luzon, Spanish authority became increasingly real. In the north and in the interior, it remained intermittent, dependent on local alliance. In the Visayas the pattern was similar. The conquest of Luzon, in short, was a process of negotiation, force, and gradual accommodation that extended over decades, not a rapid military occupation.

Reports of the Luzon campaigns tend to compress very different encounters into the same formula: a column approaches, chiefs are summoned, a demand is made, and the place is described as pacified. Yet a settlement agreeing to peace while armed soldiers were present might later dispute tribute; another might remain unreachable once the expedition's boats returned to Manila. The documentary vocabulary of pacification should be analyzed as a claim made by the conquering administration rather than as an objective measurement of consent. It also concealed costs imposed on those whom the reports classified as defeated or newly subject.

The spread of Spanish influence along the coast did not mean the interior was unconnected to politics. River routes linked lowland farming, fishing, trading communities, and upland producers of forest goods or metals. A Spanish agreement with a coastal chief might improve access to a river mouth but leave the upper valley outside fiscal reach. Differences among Cainta, Pampanga, Ilocos, Pangasinan, and Bicol are therefore analytically important. Each had its own combination of local leaders, economic relationships, military resources, and reasons to accommodate or resist the newcomers.

Division IV — Conquest Becomes Colonial Government: Alliances, Tribute, Religion, and Labor Section 73.13

Indigenous Chiefs, Intermediaries, and the Reorganization of Authority

Spanish officials could not govern the archipelago directly. They lacked the numbers, the languages, and the local knowledge. Instead, they worked through existing leaders, whom they called by Spanish terms or by the local titles of datu, lakan, and rajah, to collect tribute, assemble laborers, settle disputes, and communicate with communities. This reliance on intermediaries was a central feature of early colonial rule. John Leddy Phelan, in his study of the early Spanish colonial period, emphasized the part played by local chiefs in making the system work and in shaping its development.

For indigenous elite families, cooperation brought benefits and constraints. Leaders who worked with the Spaniards could retain status, receive recognition of their rank, and sometimes secure exemptions or privileges. Their role as collectors of tribute and organizers of labor gave them an official function that complemented their traditional authority. At the same time, they were responsible for delivering what the Spaniards demanded, which put them in a difficult position between colonial officials and their own followers. A chief who was seen to demand too much risked losing support; one who delivered too little risked Spanish punishment.

The arrangement also began to change the character of chiefly authority. Traditionally, a leader's power depended on his ability to attract and hold followers through generosity, success in raiding and trade, and kinship. Under Spanish rule, power increasingly derived also from a leader's relationship with colonial officials. Over time this strengthened some leaders and weakened others, and it contributed to the emergence of a colonial-era local elite. These shifts took decades to unfold, and in the 1570s the process was just beginning.

The intermediary system can be understood through the changing function of tribute. A datu's obligations to followers had involved reciprocal expectations of protection, prestige, feasting, assistance, and access to goods. When colonial officials demanded a specified assessment, they encouraged chiefs to turn those flexible relationships into a more regular collection procedure. Those who gained recognition might increase their local standing, but had to answer for arrears or failures that they could not always control. Households unable to pay were not necessarily rejecting a chief's authority; they might be responding to poor harvests, loss of labor, or simultaneous obligations to several claimants.

This process also affected the historical record. Spanish archives preserve communications with recognized chiefs and officeholders more readily than the experiences of fishing crews, women managing household stores, or people who fled. The apparent prominence of local elites in the documents reflects both their real influence and the colonial need for intermediaries. Students should therefore distinguish the existence of negotiation among elites from the consent of whole communities. In later decades the officeholding families known as the principalía would become important, but that later configuration should not be read back as an already completed institution in 1571.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Chiefly Authority, c. 1565–1582

What changed? Chiefs acquired official duties as tribute collectors and labor organizers, and their standing came increasingly to depend on relations with Spanish officials.

What remained continuous? Chiefs continued to lead kin-based communities, to mediate disputes, and to depend on followers' loyalty.

When did change occur? Gradually and unevenly, beginning with the first alliances in the 1560s and accelerating as encomiendas and tribute systems spread in the 1570s.

Who experienced change most directly? Leaders in areas near Manila and Cebu, where Spanish presence was strongest.

Who experienced greater continuity? Communities in the interior and in areas not yet reached by Spanish officials.

Why does the pattern matter? It shows that colonial rule worked through, and changed, existing structures rather than simply replacing them.

Section 73.14

Tribute, Encomienda, and the Political Economy of Conquest

Tribute was the basis of the early colonial economy. Spanish officials assessed households or communities for payments in kind or in money, and they assigned the right to collect these payments to individual Spaniards through encomiendas, as the vocabulary box above explains. Legazpi began distributing encomiendas in the early 1570s, rewarding soldiers and officers who had taken part in the conquest, and the crown authorized the practice. The system provided an inexpensive way to compensate conquerors and to support a garrison, since the encomendero's income came from the indigenous population rather than the royal treasury.

An encomienda gave its holder the right to collect tribute, not ownership of land or of the people. In law, the encomendero owed the crown military service and owed the community protection and support for religious instruction. In practice, the distinction was often ignored. Encomenderos demanded more than the assessed amounts, used forced labor, and seized goods, and the presence of Spaniards in many communities was itself a burden. Bishop Salazar and other critics documented such abuses, as Section 73.18 explains. Officials in Manila and in Spain attempted to regulate the system but had limited ability to enforce their rules.

Tribute transformed economic relationships. Households that had previously contributed to their chiefs through customary obligations now owed payments to Spaniards as well, often at a time when conquest and warfare had already disrupted production. Spanish demands for rice, cloth, gold, wax, and other goods diverted resources from local use and from established trade. Some communities responded by increasing production or shifting to goods that could be sold, others by resisting or moving away. The effects varied by region and by the kind of goods demanded.

The political economy of conquest was also shaped by the relationship between the crown and the conquerors. The crown wanted revenue and orderly government; the encomenderos wanted income; and the indigenous population wanted to protect its own livelihood. These interests conflicted, and the resulting tensions shaped the early colonial period.

The mechanics of collection mattered. Assessments had to be translated into quantities of rice, cloth, wax, gold, or other goods that differed in availability among regions. An official's demand could require a household to obtain an item through exchange rather than simply deliver something it regularly produced. Transporting collections to an administrative center consumed labor and boats; an encomendero or his agents could profit by manipulating measures, prices, or the timing of payment. Even a formally moderate tribute could become severe when it coincided with hunger, war, or a farmer's need to tend fields.

The distinction between royal and private encomiendas was also important. In theory the crown's revenues and those assigned to individual grantees were governed by rules and inspections; in practice, communities experienced the concrete demands of collectors and local magistrates. Later documented petitions show grievances against royal alcaldes as well as private beneficiaries. Consequently the system should not be reduced to the personal misconduct of a few greedy conquistadors. It joined conquest finance, delegated authority, coercive labor demands, and the limited capacity of metropolitan institutions to monitor a distant colony.

Section 73.15

Augustinian Missionaries and the First Religious Institutions

Missionary activity was part of the expedition from the start. Five Augustinian friars accompanied Legazpi, including Urdaneta and Martín de Rada, and the crown's instructions emphasized the conversion of the population. At Cebu and later at other sites, the friars baptized individuals, built chapels, and began to learn local languages. Their early work was limited by their small numbers and by their dependence on interpreters, and the baptisms they recorded were scattered and uneven.

The relationship between the friars and the military settlement was close and sometimes tense. The friars relied on the garrison for protection and on the encomienda system for support, yet some became critics of Spanish abuses. Others were closely tied to the military leadership. The missionary effort was also intertwined with politics: a leader who accepted baptism could be seen as accepting Spanish friendship, and Spanish officials often treated baptism as evidence of submission.

Indigenous reception varied. Some leaders and communities welcomed the friars, perhaps seeing advantages in association with the newcomers, or perhaps finding Christian ideas and rituals compatible with their own. Others ignored or rejected missionary efforts. Baptism cannot be interpreted automatically as a lasting change of belief. It could reflect political accommodation, curiosity, coercion, or genuine religious commitment, and many converts continued to practice indigenous rituals alongside Christian ones. Scholars such as Vicente Rafael have examined how translation shaped the process of conversion in Tagalog society, showing that concepts did not pass unchanged between languages.

This section introduces only the earliest phase of the mission. The development of parishes, the arrival of other religious orders, the policy of resettlement into larger towns, and the long-term character of colonial Christianity are the subjects of Chapter 75.

The mission operated through translation as much as through ceremony. Friars needed interpreters to explain doctrine, understand disputes, and identify the kin groups whose leaders could organize gatherings. Religious instruction could change the standing of a local mediator who became indispensable to the mission, while resistance to a church building or baptism might reflect political caution as well as disagreement over belief. The earliest documentary claims of mass conversions also require attention to the difference between a counted baptism and an enduring parish community. The latter demanded resident clergy, repeated teaching, material support, and the accommodation or suppression of existing rites.

Tensions between some friars and secular officers followed from the contradictions of conquest. Missionaries needed protection and transport provided by the same government whose tribute and labor practices could make Christian instruction unpopular. Some denounced abuses in order to defend prospective converts and the church's authority, but that advocacy did not make them neutral observers of indigenous religion. A careful account of early Catholic institutions must recognize both their contribution to colonial governance and their ability, at times, to criticize its methods.

Section 73.16

Warfare, Displacement, Extraction, and Everyday Consequences

For ordinary people the conquest meant disruption. In Cebu and around Manila Bay, fighting destroyed houses and crops, and the burning of settlements forced families to flee. Farming households faced demands for rice and labor at planting and harvest time. Fishing and coastal communities were called on to supply boats and crews. Dependent laborers and enslaved people, categories of social dependence documented in earlier chapters, could be seized, sold, or transferred as a consequence of warfare. Women and children were particularly vulnerable to capture and to the disruption of households, and Spanish records occasionally note their seizure as part of campaigns.

Provisioning demands were a constant burden. A garrison of a few hundred Spaniards, together with allied auxiliaries, consumed large quantities of rice and other food, and they often obtained it by requisition. Tribute added further demands. Merchants, who had been part of the commercial life of Cebu and Manila, found their trade affected by Spanish regulation and by the arrival of a new, armed commercial competitor. Some people left coastal areas for the interior, others moved to different islands, and some drifted into Spanish settlements for work or protection.

The demographic consequences are uncertain. Estimates of the archipelago's population in the 1560s vary widely, from roughly half a million in some older scholarship to over a million in more recent reconstructions, reflecting the absence of reliable counts. Linda Newson has argued that conquest, forced labor, warfare, and disease contributed to substantial population decline in the early colonial period, challenging older assumptions that the demographic effects were modest. Her work also shows regional variation, with the effects in the Visayas differing from those in Luzon. Students should avoid attributing population decline to war alone, since epidemic disease, hunger, and flight also played roles, and the sources are limited.

These consequences were not experienced uniformly. Communities near Spanish settlements bore the heaviest burdens, while those in remote areas often escaped direct contact for decades. Within communities, chiefs and their families were generally better able to protect themselves than commoners and dependents.

The rhythm of ordinary work makes those impositions tangible. A family called upon to supply an oarsman lost labor needed to fish, plant, repair a house, or maintain irrigation; an absence that lasted weeks could disrupt several households, not merely the man sent aboard. When requisitioned rice was taken below a fair price or without timely compensation, a village had to decide which reserves to consume, exchange, or conceal. Warfare had already disturbed the movement of food between settlements, so demands by soldiers and administrators could produce further scarcity even where crops had not failed everywhere.

Conquest also rearranged mobility. Families might enter new settlements to trade or seek protection, while others abandoned exposed coasts or fled tribute districts. Such movement complicates later population statistics: a fall in the recorded taxpayers of one village could reflect mortality, migration, concealment, altered enumeration, or combinations of these causes. The evidence does not permit a precise local demographic curve for each year of the conquest. The surviving complaints nevertheless reveal specific injuries, including the burden of paying assessments for people who had died or moved away. These experiences belonged to an evolving system, not solely to the days of open battle.

Division V — Resistance, Colonial Limits, and the Uneven Establishment of Spanish Authority Section 73.17

Limahong's Attack on Manila, 1574, and the Fragility of Spanish Rule

In late November 1574, a fleet commanded by the Chinese maritime leader Limahong (the Spanish rendering of a name known in Chinese sources by other forms) attacked Manila. Limahong led an armed force operating outside the control of the Ming state, and Spanish accounts report that it numbered in the thousands, though such figures are uncertain. His fleet had been pursued by Ming authorities and had sailed to the Philippines in search of a base. The attack caught the Spanish settlement by surprise. Martín de Goiti, the veteran commander of the first Manila expedition, was killed, and part of the town was burned. The Spaniards and their allies, including Lakandula's followers and other indigenous forces, rallied and repelled the assault, and Limahong withdrew.

The attack must be understood in its regional context. Limahong's force was a maritime raiding and trading enterprise of a kind common in the South China Sea in the sixteenth century, when the Ming ban on private maritime trade had encouraged both smuggling and armed groups. His fleet should not be confused with the Chinese merchants who had traded in the archipelago for centuries and whose commerce was becoming essential to Manila. The Spaniards, after the attack, treated Chinese traders with suspicion for a time but depended on them. The distinction between an armed expedition and a commercial community is central to understanding the episode.

After the assault, Limahong sailed north to Pangasinan, where he established a fortified base at the mouth of a river and attempted to found a settlement. The Spaniards organized an expedition under Juan de Salcedo to dislodge him. The force included Spanish soldiers and a large number of indigenous allies from Pampanga and elsewhere, and it was supported by the arrival of a Ming naval officer who sought to capture Limahong. After a siege, Limahong escaped by sea in 1575, and his later fate is uncertain. The episode demonstrated that Spanish forces could mobilize substantial indigenous support, and it increased Spain's contacts with the Ming court.

The consequences of the attack were considerable. It showed that Manila was vulnerable, prompting the improvement of defenses. It underscored the colony's dependence on indigenous allies, whose participation was essential to its defense. And it contributed to the development of diplomatic and commercial contacts with China, themes examined in Chapter 74.

The campaign in Pangasinan brings additional actors into focus. J. Travis Shutz's study compares Spanish and Ming Chinese sources and shows that the effort against Limahong involved a temporary convergence of Spanish, Ming, and Luzonese interests. Cooperation did not mean that these parties shared a permanent understanding of jurisdiction or trade. Ming agents sought a wanted maritime leader, the Spaniards sought security and diplomatic recognition, and Luzonese forces responded within their own regional circumstances. The episode should not be simplified as a binary conflict between Europeans and Chinese people; indeed, Chinese mariners and merchants occupied several positions within its events.

Section 73.18

Indigenous Grievances, Missionary Criticism, and the Colonial Order by 1582

By the early 1580s, the costs of the colonial system had provoked complaints. Indigenous leaders and communities objected to excessive tribute, forced labor, and the seizure of goods, and some resisted by flight or by refusing to pay. Spanish sources also record tensions with leaders who felt that promises made during the conquest had not been kept; in the mid-1570s, for example, Spanish reports describe unrest involving Lakandula and others over tribute and treatment, which officials worked to calm. The evidence is thin and was written by Spaniards, but it indicates that indigenous leaders continued to bargain with the colonial government rather than simply accepting its terms.

The arrival of the first bishop of Manila, Domingo de Salazar, in 1581 brought a new critic into the colony. Salazar, a Dominican, was appalled by what he saw as abuses by encomenderos and officials, and he advocated for the rights of indigenous people. In 1582 he convened a synod in Manila and wrote to Philip II describing conditions in the colony. His advocacy was influenced by the Spanish debate about the treatment of indigenous peoples in the Americas and by his own position as a churchman with his own institutional interests.

Students must read such complaints carefully. Indigenous grievances reached the Spanish record through intermediaries, such as friars and interpreters, who selected, translated, and framed what they heard. Episcopal advocacy reflected the bishop's own objectives, including the assertion of church authority against secular officials. Officials' replies reflected their interests in defending their conduct. The documents reveal disputes among colonial institutions, but the 15 June 1582 deposition also identifies indigenous petitioners and preserves the substance of their complaints through interpreters. Their voices were recorded through Spanish administrative procedures rather than as independent, unmediated transcripts.

Salazar's June 1582 letter is especially revealing because it enclosed a dated statement received on 15 June from named indigenous leaders of Tondo and Capaymisilo. Among those identified were Don Luis Amanicaldo, Don Martín Panga, Don Gabriel Luanbacar, Don Juan Bautangad, Salalila, Calao Amarlenguaguay, and Doña Francisca Saygan. They spoke through the interpreters Francisco Morantes and Andrés de Cervantes. The translation of their testimony reports that local magistrates compelled rowing without adequate pay, took rice at prices of their own choosing, and levied tribute for absent or deceased people. It describes flight from communities and the threat of the stocks or whipping when payment was not made.

These complaints should not be flattened into Salazar's personal sentiments. The petitioners supplied concrete grievances and presented them as reasons to appeal to the king; Salazar selected and transmitted the appeal as a bishop seeking relief and support for his position. Their words reached the surviving English edition through several layers: oral statements, interpreters, a Spanish record, manuscript preservation, and editorial translation. This mediation limits access to original phrasing, but it does not cancel indigenous agency. A named woman among the complainants also makes visible a political participant too often omitted from accounts centered solely on male chiefs and soldiers.

Section 73.19

Where Spanish Authority Remained Limited

By 1582 Spanish settlement was concentrated in a few places: Manila and its environs, parts of the Visayas such as Cebu and Panay, and a handful of other towns and encomienda districts. Beyond these centers, Spanish power depended on occasional expeditions, local alliances, and the cooperation of chiefs. Much of Luzon's interior, including the Cordillera highlands, and many communities elsewhere remained outside Spanish administration, paying no tribute and seeing no Spanish officials.

Mindanao and Sulu were not conquered or incorporated during Legazpi's lifetime or in the years covered by this chapter. Muslim polities there, with their own political structures, fleets, and commercial networks, maintained their independence, and relations with the Spaniards involved hostility, raiding, and diplomacy that developed over the following centuries, as Chapter 76 explains. Spanish expeditions into the south in these years were limited in scope and did not establish lasting control.

Maps of the period sometimes label the entire archipelago with a Spanish name, as the cartographic tradition of the late sixteenth century did. Such maps record Spanish claims and geographic knowledge, not administration. To understand the extent of Spanish authority, historians distinguish several categories: nominal claims proclaimed in documents and ceremonies; tributary relationships in which local leaders acknowledged Spanish overlordship and paid some tribute; military garrisons and fortified towns; local alliances based on mutual interest; and areas outside effective Spanish administration. In 1582 the first of these categories covered much of the archipelago, while the fourth was important and the third was small.

The limits had several causes: the small Spanish population, perhaps a few thousand at most across the colony, the lack of money and manpower, the difficulty of sea and river travel, the resistance or avoidance of local communities, and the colony's dependence on a few commercial and administrative centers. These limits shaped later colonial history. Policies of resettlement, missionary expansion, and military campaigns in subsequent decades aimed at extending real control, and they met continuing resistance, as Chapters 75 and 76 show.

A useful test of Spanish authority is to ask whether a town regularly received officials, paid enforceable assessments, could appeal to colonial courts, and housed a resident military or missionary presence. A ceremonial act in a distant village satisfies none of these tests by itself. Some communities were closely connected to Manila by water and submitted recurring payments, while others negotiated only when armed expeditions arrived. Measuring authority in this way produces a patchwork rather than a single color on a political map. It helps explain why subsequent administrations devoted so much effort to relocation, parish organization, local offices, and repeated campaigns.

Section 73.20

The Spanish Conquest of the Philippines: Historical Synthesis

The preceding sections show that the Spanish conquest was a composite process. Military force mattered: firearms, ships, and armor gave small Spanish contingents advantages in particular engagements. But force alone did not establish the colony. Indigenous alliances supplied labor, fighters, food, and knowledge. Rival interests among local leaders opened opportunities that Spaniards exploited. Missionaries provided a religious and legal rationale and some of the personnel for governance. Maritime logistics, in particular the return route discovered in 1565 and the supply base at Panay, sustained the enterprise. Economic extraction through tribute and encomienda paid for the conquest and reorganized relations in indigenous societies.

The scholarly literature highlights different aspects of this process. Phelan emphasized the role of local chiefs and the ways indigenous responses shaped the colonial system. Newson showed that conquest and the early colonial economy could be far more destructive than older interpretations suggested. Scott and Junker reconstructed the indigenous political economies that the Spaniards encountered. These approaches complement each other. The same chiefs who made Spanish government possible were also its subjects, and cooperation could coexist with devastating demographic and social effects.

The document trail gives this synthesis a human scale. The short 1565 letter from Cebu records a commander requesting aid, not a secure empire. The 1570 possession act translates a contested confrontation into claims of royal title. The 1571 account of Bangkusay records both indigenous military resistance and the decisive participation of indigenous auxiliaries, followed by prisoners taken in victory. Finally, the 1582 petition preserves named leaders explaining how forced rowing, rice seizures, and tribute collection affected their people. Read together, these sources show that the conquest was made through force, local political negotiation, and institutional demands whose effects remained contested long after a place had been declared subject.

Historical Synthesis

Conquest, Domination, and Administration

Conquest as an event

Specific landings, battles, and acts of possession, such as Cebu in 1565, Maynila in 1570, and Bangkusay in 1571.

Colonial domination as a relationship

The evolving relations of tribute, intermediaries, missions, and municipal institutions that began to take shape in the 1570s.

Effective administration as a geographic process

Real Spanish control concentrated around Manila and some coastal areas and absent over much of the archipelago.

Continuing resistance and adaptation

Flight, refusal of tribute, negotiation, armed opposition, and the use of Spanish institutions by indigenous leaders.

Synthesis. The Spanish conquest of the Philippines was neither a single military event nor the straightforward replacement of indigenous societies by a European state. From 1565 onward, Spanish officials established settlements at Cebu and Manila by combining military force, indigenous alliances, maritime logistics, and claims of royal sovereignty. Existing rulers and communities responded differently, choosing cooperation, negotiation, resistance, or withdrawal according to their own circumstances. The establishment of tribute, encomiendas, municipal institutions, and Christian missions began to reshape political and social relationships, often through coercion and conflict. By the early 1580s, Spain possessed an enduring colonial center, but its effective authority remained geographically uneven, dependent upon local intermediaries, and contested by populations whose institutions and identities had not disappeared.

The conquest opened the way to three subsequent developments. Manila's position on the South China Sea and its link to New Spain made it the center of the galleon trade (Chapter 74). The missions and parish system expanded and made Christianity a defining feature of colonial society (Chapter 75). And the unresolved relationship with the Muslim polities of Mindanao and Sulu produced centuries of conflict and exchange (Chapter 76). None of these outcomes was determined in 1582, and each depended on the foundations laid in the decades of conquest.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 73 has traced the establishment of Spanish authority in the Philippines between 1564 and the early 1580s. It has argued that the conquest must be understood as a combination of force, negotiation, indigenous participation, and institutional development, and that it produced an enduring colonial center without bringing the archipelago under comprehensive Spanish control.

The Legazpi expedition, organized in New Spain under Philip II's orders, succeeded where earlier expeditions had failed because it found a return route across the Pacific, thanks to Urdaneta's navigation, and because it was designed for settlement. The 1565 landing at Cebu involved negotiation and fighting with Rajah Tupas and other leaders, who were independent political actors with their own interests. Spanish sources describe submission; indigenous leaders may have understood the agreements differently.

The first settlement was fragile. Hunger, dependence on local provisioning, and the Portuguese confrontation of 1568, which was an Iberian dispute and not indigenous resistance, led to the relocation of the principal base to Panay in 1569. That move shifted Spanish attention north, toward Luzon's larger populations and Chinese trade.

In 1570 Martín de Goiti and Juan de Salcedo led a first expedition to Manila Bay, where negotiation turned to fighting and the settlement of Maynila was burned. The act of possession of 6 June 1570 shows how legal language justified force and claimed sovereignty, but it did not create effective control. The 1571 expedition under Legazpi was a separate episode that resulted in the establishment of Spanish Manila.

The leaders of Tondo and Maynila, Lakandula, Rajah Matanda, and Rajah Sulayman, responded differently to the Spaniards, and their choices reflected their own circumstances. The battle at Bangkusay in June 1571 illustrates both the military advantages of Spanish arms and allies and the limits of the evidence: later traditions name a resistance leader whom the contemporary accounts do not securely identify.

The municipal government created on 24 June 1571 gave Manila legal status as a Spanish city, but the city coexisted with indigenous communities and did not extinguish local authority. Expeditions across Luzon extended Spanish claims through negotiation and force but rarely produced lasting administration.

Conquest quickly acquired institutional consequences. The Spaniards governed through indigenous chiefs, who gained official roles and faced new pressures. Tribute and encomienda rewarded conquerors and burdened communities, with abuses recorded by contemporaries. Augustinian missionaries began the first baptisms, though baptism did not equal lasting transformation of belief. Warfare, displacement, and extraction affected households unevenly, and Linda Newson's work suggests the demographic effects were substantial, though estimates are uncertain and vary regionally.

Limahong's 1574 attack, which killed Goiti, showed the colony's vulnerability and its dependence on indigenous allies. It must be distinguished from the activities of Chinese merchants generally. Bishop Salazar's 1582 letter and other evidence of grievances show that the colonial order was contested, though the documents mediate indigenous voices.

By 1582 Spain controlled Manila and some other places, claimed much more, and governed little of the interior, Mindanao, or Sulu. The distinction between conquest as an event, colonial domination as an evolving relationship, and effective administration as a geographically uneven process is the chapter's central analytical point.

The foundations laid in these years shaped later developments in the Manila galleon trade, colonial Christianity, and the long frontier with the Muslim south, which Chapters 74 to 76 take up in turn.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a colonial order began not with a decisive victory but with a series of uncertain, negotiated, and often violent encounters whose outcomes were shaped by food, ships, alliances, and the decisions of people on both sides. Treating the conquest as a one-sided military triumph or as a purely indigenous story would each miss the combination of forces that produced it.

The chapter also reveals a larger pattern in early modern empires: claims made in documents and ceremonies often exceeded what the claimants controlled. The Spanish Philippines in 1582 was an enduring colonial center surrounded by a much larger world of tributary relationships, alliances, and communities that remained effectively independent. Understanding that gap is essential for reading colonial sources and maps.

Many of the chapter's developments shaped what followed. The Manila settlement became the hub of a trans-Pacific commercial system (Chapter 74); the first missionary efforts grew into a colonial church (Chapter 75); and the unconquered south became a long frontier of conflict and exchange (Chapter 76). Indigenous elites that had mediated the conquest continued to mediate colonial rule, and the grievances voiced in the 1570s and 1580s reappeared in later centuries.

Finally, the chapter contributes to the textbook's larger argument that Southeast Asian societies were historical actors. Their leaders chose among alliance, resistance, and withdrawal, and their choices, along with Spanish ambitions and limitations, produced the colonial Philippines.
