The Transformation and Decline of Srivijaya

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Srivijaya did not vanish in a single afternoon of naval warfare. Between roughly the tenth and the thirteenth centuries, a maritime network centered on the rivers and straits of Sumatra and the Malay Peninsula was shaken by invasion, reorganized around new centers, and gradually overtaken by new regional rivals, while the trade, Buddhism, and Malay-speaking communities it had sustained continued to flourish.

In the year 1025, according to the Tamil inscriptions of the Chola king Rajendra I, a fleet crossed the Bay of Bengal and attacked a series of ports on the Malay Peninsula and Sumatra, taking captive the ruler of a place the inscriptions call Kadaram and carrying off treasure. For generations, textbooks treated this raid as the moment at which Srivijaya, the great Buddhist maritime power of the Strait of Malacca, was destroyed. The reality is both more interesting and more difficult to reconstruct. Chinese sources continue to record envoys from a place they call Sanfoqi long after 1025; a Chinese court in the 1070s and 1080s was receiving missions from the river port of Jambi; and a stone image inscribed in 1286 shows a Javanese king dispatching a Buddhist statue to a Sumatran court on the upper Batanghari.

This chapter examines that long transformation. Its central question is how Srivijaya's maritime political network changed between the tenth and thirteenth centuries, and why its history is better understood as a transformation of regional power than as the sudden collapse of a unified empire. Answering the question requires us to hold together several processes that unfolded at different speeds: the expansion of the Chola state of southern India, the reorientation of Chinese maritime commerce under the Song dynasty, the rise of competing ports on the Malay Peninsula and in Sumatra, the growing power of East Javanese kingdoms, and the persistence of river-based Malay communities that had always been the foundation of Srivijaya's wealth.

The chapter also asks students to think carefully about names. The word "Srivijaya" appears in a handful of inscriptions from the seventh century onward; the Chinese name Sanfoqi, used in Song-era sources, may refer to rulers, ports, and networks that changed from one century to the next. A familiar label can create an illusion of continuity, or conceal real change. Historians must therefore ask not only what happened but what, exactly, each source means when it refers to Srivijaya, Sanfoqi, Kadaram, Malayu, or Zhanbei.

The evidence is uneven. Royal victory inscriptions from southern India, copper-plate charters preserved in the Netherlands, Chinese diplomatic and geographical writings, a Tamil merchant inscription from northern Sumatra, an Old Malay and Sanskrit inscription in Kawi script on a statue pedestal, and the brick ruins along the Musi and Batanghari rivers each illuminate a part of the story. None provides a complete narrative, and each reflects the interests of its makers.

This chapter builds on the earlier treatment of Srivijaya's foundation and Buddhist maritime networks in Chapters 28 and 29 and on the discussion of the early Malay Peninsula in Chapter 26; it does not retell those accounts. It ends by preparing for Chapter 48, which examines Singhasari and the expansion of East Java, and for Chapter 49, on the rise of Majapahit. Throughout, the guiding commitment of this textbook applies: the societies of the Malay world were historical actors, not passive intermediaries between India and China.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the main categories of evidence (Tamil, Chinese, Old Javanese, Old Malay, and archaeological) used to reconstruct Srivijaya after c. 950 and the limits of each.
  2. Explain why Srivijaya is better understood as a network of ports, river communities, and alliances than as a permanently centralized territorial empire.
  3. Trace the rise of Chola maritime power and the diplomatic, religious, and commercial relationships between the Cholas and Srivijaya before 1025.
  4. Reconstruct the Chola naval campaign of 1025 and distinguish the claims of royal victory inscriptions from independently confirmed results.
  5. Evaluate competing explanations for the Chola attack without treating any single motive as conclusively established.
  6. Assess the evidence for increasing prominence of Jambi and Malayu and the debate over whether Srivijaya's capital moved from Palembang.
  7. Analyze how Song China's maritime expansion, Javanese rivalry, and peninsular ports reshaped political and commercial relationships in the straits.
  8. Compare three interpretations of Srivijaya's fate (military decline, political decentralization, and economic transformation) and explain why none alone is sufficient.

Historical Context

By the late tenth century, the polity known in Sanskrit-derived inscriptions as Srivijaya had been a significant presence in the Malay world for more than three hundred years. Its earliest datable inscriptions, from the 680s, were written in Old Malay and associated with the Palembang region of southeastern Sumatra; Chinese pilgrims, including Yijing, described it as a center of Buddhist learning and shipping. Chapters 28 and 29 examined how its rulers drew on river-mouth settlements, the loyalty of seafaring communities, and the profits of the China–India trade, and how its Buddhist networks linked Sumatra to Nalanda, Nagapattinam, and the Chinese court. Chapter 30 and Chapter 31 considered the connection between the Sailendra family and Central Java.

By about 1000, however, the political world around Srivijaya was changing. In southern India the Chola dynasty was expanding its territory and its naval reach. In China the new Song dynasty (founded 960) was encouraging overseas trade as a source of revenue and, over the next two centuries, shifting commercial gravity toward its southern ports. In Java, as Chapter 32 explained, political authority had moved eastward, and East Javanese courts were developing their own commercial and diplomatic ambitions. On the Malay Peninsula and in northern and eastern Sumatra, ports that had once looked toward Srivijaya were acquiring their own connections with merchants and courts across the Bay of Bengal and the South China Sea.

Historians have long debated what these changes meant. The older view, influential in the mid-twentieth century, saw a powerful, centralized thalassocracy broken by the Chola raid and then fading away. Revisions beginning in the later twentieth century, notably the work of O. W. Wolters on Malay history, and more recent studies by Hermann Kulke, So Kee-Long, Pierre-Yves Manguin, and others, have emphasized a looser political structure, a persistent river-based Malay world, and a gradual redistribution of power. This chapter follows the second approach while presenting the first fairly and noting where the evidence remains unresolved.

Division I · Section 47.1

Reconstructing Srivijaya's Transformation: Inscriptions, Chinese Records, Tamil Sources, and Archaeology

Few major polities of premodern Asia are as dependent on outside evidence as Srivijaya. Only a small number of inscriptions in Old Malay survive from the Sumatran heartland, and most date from the late seventh century, long before the period examined here. For the eleventh through thirteenth centuries, almost no royal records of the kind that would let historians follow successive rulers, capitals, and administrative arrangements have been found. The story must therefore be assembled from sources produced elsewhere, supplemented by archaeology that is rich in some places and thin in others.

Chinese records are the most continuous. Tang-era pilgrims transcribed the name as Shilifoshi; Song-era documents use Sanfoqi. The Song court recorded missions, titles, gifts, and requests, and later writers such as Zhou Qufei (1178) and Zhao Rukuo (1225) collected descriptions of trade and ports. These texts were written to serve Chinese administrative or commercial interests. They record what reached the Chinese court or its merchants, not necessarily how Malay rulers understood their own authority, and the same transcription can cover very different realities across centuries.

Tamil inscriptions provide a second, contrasting perspective. The Chola royal inscriptions that describe the 1025 expedition are praise-poems for a conquering king. Copper-plate charters, now partly preserved in Leiden, record a different relationship: a Srivijayan ruler founding a Buddhist monastery in a Chola port and a Chola king granting revenue to support it. A Tamil merchant-guild inscription of 1088 from Barus in Sumatra adds evidence of South Indian commercial organization on the Sumatran coast.

Indigenous evidence is scarcer but critical. The Padang Roco inscription of 1286, written principally in Old Malay, with a Sanskrit verse, in Kawi script on the pedestal of a statue, is among the few precisely datable local documents of the later period. Archaeological survey and excavation in the Palembang region and the Batanghari basin add settlements, temples, ceramics, and imported goods, though these cannot name rulers or dates with the confidence of a text.

Combining these sources requires care. A Chola victory claim and a Chinese diplomatic record may describe the same world while measuring different things. The distinction at the heart of this chapter is between political decline, the weakening of a particular ruler's or court's ability to command allegiance, and changing commercial prominence, in which trade moves among ports without disappearing. Evidence for one is not automatically evidence for the other.

A source-critical reading must proceed in stages rather than treating every named port as proof of an empire of fixed size. First, date the statement itself: an inscription commemorating a conquest differs from a later merchant's report about a trading port. Second, identify what the writer was trying to accomplish: royal praise, diplomatic recognition, protection of commercial privileges, or a religious endowment. Third, compare names across languages without assuming that similar transcriptions necessarily refer to the same ruling house. Fourth, locate the geographical claim in a landscape of river channels, shifting shorelines, and multiple anchorages. A single entry in a Chinese compilation can support the existence of a recognized diplomatic partner without demonstrating that one king appointed officials throughout Sumatra and the peninsula.

In practice, the most productive questions are often narrower than the ambition to reconstruct the whole polity. Did a particular ruler maintain relations with the Chola court? Did a group of traders organize at Barus in 1088? Did a Javanese king send an image to Dharmasraya in 1286? Such questions can be answered from documents with known contexts. Claims about how all these developments fit into one continuous political genealogy require additional inference. Keeping those levels separate allows the chapter to develop a connected history without concealing the gaps on which disagreement among historians depends.

Historical Vocabulary
Sanfoqi

Definition. The Chinese name, used in Song-era sources, for the maritime polity or network associated with Srivijaya. The literal sense of the individual Chinese characters does not establish the origin or meaning of this foreign-name transcription; the etymology remains uncertain.

Historical context. The name appears in Chinese records of tribute missions and in geographical works from the late tenth century. It replaced earlier Tang-era transcriptions such as Shilifoshi. Chinese authors used it for a state in the Malay world whose ships and envoys reached Chinese ports.

Why it matters. It is the main continuous label for Srivijaya in the eleventh through thirteenth centuries and thus the evidentiary basis for claims that Srivijaya persisted after 1025.

Potential misunderstanding. A constant label does not prove a constant dynasty, capital, or territory. Sanfoqi may refer to different rulers and centers at different times, a point discussed further in Sections 47.11 and 47.16.

Division I · Section 47.2

Srivijaya's Maritime Political System around 1000 CE

This section summarizes, rather than retells, the structure examined in Chapters 28 and 29. By around 1000, Srivijaya is best understood not as a bounded territorial state but as a network of river-mouth ports, riverine settlements, and seafaring communities bound to one or more rulers by oaths, ritual obligations, trade privileges, marriage, and the possibility of force. The mandala model introduced in Chapter 1 is a helpful, though modern, analytical description: authority was strongest near a ruling center, weaker with distance, and overlapped with the spheres of other centers.

The material base lay in the Malay-speaking communities of the rivers of Sumatra and the peninsula. Upriver communities produced forest goods, resins, camphor, aromatic woods, and gold, which were collected at downstream ports. Coastal and seafaring groups, including those later called Orang Laut, supplied navigational skill, escort, and when necessary naval force. Rulers profited from tolls and from the right to determine where passing merchants could trade. The inscriptions of the seventh century already speak of oaths of loyalty and punishments for those who betrayed the ruler, suggesting that fidelity had to be continually reinforced.

Religious patronage was equally important. Srivijayan rulers sponsored Buddhist monasteries and scholars and corresponded with centers in India and China. Such patronage created networks of obligation and prestige in addition to commercial and military ones. The Sailendra family name, attested in Sumatran and Javanese contexts, reminds us that dynastic and religious ties across the Java Sea were part of this world.

Three implications follow. First, since the system depended on personal and negotiated loyalties, it could be rearranged without a formal "collapse": a port could change patrons or trade with a different partner without an inscription announcing the change. Second, because wealth came from intermediary commerce, the system was vulnerable if merchants or fleets found other routes or ports. Third, a naval raid on its ports, however damaging, would not automatically dismantle riverine communities or ritual relationships. Each point recurs through this chapter.

The distinction between control of a route and command over every vessel on it is essential. Ports could attract shipping through markets, safe anchorage, interpreters, supplies, and connections to inland producers; a ruler's political influence was partly a consequence of providing those services. The maritime network was therefore not only a system of coercion. Local authorities had to retain merchants and skilled navigators who might otherwise transfer their business to another harbor. The attraction of a leading entrepôt could be lost gradually, even while its temples, population, and local ruling families continued to function.

This makes the relationship between the coast and the interior central to the history of power. Imported ceramics or foreign textiles were valuable at the coast partly because inland communities exchanged locally produced goods for them. River transport connected these different economies, but also limited the ability of a ruler at one river mouth to control the products of another basin. Srivijaya's political reach thus depended on alliances with people who had access to resources and transport beyond the court's immediate presence. The resulting system could display considerable resilience while remaining vulnerable to changes in commercial and political loyalties.

Division I · Section 47.3

The Straits of Malacca and Sunda: Geography, Trade, and Political Competition

Srivijaya's power rested on the geography of the straits. The Strait of Malacca, between the Malay Peninsula and Sumatra, funnels shipping between the Bay of Bengal and the South China Sea; the Sunda Strait, between Sumatra and Java, provided an alternative passage into the Java Sea. Monsoon winds, examined in Chapter 4, shaped the timing of voyages: ships crossing the Indian Ocean or South China Sea often had to wait weeks or months for seasonal winds to change, making ports in the straits natural places to wait, repair, trade, and store goods.

Rivers added another dimension. The Musi, Batanghari, and other Sumatran rivers flow through lowland swamps and forests to estuaries on the east coast, creating natural corridors between the interior and the sea. A ruler who commanded the mouth of a river could tax or direct the flow of forest goods and could offer visiting ships anchorage, water, and markets. The same logic applied on the Malay Peninsula, where portage routes across the narrower parts of the land provided alternatives to the full sea voyage.

This geography both created and constrained Srivijaya. Because the straits had many channels, estuaries, and harbors, it was difficult for any ruler to control every one. Ports along the coasts could offer merchants better terms or safer harbors. Rival rulers could base fleets in neighboring rivers. The same conditions that made it possible for a single center to claim leadership also made it possible for others to compete. Geography did not determine outcomes, but it shaped which strategies were available.

The seasonal character of the sea routes also helps explain why a port's importance could exceed the size of its immediate population. A ship arriving with one monsoon might await a change of wind before continuing toward the South China Sea or the Bay of Bengal. During that interval the crew needed provisions, protection, and opportunities to exchange cargo. Commercial centers consequently assembled people and goods from places well beyond their local hinterlands. The geographical advantage was real, but its benefits were shared among shipowners, pilots, brokers, producers, and competing rulers; the mere existence of a bottleneck did not guarantee a monopoly to one court.

Maps that draw Srivijaya as one continuous block of territory can obscure this pattern. The critical points were river confluences, navigable estuaries, harbor settlements, and routes through or around the peninsula. Control over one of these nodes could be significant even if there were no permanent garrison in a distant village. Conversely, a merchant's decision to use another river or port could shift commercial activity without leaving evidence of conquest. The geography helps explain both the emergence of Srivijayan influence and the gradual transfer of commercial prominence to other centers.

Division I · Section 47.4

New Pressures in the Tenth and Early Eleventh Centuries

Three developments in the decades around the year 1000 altered the environment in which Srivijaya operated, even before the events of 1025.

The first was Javanese political competition. Chinese records of the early 990s report a Javanese envoy describing hostilities with Sanfoqi, and an Old Javanese inscription of the later 1040s records the destruction of the court of King Dharmawangsa in 1016 in an attack by a Javanese rival, after which Airlangga eventually emerged as a major ruler. The Javanese texts do not name Srivijaya as the author of the 1016 attack, and some scholars have inferred Sumatran involvement only indirectly. What is clear is that, by the end of the tenth century, East Java was both commercially active and politically assertive, and that relations between Java and the straits were contentious.

The second was the expansion of the Chola dynasty, treated in Section 47.5. Chola rulers extended their authority in southern India and across the sea to Sri Lanka and the Maldives. Their ports on the Coromandel coast were connected to merchants who traveled to the Malay world, and their rulers developed an interest in maritime prestige and revenue.

The third was the changing Chinese environment. The Song court established offices to supervise foreign shipping at Guangzhou and later at other southern ports and welcomed the revenue from customs and purchases of foreign goods. These policies changed incentives for merchants, officials, and rulers throughout the maritime world, as Section 47.10 explains.

None of these developments was inherently fatal to Srivijaya. In 1003, according to Chinese records, a mission from Sanfoqi reported that a temple had been built for the Chinese emperor; at about the same time a Srivijayan ruler was supporting a Buddhist foundation at Nagapattinam. Srivijaya's rulers were adapting to new conditions through diplomacy and patronage. Yet the surrounding environment was now more crowded with powerful actors, and the ability of any one center to manage the straits through ritual and negotiation was being tested.

The Song accounts of 992 are especially revealing because they preserve perspectives from both sides of a rivalry. A Srivijayan envoy delayed his return after hearing that Java had attacked his homeland and sought Chinese diplomatic support; an embassy from Java also described hostilities with Sanfoqi. These are reports of claims brought to a foreign court, not independent observations of a battle. Together, however, they establish that political competition between Java and a Sumatran-centered network was sufficiently serious to affect travel and diplomatic communication. That context matters when interpreting the Chola attack three decades later: the maritime world was already politically divided.

The record of a Srivijayan religious mission in 1003 points in a different direction. Court representatives reported that a Buddhist foundation had been built in honor of the Song emperor and asked for an auspicious name and a bell. Such an act did not constitute Chinese occupation or evidence of unqualified dependency. It was a way for a Malay ruler to turn religious patronage into an internationally recognizable form of diplomatic connection. The juxtaposition of these reports reveals a polity engaging in rivalry and cooperation at the same time, rather than proceeding along a simple path from prosperity toward collapse.

War, diplomacy, and the long aftermath of 1025

Division II · Section 47.5

The Rise of the Chola Maritime Power under Rajaraja I and Rajendra I

The Chola dynasty, based in the Kaveri delta of southern India, rose to regional dominance in the late tenth century. Rajaraja I (r. c. 985–1014) expanded Chola authority across much of the Tamil country and into neighboring regions, built the great Brihadisvara temple at Thanjavur, and extended Chola control over parts of Sri Lanka and the Maldives. His son Rajendra I (r. 1014–1044) continued this expansion, campaigning in Sri Lanka, northward toward the Ganges, and eventually across the Bay of Bengal.

This is not the place for a full account of Chola history, which belongs in South Asian textbooks. For our purposes three points matter. First, the Chola state commanded significant naval capacity. Its fleets crossed to the islands of the Indian Ocean and could operate from the ports of the Coromandel coast. Historians debate how large these fleets were and how often they were used for sustained operations, since the inscriptions are not detailed technical records. Second, the Chola world was closely connected to commerce. Merchant guilds, active in Tamil country and across Southeast Asia, supplied revenue and information and were patrons of temples. Third, the Chola monarchy placed great emphasis on conquest as an expression of royal prestige; inscriptions listing victories were integral to royal legitimacy, as the Primary Source Window in Section 47.7 shows.

The Cholas also looked eastward diplomatically. Chinese records note Chola embassies to the Song court in 1015, 1033, and 1077. These missions suggest that Chola rulers sought direct relations with the Chinese court and an interest in the profitable trade between South India and China, a trade in which Srivijayan ports were intermediaries.

Students should avoid reading this expansion as inevitable or as a plan formulated in advance. Chola rulers pursued many aims in many directions, and their relationship with Southeast Asia emerged from changing circumstances. The point for this chapter is that, by the early eleventh century, a state with the means and motives to project power across the Bay of Bengal had emerged, and Srivijaya's rulers were already in relationship with it.

Naval capability should not be equated with a permanent, centrally administered fleet of a particular size. The surviving Chola records celebrate successful movement of forces across water but give much less information about how ships were obtained, how crews were recruited, how provisions were stored, or how long they could remain overseas. Coastal networks, ports already linked by trade, and experienced mariners would have mattered to any large expedition. Those practical requirements complicate modern reconstructions that describe the campaign as a straightforward projection of land-based military power across an empty sea.

Chola political culture also linked military success to durable records of kingship. A victory inscription publicized royal achievement to audiences who were familiar with the ruler's temples, grants, and other claims. That does not make the expedition fictitious: it explains why the inscription foregrounds captured kings, valuable objects, and named targets rather than describing negotiations and the daily burden of transport. The sources are most useful when read for what the Chola court wished to commemorate, then checked against the distinct question of whether Southeast Asian societies remained governed by their own rulers in the decades afterward.

Division II · Section 47.6

Srivijaya–Chola Diplomacy, Buddhist Patronage, and Commercial Competition

One of the most important pieces of evidence for the pre-1025 relationship is the group of copper-plate charters preserved at Leiden, issued by Chola kings. They record that a ruler of Srivijaya and Kataha (the latter generally identified with Kedah), a member of the Sailendra lineage, built a Buddhist vihara at Nagapattinam, an important Chola port. The vihara was named after his father, and the Chola king granted the revenue of a village to support it. The charters, issued in the time of Rajaraja I and confirmed under Rajendra I, show Srivijayan and Chola rulers acting as partners in the support of Buddhism and in the protection of Tamil and Malay commercial interests.

The Chinese record of 1003 offers a parallel. A mission from Sanfoqi reported the building of a Buddhist temple intended for the Chinese emperor's long life, and the Song court responded by naming the temple and bestowing a bell. The same ruler, in other words, was cultivating relations with both the Chola and Song courts through Buddhist patronage. This reveals a Srivijayan diplomacy that used religion as a way to secure goodwill and trading privileges in the two largest markets of the Indian Ocean and South China Sea.

At the same time, there were reasons for tension. Tamil merchant guilds operated in the Malay world, and they were not simply agents of Srivijaya. Where Srivijayan rulers asserted rights to tolls and anchorages, guild merchants, whose interests also coincided with those of Chola temples and rulers, had incentives to seek better terms. The direct relationship between the Chola court and the Chinese court, evident in the embassies of 1015 and later, also reduced the need for Srivijayan intermediaries.

The upshot is that 1025 was not the first contact between Srivijaya and the Chola world. The relationship had been cooperative at the level of religious patronage and had involved competition at the level of commerce. How and why these relations deteriorated into war is not documented in any source that explains the immediate trigger, which is why the motives for the attack remain debated (Section 47.8).

The Nagapattinam charters show why the relationship is difficult to reduce to hostility between rival states. A Buddhist foundation sponsored by a Malay-linked ruler in a Chola port depended on Chola royal protection and on revenue from local land. It connected religious merit to the practical security of a community that included monks, pilgrims, and merchants. The foundation also created a place in South India where visitors from across the Bay of Bengal could find familiar institutional relationships. Patronage of this kind was an exercise of diplomacy and prestige, but it could also facilitate the movement of persons and ideas across the sea.

It would be a mistake, however, to assume that the existence of a monastery resolved conflicts over maritime commerce. Royal sponsorship and merchant interests did not always coincide, and the same routes could carry both peaceful embassies and armed fleets. Nor does a Buddhist grant prove that all Srivijayan subjects were Buddhist or that one court governed every port used by Tamil traders. This evidence demonstrates cooperation between identifiable ruling circles before 1025; it does not reveal the immediate dispute that subsequently brought the Chola navy to Southeast Asia.

Division II · Section 47.7

The Chola Naval Campaign of 1025: Ports, Royal Captivity, and Military Objectives

The principal sources for the expedition are Chola inscriptions: a shorter record of about 1025 and a fuller account in the Thanjavur inscription of about 1030–31. They describe a fleet sent by Rajendra I across the sea, the capture of Kadaram and its king, the taking of treasure, and the seizure of a series of other places. The ruler captured is named Sangramavijayottungavarman. The places listed include Srivijaya, Pannai, Malaiyur, Ilangasoka (usually identified with Langkasuka on the peninsula), Madamalingam (often identified with Tambralinga), Ilamuridesam (usually associated with Lamuri in northern Sumatra), and Manakkavaram (often identified with the Nicobar Islands), among others. Not all identifications are secure.

Even accepting the Chola list at face value, the geography is striking. The named places are spread widely, from the peninsula to northern Sumatra and islands in the Bay of Bengal, and include places associated with several different polities. This suggests a series of coastal raids against ports that were important for shipping, not a land conquest of a territorial state. Nothing in the inscriptions describes a Chola administration established in Southeast Asia, and no Tamil governors or garrisons are attested in the Malay world as a result.

The inscriptions are, however, specific about plunder. They describe the seizure of treasure, elephants, and a prestigious jeweled gateway, and the capture of the king of Kadaram. Such details served the purposes of royal praise, but they are also the kind of claim that would be risky to invent for an audience that included merchants and officials who knew the sea. Many historians accept that a major naval raid took place, while treating details of numbers, results, and the exact sequence with caution.

Independent confirmation is limited. Chinese records do not describe the raid in detail. The best corroboration is indirect: later Chola inscriptions that continue to refer to the conquest, the eventual attention given to Kadaram in the later eleventh century (Section 47.9), and the evident reorganization of power in the straits in the following decades. Historians therefore distinguish three levels of certainty: that a Chola naval expedition attacked Southeast Asian ports is well supported; that the king of Kadaram was captured is plausible on the strength of the Chola claim; and the claimed extent of the conquests is uncertain.

Historians must also distinguish the date of an expedition from the date on which a king's achievements were recorded. The sequence of royal inscriptions includes references to the overseas success in records issued after the campaign itself. The list of places is a commemorative geography of victory, not an itinerary giving the order of landings. Even when a name can be plausibly associated with a modern site, the evidence may not establish whether that location was attacked by the main force, a detachment, or an allied contingent. A carefully written reconstruction must therefore separate securely attested features—the overseas expedition and the royal claims—from hypothetical sailing routes and target sequences.

The capture of a ruler deserves similar scrutiny. The inscriptions name Sangramavijayottungavarman as king of Kadaram, but the identification of this figure with the wider Srivijayan political leadership is partly an interpretive problem. Such royal titles could be associated with networks extending across Sumatra and the peninsula, while regional centers maintained their own interests. Military action against several ports may have exposed political fault lines among those centers rather than producing a single moment of imperial transfer. The broader significance of 1025 lies in the reach of Chola power and the demonstration that important ports and rulers could be attacked, not in documentary proof of long-term Chola rule in the straits.

Division II · Section 47.8

Why Did the Cholas Attack? Competing Historical Interpretations

No surviving text states the reasons for the expedition. Historians have offered several explanations, which are best treated as possibilities that are not mutually exclusive.

Commercial access. One interpretation holds that Chola rulers and Tamil merchant guilds wanted greater access to the China trade and resented Srivijayan tolls and restrictions. The direct Chola embassies to China fit this reading, as does the presence of Tamil guilds in Southeast Asia. The weakness of this explanation is the absence of any text that mentions such a dispute, and the fact that Chola inscriptions emphasize prestige rather than trade.

Maritime competition. A related view stresses naval rivalry. If Srivijaya controlled passage through the straits and could tax or obstruct shipping, a strong Chola navy might have sought to demonstrate that the Bay of Bengal was not Srivijaya's to command. The difficulty is that we lack direct evidence of obstruction of Chola ships.

Royal prestige. Chola kings expected victories to enhance their status. A campaign that brought back a captured king and treasure from distant lands would suit the ideals of conquest evident elsewhere in Rajendra's reign, such as his expedition toward the Ganges. On this view the expedition's goals were as much ceremonial and political as economic.

Political alliances and rivalries. Some scholars note that Srivijaya's relationships with other states, including Javanese kingdoms, may have affected the calculation, and that Chola rulers might have taken advantage of Srivijayan weakness or of a request by rivals. The evidence is thin.

Indian Ocean strategy. Finally, the raid may have been part of a broader strategy of projecting power across the Bay of Bengal, including operations in Sri Lanka and the Maldives. This accounts for the wide spread of targets but does not explain the choice of Southeast Asia in particular.

These explanations suggest the value of asking questions of the sources rather than choosing one answer. Because the sources are royal records written by the winner, motives most likely reflect a mixture of material and symbolic aims. The question remains open.

The existence of several plausible explanations is not a license to combine them into a story of motives that the sources never state. A commercial hypothesis should be tested against evidence of tolls, disputes, shipping routes, or restrictions on merchants; a theory of alliance should identify the allied ruler and contemporary testimony to an agreement. An interpretation emphasizing royal prestige finds support in the form and vocabulary of the Chola victory records, yet cannot alone demonstrate that prestige was the only objective. The historian's task is to say which sources bear on each proposed mechanism and to acknowledge when an argument remains circumstantial.

Comparison with later interventions also requires caution. The Chola court's claim to have restored a ruler in Kadaram in the later eleventh century suggests that South Indian kings retained an interest in events across the Bay of Bengal. It does not prove that the motivation for that later episode was identical to the motivation of 1025. The local political circumstances may have changed. These different layers of evidence support the interpretation that the campaigns emerged from a world of overlapping commercial, diplomatic, and dynastic interests rather than from one permanent policy of conquest.

Division II · Section 47.9

The Aftermath of 1025: Political Disruption without Immediate Disappearance

The raid of 1025 was a major shock. A king was reportedly captured, treasure taken, and ports raided across a wide area. Such an event would have damaged the prestige of the rulers concerned and could have encouraged rivals and subordinate ports to reconsider their loyalties. Rulers whose authority rested on a reputation for strength and ritual legitimacy were particularly vulnerable to such a blow.

Yet the evidence does not show an immediate end to Srivijayan institutions. Chinese records continue to register missions from Sanfoqi in the decades after 1025. Chola inscriptions do not claim to have established a permanent administration. And a Chola inscription from the late 1060s, attributed to Virarajendra, claims that the king conquered Kadaram and gave it to a ruler who sought his help, implying that Kadaram retained political significance and that Chola intervention there was not a single episode. Because it too is a royal Chola claim, it should be read cautiously, but it suggests continuing Chola engagement with the peninsula in the later eleventh century.

Several consequences can be proposed with reasonable confidence. The prestige of the old network was damaged. Ports on the peninsula and the northern Sumatran coast gained room to act independently. Merchants had increased reasons to diversify their routes. And rulers in the Jambi region and elsewhere found opportunities to assert themselves in Chinese eyes. These changes unfolded across decades, not at once, and they depended on events and decisions that the sources only partly reveal.

The key lesson is that 1025 should be understood as a turning point in a longer process, not as the end of Srivijaya. A shock can accelerate changes that are already under way without by itself being their cause.

To assess the raid's impact, it is useful to separate three time scales. In the immediate term, the seizure of movable wealth and the reported capture of a ruler were serious losses for those attacked. In the following decades, port leaders and commercial groups may have recalculated the value of existing alliances. Over a longer period, Chinese shipping patterns, the rise of other river systems, and Javanese political ambition altered the strategic environment irrespective of the raid. None of these effects can be measured simply by counting the names in the Chola victory list.

A further difficulty is the absence of a continuous series of local royal documents that would identify who controlled each port before and after the campaign. Chinese embassies show that recognizable diplomatic relationships survived; they do not show that every earlier relationship endured unchanged. Similarly, later Chola references to Kadaram indicate renewed intervention or patronage but cannot demonstrate decades of occupation. The proper conclusion is narrower and more defensible: 1025 was a major regional disruption within an ongoing transformation, and its effects must be reconstructed alongside changes whose causes cannot be reduced to a single expedition.

Division III · Section 47.10

Song China's Maritime Expansion and Changing Commercial Networks

The Song dynasty made overseas commerce a significant part of its fiscal and commercial life. Offices supervising foreign shipping were established at Guangzhou in the early Song and later at other southern ports, and the court collected duties and made purchases of imported goods such as aromatics, resins, spices, and ivory. After the Song lost the north in the early twelfth century and relocated its capital to the south, maritime revenue became even more important to the state. Quanzhou, in Fujian, rose to prominence as a port in the later eleventh and twelfth centuries.

Two consequences for Southeast Asia deserve emphasis. First, Chinese ships and merchants themselves became more active in the South China Sea and beyond. Where earlier trade had often depended on Southeast Asian and Indian Ocean ships carrying goods to Chinese ports, increasing numbers of Chinese vessels traveled to Southeast Asian ports to buy goods directly. Historians debate the speed and scale of this development, but the general direction is widely accepted. Second, the Song court's tribute and trade regulations created incentives for ports to send missions in their own names, since a recognized mission could bring privileges and legitimacy.

For Srivijaya, these changes had mixed effects. A network that had benefited from being the necessary intermediary between Chinese and Indian Ocean traders found that direct voyages were becoming more common. At the same time, demand for Southeast Asian products was strong, so ports that could supply forest goods, spices, and aromatics prospered. The consequence was not a collapse of trade but a redistribution of its benefits: ports on the Malay Peninsula, in Java, in northern Sumatra, and on the Sumatran east coast could each seek their own links with Chinese and Indian merchants.

This is the central economic point of the chapter. Trade continued and in many places expanded after 1025. What weakened was the earlier concentration of political influence in a particular center or network of rulers, not the commerce of the region as a whole.

The commercial consequences of Song policy operated through specific institutions. Chinese officials regulated the arrival of foreign ships, assessed duties, and managed some of the purchase and sale of imported goods. Merchants responded to the relative security, purchasing power, and opportunities provided by different ports. Southeast Asian leaders in turn sought recognition at the Song court because a favorable diplomatic relationship could accompany opportunities for exchange. Political prestige and commercial access were interwoven, but neither guaranteed the other: a port that sent embassies might still compete with neighboring ports for cargo.

Overseas trade must also be viewed from the producing side. Resins, aromatic woods, forest products, and other commodities did not originate in the harbor where they were exported. They moved through multiple transactions involving upland collectors, river communities, and coastal brokers before reaching Chinese or Indian Ocean merchants. New direct routes altered the bargaining position of those participants rather than making Southeast Asian intermediaries disappear. A reorientation of shipping could diminish the political leverage of one group of rulers even while enriching another port in the same broad maritime world. That distinction is central to understanding why economic vitality and political decentralization could occur together.

Division III · Section 47.11

Srivijaya in Chinese Sources during the Eleventh and Twelfth Centuries

Song records, including the dynastic history and administrative compilations, list missions from Sanfoqi across the eleventh and twelfth centuries. They also note requests and rewards, such as Srivijayan interest in the temple associated with the emperor's welfare. Two Chinese authors give fuller descriptions. Zhou Qufei, writing in 1178, described Sanfoqi as an important country of the southern seas through which many merchants passed. Zhao Rukuo, in a work of 1225, described the polity as controlling the strait and listed numerous dependencies on the peninsula and Sumatra, including places associated with Kedah, Langkasuka, Tambralinga, Palembang, and Lamuri.

These accounts must be read carefully. Zhao Rukuo's information was gathered from merchants and officials, and its list of dependencies may reflect trade connections or traditional claims rather than administrative control. The text speaks of the ruler's use of chains or other means to regulate passage, but modern historians treat such details as reports whose reliability is hard to test. Moreover, some of the information may derive from earlier sources, so that the Chinese description of Sanfoqi in 1225 may be partly a description of Sanfoqi as it had been understood earlier.

The continued appearance of Sanfoqi in Chinese sources does not prove that the same rulers governed from the same center. The Chinese court used a name for a recognizable set of sea-borne partners, and it adopted new names, such as Zhanbei for Jambi, when circumstances required. When a name persists, historians should ask whether it designates a single state, a succession of states, or a group of polities that the Chinese viewed collectively.

The history of these Chinese texts is itself important. A Song-era geographical description might incorporate reports gathered by merchants years before its compilation, while a later dynastic history might organize earlier diplomatic entries into a continuous account under one country name. Those procedures created useful compilations, but also made retrospective political continuity appear firmer than contemporary observers may have believed. Translating a label such as Sanfoqi into the modern name Srivijaya without noting the period and the source risks treating the work of historical interpretation as if it were a direct statement in the original record.

The value of the mission registers lies partly in their specificity: a dated embassy shows that an envoy claimed to represent a ruler or political community recognizable to the Song authorities. To infer the geographical extent of that ruler's power requires other evidence. When later writers listed dependencies, their information may represent maritime relationships or inherited descriptions rather than tax districts governed from one capital. The chapter therefore reads the Chinese record neither as wholly misleading nor as a transparent map of political territory; it is indispensable evidence for diplomacy and commerce whose implications must be tested against archaeology and local inscriptions.

Division III · Section 47.12

Palembang, Jambi, and Malayu: Was Srivijaya's Capital Transferred?

For much of the twentieth century, histories described Srivijaya's capital as moving from Palembang to Jambi after the Chola raid. The argument relied on Chinese records. A mission from Zhanbei, the Chinese name for Jambi, appears in the Song annals in 1079, and in 1082 the Song court received correspondence from the ruler of Jambi. Because Chinese records of earlier periods referred to Sanfoqi, and later records give more attention to Jambi, scholars inferred that the center of power had shifted. Jambi was also identified with Malayu, a Malay polity known from earlier Chinese sources and from the Chola list of conquests.

The inference is reasonable but not conclusive. The Chinese records show that Jambi sent missions and was treated as a distinct partner. They do not say that Jambi replaced Palembang, nor do they say who ruled Sanfoqi at any given moment. Historians have also noticed that Chinese records continued to refer to both names, that Palembang is described in the thirteenth century as a dependency of Sanfoqi, and that archaeological evidence at both Palembang and the Batanghari region shows activity over many centuries.

The most careful current view is that the evidence points to growing political and commercial prominence at Jambi in the later eleventh century, within a landscape of several competing centers, but does not settle whether a single court relocated. It is possible that rulers moved, that different rulers claimed authority in different places, or that Chinese records simply reflect changes in which ports were engaging in diplomacy. The question remains unsettled.

The much discussed missions of 1079 and 1082 show how fragile a simple capital-transfer theory can be. Chinese records use terms that have been read as referring to Jambi alone, to Sanfoqi in association with Jambi, or to different participants in a diplomatic arrangement. Scholars who emphasize a rising Jambi center point to the increasing frequency and prominence of these missions. Others question whether the terminology establishes that Palembang had ceased to be politically important. The surviving notices do not record a formal move of a royal residence, an abdication, or the installation of an entirely new administrative capital.

One productive alternative is to treat the evidence as a question about relative leadership among maritime and riverine centers. Jambi may have become the principal participant in a particular diplomatic relationship while Palembang continued to sustain a local economy and cultural institutions. Some ports could recognize one ruler for purposes of exchange and another for military protection, with allegiances changing over time. Because the written record is sparse, this remains a model rather than an established description of every relationship. Its virtue is to make explicit the political mechanisms that a claim of 'moving the capital' might otherwise conceal.

Division III · Section 47.13

Archaeology and Religious Continuity in the Musi and Batanghari River Systems

Archaeology provides an independent check on the written record. In the Palembang region, excavations have found evidence of settlement, boat remains, ceramics, and Buddhist images across a dispersed landscape of riverbank and island sites, rather than a single compact walled city. Chinese ceramics and other imports appear in the later centuries, consistent with continued participation in commerce. Work by scholars such as Pierre-Yves Manguin has emphasized that Palembang was a riverine, spread-out settlement and that its archaeological record does not show sudden abandonment.

On the Batanghari, the vast complex at Muara Jambi contains numerous brick temple remains, canals, and settlement areas spread across several kilometers of riverbank. The temples were Buddhist, and the complex appears to have been active in the period from about the eleventh to the fourteenth centuries, though dating remains debated and different structures are likely to belong to different phases. Finds include imported ceramics and other goods.

Religious connections add further evidence. The Buddhist scholar Atisa (Dipamkara Srijnana) is traditionally said to have traveled to a place called Suvarnadvipa in the early eleventh century to study with a teacher associated with the island world. The identification of that place and its teacher with a specific site is debated, with Sumatran locations including Muara Jambi proposed by some scholars. Whatever the exact location, the tradition indicates that Sumatran Buddhism remained intellectually significant to Asian Buddhists.

Taken together, the archaeological record supports the idea of a prosperous network of river settlements in which leadership may have shifted among centers but religious and commercial life continued.

Physical evidence is especially valuable for investigating settlement continuity, but only when the limits of dating are kept in view. Pottery types, imported ceramics, building materials, and environmental deposits can suggest periods of activity without assigning every object to the reign of a named king. At Muara Jambi, the surviving brick monuments and associated occupation evidence show substantial religious investment in the Batanghari basin. Such material establishes that the region supported communities capable of organizing labor and maintaining religious sites; it does not by itself tell us whether those communities were governed from Palembang, from a court near Jambi, or by more local institutions.

The wider river landscape is as important as the standing monuments. Temples required supplies, transport, and builders, while trade goods moved between upriver settlements and estuarine markets. Archaeologists must distinguish materials deposited by long occupation from those resulting from isolated construction episodes or later reuse. The absence of a destruction layer at one excavated locality cannot establish that no conflict occurred across the region; conversely, evidence of damage does not automatically identify the Chola fleet as its cause. Placing archaeology alongside dated inscriptions and Chinese mission records makes it possible to argue for continuing prosperity without claiming a degree of political certainty the evidence does not support.

Division IV

Regional Competition and New Political Centers

Peninsular ports, Java, and Malayu–Dharmasraya

Division IV · Section 47.14

Kedah, the Malay Peninsula, and the Changing Politics of the Straits

The peninsula was never simply a periphery of Sumatra. Kedah, known in Tamil sources as Kadaram and in Leiden charters as Kataha, appears in the Chola inscriptions as the seat of a king and in the Leiden plates as linked to the Srivijayan ruler's titles. Archaeological remains in the Bujang Valley, including Hindu and Buddhist structures, imported goods, and a Tamil-language inscription of a merchant guild found in a different part of the peninsula at Takua Pa, show a community engaged with South Indian and Chinese commerce over centuries.

Peninsular ports could navigate between several powers. They could trade with Tamil merchants, send goods to China, accept the patronage of Sumatran rulers, or deal with Javanese and Chola power. The Chinese account of 1225 listing dependencies on the peninsula suggests that many were considered connected to Sanfoqi, but the nature of that connection, whether tribute, trade preference, or loose allegiance, is unclear. Other evidence, such as the Chola references to Kadaram in the later eleventh century, shows that these ports were also of interest to outsiders in their own right.

To describe peninsular ports as permanent dependencies of Srivijaya is therefore misleading. They were participants in a shared economic world with their own rulers, merchants, and options. Their relationships with Srivijaya's rulers varied across time and could be cooperative, competitive, or subordinate. After 1025, the peninsula's ports had more reason to look to their own interests, and some, such as Tambralinga in the north, would later have different alignments, topics treated further in later chapters.

Peninsular ports had choices because they were connected to several commercial worlds at once. A harbor facing the Bay of Bengal could receive Tamil merchants and goods from Sri Lanka while also maintaining links to the peninsula's eastern coast and to Chinese shipping. Overland crossings through narrower parts of the peninsula provided routes for some commodities and persons to move between maritime systems. This created opportunities for rulers to negotiate customs arrangements and political relationships with more than one powerful patron. A claim that a port belonged to Sanfoqi must therefore be read as evidence of a relationship, not as automatic proof that its local leadership lacked initiative.

The Chola raid makes this problem particularly visible. Kadaram's prominence in the victory inscriptions indicates that the Kedah region was important to the expedition, but royal praise does not clarify every feature of its administration. Subsequent claims of intervention and restoration likewise point to local political contests that a distant navy could influence without settling permanently. As maritime commerce changed, smaller ports could bargain over access, tribute, and the protection of merchant communities. Their importance in the story lies in those decisions, not merely in the fact that outside empires named them among their supposed dependencies.

Division IV · Section 47.15

Java, Maritime Rivalry, and the Growing Power of East Javanese Kingdoms

While the Malay world was reorganizing, Java was becoming a more prominent actor. After the political shift to East Java described in Chapter 32, the kingdom of Airlangga (r. 1019–1042) and the later kingdom of Kediri developed prosperous agrarian and commercial bases. Chinese authors of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries regarded Java as a rich and important country, and its ports were centers for spices and other goods carried to Chinese and Indian Ocean markets.

For Srivijaya's successors, Java was both partner and rival. Dynastic and religious links across the Java Sea had existed for centuries, but Javanese rulers also had commercial ambitions. The 990s hostilities noted in Chinese records show early competition. By the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, Javanese ships traveled widely, and Javanese rulers had the resources to project influence into Sumatra and the peninsula.

This section concerns the implications for Srivijaya. The more complete story of Singhasari, including Kertanegara's religious and political experimentation, belongs to Chapter 48. For present purposes the important point is that, by the later thirteenth century, a Javanese court possessed both the ambition and the means to intervene directly in the Malay world, and that the Malay rulers of Sumatra had to take it into account.

The relationship with Java was not a single sequence of continuous war. Courts in the islands shared religious forms and participated in overlapping commercial networks, and their political ambitions changed as dynasties rose and fell. An East Javanese ruler could seek recognition, traders, or influence around Sumatra without possessing the ability to govern the island directly. The reports of hostility around 992 demonstrate that competition could become violent, but the absence of a continuous local record makes it dangerous to draw an unbroken line from those hostilities to Kertanegara's intervention nearly three centuries later.

What changed by the later thirteenth century was the strength and orientation of political actors based in eastern Java. Javanese court literature recalled military activity beyond Java, while a dated inscription from Dharmasraya provides contemporary evidence that an East Javanese king could sponsor a conspicuous religious gift in Sumatra. This is a more precise conclusion than assuming that Singhasari 'replaced' Srivijaya. The evidence points to contested relationships and new forms of regional influence. Chapter 48 will examine the internal development of the Javanese court; here its significance is the widening set of political choices confronting Malay rulers.

Division IV · Section 47.16

Srivijaya and the Malay World in the Twelfth and Thirteenth Centuries

For the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, Chinese works remain our main evidence, supplemented by the Tamil inscription at Barus and the later Javanese record. The 1088 Barus inscription shows a South Indian merchant guild operating on the northwestern Sumatran coast, a reminder that commerce had many centers beyond the Musi and Batanghari. Chinese accounts of Sanfoqi in these centuries describe a rich country and a series of dependencies, but they do not give rulers' names or a detailed political history.

What was Sanfoqi in these centuries? Three possibilities are discussed by scholars. It may have been a state with a recognizable ruling house that continued to command allegiance from many ports. It may have been a trading network or confederation of ports linked by common commercial interests and traditions of Srivijayan prestige. Or it may have been a name that the Chinese applied to successive political authorities in the straits, such as rulers at Jambi or Palembang, without regard to continuity. The evidence does not definitively choose among these, and the correct answer may differ for different decades.

Work by So Kee-Long and others on Chinese accounts and shifting trade patterns suggests that Chinese observers adapted their descriptions to follow changing realities, and that new ports and new names appear as trading patterns shifted. This supports the broader reading of the period as one of redistribution, not a collapse.

The Tamil inscription dated 1088 at Lobo Tua near Barus supplies an unusually concrete illustration of what organized commerce meant in the absence of detailed local court histories. The inscription records a South Indian merchant collective making decisions concerning trading obligations and the conduct of its members in a Sumatran port. It demonstrates that corporate trading associations could operate far from the Tamil homeland and regulate aspects of their activity locally. It does not identify Barus as a direct possession of the Chola state or establish its subordination to a particular Sumatran ruler. Merchant presence, royal influence, and political sovereignty are separate questions.

This kind of evidence helps correct the court-centered perspective of diplomatic compilations. Merchants were not simply moved from one port to another by royal command; they assessed security, credit, cargo demand, local partners, and the cost of reaching buyers. The appearance of distinct regional identities in Chinese records may partly reflect the choices of those commercial communities and the authorities that sought their trade. At the same time, the continuing use of Sanfoqi shows that older political labels could retain practical meaning. The history of the period is best understood as a negotiation between inherited prestige and new commercial circumstances rather than a clean replacement of one maritime empire by another.

Division IV · Section 47.17

Singhasari's Pamalayu Expedition of 1275 and the Padang Roco Inscription of 1286

Later Javanese texts, including the fourteenth-century Nagarakretagama and the Pararaton, refer to a Javanese expedition to Malayu, called Pamalayu, in 1275, during the reign of Kertanegara of Singhasari. These are later texts, written decades after the events, and historians treat their details cautiously. Their value is greatly increased by an independent contemporary document: the Padang Roco inscription.

The inscription, dated to 1286, is on the pedestal of a statue of the Buddhist deity Amoghapasa, found in the upper Batanghari region. It records that King Kertanegara ordered the image, with accompanying figures, to be brought from Java and set up at Dharmasraya. It reports that the Malay people there, including their ruler, were delighted. The document thus shows a Javanese king sending a significant religious object to a Malay court and presenting the act as an honor, and it identifies the Malayu ruler as a king in his own right.

What the inscription does not show is Javanese administration of Sumatra. Gifts of sacred images can express alliance, patronage, or claims of superiority, and the text allows more than one interpretation. It is best read as evidence of a political relationship between Singhasari and Malayu, whose precise character, whether of alliance, subordination, or a more ambiguous arrangement, remains debated. The expedition and the statue are, in any case, signs that the Sumatran Malay world had become a field of Javanese diplomacy and ambition, an issue taken up in Chapter 48 and again in Chapter 49.

The original inscription deserves closer attention than an account paraphrasing its supposed political message. A modern edition based on direct examination of the stone identifies the principal language as Old Malay, with a concluding Sanskrit verse; both are written in Kawi script, and the Old Malay contains substantial Old Javanese influence. Its elaborate calendrical formula gives the date corresponding to 22 August 1286. The text reports that a revered image of Amoghapāśa, accompanied by associated figures and precious symbols, was brought from Java to Sumatra and installed at Dharmasraya by command of Kertanegara. It names Javanese officials involved and recognizes the participation of the Malay court. Those details give the document a stronger evidentiary status than a later narrative celebrating a campaign.

Yet even a contemporary inscription is political speech. Its language of religious merit and royal honor represents the relationship in a form acceptable to those who erected it. The joy attributed to the Sumatran participants should not be read as a transcript of their private opinions, just as a diplomatic gift should not automatically be classified as a sign of conquest. The inscription establishes contact, royal sponsorship, and a ceremonial transfer; whether the larger Pamalayu expedition enforced a subordinate relationship remains a separate question requiring additional evidence. A further inscription associated with Ādityavarman in 1347 belongs to the object's later history and must not be confused with the event commemorated in 1286.

Division V

Political Continuity, Historical Interpretation, and Legacy

The meaning of transformation in the Malay maritime world

Division V · Section 47.18

Malayu–Dharmasraya and the Continuing Political Traditions of Sumatra

Malayu is an old name. Chinese records from the seventh century mention a place called Moloyu, and the name appears in the Chola list of conquests as Malaiyur. By the later thirteenth century, the center of a Malayu polity lay at Dharmasraya in the upper Batanghari region, as the Padang Roco inscription shows. Its ruler bore a royal title and presided over a court able to receive a Buddhist image from Java.

It would be tempting to describe Malayu–Dharmasraya as Srivijaya under another name. The evidence does not justify so simple a statement. Malayu was an ancient locality with its own history, and the relationship among Palembang, Jambi, Malayu, and Dharmasraya across the preceding centuries remains a subject of research. What can be said is that Malayu–Dharmasraya represented a continuation of Sumatran Malay royal traditions, including Buddhist patronage, river-based authority, and diplomatic engagement with powers across the sea, and that it occupied a political space once associated with the Srivijayan network.

The political importance of Sumatra's river systems persisted. Gold and forest products from the interior, riverine communication, and the support of upriver communities remained foundations of power. Later developments, including the career of Adityawarman, who left a later inscription on the same Amoghapasa statue in the fourteenth century, show that the area remained a significant political arena, a topic for later chapters.

There is a geographical reason for this continuity. The Batanghari system connected highland areas with the coastal world, making the river corridor important to rulers who sought access to resources, people, and routes of exchange. Religious foundations could also preserve local prestige across changes in political relationships, while the movement of sacred images allowed rulers to articulate alliances in forms understood across the Java Sea. These mechanisms help explain how a Malay royal tradition could remain influential even if the configuration of Srivijayan overlordship had altered substantially.

The difference between continuity and succession is crucial. A later ruler who employed familiar titles, patronized Buddhism, or controlled a Sumatran river system did not necessarily inherit a single continuous bureaucracy from a seventh-century Srivijayan court. Political traditions could be adapted, recombined, and claimed by new lineages. The Padang Roco and later Amoghapāśa inscriptions provide evidence of such continuing political and religious investment, while leaving much of the intervening genealogy obscure. A first-edition narrative should explain that uncertainty rather than impose a seamless dynastic list.

Division V · Section 47.19

Merchants, Port Communities, Buddhism, and the Transformation of the Malay Maritime World

While rulers and capitals shifted, many features of daily and commercial life endured. Merchants from South India, China, Java, and the Malay world continued to trade in aromatic woods, resins, spices, gold, textiles, and ceramics. Tamil guilds, attested at Takua Pa and Barus, and Chinese merchants, increasingly present, joined local traders in port communities that were multilingual and religiously diverse. River communities continued to supply forest goods, and seafaring groups continued to provide pilots and escort. Such ordinary economic relationships, rather than royal inscriptions, were the foundation of the region's prosperity, though they are less visible in surviving sources.

Buddhism likewise persisted. The temples of Muara Jambi, the transmission of Sumatran learning to other parts of Asia, and the dispatch of Buddhist images from Java to Sumatra all show a religious world that crossed political changes. Hindu and Buddhist traditions coexisted in the region, and rulers patronized both.

New elements also appeared. By the later thirteenth century, ports in northern Sumatra were developing distinct identities. Marco Polo, passing through the region around 1292, described several small kingdoms on the northern coast, and a gravestone of 1297 from Pasai records the death of a Muslim ruler. These are evidence of growing Muslim commercial and political communities in northern Sumatra, which Chapters 62 and 63 examine. Their appearance does not mean that Islam had replaced earlier traditions in the Malay world, only that a new network was beginning to shape trade and politics in the straits.

The transformation of the Malay maritime world was thus one of political reconfiguration within continuing commerce and religion.

The port town is best approached as a place where several kinds of work intersected. Ships required pilots, water, timber, food, repair, and protection; traders needed translators, brokers, storage, and trusted partners; goods arriving from the interior had to be assembled and transferred between river craft and seagoing vessels. None of those activities can be reduced to the orders of a single palace. The Barus merchant inscription demonstrates collective decision-making among one group of merchants, while archaeological finds of imported ceramics and local religious structures show that ports and river settlements participated in sustained exchanges. They do not permit a precise reconstruction of every occupation or household.

Religious life was likewise embedded in practical institutions. Monasteries and temple complexes required land, donors, materials, and ongoing care; pilgrims and teachers depended on maritime routes that could carry them between South Asia and the Malay world. Tibetan traditions concerning Atiśa's extended study in Suvarṇadvīpa offer a further perspective on the reputation of the Buddhist intellectual world, though their biographical detail should not be mistaken for a contemporary travel log. The endurance of these networks across political shifts explains why the loss of a particular court's dominance need not entail the disappearance of its cultural influence.

Division V · Section 47.20

Did Srivijaya Decline, Collapse, or Transform? A Historical Synthesis

Historians have offered three broad interpretations of what happened to Srivijaya, each supported by part of the evidence.

The first stresses military decline following the Chola attacks. It points to the capture of a king, the plunder of ports, and the weakening of prestige, and notes that Chinese records later emphasize Jambi rather than Palembang. Its strength is that 1025 was clearly a serious shock. Its weakness is that it relies heavily on the Chola inscriptions and on the assumption that the polity depended on a single center, while the evidence of continued missions, later Chola engagement, and persistent institutions suggests resilience.

The second stresses political decentralization and changing centers of power. It sees the loosely structured network of ports and river polities gradually reorganized around different leaders, with Jambi, Malayu, and peninsular and northern Sumatran ports gaining independence. Its strength is that it fits the fluid, mandala-like character of the original system. Its weakness is that, given the thin evidence, it can be difficult to test.

The third stresses economic transformation resulting from altered trade networks. It points to Song policy, direct Chinese voyages, Tamil guild activity, and the rise of Javanese and other ports, arguing that Srivijaya's intermediary advantage eroded. Its strength is that it explains why change continued after 1025. Its weakness is that trade data are limited and prosperity itself continued.

No single battle, dynasty, or date adequately captures the process. The weight of the evidence supports describing it as a transformation, in which a network of leadership centered in Palembang-related rulers was gradually replaced by a more plural landscape of Malay river polities, peninsular ports, northern Sumatran towns, and increasingly assertive Javanese courts. Whether one calls the end result "decline" depends on what one means by Srivijaya: as a particular political order, it faded; as a Malay maritime world, it adapted.

At the close of the thirteenth century, new centers of influence were emerging: Malayu–Dharmasraya in Sumatra, Muslim ports in the north, and an East Java whose rulers were casting their eyes across the sea. Chapter 48 turns to Singhasari and the expansion of East Java, and Chapter 49 to the rise of Majapahit.

It is worth specifying what would count as stronger proof for each interpretation. A theory of direct political collapse would benefit from closely dated evidence that officials, royal institutions, or settlement systems ceased to function after a particular military attack. A theory of capital relocation would require convincing evidence that the same ruling institution or line moved from one identifiable center to another. A theory of commercial redistribution can be tested by changing assemblages of imported goods and by independent records of ships and merchants using different ports. The available sources offer fragments of these patterns rather than a comprehensive series for every decade.

The historical significance of the case lies partly in this methodological discipline. Srivijaya's legacy should not be measured only by whether a court with that exact name continued to send envoys or command ports. Political names, religious institutions, Malay-language communities, and maritime practices have different histories and different kinds of evidence. The most defensible synthesis recognizes a weakening or restructuring of one configuration of authority alongside continuity in regional commerce, Buddhist patronage, and the capacity of new Malay centers to negotiate their place in the wider world.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the maritime network associated with Srivijaya changed between roughly the tenth and thirteenth centuries. Its central argument is that the period is best understood as a transformation of regional power, not the sudden collapse of a unified empire. Srivijaya was a network of ports, river communities, and alliances held together by negotiated loyalties, ritual obligations, and commercial advantage, and such a system could be rearranged without a single moment of destruction.

The evidence is uneven. Chinese records provide continuity but reflect Chinese interests and a name, Sanfoqi, that may cover changing realities. Chola inscriptions give the only narrative of the 1025 expedition but are royal praise. Leiden copper plates, the Barus inscription, the Padang Roco inscription, and archaeology at Palembang and Muara Jambi complete the picture in part. Historians must therefore distinguish between political decline and changing commercial prominence.

Before 1025, Srivijaya was already operating in a more crowded environment. Javanese rivals, the expanding Chola state, and a Song dynasty promoting maritime trade all altered the conditions under which Srivijayan rulers negotiated. Their diplomacy, including Buddhist patronage at Nagapattinam and a temple dedicated to the Chinese emperor, shows adaptation to these changes.

The Chola naval campaign of 1025 was a major military shock. Chola inscriptions record the capture of the king of Kadaram, plunder, and attacks on numerous ports. They do not record a lasting occupation. The motives for the attack remain debated, and commercial, strategic, and ceremonial explanations may all have played a part.

After 1025, Chinese records continue to register missions from Sanfoqi, and later Chola inscriptions show continued interest in Kadaram. At the same time Jambi increasingly appears as a distinct diplomatic partner, particularly in 1079 and 1082. Whether this reflects the transfer of a capital from Palembang or the rise of competing centers is an open question, and archaeology at both Palembang and Muara Jambi points to continued settlement and Buddhist activity.

Song China's maritime expansion changed the commercial setting. More direct voyages, new ports, and growing demand for Southeast Asian goods redistributed the benefits of trade. Peninsular ports such as Kedah, Javanese ports, and northern Sumatran towns developed their own ties, so that the intermediary advantage once enjoyed by Srivijaya's leaders eroded even as regional commerce flourished.

In the later thirteenth century, East Java under Kertanegara intervened in the Malay world. The Pamalayu expedition of 1275, described in later Javanese texts, and the Padang Roco inscription of 1286 show a Javanese king sending a Buddhist image to Dharmasraya, where a Malay ruler presided over a royal court. The inscription is contemporary evidence of a relationship but not proof of Javanese administration.

The legacy of Srivijaya lay in the persistence of Malay river polities, Buddhist institutions, merchant communities, and diplomatic traditions. New centers, including Malayu–Dharmasraya, northern Sumatran Muslim ports, and Javanese courts, took on greater importance. These developments lead directly to Chapters 48 and 49.

Chapter Reflection

The history of Srivijaya's transformation shows how historians must read the relation between names and realities. The label "Srivijaya" or "Sanfoqi" suggests a single, stable entity; the sources reveal a changing landscape of ports, rivers, rulers, and alliances. Learning to see this difference is a skill that applies far beyond Southeast Asia.

The chapter also shows how dramatic events can be misleading. The Chola expedition of 1025 is vivid and well recorded, but the slower processes of changing trade, shifting centers, and new regional competitors did more to reshape the Malay world. Attending to these slower processes helps avoid explanations that rest on a single battle or a single date.

Finally, the chapter reinforces this textbook's central commitment. Malay rulers, merchants, monks, and river communities were not passive intermediaries. They negotiated with Chola and Song courts, sustained Buddhist institutions, built new centers, and responded to Javanese ambition. What emerged by 1300 was a plural world of Malay river polities, peninsular ports, northern Sumatran towns, and East Javanese courts, the world into which Singhasari and Majapahit, and later the Muslim sultanates, would move.
