The First Toungoo Empire

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

In 1557 King Bayinnaung had a great bell inscribed in Burmese, Mon, and Pali and donated it at the Shwezigon Pagoda at Bagan. Its text proclaims conquests and merit together: it is evidence of how a ruler wished to be seen, not a neutral record of events.

Within about three decades, Toungoo, a modest inland principality on the Sittang River, became the center of the largest political network yet assembled on mainland Southeast Asia. Its rulers took the commercial ports of Lower Burma, reunified the Irrawaddy valley, subdued Shan principalities, received the submission of Lan Na, twice defeated Ayutthaya, and campaigned in the Mekong valley against Lan Xang.

This chapter explains that expansion and its fragility. Its governing argument is that the First Toungoo Empire combined agricultural populations, commercial wealth, military manpower, and networks of subordinate rulers, but much of its authority rested on the personal leadership of the monarch, continuing military success, and negotiated allegiances. These conditions made rapid growth possible and made the structure hard to sustain after 1581.

A central distinction runs through the chapter: Bayinnaung's realm was not a uniformly administered territorial state. Regions experienced very different degrees of royal control, local autonomy, tribute, and military intervention. The chapter also avoids reading modern ethnic or national categories into the sixteenth century; Mon, Bamar, Shan, Tai, and other peoples held varied positions, and loyalty cannot be deduced from language alone.

The evidence is uneven. It includes contemporary inscriptions such as the 1557 bell, later Burmese and Mon chronicles, Ayutthayan and Lan Na chronicles, and European travelers, including Cesare Federici and Ralph Fitch. Each reflects a particular perspective, and army sizes and some dates in chronicles require caution. The chapter follows the scholarly framework of Victor Lieberman on Toungoo's rise, collapse, and restoration and draws on research by Pamaree Surakiat on its wars within a wider mainland political system.

The chapter follows Chapter 83, which concluded the treatment of Mataram and the Dutch in the archipelago, and leads to Chapter 85, which examines the more durable Restored Toungoo state. The First Empire should not be treated as administratively equivalent to that later kingdom.

To understand this expansion, students must distinguish between a monarch's claim to rule and the capacity to act upon that claim. The king at Pegu could receive the homage of a ruler in Chiang Mai or Ayutthaya without administering all of that ruler's territories. Tribute and dynastic marriages acknowledged hierarchy; the dispatch of troops, submission of hostages, and appointment of governors made it more concrete. The chapter compares these relationships rather than presenting the empire as an evenly colored territory on a modern map.

Military history is inseparable from social history. The royal victories praised in chronicles depended on the work of cultivators, boatmen, elephant handlers, builders, artisans, and families whose labor was redirected toward campaigns. Court records tell us much about royal success and little about those who bore the burdens of mobilization. The chapter therefore combines political chronology with analysis of household labor, population movement, economic exchange, and Buddhist patronage.

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  3. Distinguish conquest, tribute, overlordship, and administrative incorporation.
  4. Compare Bayinnaung's relationships with the Shan states, Lan Na, Ayutthaya, and Lan Xang.
  5. Analyze military organization, logistics, and the social costs of mobilization.
  6. Assess the roles of Mon, Bamar, Shan, and Tai communities without equating ethnicity with allegiance.
  7. Interpret the Shwezigon bell inscription, Mon chronicle tradition, and Fitch's account as sources.
  8. Evaluate Buddhist kingship alongside coercive imperial practice.
  9. Explain why the empire fragmented after 1581 using multiple causes.
  10. Contextualize the First Toungoo Empire among other early modern mainland polities.

Historical Context

After the decline of the Pagan kingdom, the Irrawaddy and Sittang valleys were politically divided. Ava dominated the upper Irrawaddy, Hanthawaddy (centered on Pegu) held the commercially rich delta and coast, and Shan principalities controlled upland routes and at times threatened Ava. Smaller centers such as Toungoo, Prome, and Martaban pursued their own strategies. The 1527 Shan capture of Ava weakened the old inland core and sent people and specialists south and east.

This was also a period of commercial growth. Ports linked the Bay of Bengal to Southeast Asian and Asian trade, and the Portuguese had been active in the region since the conquest of Melaka (Chapter 69). Firearms and artillery were available to rulers who could hire or recruit specialists, though they were one factor among many. Toungoo's rise should be read against this setting rather than as a simple product of any single advantage.

Long-standing Buddhist institutions gave ruling houses a shared vocabulary of legitimacy, but they did not create a single political culture. Courtly languages, genealogies, local cults, and arrangements for service varied. The kings of Pegu would need to govern people from many different traditions. This diversity offered access to specialized military and commercial networks while making regional cooperation dependent on negotiation.

Chronicles associated with Burmese, Mon, Shan, and Siamese courts often describe the same campaigns from different perspectives. A ruler depicted as a righteous unifier in one tradition may appear as an ambitious invader in another. The dated Bayinnaung bell inscription supplies an invaluable contemporary comparator, while European travelers emphasize the court and commodities visible to outsiders. Students should distinguish direct testimony from later interpretation and should not convert chronicle army numbers into verified statistics.

Division I — The Origins of Toungoo and the Rise of Tabinshwehti
Section 84.1

The Irrawaddy and Sittang Valleys: Geography and Political Fragmentation

The Irrawaddy valley runs from the dry central zone, with its irrigated Kyaukse district, south to the delta, while the Sittang valley lies to the east between the Pegu hills and the Shan plateau. Toungoo stood on the upper Sittang, a position between Ava, the Shan uplands, and the delta ports of Pegu and Martaban. After Pagan's decline, Ava, Hanthawaddy, and Shan rulers competed for manpower and commerce. The central question is how a principality located between such centers could become the base of an empire.

A geographical survey begins with three interacting zones rather than a single valley. The upper Irrawaddy basin contained the old royal cities and irrigated dry-zone districts, where tanks, canals, and seasonal river flows helped produce dependable grain. Lower Burma joined delta agriculture with access to the Gulf of Martaban and the Bay of Bengal. The Sittang corridor connected these zones while opening eastward toward the Shan hills. Boats could carry bulky cargo more efficiently than pack animals, but rapids, currents, seasonal floods, and contested landing places made river transport a political as well as an environmental question. A ruler needed grain stores and people willing to move it, not merely a map of navigable water. Much of what made Toungoo valuable was its position at the meeting point of routes, without being so exposed as a great coastal capital.

The area traditionally called the central dry zone should not be imagined as a uniform desert. Rainfall varied, and settled cultivation depended on localized water management and on the labor that maintained dams, channels, and embankments. The Kyaukse district had long been associated with agricultural wealth and monarchy. Inland courts sought to command rice, fields, draft animals, and taxable households, but control over them depended on the cooperation of governors and village authorities. Farther south, river distributaries, tidal waters, and rainy-season flooding created different opportunities. Delta settlements could supply food, forest and river products, and access to merchants trading across the northern Indian Ocean. The geographical contrasts mattered because Toungoo's expansion joined districts with different social institutions and different relationships to trade.

Politically the region had been divided since the weakening of the Pagan-centered order. Ava claimed the mantle of upper-valley kingship but confronted rivals in the Shan principalities and the persistent autonomy of subordinate lords. Hanthawaddy developed its own royal traditions and commercially connected Mon-speaking heartland, while Prome and Toungoo could exploit the rivalry between northern and southern centers. Shan rulers were not a single collective government: they led distinct courts, competed with one another, and sometimes intervened in the affairs of Ava. The label 'Shan' summarizes a variety of Tai-speaking peoples and political formations; it cannot explain every alliance. Likewise, not all inhabitants of the delta were Mon or all soldiers serving Ava were Bamar. Political, social, and linguistic boundaries crossed one another.

The geography of neighboring kingdoms would later shape Bayinnaung's reach. To the east, ranges and passes separated the Sittang valley from Lan Na and from the river systems that fed the Chao Phraya basin. Farther east the Mekong and its tributaries connected settlements while hills and forests gave retreating rulers possibilities unavailable on open plains. Towards Arakan, the coastal mountain barrier made supply difficult despite maritime approaches. Each campaign moved troops through an environment with a different seasonal calendar and network of food supplies. Imperial 'extent' on a map therefore tells us less than the locations of garrisons, tribute routes, ports, and subordinate capitals. A force could occupy a court for a season without exercising routine authority over its hinterland.

These geographical features made Toungoo's rise possible but did not determine it. Hanthawaddy was richer, Ava carried older prestige, and upland chiefs commanded effective fighting forces. The question is why a relatively modest court could draw on soldiers, refugees, locally produced food, and the ambitions of its neighbors at the right moment. The answer lies in a conjuncture of military leadership and political fragmentation. As succeeding sections demonstrate, Toungoo converted a frontier advantage into a kingdom, then used the delta's commerce and the upper valley's manpower to build an empire. That combination of inland and coastal resources, rather than either one alone, explains the state's possibilities and its later vulnerabilities.

Section 84.2

Mingyinyo and the Creation of an Independent Toungoo Kingdom

Mingyinyo, ruling from the late fifteenth or early sixteenth century (the exact start is debated), built Toungoo into an autonomous kingdom as Ava weakened. Refugees, military specialists, and regional nobles moving to a relatively secure Sittang valley strengthened its agricultural and military resources. The Shan capture of Ava in 1527 intensified this movement. An autonomous kingdom is not yet an empire; the larger project was the work of his successor.

Mingyinyo's career belongs to a world in which a 'principality' was not simply a town governed from above. Surrounding villages, river crossings, local shrines, and dependent rulers formed a sphere whose actual boundaries changed as alliances shifted. Contemporary evidence for his early years is comparatively sparse; chronicles compiled later give more confident dates and stories than the surviving documentation permits. The relative sequence is clearer than the detail. Toungoo strengthened itself while Ava faced recurrent danger, drawing households and retainers into the upper Sittang region. It offered a degree of refuge from conflicts elsewhere without being entirely insulated from them. Its leaders could use resettlement to increase cultivation and to build a more substantial base of royal servants.

Population movement was a practical source of power. Displaced farmers could clear or restore fields, craftsmen could repair fortifications and weapons, and soldiers who had lost patrons could enter the service of a new court. Yet these gains were neither automatic nor costless. Arrivals required land, food, and arrangements with existing communities. Some people moved voluntarily in search of security and patronage; others followed military leaders under pressure or had little choice. Sources written from a royal standpoint celebrate the acquisition of manpower, while preserving much less about negotiations inside the villages that absorbed it. The growth of Toungoo must therefore be understood as a social transformation as well as an accumulation of soldiers.

Ava's seizure by Shan forces in 1527 accelerated the transformation of regional politics. It weakened an older source of royal prestige and loosened the ties binding officials and service families to the upper-valley court. Some people found a new political home in Toungoo, while others pursued arrangements with Shan rulers or defended their local authority. Mingyinyo did not suddenly inherit Ava's entire kingdom. Rather, a weakened regional hierarchy opened space for independent decisions and for new forms of military coordination. Royal claims to autonomy could expand faster than routine control over distant territory. Later narratives tend to describe the moment as a simple transfer of leadership from Ava to Toungoo, but that formulation conceals years of dispersed and competing ambitions.

Toungoo's strategic location worked in two directions. Its inland setting protected the court from some seaborne attack and allowed access to agricultural settlements, while proximity to lower-valley routes made intervention toward Pegu possible. The Sittang corridor could carry troops and information north and south; it also gave opponents potential approaches. Securing road junctions and river landings mattered as much as constructing palace walls. A modest court could strengthen itself through marriages, gifts, religious patronage, and the assignment of local offices. These practices created a network of mutual obligations, but obligations could be renegotiated when a king died or a stronger rival appeared.

The achievement attributed to Mingyinyo is accordingly best understood as political consolidation. His successor inherited more than a family title: he had a defensible court, a following capable of sustained campaigns, and access to regions where older sovereignty was contested. He did not inherit the wealthy port system or broad alliances that Bayinnaung later commanded. Those were created through subsequent conquest and accommodation. This distinction prevents the chapter from projecting later imperial institutions backward into the kingdom's origins. The First Toungoo Empire was built in stages, and the first stage was the labor of establishing a credible alternative center of power.

Section 84.3

Tabinshwehti's Accession and the Conquest of Hanthawaddy, 1530–1539

Tabinshwehti became ruler around 1530–1531. Hanthawaddy's commercial wealth and ports made it an attractive objective, and campaigns against Pegu and surrounding settlements culminated in the capture of the capital around 1538–1539. Commanders, among them Bayinnaung, and local elites who cooperated were important. The conquest of Pegu, the displacement of its ruling establishment, and the consolidation of control over Lower Burma were related but distinct developments; the court's move toward Pegu changed its political and cultural orientation.

Tabinshwehti's accession altered the scale of Toungoo's ambitions. Whereas his predecessor had concentrated on consolidating the Sittang principality, the young ruler directed sustained effort toward Lower Burma. Hanthawaddy possessed a commercially connected coastline, an established urban court, and populations and institutions that a conqueror might use to finance further warfare. But wealth did not imply military weakness. The rulers of Pegu had their own allies, fortifications, river routes, and legitimacy; attacks that failed could exhaust Toungoo before its army reached the delta. The decision to campaign south therefore involved risks that later success can conceal. It depended on repeated attempts, coordination among commanders, and the progressive weakening or isolation of defending authorities.

The capture of Pegu in 1538–1539 marked a turning point, although it should not be treated as the instantaneous conquest of all Lower Burma. A capital was a concentration of ritual prestige, elite households, stores, and diplomatic connections, but outside settlements could refuse a new ruler's summons. Control of river approaches and military support from neighboring communities had to be secured separately. Chronicle narratives often credit decisive maneuvers to the king or to his celebrated commander Bayinnaung; the precise reconstruction of individual episodes deserves caution when versions disagree. The larger process, however, is clear: an inland challenger gained access to a coastal royal center and its resources, changing the political balance of the entire western mainland.

War also changed the choices available to Hanthawaddy's elites. Some were killed, displaced, or stripped of office; others could retain authority by recognizing the new sovereign or by entering his household. Religious establishments and commercial specialists could seek stability through accommodation. Toungoo could not simply replace every official, merchant, captain, and local intermediary with men from the Sittang valley. Governing the port required knowledge of its commercial languages, customs, revenue practices, and relationships with ships from distant harbors. The conquest therefore initiated bargaining over titles, privileges, and the distribution of offices. What might appear in a royal list as a conquered 'province' was in reality a complex network of people whose cooperation had to be maintained.

The decision to use Pegu as a principal center gave the new dynasty an altered geography. The capital could draw on delta grain, commercial duties, and maritime connections in addition to Toungoo's inland following. A more diverse court could receive Mon-speaking notables, Bamar-speaking commanders, religious figures, and foreign specialists. This was not a modern multicultural program; political inclusion served dynastic power and could coexist with severe coercion. It nevertheless made it possible for the rulers to mobilize resources beyond the territory in which the dynasty had originated. The importance of Bayinnaung at this stage lies in his command and dynastic connections, not in any evidence that one soldier alone created the new state.

Historical interpretation also depends on the records selected. Burmese court traditions tend to narrate expansion through the royal genealogy and the conduct of campaigns. Mon traditions may emphasize the fall and transformation of Hanthawaddy, while European reports focus on trade, warfare, and the opportunities created by changing rulers. None provides an impartial voice of the delta population as a whole. To reconstruct the transformation, students should ask what changed after occupation: who governed the principal towns, which roads remained contested, how merchants secured protection, and how the royal court justified its new authority. The answer is not merely that Pegu 'fell.' Toungoo's acquisition of a maritime capital opened the economic and political foundation for a larger imperial project.

Section 84.4

Martaban, Prome, and the Consolidation of Tabinshwehti's Kingdom

In the early 1540s Toungoo took Martaban, with its maritime connections, and Prome, which commanded the Irrawaddy corridor. These campaigns relied on fortifications and sieges, river transport, artillery, war elephants, and specialists from different communities, including Portuguese and other foreign soldiers. European weapons helped but do not alone explain Toungoo's victories; manpower, supply, and the defections of local elites mattered as much. Conquest changed relations among the court, local governors, merchants, and commanders and turned a regional campaign into a broader political project.

The southward and northward campaigns of the early 1540s demonstrate that victory at Pegu did not secure the neighboring ports and river towns automatically. Martaban was an important outlet toward the Gulf of Martaban and the routes across the Kra region, while Prome occupied a strategic position on the Irrawaddy corridor linking the delta to the interior. Each could support opponents or interrupt supplies if left beyond Toungoo's influence. Martaban's fall is usually dated to 1540–1541, and Prome's to 1542. These operations unfolded in different landscapes and against different coalitions; compressing them into a single campaign obscures the process by which a ruler secured the approaches to his newly acquired capital.

Siege warfare was central to that process. Defenders behind walls could outlast a stronger field army if they maintained access to grain, water, and outside assistance. Attackers therefore sought to cut supply routes, damage defenses, win over allies, and prevent reinforcements from arriving. The siege of Prome illustrates the seasonal problem especially clearly. Chronicles and later accounts describe prolonged operations and efforts to provide food when conditions were unsuitable for rapid assault. The requirement to cultivate, acquire, or transport grain during an extended campaign reveals the dependence of military power on agriculture. A king's ability to summon large numbers of men was of limited value if those men could not remain in the field long enough to force surrender.

Firearms and artillery entered these wars through wider commercial and military networks. Portuguese mercenaries and other foreign specialists served competing rulers, but an emphasis on European gunners alone would distort the record. Southeast Asian armies combined infantry, war elephants, cavalry, locally familiar weapons, boats, and regional knowledge. Weapons were useful when ammunition, powder, skilled crews, and favorable ground were available. A defended river port might be vulnerable to a combination of blockade and artillery that would not succeed in mountainous country. Military results also depended on defections, negotiations, and political calculations by local officers. A technical advantage could magnify an already favorable coalition; it rarely substituted for one.

The conquest of Martaban linked Toungoo more firmly to maritime commerce. Merchants carrying cotton cloth, metals, horses, luxury goods, and other commodities entered through ports connected to the Indian Ocean. Royal authorities could seek customs duties and protect commercial routes, but they depended on brokers and port officials. Prome's capture was different in emphasis: it improved access between the southern court and upstream settlements and reduced the ability of opponents to cut a major line of communication. Together the campaigns joined strategic centers whose value arose from their distinct functions. The dynasty did not become powerful by gathering towns indiscriminately; it sought control of the connections that made further operations possible.

Consequences were unevenly distributed. Former ruling families might be killed, transferred, pensioned, or used within the new court; soldiers could acquire rewards; townspeople and farmers often faced loss of property or labor obligations. Some merchants welcomed a stronger guarantee of transit, while others resented restrictions or new levies. The political order emerging after the sieges was therefore reconstructed through both coercion and negotiation. Students should evaluate these campaigns not only as stages in a victorious itinerary but as experiments in governing conquered places. The question they raise for the whole chapter is how many such settlements a monarch could integrate before the demands of expansion exceeded the institutions available to sustain it.

Division II — Tabinshwehti's Legacy and Bayinnaung's Reconstruction
Section 84.5

Arakan, Ayutthaya, and the Limits of Tabinshwehti's Expansion

Campaigns toward Arakan in the 1540s failed to establish lasting control across the difficult coastal and mountain frontier. The 1548–1549 expedition against Ayutthaya, which crossed the Tenasserim region, met severe logistical difficulties and was unsuccessful. Rapid success in Lower Burma did not guarantee similar outcomes elsewhere, and an unsuccessful invasion is different from permanent subordination. These campaigns introduced the mainland landscape that Bayinnaung later attempted to dominate.

Tabinshwehti's failure to secure his more distant objectives is important because it prevents the earlier victories from appearing inevitable. Arakan, on the western coast, possessed defensible approaches and a maritime political system with its own commercial and diplomatic connections. Routes across the Arakan Mountains were demanding, and the conquest of a town near the frontier did not guarantee the capture of the royal center at Mrauk-U. Even a successful advance could be undermined by difficulty carrying supplies and by the ability of defenders to withdraw or maintain links by sea. Military campaigns in such terrain had to contend with monsoon rainfall, rivers, disease, and the cooperation or hostility of populations along the route. The failure was not necessarily evidence of a weak army; it showed the limits of deploying one army across different landscapes.

The expedition against Ayutthaya in 1548–1549 presented another challenge. Crossing the Tenasserim region required movement through passes and across a coastal hinterland before the attacking forces could threaten the Chao Phraya basin. Siamese defenders possessed a large agrarian kingdom, fortified centers, local knowledge, and their own systems for gathering soldiers and food. Accounts of the campaign survive in Burmese and Siamese narrative traditions whose details diverge. Later celebrated stories of individual heroism should not be mistaken for unproblematic eyewitness reports. The major historical result is clear: the expedition did not establish Toungoo overlordship over Ayutthaya. Its inability to do so contrasted with the more durable political transformations accomplished closer to Pegu.

The wars were nevertheless significant diplomatic events. Rulers who fought one another could negotiate the release of prisoners, recognition of rank, exchange of presents, or trading privileges. A failed invasion might produce limited concessions without resulting in occupation. Commercial access through ports such as Mergui and Tavoy heightened the importance of the isthmus; the region connected inland courts with the Bay of Bengal and the wider Indian Ocean. Students should not assume that all Toungoo campaigning had only one objective. Prestige, protection of allies, control over routes, and the desire for tribute could overlap. Whether a campaign 'succeeded' therefore depends partly on which goals are being measured and which contemporary or later source defines them.

Repeated campaigns also altered relationships within Toungoo itself. Commanders who won recognition could strengthen their position at court; commanders blamed for setbacks could lose favor. Recruiting another army demanded agricultural labor and supplies at home, while men away on campaign could not cultivate fields or meet other obligations. The monarchy had to weigh the gains of renewed intervention against dissatisfaction among households and subordinate leaders. The court's success in Lower Burma made further expansion conceivable but did not remove these costs. Even a king with a prestigious capital could lack the capacity to station troops permanently in every conquered place.

The contrast with Bayinnaung, who later won submission from some of the same kingdoms, should not be turned into a simplistic comparison between a failed ruler and a successful one. Bayinnaung inherited a different political environment and could mobilize resources and alliances accumulated during the earlier period. Meanwhile the neighboring states also changed. The historical problem is to identify the changing balance among military reach, diplomatic concessions, local resistance, and imperial ambitions. In this way Tabinshwehti's western and eastern campaigns introduce the theme that will dominate the second half of the chapter: winning recognition across a broad region was feasible, but sustaining that recognition required institutional capacity that conquest alone could not create.

Section 84.6

Pegu as a Royal Capital: Mon, Bamar, and Multicultural Kingship

Tabinshwehti's entourage included Bamar-speaking leaders, while many people around Pegu were Mon-speaking. The court accommodated Mon court culture through ceremony, appointments, marriages, and patronage of Buddhist institutions. Political allegiance did not follow ethnic identity automatically, and modern scholarship rejects reading the early dynasty as an exclusively Bamar project.

The move to Pegu created a political problem as consequential as the military victories themselves: how could a dynasty with roots in the upper Sittang basin govern a populous lower-valley society possessing its own history of kingship? Hanthawaddy's rulers had cultivated Buddhist legitimacy, court ceremonial, and relationships with prosperous port towns. Many inhabitants spoke Mon, while Toungoo's military circle included Bamar-speaking families and other communities. A conqueror who ignored the forms by which local elites recognized legitimate authority risked making himself dependent on permanent occupation. Tabinshwehti therefore had reason to adapt court practice and to seek recognition as more than the commander of a victorious foreign army.

Royal marriage, clothing, titles, and ceremonial were practical instruments of this adaptation. Scholarship has noted the king's marriage to a Mon princess and adoption of styles associated with the Lower Burma court. These acts should not be romanticized as ethnic harmony or read as a simple 'change of nationality.' They communicated to influential families that older local practices could survive under new dynastic leadership. The new court simultaneously offered positions to Toungoo commanders and service households who had helped win the kingdom. That balance created opportunities for cooperation and new grounds for resentment. The interests of a prosperous merchant, a dispossessed noble, a village headman, and a newly installed commander could be quite different even when they were assigned the same broad ethnic label.

Religious patronage contributed to this process. Mon-speaking communities had influential Theravada Buddhist institutions, and the new king could strengthen his position by supporting monasteries, pagodas, and recognized religious personnel. Such support communicated public respect for the moral order associated with kingship. It did not require the abandonment of Toungoo's earlier patrons. Rather, the ruler could extend the network of institutions for which he claimed responsibility. Religious merit-making and royal generosity were politically visible: inscriptions and donations could create lasting records of a sovereign's participation in the life of the new capital. Yet piety and coercion were not mutually exclusive. Campaigns against rivals continued, and residents of conquered places might experience military exactions while royal gifts were celebrated in court records.

The Mon-language Nidana Ramadhipati-katha is useful precisely because it offers a perspective that does not simply duplicate the later Burmese royal chronicles. It should be treated as a historically transmitted literary source, not as a pristine contemporaneous transcript of court conversations. Scholars have used material associated with it to explore how Mon elites recalled royal relationships and service under the expanding Toungoo state. When later memories praise common service among Mon, Bamar, and Shan retainers, they tell us something about ideals of loyalty and the political language of the court. They do not prove that all communities experienced incorporation in the same way. The date and circumstances of each transmitted passage must be checked before precise quotations are supplied to students.

Pegu's commercial connections multiplied the significance of accommodation. Merchants needed protection for houses, ships, warehouses, religious communities, and contracts, and the court needed their skills to convert customs and commercial traffic into wealth. Royal officers could not generate trade merely by proclaiming authority; they had to negotiate arrangements that allowed traders to operate and merchants to seek remedies when disputes arose. The result was a composite political center in which prestigious Buddhist kingship, maritime commerce, and military command reinforced one another without becoming a uniform social order. This is the setting in which Bayinnaung subsequently reconstructed the kingdom and enlarged it. The dynasty's strength was not that it erased local distinctions, but that it could temporarily coordinate several kinds of power.

Section 84.7

The Assassination of Tabinshwehti in 1550 and the Fragmentation of Royal Authority

Tabinshwehti was assassinated in 1550. Rival claimants, subordinate rulers, and court factions then split the realm; Toungoo, Pegu, Prome, and other dependencies went separate ways. His territorial gains had not yet become a self-sustaining structure, and loyalty depended heavily on the ruler. A kingdom enlarged through personal military leadership could lose cohesion when that leadership disappeared, a theme the chapter returns to in 1581.

Tabinshwehti's assassination in 1550 interrupted that coordination. Later chronicles offer dramatic explanations centering on court intrigues and the ruler's personal conduct, but the evidentiary basis for particular motives is uneven. What can be established more securely is the political result: authority fragmented rapidly among competing claimants and local rulers. The court at Pegu could no longer reliably command the same combination of soldiers, revenue, and obedience that had followed the conquering king. Some commanders defended their own bases; others supported challengers or negotiated with regional elites. The crisis revealed that the victories of the preceding decade had not yet created a succession mechanism capable of preserving the whole network when the monarch disappeared.

The political map following the assassination was not simply a collection of rebels against one legitimate ruler. Each major city had its own court, military following, and recent experience of subordination. Prome controlled an important Irrawaddy corridor, Toungoo was the dynasty's original base, and Pegu carried the prestige and resources of the recently conquered Lower Burma kingdom. Leaders based in each place could plausibly claim the right to determine who should rule locally. The breakdown might therefore appear to contemporaries as an opportunity to restore autonomy, to obtain a new patron, or to promote one's own dynastic ambitions. Describing every opponent of Bayinnaung as an illegitimate insurgent would merely reproduce the victor's perspective.

The speed of fragmentation illustrates the distinction between military service and stable administration. A commander who had mobilized troops for Tabinshwehti might consider his obligation ended with the king's death. Households shifted toward protectors able to guarantee security nearby. Taxes or food shipments that once reached Pegu might be retained by local governors. A frontier ruler who had sent tribute to avoid invasion could delay or stop doing so when the threat disappeared. To enforce the old arrangements, a successor needed not only military victories but also appointments, negotiations, and a credible claim to grant rewards. The royal center's capacity to collect supplies depended on its standing across a web of intermediaries, many of whom now had strong incentives to bargain.

This crisis brought Bayinnaung to the forefront. His close ties to the deceased ruler and his distinguished military service gave him a claim to continue the dynasty's project. But a claim was not the same as possession of the principal towns. Bayinnaung had to assemble a following, secure food and weapons, defeat rivals, and persuade commanders that he could protect them better than competing princes. By doing so he also demonstrated how much political effectiveness still depended on personal reputation. His success cannot be explained solely by inheriting a ready-made state, for that state had largely dissolved. He rebuilt military and administrative relationships while campaigning.

The assassination thus introduces a recurring pattern without proving that the later catastrophe of 1599 was unavoidable. The first fragmentation occurred after the sudden death of a ruler whose conquests were recent. The second developed over nearly two decades following the death of a ruler who had governed a much larger territory and had designated a successor. The scale, timing, and choices were different. The comparison is valuable because it directs attention to what had been institutionalized and what still required a commanding monarch. Bayinnaung could recover the kingdom quickly, but that recovery did not automatically remove the weakness exposed by his predecessor's death.

Section 84.8

Bayinnaung's Reunification Campaigns, 1550–1555

Bayinnaung, a former commander and close associate of Tabinshwehti, defeated rivals, recovering Toungoo in 1551, Pegu in 1552, and Ava in 1555. He appointed subordinate rulers and rebuilt military and fiscal resources. The capture of Ava was especially significant because it joined Upper and Lower Burma. His first achievement was reconstructing a collapsed political system before extending it far beyond earlier limits. The 1557 bell inscription is unusually valuable contemporary evidence for these phases.

Bayinnaung's recovery of the kingdom began as a struggle for viable bases rather than an uninterrupted victorious march. His supporters had to obtain provisions and defend positions while rival leaders maintained authority in former Toungoo territories. The sequence makes clear what mattered strategically. Toungoo, the dynasty's original center, was recovered in January 1551; Prome followed in August of the same year; Pegu was taken in March 1552; and Ava was conquered in 1555. These dated events appear in scholarly readings of the king's Shwezigon bell inscription, which provides a contemporary royal record for several early victories. Converted calendars and differing chronicles still require scrutiny, but the inscription anchors the reconstruction more firmly than a purely retrospective narrative could.

Prome's recovery deserves special emphasis because it is often omitted from compressed timelines. A ruler based in Pegu would need movement through the Irrawaddy corridor to communicate with Upper Burma. Control of Prome helped deny opponents access to the central river route and supplied a potential staging point for northern operations. Its seizure did not assure unrestricted travel, but it changed the balance of military resources. The ordering of campaigns also reveals Bayinnaung's method: he secured a succession of usable bases, then widened his sphere. It was more realistic to build from connected centers than to strike a distant enemy while leaving rival armies behind him. Strategic geography, so important to Toungoo's early rise, remained decisive in reconstructing the kingdom.

Pegu was indispensable because it offered more than a court seat. Delta grain, port revenues, merchant contacts, and access to maritime goods could sustain troops and reward followers. Its recovery in 1552 allowed Bayinnaung to make the Lower Burma capital the center of a renewed monarchy, notwithstanding its conquest by his predecessor. Royal ceremonies and religious gifts reinforced the claim that legitimate kingship had been restored. But Bayinnaung also had to recruit people whose allegiance had changed during the civil war. Some formerly opposed commanders could be pardoned or confirmed in office; others were punished or replaced. This combination of conciliation and coercion recreated a network of service linking the court to its provinces.

Ava's conquest in 1555 was symbolically and materially important. The city had been a principal seat of upper-valley kingship, and its capture linked the maritime court to the older agricultural and political heartland. Bayinnaung could now claim authority across more of the Irrawaddy system and draw on manpower and resources from both regions. Yet the integration of Upper and Lower Burma was not the replacement of local governing practices by a single bureaucracy. Appointed princes and provincial officers retained substantial responsibilities, and their cooperation was essential. The king's growing military power made rebellion hazardous, but the arrangements still needed regular negotiation. The farther Toungoo expanded, the more those relationships would multiply.

The bell inscription should be read as both evidence and performance. It records dated accomplishments with valuable precision, while placing them within a Buddhist king's account of merit, legitimate titles, and royal greatness. This dual character makes it unusually effective in the classroom. Students can compare an inscription's chronology with later chronicles, then ask why a sovereign selected particular events for preservation on a donated religious object. A source may be contemporary and specific without being neutral. In the same way, Bayinnaung's recovery was a genuine political achievement that consolidated an enlarged royal core, but it should not be mistaken for proof that the empire which followed possessed equally durable institutions throughout its vast territory.

The Conquest of the Shan Principalities, 1556–1557

After Ava, Bayinnaung campaigned against Shan principalities such as Hsipaw, Momeik, Mohnyin, and Mogaung. These had commercial, military, and tributary ties linking the Irrawaddy valley with southwestern China. He used campaigns, appointments, hostages, and recognition of local authority, and he promoted religious measures including restrictions on certain ritual sacrifices.

The northern campaigns extended Toungoo authority into a patchwork of principalities whose political histories were not reducible to resistance against Burma. Shan rulers governed towns, agricultural districts, and upland routes connecting the Irrawaddy basin with regions toward Yunnan. Their alliances and rivalries had helped shape Ava's politics for generations. Some were accustomed to paying tribute to more than one powerful neighbor; others asserted substantial independence. Bayinnaung's campaigns after 1555 confronted these distinct courts, not a unified Shan nation. The military submission of Hsipaw, Momeik, Mohnyin, Mogaung, and other centers therefore brought a range of obligations and accommodations, whose stability depended on the position of each ruling family.

A campaign against a principal city could establish royal prestige quickly. More difficult was ensuring that the same ruler would continue to provide troops, tribute, and safe passage once the imperial army departed. Bayinnaung used the familiar repertoire of mainland kingship: recognition of compliant lords, replacement of opponents, hostages from influential families, marriage connections, and expectations of attendance at the superior court. The concrete obligations could differ widely. A border principality might send symbolic offerings and remain internally autonomous, whereas a strategically important district might require a resident royal representative or military garrison. Thus the political map should indicate gradients of power rather than a sharp boundary between 'conquered' and 'unconquered.'

The Shwezigon bell inscription offers a contemporary window into the royal argument for expansion. Donated in 1557, it was inscribed in Burmese, Mon, and Pali, combining records of campaigns, royal titles, dates, and religious donation. Its multiple languages were appropriate to a court ruling constituencies with different literary traditions while invoking the transregional authority of Pali Buddhist culture. Its account of captured places is indispensable for chronology, yet it presents victory as part of a virtuous monarch's work. A village compelled to supply porters or a chief who retained his office under pressure might have interpreted the same events differently. The inscription's political perspective does not reduce its evidentiary value; it defines how that value should be used.

Royal policies in the Shan regions also carried religious consequences. The king is remembered for efforts to suppress particular sacrificial practices and to promote recognized Buddhist forms of worship. Such policies could advertise his claim to protect the Buddha's teaching while extending the reach of central orders. Their effectiveness should not be assumed from the mere presence of a prohibition in royal or later narrative. Upland communities possessed varied rituals, and compliance could be negotiated or incomplete. Nor does the inscription permit a complete reconstruction of local religious life. Students should distinguish the king's intentions, the words of the royal record, and demonstrable changes in the conduct of populations subjected to his authority.

The Shan campaigns enlarged the empire's strategic options. Secure cooperation from northern lords could provide routes, horses, troops, and intelligence for ventures toward Lan Na and beyond. At the same time, each new tributary relationship generated potential costs. When a ruler died, a successor might dispute the terms of submission. When Pegu demanded another army, a local prince could decide that distant war served his interests poorly. Bayinnaung had the power to enforce many obligations personally, but the number of relationships requiring supervision was steadily growing. In that tension between greater resources and greater commitments lay one of the structural contradictions of his successful expansion.

Lan Na and the Conquest of Chiang Mai, 1558

Lan Na lay between the Shan uplands and northern Siam. Bayinnaung's 1558 campaign brought the submission of Chiang Mai and its local rulers, tributary obligations, and military cooperation. Lan Na's own institutions continued, so overlordship should be distinguished from direct administrative incorporation. Expansion here created opportunities for further campaigns but added to the empire's geographic and political complexity.

Lan Na was a long-established Tai-speaking kingdom with its own dynastic and Buddhist traditions, not an administrative district waiting to be annexed. Its principal urban center, Chiang Mai, commanded routes linking the northern mountains, the Shan states, and the Chao Phraya basin. Political changes and disputes within Lan Na created openings for outside intervention, but local rulers and nobles retained considerable influence. Bayinnaung's conquest in 1558 therefore took place within an existing regional contest. The king's success was not simply a case of an army crossing a frontier and replacing every local institution; it involved the submission of a royal center whose elite could negotiate the terms of survival.

The approaches to Chiang Mai posed their own military challenges. Routes through uplands and river valleys demanded knowledge of terrain and arrangements for food and transport. Bayinnaung's prior success among Shan principalities increased his ability to mobilize guides, soldiers, and supplies from the north. That support could help extend military reach, but it also meant the campaign rested on obligations imposed upon newly incorporated rulers. The army's passage should be imagined as a series of coordinated movements among inhabited settlements rather than a single continuous line on a modern map. Control of certain passes and supply depots mattered more immediately than nominal sovereignty over a broad mountainous region.

Following Chiang Mai's submission, the local monarchy and courtly institutions continued in altered form. A subordinate ruler might receive recognition from Pegu while maintaining control over many internal affairs. Tribute, military assistance, and the presentation of dependents or hostages signified a relationship of superiority. The obligations had a ritual dimension: attendance at a sovereign's court was a public acknowledgment of rank. Yet the meaning of a tribute mission could differ between the parties, especially when a subordinate polity sought to preserve its own dignity. Such arrangements were neither modern federalism nor straightforward colonization. They were personalized hierarchies subject to change with military conditions and royal succession.

Lan Na's incorporation also had consequences for neighboring societies. Merchants, monks, royal relatives, and soldiers moved along routes connecting Chiang Mai to other towns. Military contingents could be called away from fields or from local defense to serve campaigns in Siam or Laos. Royal and monastic patronage could establish relationships across Buddhist communities, while differences in ritual practice and legal custom remained. An imperial court could promote common ceremonies without directly supervising village-level worship. It is therefore misleading to judge incorporation only by whether the king at Pegu could issue an order. One must ask whether that order reached local officials and what incentives those officials had to enforce it.

The political value of Lan Na was substantial but ambiguous. It supplied prestige, strategic depth, and an additional reservoir of military resources. Its position allowed Bayinnaung to pursue campaigns against Siam and to pressure upland rivals. But the more the king relied on Lan Na's princes and troops for distant wars, the more he exposed the relationship to strains. A subordinate ruler could comply while the empire seemed invincible and reassess matters after a succession or military defeat. Chiang Mai thus stands as a model of imperial overlordship: significant enough to reshape regional diplomacy, yet insufficiently uniform to be described as the simple extension of Pegu's provincial administration.

Section 84.11

Bayinnaung and Ayutthaya: The Campaigns of 1563–1564 and 1568–1569

The campaign of 1563–1564 produced Ayutthaya's submission and obligations imposed on its court, followed by resistance. The renewed campaign of 1568–1569 led to the conquest of Ayutthaya in 1569, the installation of a ruler acceptable to Bayinnaung, and the removal of royal captives and hostages. Toungoo authority over Siamese institutions remained limited and Ayutthaya continued to exist as a kingdom. The two campaigns must not be merged into one war. Pamaree Surakiat emphasizes that they formed part of an evolving transregional system, more complicated than retrospective narratives of permanent Thai–Burmese hostility.

The wars against Ayutthaya were not simply frontier battles between permanently opposed peoples. Both courts participated in a wider political world of Buddhist kingship, tributary relationships, dynastic rivalry, and commercial competition. Ayutthaya controlled a prosperous Chao Phraya basin and influential maritime routes, while Bayinnaung now commanded the Irrawaddy valley, Pegu's ports, Shan dependencies, and Lan Na. The campaigns of 1563–1564 and 1568–1569 arose partly from the question of which monarch could demand recognition from another. Tribute, elephants, hostages, and diplomatic attendance were political symbols as well as practical resources. Their exchange or refusal could therefore bring a dispute to war even when neither state intended to administer the other's villages directly.

The campaign of 1563–1564 produced Ayutthaya's submission and a settlement that recognized Bayinnaung's superior position. The route toward the Siamese capital required passage through contested settlements and the coordination of numerous contingents. Once superior forces reached the central kingdom, the defenders faced difficult choices about negotiation, fortification, and the protection of their royal family. Later chronicles preserve elaborate accounts of the war, and historians disagree over aspects of sequence and motive. The broad outcome is nonetheless clear: Toungoo established an overlord relationship but did not abolish Ayutthaya's court or its administrative traditions. The settlement carried the seeds of further conflict because the degree of submission acceptable at Pegu was difficult to reconcile with the interests of Siamese nobles and rulers.

The renewed war of 1568–1569 followed resistance to the earlier settlement. Bayinnaung assembled another major expedition, and Ayutthaya fell in 1569 after prolonged pressure. The Toungoo king installed Maha Thammaracha on the throne, a ruler whose political position made him acceptable to the conqueror. Members of the royal family and other persons of high status were taken or held under arrangements that helped enforce the settlement. Such measures were intended to bind the Siamese court to Pegu, not to transform Ayutthaya into a uniform Toungoo province. The distinction matters: local officials still dealt with land, justice, trade, and religious affairs, even as their rulers faced obligations to send troops, gifts, or royal relatives to the superior monarch.

The conflicts had effects beyond the palaces. Large armies moved across productive districts, consumed stored food, damaged cultivation, and compelled labor. Captives might be resettled near the victorious court, where they became part of its manpower resources. Households separated by war seldom left direct written testimony, making their experiences harder to reconstruct than the actions of princes. Elite narratives record royal speeches, elephants, and dramatic encounters because these confirmed the dignity of kingship; peasants and laborers appear more often as anonymous numbers. A careful textbook must therefore ask who provided the elephants' fodder, maintained roads, navigated rivers, or replaced men absent from planting and harvest. Those questions place the celebrated campaigns within the social economy that sustained them.

The two wars also warn against nationalist retrospective interpretation. In later histories they have sometimes been narrated as eternal rivalry between 'Burmese' and 'Thai' nations. Sixteenth-century allegiances were more intricate. Tai-speaking rulers could be aligned with Pegu; Bamar-speaking commanders had political interests of their own; and subordinate royal houses could change sides. Pamaree Surakiat's research situates the wars within the growth of larger political networks on the mainland. The 1569 conquest was a remarkable achievement, but it required continued negotiation with Ayutthaya's elites. After Bayinnaung's death, the limits of the settlement became clear. The lesson is not that the conquest was unreal; it is that visible submission and long-term obedience were different historical outcomes.

Section 84.12

Lan Xang, Setthathirath, and the Limits of Imperial Expansion

Bayinnaung campaigned toward Lan Xang in 1565 and again in the 1570s. King Setthathirath, using the mountainous terrain of the Mekong valley, could withdraw from threatened capitals such as Vientiane and Luang Prabang. Capturing a settlement did not compel sustained obedience, and occupation, disease, disrupted provisioning, and resistance beyond the main towns undermined campaigns. Lan Xang exposed the limits of turning temporary military victory into durable control.

Lan Xang presented a particularly difficult imperial challenge because its political geography allowed rulers to preserve authority without holding a single threatened city. The Mekong valley connected settlements along waterways, but access from the west crossed uplands and forests where supply was demanding and information uncertain. Setthathirath, one of the era's most capable regional rulers, could use movement between centers, alliances, and retreat to deny Bayinnaung a decisive political settlement. The capture of a capital might furnish victory for a chronicle, but the king and his followers could continue resistance elsewhere. An army that advanced far from its bases had to decide whether to pursue mobile opponents, garrison towns, or withdraw before food and seasonal conditions turned against it.

The campaign commonly dated to 1565 illustrates these problems. Toungoo forces entered Lan Xang and could claim important successes, but Setthathirath avoided an enduring surrender that would have made him a dependable subordinate. The position of Vientiane and other centers changed through repeated operations; a single entry in a chronology cannot adequately capture that movement. Later in the 1570s, after Setthathirath's death and subsequent political turmoil, Toungoo forces again advanced. The capture of Vientiane in 1574 and the installation of a client ruler gave Pegu a stronger claim to regional overlordship, yet neither act established uniform control of the Mekong hinterland. The broader problem of succession in Lan Xang interacted with external pressure.

Campaigning here was not a mere contest between a numerically large army and smaller defenders. Food could be hidden or removed, river crossings blocked, and envoys sent with promises that the distant court might not be able to enforce. Forested terrain and disease threatened marching troops, and a victorious general might find that occupying an empty court consumed resources without producing tribute. From the perspective of a subordinate prince, a temporary accommodation could preserve followers until the danger passed. Toungoo's claims to sovereignty were therefore simultaneously genuine diplomatic facts and vulnerable political aspirations. The presence of a royal appointee should be analyzed together with the continued autonomy of local nobles and the feasibility of collecting taxes or soldiers.

Lan Xang's Buddhist institutions formed another dimension of the encounter. Courts could invoke shared Theravada ideals while disagreeing sharply about who should offer homage to whom. Royal merit-making, possession of sacred images, and the patronage of monks were not outside politics; they could strengthen claims to legitimate rule. Nor did shared religion ensure compliance. A ruler who recognized Bayinnaung's religious generosity might still reject tribute demands that threatened his household or allies. Likewise, a local community could maintain its monasteries under changing rulers while experiencing military requisitions. Religious commonality provided a diplomatic language, not a guarantee that the empire could overcome geographical and political resistance.

The comparison with Ayutthaya clarifies the diversity of Toungoo's eastern empire. Ayutthaya had a populous river-basin capital whose capture could be followed by the installation of a cooperating ruler and continuing court institutions. Lan Xang offered more dispersed centers and opportunities for strategic retreat. Both suffered invasion, but their relationships with Pegu were not identical. Students should avoid ranking conquered peoples according to supposedly national qualities of resistance; the differing outcomes can be explained by geography, military timing, dynastic circumstances, and the choices of leaders. Lan Xang demonstrated that the empire could project force across extraordinary distances while finding it much harder to convert a temporary political success into an enduring chain of everyday obedience.

Section 84.13

Armies, War Elephants, Firearms, and the Logistics of Conquest

Armies combined infantry, cavalry, war elephants, firearms and artillery, and river and sea transport. Recruitment drew on royal levies and on contingents that subordinate rulers owed, so conquest enlarged the pool of manpower. Campaigns were limited by seasons, food, pack animals, and corridors. Chronicle army figures are not verified statistics and should not be treated as such.

The Toungoo military system combined armies raised near the royal center with contingents supplied by subordinate rulers. A victorious campaign could increase the available recruiting base because defeated chiefs were then expected to provide men for later expeditions. This created a powerful expansionary mechanism: troops from one region could be used to subdue another, and the prestige of repeated victory encouraged further compliance. Yet service did not mean identical training or equipment. Some men served as infantry, others as mounted soldiers, elephant handlers, boatmen, artillery crews, porters, or guides. Their effectiveness depended on experience and on command relationships that linked regional officers to the royal household. A nominal army total tells little about how many men could fight in a particular place at a particular time.

War elephants had important tactical and ceremonial functions, but their deployment required elaborate support. They needed food, water, handlers, and suitable ground. Dense forest, steep passes, and muddy river crossings could sharply limit their value. Firearms and artillery were increasingly present, partly through access to Portuguese and other specialists and to wider Indian Ocean markets. Those weapons mattered at sieges and in defending fortified approaches, but they required powder, shot, skilled maintenance, and transport animals or boats. A cannon was not an independent explanation for conquest. Combined operations, local intelligence, political defections, and the ability to keep an army supplied were often decisive. The very diversity of Toungoo's forces was a source of flexibility and a challenge to coordination.

Logistical capacity determined the practical radius of power. Campaigners had to calculate how long grain stores would last, where rice could be purchased or seized, and whether river levels would permit transport. A moving army consumed food faster than a smaller civilian household, and armies could destroy the very agricultural resources on which later revenue depended. Coastal routes offered access to maritime cargo but also exposed transport to hostile fleets or weather. Upland roads required porters and pack animals, while monsoon rains could turn roads into impassable tracks. Military leadership was therefore partly the art of securing cooperation from those who held stores, boats, and the keys to passes. These tasks are rarely celebrated in chronicles as vividly as a royal duel, but they explain why some sieges succeeded and some distant invasions failed.

Recruitment had enduring social costs. A farmer ordered to serve could miss planting or harvesting; a village obliged to supply porters might lack people to repair irrigation; and a family losing its main laborer might fall into debt. Political authorities could compensate service with exemptions, privileges, or access to land, but such rewards were distributed unevenly. Captives and transported households were used to bolster the population of favored districts, a policy that could help the royal core while weakening distant places. War thus transferred labor and resources as much as it transferred crowns. Written sources commonly count prisoners or households from the viewpoint of the victorious king, whereas individuals experiencing displacement appear only indirectly.

Military success also changed the incentives of regional lords. While Bayinnaung won campaigns, serving him could offer prestige, protection, and a share in rewards. If expeditions failed or the monarch died, the same lord might question why he should continue providing troops for remote wars. The First Toungoo system relied on repeated mobilization to sustain authority, but increasing demands could erode the manpower available to meet them. Lieberman's analysis of royal service populations and administrative weakness is especially valuable here. The army was not merely the instrument of the empire; its organization was one of the principal ties holding the empire together. When that tie became overburdened, political disintegration could become very rapid.

Section 84.14

Provincial Administration, Tributary Rulers, and Imperial Allegiance

Authority varied by zone: a royal core in Lower and Upper Burma, dependent provinces under appointed governors, subordinate kingdoms with their own rulers, and distant tributaries. The tools were appointments, court attendance, gifts, hostages, marriage alliances, and troop obligations. Local rulers often retained control of revenue, law, and religious institutions. This worked while a strong conqueror could enforce it and was unstable after his death. The more systematic provincial administration of the seventeenth-century Restored Toungoo dynasty (Chapter 85) should not be imposed on Bayinnaung's system.

A map painted in one imperial color hides the central problem of Toungoo government: authority had different meanings at different distances from Pegu. In the royal core, the king could assign officers, mobilize service households, supervise major stores, and intervene directly in disputes. In adjacent districts he depended more heavily on appointed governors and hereditary notables. In subordinate kingdoms, the local court often retained most routine governing functions while accepting the superior king's right to demand homage, military support, or political hostages. These relationships were not static legal categories. They changed with the ruler's military power, succession, distance, and the position of competing neighbors. It would be misleading to apply the language of a modern centralized state without substantial qualification.

The crown's principal instruments were both coercive and conciliatory. Royal families could be tied through marriage; defeated nobles could be confirmed in office; sons or relatives of subordinate rulers might reside at Pegu as guarantees of loyalty or as privileged dependents. A ruler might send presents and attendance at major court ceremonies to acknowledge superiority while negotiating the burdens of military service. An imperial command could be enforced by a neighboring governor or by sending another expedition, but neither solution was cheap. Court patronage therefore offered an alternative to force. A network of obligations worked best when local elites believed compliance brought more security and benefit than rebellion. The relationship could deteriorate rapidly when that calculation changed.

Control over people was often more important than control over abstract land. Officials sought to identify households available for royal service, tax, farming, and transport. The movement of skilled workers, captives, and soldiers after war altered the distribution of labor among regions. Local nobles guarded their own followings because retaining workers strengthened their bargaining position. This tension between royal demands and elite autonomy helps explain why military victory did not produce a single reliable administrative chain. A subordinate prince might present tribute publicly while withholding troops or shifting households out of reach. The king's power to intervene was real, but the costs of constant intervention increased as the empire spread from the delta toward northern hills and the Mekong valley.

Diversity was also institutional. The societies brought under Toungoo authority had their own royal titles, councils, legal customs, and monastic organizations. The court did not have a uniform set of officials capable of replacing all of them. In Lan Na, Ayutthaya, and the Shan states, preserving local political structures could make incorporation feasible; it also meant the empire remained dependent on rulers whose principal interests were local. Religious gifts and the recognition of titles could express a hierarchy acceptable to both parties for a time. Yet the central king and the subordinate ruler might understand the obligations differently. A map showing Pegu's 'rule' should therefore be accompanied by examples of actual tribute, court attendance, or troop levies before claims of effective control are made.

The later Restored Toungoo monarchy developed different and in several respects tighter administrative arrangements. Those developments belong to Chapter 85 and must not be imported backward as if Bayinnaung had already created a durable ministerial and provincial bureaucracy. Lieberman argues that the weakness of controls over regional leaders and the burdens on royal service personnel contributed significantly to the First Empire's collapse. The strength of the sixteenth-century court lay partly in its capacity to enlist preexisting institutions in a broad coalition. Its weakness lay in the absence of mechanisms that could enforce the coalition's obligations without repeated campaigns. This balance helps explain both the astonishing scale of Bayinnaung's success and the rapidity with which that success proved reversible.

Section 84.15

Pegu, the Commercial Economy, and Everyday Life under Imperial Rule

Pegu connected the delta and the Bay of Bengal with Portuguese and Asian networks trading rice, timber, textiles, metals, forest products, horses, and other goods. Customs revenue, port activity, royal patronage, and religious establishments supported the court. Conquest redistributed populations and labor obligations.

Pegu's wealth rested on the conjunction of port commerce, delta agriculture, inland routes, and the prestige of a powerful court. Ships from ports around the Bay of Bengal and farther afield brought textiles, metals, horses, and luxury goods; merchants carried away rice, timber, forest products, and commodities obtained through inland connections. At the river approaches, cargo could require transfer to smaller craft or overland carriers before reaching market districts. Commercial activity consequently supported boat crews, brokers, interpreters, warehouse workers, traders, and local officials. Royal customs and gifts from visiting merchants supplied revenue, but a prosperous harbor depended on enough stability for people to risk sending valuable goods. A court could enrich itself from trade while still being dependent on the decisions of merchants outside its control.

Urban life was differentiated by status, occupation, and neighborhood. Royal and noble households maintained servants and craft specialists, while monks and monasteries attracted donations and visitors. Merchants from different linguistic and religious backgrounds relied on brokers capable of negotiating with authorities and with one another. Markets required food sellers, carriers, cloth dealers, metalworkers, and craft production to meet both ordinary needs and aristocratic demand. The impressive elephants, palaces, and religious buildings described by visitors were visible signs of concentrated wealth, but not all residents shared equally in that wealth. Those who maintained roads, cleaned streets, repaired boats, or worked under compulsion seldom left written accounts. A history of Pegu's prosperity must reconstruct their labor rather than equate the city's splendor with general well-being.

Much of the labor sustaining the capital originated outside it. Rice-producing villages supplied grain; forest districts provided timber and other goods; regional service groups were summoned to build fortifications, carry goods, and serve armies. Women managed households, fields, markets, and religious donations, often under additional pressure when men were away on campaigns. Status varied among free farmers, dependent retainers, debt-bound laborers, captives, and enslaved people, categories that should not be collapsed into a single condition. War could move entire groups of people toward the royal center or its favored settlements. Such transfers strengthened some districts at the cost of others, and their consequences lasted beyond the campaign in which they occurred.

European travel narratives are especially valuable for the commercial landscape, but they demand close reading. Cesare Federici visited the region before Ralph Fitch, whose account describes a short stay in Pegu in late 1586 and early 1587. Fitch reported on the movement of goods, urban form, royal elephants, monastic institutions, and practical features of trade. Michael Charney's edited publication of the account notes passages repeated from Federici, which cannot be treated automatically as Fitch's independent observations. A historian should therefore distinguish an event Fitch dated to his own journey from a generalized description borrowed from an earlier text. Foreign visitors were also interested in profitable commodities and extraordinary scenes; their silence about village obligations or private family life does not demonstrate that such matters were unimportant.

Pegu's later decline makes the social foundations of its wealth especially visible. As repeated campaigns placed escalating demands on service households and agricultural production, men and food were diverted from the activities that supported the court. Merchants could avoid an insecure port, families could flee a devastated district, and officials might keep the revenue they once forwarded to the capital. An empire's capital could retain treasure and splendid buildings even after its surrounding labor system had weakened. This distinction between accumulated wealth and continuing productive capacity helps explain the crisis of the 1590s. Pegu was not simply the stage on which great kings acted; it was a social and economic system whose people made imperial expansion possible and ultimately bore its costs.

Agricultural and Urban Households under Imperial Rule

Farming households supplied rice, labor, and soldiers; port workers, artisans, and merchants served the market; women and children managed households when men were away on campaign. Captives and enslaved people were moved to new settlements and royal service. The surviving evidence mostly reflects the interests of courts and foreign visitors, so the social costs of expansion are the least visible part of the record.

Section 84.16

Theravada Buddhism, Religious Patronage, and Cultural Integration

The monarchy supported Theravada institutions through donations, pagoda building, and monastic patronage, notably at Bagan and the Shwezigon Pagoda. Merit-making helped legitimacy among Bamar, Mon, Shan, Tai, and other communities, and shared Buddhism could support political relationships without removing regional differences or ensuring obedience. Local ritual practices coexisted with Buddhist institutions, and royal efforts to regulate them had limits. Royal piety and coercive expansion existed side by side.

Theravada Buddhism provided the Toungoo kings with a moral language shared, though not uniformly practiced, across many of the territories they sought to dominate. A ruler who repaired pagodas, donated land or bells, and protected monks publicly presented himself as a guardian of the Buddha's teaching. Such activity could reinforce dynastic claims among different communities more effectively than military victory alone. Yet religious institutions were not simply instruments under royal command. Monasteries possessed respected teachers, local benefactors, accumulated property, and traditions of learning. Their relationships with the royal court could produce mutual advantage while remaining vulnerable to interference when donations or regional loyalties were contested. The king's claim to piety was therefore part of a continuing negotiation over authority.

The Shwezigon bell is an unusually tangible example. Bayinnaung donated it in 1557 at the pagoda in Bagan; its inscription combines dated records of conquest with the names, titles, religious intentions, and donations of the monarch. Burmese and Mon textual passages address different literate constituencies, while Pali invokes the authority of Buddhist sacred language. The bell was situated in an established sacred landscape, linking a new conqueror to the prestige of earlier rulers without requiring him to reside in the old capital. The fact that the inscription survives allows historians to test some claims of later chronicles against a contemporary source. But it is still royal self-representation: the people supplying goods and labor for campaigns appear through the monarch's chosen account rather than in their own words.

Bayinnaung also pursued efforts to regulate certain religious and ritual practices in conquered regions. Sources associate his northern campaigns with opposition to particular sacrificial ceremonies and with attempts to promote forms of worship he regarded as proper. Such accounts should be interpreted carefully. Royal proclamations reveal an aspiration to govern conduct, but they do not prove that all communities obeyed. Local beliefs and practices often existed alongside Buddhist monastic institutions. In the Shan principalities, Lan Na, and the Irrawaddy basin, the details of religious affiliation and patronage varied. A claim of shared Theravada identity could support diplomacy while leaving unresolved conflicts over political submission, inheritance, and the allocation of resources.

Merit-making was intimately connected with social and economic organization. Donations of fields, precious objects, labor, or revenue supported the maintenance of religious communities. These grants could alter who exercised control over a village's production or whose labor was available for secular service. Monks might mediate disputes or offer sanctuary and counsel, but evidence for their actions is uneven and often shaped by court or monastic perspectives. Women in royal and nonroyal households participated in religious patronage, though their activity is recorded with far less consistency than that of kings. The religious economy therefore deserves attention as a system involving people and resources, not merely as an accumulation of impressive temples.

An empire that fought repeated wars while proclaiming Buddhist benevolence may seem contradictory to modern readers. The contradiction should be investigated rather than resolved by assuming either cynical hypocrisy or universal acceptance of warfare. Kings could understand conquest as establishing righteous order, securing the conditions for the religion's flourishing, and demonstrating the merit associated with effective rule. Subjects forced to march, supply provisions, or surrender captives might understand the same policy differently. Surviving records preserve both pious claims and evidence of coercion. This distinction is essential to scholarly history: the court's ideals were historically powerful, but they did not determine the experience of every community placed under its authority.

The political usefulness of religious patronage had limits comparable to those of military appointments. A subordinate ruler could receive gifts or support a royal pagoda while preserving local autonomy. A monastery's acknowledgment of the emperor's generosity did not necessarily translate into a reliable troop levy from its neighboring villages. Buddhism offered common symbols capable of sustaining a broad political network, but effective governance still required food, soldiers, communication, and the cooperation of regional officers. In this sense religion was integral to Toungoo political life without being sufficient to solve the basic problem of imperial durability.

Section 84.17

Bayinnaung's Imperial System at Its Greatest Extent

By the later 1570s, control was strongest in Lower and Upper Burma, weaker in the Shan principalities and Lan Na, and weakest in Ayutthaya and Lan Xang. Expeditions reaffirmed authority, and some subordinate rulers cooperated while others complied under pressure. Repeated campaigning, tribute demands, and distance strained the system, so its apparent strength also hid significant vulnerabilities.

By the later 1570s Bayinnaung commanded a political network whose scale was extraordinary for mainland Southeast Asia. Pegu served as its ceremonial and administrative center, with the rich Irrawaddy basin and Lower Burma providing a substantial royal core. The Shan principalities and Lan Na were connected through military submission and differing tributary arrangements. Ayutthaya had a ruler installed after the conquest of 1569, while the Toungoo court claimed authority in relation to Lan Xang after repeated campaigns. The scale of these relationships gave Bayinnaung a reputation extending beyond the regions he governed directly. Yet a list of 'subject kingdoms' is not a measure of daily control. Some rulers sent troops regularly; others provided tribute or acknowledgment while conducting their own domestic affairs.

The royal court's power was visible in its capacity to summon men and resources across large distances. A ruler based in the northern mountains might supply units for campaigns in another valley; cargo could arrive at Pegu through routes maintained by subordinate officers; royal marriages and hostages linked dynastic households. These actions reflected real military and diplomatic influence. They also created a hierarchy in which honor and political obedience mattered greatly. A local court might seek recognition from Bayinnaung because his support strengthened its position against rivals. Allegiance could therefore be both strategic and sincere within the political norms of the day. But those advantages were conditional, and the relationship had to be renewed as rulers died or local circumstances changed.

Much of the apparent stability rested on the balance between reward and obligation. Bayinnaung could redistribute offices and benefits, protect loyal princes, and punish those who refused his summons. Campaign success strengthened the expectation that resistance would be costly. Yet repeated military service diverted labor from agriculture, imposed burdens on households, and sometimes transferred people from one region to another. The king's victory in one theater could create resources for a new war while leaving less capacity for recovery at home. A political network that required frequent demonstrations of supremacy might therefore become more demanding as it enlarged. This is not evidence that collapse was preordained, but it shows why sustaining the structure required exceptional political and logistical management.

Distant tributary kingdoms retained choices unavailable to provinces close to Pegu. Ayutthaya's rulers could assess the prospects of outside assistance and the health of their own agricultural base. Princes in Lan Na or the Shan regions might seek local support or negotiate with neighboring courts. Lan Xang's changing leadership and difficult terrain complicated direct intervention. The throne at Pegu could not easily supervise every succession or dispute. The more the monarchy depended on local dynasties, the greater the risk that an apparently obedient ruler would reassert autonomy when the imperial army was occupied elsewhere. The political geography of the empire was thus an overlapping hierarchy of claims, military routes, and negotiated obligations rather than a fixed expanse of uniform rule.

A responsible historical atlas should display several categories. Core territories where royal officers could exercise frequent influence are different from districts governed by appointed princes; both are different from kingdoms acknowledging sovereignty through tribute while retaining much of their own administration. The exact boundary between categories is not always recoverable, and particular places could move between them. One should also recognize the chronology of expansion: a territory may have submitted in one decade, rebelled in another, and negotiated a new agreement thereafter. A map of maximum extent is useful as a guide to ambition and diplomatic reach, but it is not a photograph of governmental control at any one moment.

Bayinnaung's death would test which relationships had become durable enough to survive a change of monarch. The question is not whether the empire was 'real': contemporaries had ample reason to treat its power seriously. Rather, it is how much of that power was secured by enduring institutions and how much by the extraordinary prestige of a successful conqueror. The answer varies by region. In the royal core, forms of service and administration had greater continuity; in remote tributaries, succession altered the political calculation dramatically. This diversity of control, revealed most clearly after 1581, is the key to understanding both the empire's magnitude and its vulnerability.

Section 84.18

Bayinnaung's Death in 1581 and the Accession of Nanda Bayin

Bayinnaung died in October 1581 and his son Nanda Bayin succeeded. He inherited a network of obligations built on his father's personal authority, with court factions, commanders, and provincial rulers all needing to be managed. His eventual failure should not be taken as proof of personal incompetence: his own decisions must be distinguished from the structural problems he inherited.

Bayinnaung's death in October 1581 opened a succession in which Nanda Bayin possessed a formal dynastic claim but faced a demanding political inheritance. The new monarch had access to Pegu's prestigious court and to institutions developed under his father. He was not a powerless outsider. Yet many major princes and governors had entered their positions through personal relationships with Bayinnaung, and they had accepted obligations under the shadow of his military achievements. A successor needed to renew those arrangements in a setting where regional rulers might now reassess the advantages of obedience. Royal authority could be inherited in title, but the practical capacity to command tribute and soldiers had to be demonstrated repeatedly.

The problem was particularly difficult in a political network joining distant courts with different traditions. The king at Pegu might demand that a ruler in Lan Na attend his ceremonies, provide a relative at court, or send troops for an expedition against Siam. That ruler had to balance the demand against local threats and the expectations of his own following. If several subordinate regions faced difficulty at once, an imperial response in one place might leave another inadequately supervised. The communication delays and seasonal limits of movement made the problem worse. What had functioned as a workable hierarchy under a triumphant monarch could become a set of simultaneous negotiations after the change of ruler.

Nanda Bayin's early choices must be assessed within these constraints rather than judged solely from the final outcome. Demonstrating resolve could discourage rebellion, but aggressive demands also risked encouraging disobedience. Pardoning a defiant prince might preserve manpower while appearing to weaken royal prestige. A major campaign could display authority if successful or damage it further if supplies failed and a subordinate army suffered losses. The tension was structural and political: institutions were insufficient to secure routine compliance across all dependencies, while the ruler's military decisions could deepen the existing problem. Later narratives that blame the king's character alone obscure the pressures created by decades of expansion.

Ayutthaya provided the most consequential challenge. Its ruler had been installed within Bayinnaung's political settlement but retained a court, officials, local military resources, and interests distinct from those of Pegu. The death of the emperor created an opportunity for change, and the accession of Naresuan to an increasingly independent position strengthened the possibility of open defiance. In 1584 Ayutthaya asserted independence from Toungoo overlordship. The ensuing wars were not merely the restoration of an old border: they involved an attempt by Pegu to reestablish a hierarchy of prestige and obligations that had been central to Bayinnaung's empire. Failure would encourage other rulers to calculate that independence was possible.

Succession politics within the core were equally important. Princes serving as governors or military leaders possessed their own retainers and could accumulate influence over districts from which food and soldiers were drawn. The crown required their cooperation but also feared their autonomy. When armies suffered setbacks, the prestige of both the king and his officers could be damaged. Royal punishments and forced service might temporarily compel obedience but undermine the willingness of elites and households to support new campaigns. The resulting struggle was not a simple confrontation between one king and distant rebels; it involved competing priorities within the royal center and among those formally entrusted with implementing the monarch's orders.

Bayinnaung had rebuilt an empire after Tabinshwehti's assassination, showing that rapid fragmentation could be reversed by effective leadership. Nanda Bayin inherited a still larger and more complicated system. His inability to hold it together does not establish that the outcome was predetermined in 1581. Different diplomacy, succession arrangements, or military outcomes might have changed the timing and form of disintegration. Historians cannot prove a counterfactual result, but they can distinguish conditions that made the empire vulnerable from decisions and events that turned vulnerability into breakdown. That distinction should frame the study of the wars and defections of the 1580s and 1590s.

Section 84.19

Revolt, Exhaustion, and the Fall of Pegu, 1584–1599

In 1584 Ayutthaya, associated with Naresuan, asserted independence, and repeated Toungoo attempts to restore overlordship failed. Defections among subordinate rulers, heavy demands for manpower, and war damage to agriculture eroded royal authority, and provincial governments grew more independent. Arakan and Toungoo-based rivals took part in the final crisis; in 1599 Nanda Bayin surrendered and Pegu fell. The imperial system was destroyed, but not every institution or lineage tied to the dynasty disappeared. Lieberman stresses the interaction of manpower problems, defection, and fragmentation.

The assertion of independence by Ayutthaya in 1584 marked an important breach in the regional settlement created by Bayinnaung, but it did not by itself bring down the entire empire. Pegu retained substantial resources and tried repeatedly to reverse the challenge. Major expeditions during the later 1580s and early 1590s failed to restore permanent subordination, however, and each failure carried political consequences beyond the battlefield. Soldiers and porters had been assembled, fed, marched, and sometimes lost; horses and elephants required replacement; and households had forfeited labor needed for cultivation. The monarchy gained no secure tribute or compliant court to offset these costs. Military pressure became both an attempt to restore prestige and a source of further damage to the system expected to support that prestige.

Naresuan and the Ayutthayan court exploited the changing balance of power, while other regions watched the outcome closely. This should not be presented as a purely national war between unified Burmese and Siamese populations. Military alliances crossed linguistic boundaries, and local leaders responded to their own political calculations. As Pegu's forces struggled, governors in distant districts had more reason to retain resources for local defense or to negotiate independently. A provincial ruler asked to supply another expedition could increasingly question whether a weakened imperial center would protect him from his rivals. Such choices accumulated, turning isolated resistance into wider territorial defection.

The consequences for Lower Burma were severe. The repeated demand for service removed cultivators from productive work and damaged the capacity of the royal core to sustain new campaigns. Contemporary and later accounts describe depopulation, flight, hardship, and shrinking obedience. These descriptions should not be converted into precise demographic statistics without evidence. The wider pattern is nonetheless credible: a system built around royal manpower could lose its power as households evaded obligations, moved toward safer patrons, or suffered from war. A capital might still display royal wealth while surrounding fields and service communities failed to provide the resources on which renewed defense depended. This difference between stored treasure and recoverable manpower is central to the history of Pegu's fall.

The final crisis involved other sovereign powers, not only internal rebellion. The kingdom of Arakan, ruled from Mrauk-U, had its own political and commercial ambitions along the Bay of Bengal and the approaches to Lower Burma. Toungoo-based rivals of Nanda Bayin likewise saw an opportunity to claim control or to replace the imperial center. During 1598–1599 the forces of Arakan and Toungoo cooperated in the attack on Pegu, though their interests were not identical. The Arakanese hoped to strengthen their position around coastal trade and the lower river approaches; Toungoo's rulers pursued dynastic and territorial ambitions. These rival aims are illuminated by studies of Arakanese expansion and by Pamaree Surakiat's treatment of the wider regional wars.

Nanda Bayin surrendered in 1599, ending his effective rule at Pegu and bringing the First Toungoo imperial structure to an end. The immediate sequence involved siege, political desertion, and the collapse of the king's remaining support. Royal family members and officers faced shifting choices about flight, submission, or allegiance to rival courts. The transfer or destruction of wealth and buildings was a visible part of the catastrophe, but the more durable consequence was political fragmentation. Ports, garrisons, river crossings, and agricultural districts were now contested by several powers. The fall should not be confused with the disappearance of every line of the dynasty or every royal institution that had developed in the preceding decades.

The broader explanation brings together structure and contingency. Weak central controls over regional rulers made defection possible; sustained demands on royal service households made renewed war increasingly costly. Nanda Bayin's decisions, the persistence of Ayutthayan resistance, regional princes' strategic choices, and Arakan's intervention determined the precise course of the collapse. No single cause explains everything. The empire had once expanded because war supplied fresh resources and alliances; its final wars consumed resources while producing diminishing returns. This reversal is more instructive than a story of individual failure. It establishes the problem that the Restored Toungoo rulers confronted: how to rebuild central power without once again depending on unlimited military expansion.

Section 84.20

The First Toungoo Empire and the Limits of Mainland Imperial Power

Tabinshwehti and Bayinnaung reshaped the mainland through military organization, alliances, tributary ties, and mobilization of resources, but conquest differs from sustainable administration. Individual leadership, economy, geography, military organization, cultural integration, and regional resistance all contributed. Compared with Mataram (Chapter 83), which relied on Java's agrarian interior, Toungoo drew on several valleys and communities; Ayutthaya, Lan Na, and Lan Xang offer other models of premodern power (Chapter 86 treats Ayutthaya further). The collapse created both a crisis and an opening, leading to Chapter 85, where Restored Toungoo rulers built a more durable state.

The First Toungoo Empire was an extraordinary experiment in political coordination across mainland Southeast Asia. From a small inland court the dynasty acquired a rich maritime center, linked the upper and lower Irrawaddy regions, and pressed eastward into the Shan principalities, Lan Na, Ayutthaya, and Lan Xang. The narrative cannot be explained by a single decisive battle or technology. Agricultural manpower, royal patronage, military leadership, port revenue, diplomatic marriages, and the willingness of some local rulers to negotiate all contributed. Each conquest changed the resources that could be mobilized for the next. The achievement was therefore institutional as well as military, even though institutions for governing the outer territories remained uneven and fragile.

Tabinshwehti and Bayinnaung acted in different political settings. The first transformed a kingdom by acquiring Lower Burma, but his death exposed the insecurity of the new structure. The second rebuilt that political core before extending it far beyond the previous frontier. Bayinnaung's military reputation mattered, yet his success also depended on inherited resources, adaptive alliances, and the use of preexisting local governments. He did not erase the political traditions of Chiang Mai, Ayutthaya, or the Shan principalities. He established an overlordship whose practical content varied with geography and the power of subordinate courts. Treating the resulting empire as a modern state with one set of laws, provinces, and borders would obscure how it actually functioned.

The costs of expansion were not restricted to the defeated royal families. Farmers provided grain and labor, crews carried goods, artisans repaired arms, and captives were transferred across territories. Religious foundations benefited from royal merit-making even as neighboring households might bear new service obligations. Ports prospered through wide commercial networks, but political insecurity could also redirect their merchants. The court's claims to Buddhist righteousness and universal political supremacy were historically influential, but they cannot substitute for evidence about ordinary people. The chapter's strongest historical interpretation must hold together imperial achievements and the inequalities on which they rested.

The comparison with Mataram clarifies both parallels and differences. Mataram's inland agricultural base supported Sultan Agung's conquest of Javanese rivals, but its campaigns against Batavia showed the difficulty of projecting force toward a fortified commercial coast. Toungoo drew upon both inland agriculture and a wealthy port at Pegu, then used armies and tributary arrangements across multiple mainland basins. Ayutthaya also commanded an agricultural river valley and coastal trading networks, but it retained sufficient political institutions to recover independence after a period of subordination. Lan Na and Lan Xang illustrate the importance of upland and river-valley geography and the resilience of local dynastic systems. These comparisons reveal multiple ways to mobilize political power without assuming that one model was inevitably superior.

Debate over the collapse requires care. A structural interpretation emphasizes overextension, autonomous provincial rulers, and the escalating burdens of military service. A more contingent interpretation emphasizes Nanda Bayin's campaigns, the opportunities seized by Naresuan and regional rivals, and the alliance that attacked Pegu. The explanations are compatible in some respects but differ in the weight given to institutional limits and individual choices. Evidence for the debate includes inscriptions, royal chronicles, traveler accounts, records of conquest and defection, and the material geography of military routes. Students should identify what each source can support, where its viewpoint is limited, and why the absence of exact troop statistics does not prevent a serious evaluation of strategy and capacity.

The significance of 1599 was not the permanent disappearance of Toungoo sovereignty. The empire's composite network collapsed, but claims to legitimate kingship, institutions of service, Buddhist patronage, and dynastic connections survived in altered form. A later generation constructed a more concentrated state while accepting limits on distant territorial ambitions. The distinction between imperial reach and institutional durability therefore provides a direct transition to Chapter 85, on Restored Toungoo Burma. Chapter 84 has shown how conquest opened a vast political horizon; the next chapter will investigate how rulers learned, with uneven success, to rebuild governing capacity from the fragments of that horizon.

Historical Debate

Why Did the First Toungoo Empire Collapse?

The question: Was collapse mainly structural or the result of decisions after 1581?

Structural interpretation: A system built on personal rule, tribute, and constant war was fragile, with manpower strain and defection (Lieberman's emphasis).
Contingency and regional agency: Succession choices, Nanda Bayin's policies, and the resistance of Ayutthaya and others shaped timing and outcome.

Evidence: Chronicles, inscriptions, and foreign accounts. Disagreement: Mostly about causation and emphasis. Why it matters: It affects whether collapse looks inevitable. Student task: Identify evidence that would help separate the explanations.

Chapter Summary

Toungoo grew from a small Sittang principality into an empire after Ava's weakening and the movement of people and specialists into its territory. Under Tabinshwehti it took Hanthawaddy, Martaban, and Prome and made Pegu a royal center, though expeditions against Arakan and Ayutthaya failed.

His assassination in 1550 showed how dependent the realm was on one leader. Bayinnaung rebuilt it, retaking Toungoo, Pegu, and Ava, then extended authority over the Shan principalities, Lan Na, and Ayutthaya, while Lan Xang resisted.

The empire combined royal core territory, dependent provinces, and tributary kingdoms; allegiance was secured by hostages, marriage, and troop obligations rather than uniform administration. Mon, Bamar, Shan, and Tai communities took varied roles, and loyalty cannot be explained by ethnicity alone.

Expansion drew on agriculture, commerce, and conscripted labor, with heavy costs for ordinary people. Buddhist patronage legitimized the monarchy but coexisted with coercion.

After 1581, personal authority could not be passed on. Revolt, defection, and exhaustion followed, and Pegu fell in 1599. The lesson is the difference between conquest and sustainable government, which Chapter 85 develops.

The river basins and maritime coastline supplied different resources. Toungoo's position in the Sittang valley made military consolidation possible, while the conquest of Pegu gave the dynasty access to commercial revenues and political traditions of Lower Burma. Neither geography nor firearms by themselves explain this change.

Bayinnaung recovered Prome in 1551 between the reconquest of Toungoo and Pegu, a detail securely represented in scholarly work on his 1557 bell inscription. The acquisition of Ava in 1555 then connected the upper and lower Irrawaddy regions more firmly under his court.

The campaigns in Lan Na, Ayutthaya, the Shan territories, and Lan Xang produced different forms of dependence. Local rulers often continued to administer internal affairs, while tribute, dynastic ties, and military service expressed acknowledgment of Pegu's authority. Control was uneven and subject to renegotiation.

Repeated campaigns increased the imperial pool of men and provisions but also damaged the productive foundations of rule. War elephants, firearms, and numerical superiority mattered only when coordinated with dependable supplies and regional cooperation. Coastal trade and agricultural production supported one another.

The succession after Bayinnaung's death opened a prolonged political crisis rather than an instantaneous collapse. Ayutthaya asserted independence in 1584; repeated unsuccessful wars weakened the royal service system, and other rulers eventually defected. The Arakan–Toungoo coalition's assault on Pegu culminated in Nanda Bayin's surrender in 1599.

Chapter Reflection

The First Toungoo Empire shows how far conquest, commerce, and diplomacy could extend a mainland ruler's reach in a few decades, and how little of that reach could be turned into permanent institutions.

It also shows that Southeast Asian states were active builders of political systems, using Buddhist kingship, tribute, and military innovation on their own terms.

Its legacy was a lesson for successors: Chapter 85 examines the more durable state that Restored Toungoo rulers built from its fragments.
