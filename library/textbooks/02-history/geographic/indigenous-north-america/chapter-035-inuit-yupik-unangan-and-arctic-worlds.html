Inuit, Yupik, Unangan, and Arctic Worlds

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

To an outsider looking at a map, the Arctic coast can seem to be the place where inhabited land ends. For the peoples who lived there, it was closer to the opposite: a seasonally changing landscape of ice, open water, shoreline, and animal movement that was known, named, traveled, and hunted across, and in which the sea itself was a road.

From the volcanic Aleutian Islands, across the Bering Sea and the river deltas of western Alaska, along the Chukchi and Beaufort Sea coasts, through the channels and island chains of what is now Arctic Canada, and on to the fjords and ice edges of Greenland, Indigenous peoples built societies organized around the sea, the shore, and the animals that move through them. The distance is many thousands of kilometres. The peoples who lived along it are commonly grouped under the names Unangan, Yupik, Inuit, and their many regional names. This chapter is their story in the centuries before sustained colonial intrusion, told from within the Arctic rather than from the decks of the ships that would later arrive.

The chapter takes up the subjects assigned to it by the textbook's outline: Arctic technologies, maritime economies, migration, community organization, and environmental knowledge. It is deliberately not a repeat of Chapter 11, which examined the deep history of human settlement, mobility, and technological innovation across northern North America. Here that earlier history appears only as it helps explain how later Arctic peoples and the archaeological traditions associated with them took shape. The chapter also stops short of three subjects that belong elsewhere: the Norse presence in Greenland and its encounters with Indigenous peoples, which Chapter 37 examines; the Dene, Cree, Innu, and other Subarctic neighbors treated in Chapter 34; and the continent-wide synthesis of exchange, warfare, language, and communication offered in Chapter 36.

Its central argument is that the Arctic was not a marginal environment to which people merely adapted. Unangan, Yupik, Inuit, and related peoples made the Arctic into a world by turning sea ice, ocean currents, coastlines, animal migrations, and seasonal change into known and traveled landscapes. Their boats, harpoons, clothing, lamps, and houses embodied generations of environmental knowledge. The strongest way to understand Arctic technology is as social knowledge made material: tools and structures that were designed, made, repaired, and used within households, partnerships, and communities, not simply clever gadgets produced by a harsh climate.

A second argument is equally important. There was no single "Arctic culture." Unangan, Yupik, and Inuit peoples shared some technologies and some ecological problems, and their languages belong to a single family. But they spoke distinct languages or branches of that family, organized communities in different ways, depended on different combinations of whales, walrus, seals, fish, caribou, birds, and plants, and developed regional histories that were their own. A bowhead whaling village on the Chukchi Sea, a salmon-fishing community on the Yukon–Kuskokwim Delta, a winter sealing camp on Arctic sea ice, and an Aleutian village on a treeless volcanic island were not variations on one template.

The evidence for this history is varied and uneven. Archaeology provides the long chronological backbone, including house remains, harpoon heads, boat parts, bone and ivory carvings, and food remains. Indigenous oral traditions preserve histories of migration, encounter, hunting, and place. Linguistics traces relationships among languages, and ancient DNA now supplies evidence about population movement, though never about identity alone. Written descriptions of Arctic life are overwhelmingly later, produced by explorers, traders, missionaries, and ethnographers during and after the era of sustained contact. Historians must therefore be careful about the "ethnographic present," the habit of projecting a nineteenth- or early twentieth-century description backward onto centuries when practices may have differed.

The chronological frame is therefore regional. For Unangan communities the period before sustained intrusion ends in the middle of the eighteenth century; for Kalaallit in West Greenland it ends in the early eighteenth; for many Inuit communities of the central Arctic it extends into the nineteenth or twentieth century. This staggered chronology, a theme introduced in Chapter 1, is one reason the chapter treats the eve of colonization as a range of dates rather than a single year. Understanding Arctic peoples as political, economic, and intellectual actors in their own right is essential background for the later chapters on colonial administration, land claims, climate change, and Indigenous internationalism in Volumes IV through X.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Arctic should be understood as a set of inhabited, historically dynamic Indigenous homelands rather than as a marginal or empty environment.
  2. Distinguish among Unangan, Yupik (including Yup'ik, Cup'ik, Cup'ig, and St. Lawrence Island Yupik), Iñupiat, Inuvialuit, Inuit, and Kalaallit peoples, and explain how language families differ from political nations.
  3. Distinguish archaeological labels such as Dorset, Birnirk, and Thule from the names of historically known peoples, and explain the limits of equating them.
  4. Trace the eastward expansion of the Thule archaeological tradition and the formation of the later Inuit world, identifying what is well established and what remains debated.
  5. Describe regional economies from the Aleutians to Greenland, including whaling, walrus and seal hunting, fishing, caribou hunting, bird and plant harvesting, and seasonal movement.
  6. Analyze sea ice, weather, currents, and animal behavior as systems of environmental knowledge, and explain how that knowledge was learned, transmitted, and applied.
  7. Explain how Arctic technologies, including the qayaq, umiak, toggling harpoon, tailored clothing, lamp, and dwelling, embodied social and environmental knowledge, while recognizing regional variation.
  8. Evaluate the organization of households, villages, kinship, leadership, and gender roles without imposing a single political or social model.
  9. Interpret the relationship among animals, people, ritual, and oral tradition in Arctic societies, avoiding the collapse of distinct traditions into a generic "shamanism."
  10. Assess the evidence for trade, diplomacy, and warfare across the Bering Strait, the Arctic coasts, and the Arctic–Subarctic frontier.
  11. Evaluate oral tradition, archaeology, linguistics, genetics, and later written sources as historical evidence for the Arctic, including the problem of the ethnographic present.
  12. Contextualize the regionally staggered end of the pre-intrusion era, and explain why this chapter does not narrate colonial contact.

Historical Context

The Arctic and sub-Arctic coasts discussed in this chapter are not environmentally uniform. The Aleutian chain is a string of treeless, volcanic, storm-swept islands rich in kelp, fish, sea mammals, and seabirds. The Yukon–Kuskokwim Delta of western Alaska is a vast low-lying wetland of rivers, sloughs, and lakes, dense with salmon and waterfowl. The Bering Strait region is a corridor of strong currents and seasonal sea ice linking North America and Asia. Farther north and east, the Chukchi and Beaufort coasts, the river mouth of the Mackenzie, the channels of the Canadian Arctic Archipelago, the shores of Hudson Bay and Hudson Strait, the mountainous coast of northern Labrador, and the long fjord-cut coastlines of Greenland each offer distinct combinations of ice, open water, tundra, driftwood, stone, and animal life. Treating these as one "Arctic" is useful only if the differences remain visible.

Chapter 11 explained that northern North America was settled by peoples who developed sophisticated adaptations to cold-land and sea-ice environments over many thousands of years. Those earlier peoples included the ancestors of the Unangan, who occupied the Aleutian Islands for a very long time, and the so-called Paleo-Inuit traditions of the Canadian Arctic and Greenland, which archaeologists name Pre-Dorset, Dorset, Saqqaq, and related labels. Chapter 12 followed the growth of exchange and ceremonial networks across the continent. This chapter picks up a more specific thread: the emergence in the first millennium CE and early second millennium CE of maritime traditions around the Bering Strait and northern Alaska, and the movement of a descendant tradition, called Thule by archaeologists, across the North American Arctic.

Climate matters here, but only as one variable among several. Between roughly the tenth and the fourteenth centuries CE, a period often called the Medieval Warm Period or Medieval Climate Anomaly, conditions in parts of the Arctic seem to have been favorable to the movements and hunting of large marine mammals. After about 1300, a cooler interval commonly called the Little Ice Age brought changes in sea ice and animal availability that archaeologists associate with regional economic and settlement shifts. These linkages are inferred, vary by region, and are debated; climate created conditions that people interpreted and responded to, and it did not mechanically dictate what they did.

Finally, students must understand the problem of sources. For most Arctic regions before sustained European presence, there are no contemporary written texts. The most detailed accounts of hunting, clothing, houses, ceremonies, and social life were written by outsiders in the eighteenth, nineteenth, and early twentieth centuries, often with limited understanding and sometimes with missionary or administrative purposes. Throughout this chapter such sources are used with caution, and descriptions drawn from them are identified as later accounts. Where archaeology, oral tradition, and later ethnographic description converge, the evidence is strongest; where they do not, the chapter says so.

Section 35.1

The Arctic as an Indigenous World

A history of the Arctic that begins with polar explorers, whalers, and trading companies begins in the wrong place. The coasts and islands examined here were homelands, and the sea ice, in particular, was part of the inhabited landscape. For much of the year, shorefast ice attached to the coast and extended outward as a platform for travel, sealing, and whaling. Beyond it lay moving pack ice, open leads, and polynyas, areas of water that stay open or reopen through winter. In summer the ice retreated, and boats replaced sleds. To describe such places as "empty" or "frozen" mistakes one season for the whole year and an outsider's perspective for the perspective of the people who lived there.

The Arctic's productivity is concentrated rather than evenly spread. Currents, upwellings, ice edges, river mouths, straits, and polynyas gather nutrients, fish, birds, and marine mammals into particular places at particular times, and human communities arranged their movements around those concentrations. The Bering Strait, where Pacific water flows northward into the Arctic, is one such zone; the Pikialasorsuaq between Ellesmere Island and Greenland is another; the lead systems of the Chukchi Sea and the productive waters of Disko Bay in West Greenland are others. Human history in the Arctic is, to a considerable degree, the history of understanding and using these seasonal concentrations.

At the same time, the Arctic was not a single ecological or political zone. Wood was scarce on much of the coast, but driftwood arrived in quantity at some river mouths, such as the Mackenzie. Salmon dominated some western economies and were nearly absent from others. Caribou were crucial in some interior and mainland regions but not on islands without them. These differences underlie the regional treatments in Sections 35.5 through 35.11, and they explain why technologies and economies varied from place to place even among closely related peoples.

Section 35.2

From Ancient Adaptations to Historically Identifiable Peoples

The relationship between archaeological traditions and named peoples is the most delicate problem in the early part of this chapter. Archaeologists identify traditions by recurring patterns in tools, houses, art, and subsistence remains, and they name them after places or characteristic features. These names, such as Dorset, Birnirk, and Thule, are conveniences for organizing evidence. They are not the names that anyone called themselves. Material patterns alone cannot say what language a community spoke, how it governed itself, or what it called itself, and genetic ancestry likewise cannot establish identity. This chapter therefore uses archaeological labels for archaeological patterns and Indigenous names for peoples.

Even so, there are important continuities. The Unangan homeland shows human occupation for roughly eight to nine thousand years, and archaeologists generally treat the island sequence as ancestral to later Unangan communities, while noting that internal changes occurred. In the Bering Strait region, a sequence of maritime traditions, including Old Bering Sea, Ipiutak, and Birnirk, is understood by most archaeologists as part of the background to later Yupik and Iñupiat societies. For the central and eastern Arctic, the earlier Paleo-Inuit traditions were succeeded by Thule-tradition communities that are widely regarded as ancestral to later Inuit. Each of these statements is a scholarly reconstruction, supported to different degrees by different kinds of evidence.

Oral tradition adds a different kind of knowledge. Inuit across the Canadian Arctic and Greenland preserve memories of earlier peoples, among them the Tuniit, and Yupik and Iñupiat communities preserve histories of migration, famine, warfare, and place. These accounts are not archaeological reports, and they are not simply legends either. They record how communities remembered and interpreted their past, they sometimes converge with archaeological findings, and they sometimes raise questions archaeologists had not asked. The Primary Source Window below examines how historians use such accounts responsibly.

Primary Source Window

Inuit Oral Accounts of the Tuniit

Creators: Inuit storytellers and elders, recorded by outside collectors and, later, by community-based projects · Dates of recording: 1880s onward · Type: Oral tradition as recorded in ethnographic and community collections

Context: Across the Canadian Arctic and Greenland, Inuit tradition remembers earlier inhabitants, called Tuniit (also spelled Tunit or Tornit in older sources). Franz Boas recorded Baffin Island accounts during his stay among Inuit in the 1880s and published them in his study of the "Central Eskimo" in the late 1880s. Knud Rasmussen and colleagues on the Fifth Thule Expedition recorded Iglulik and other accounts in the 1920s. Since the late twentieth century, elders' testimony has been collected by Inuit-led and community oral history projects in Nunavut, including work associated with Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit.

Paraphrase of the accounts (not a quotation): Details vary by region and narrator. Many accounts describe the Tuniit as physically powerful, skilled hunters who lived in stone-built houses and who were wary of or avoided the newcomers. Some accounts speak of conflict and of the Tuniit being driven away; others describe peaceful avoidance, withdrawal, or limited contact; and some describe individuals or families being taken into Inuit communities. Stone structures found on the land were, and are, sometimes identified in oral tradition as Tuniit remains.

Analysis: These accounts are consistent in broad outline with archaeological evidence that people of the Dorset tradition occupied much of the eastern Arctic before Thule-tradition migrants arrived, and that Thule-tradition communities came to dominate the region. They also preserve a perspective archaeology cannot supply: how descendants of the newcomers remembered the earlier people, and how those memories were attached to places in the landscape. Ancient DNA studies indicate that Paleo-Inuit (Dorset) and Thule-descended populations were genetically distinct, with only limited admixture, which sits alongside, and does not contradict, accounts of limited interaction.

Limitations: The accounts were recorded centuries after the events they may describe, by outside collectors working through translators in the earlier cases, and each is shaped by a narrator's purposes and by regional storytelling traditions. They compress time and may merge different earlier peoples under one name. Identifying "Tuniit" with "Dorset" is a reasonable scholarly hypothesis but not a proven equation. The perspective of the Dorset-tradition people themselves is not represented in any source.

Questions for students:

  1. What do the accounts say about the Tuniit and about relations between them and the newcomers?
  2. Who recorded these accounts, and under what circumstances? How might that have shaped what survives?
  3. What purposes might these accounts have served within Inuit communities?
  4. What can historians learn from the accounts that archaeology and genetics cannot supply?
  5. What can historians not establish from them alone, and what additional evidence would help?
Section 35.3

Languages, Names, and Arctic Diversity

The languages of the peoples in this chapter belong to a single family, Eskimo–Aleut, which has two principal branches. One branch is Aleut, represented today by Unangam Tunuu, the language of the Unangan. The other is Eskimoan, which includes the Yupik languages and the Inuit languages. Linguists generally estimate that the two branches separated several thousand years ago, a divergence deep enough that Unangam Tunuu and the Yupik and Inuit languages are not mutually intelligible. The Yupik languages include Central Alaskan Yup'ik (with the Cup'ik and Cup'ig varieties), Alutiiq, Central Siberian Yupik (including St. Lawrence Island Yupik), and Naukan. The Inuit languages form a long dialect continuum from northern Alaska across Arctic Canada to Greenland, in which neighboring dialects are intelligible and distant ones increasingly are not.

This linguistic map does not equal a political map. Language relationships describe historical descent, and they do not show who allied with whom, who fought, or who shared an identity. Yupik-speaking communities on the two sides of the Bering Strait maintained close ties with each other and with their Iñupiaq-speaking neighbors, while neighboring communities sometimes came into conflict. Small kin-based groups, villages, and regional bands were the main units of daily political life across much of the Arctic, with larger identities often defined by region, dialect, and relationships rather than by centralized government. The names in the vocabulary box below are therefore partly regional and partly linguistic.

Historical Vocabulary
Inuit, Yupik, Unangan, and "Eskimo"

Definition. Inuit (singular Inuk) means "the people" and is used for the Indigenous peoples of Arctic Canada and Greenland, and in a broader sense for Iñupiat of Alaska as well. In Alaska, Iñupiat and Yup'ik are used as distinct names. Yupik is the broader grouping for a branch of related languages and peoples, while Yup'ik (with an apostrophe) refers to the Central Alaskan Yup'ik language and people. Unangan (Unangax̂) names the Aleut-branch people of the Aleutian Islands.

Historical context. "Eskimo" is an exonym applied to many Arctic peoples by outsiders, and its origin is debated. It is widely regarded as inappropriate in Canada and Greenland, though it remains in limited legal and technical use in Alaska, where Alaska Native peoples include Yupik and Iñupiat communities. It is also inaccurate as an umbrella for the Unangan, who are not Eskimoan speakers. This chapter uses specific names wherever possible.

Why it matters. Using the specific name keeps distinct peoples distinct and respects how communities identify themselves. Names also change with context; the Greenlandic Kalaallit, the Alaskan Iñupiat, and the Canadian Inuvialuit are all Inuit peoples with their own histories.

Potential misunderstanding. Inuit, Yupik, and Unangan are not interchangeable, and they are not subgroups of one another. Treating them as one people erases real linguistic, regional, and historical differences.

Section 35.4

Migration and the Formation of the Later Inuit World

By the early centuries of the first millennium CE, maritime hunting traditions around the Bering Strait and northern Alaska had developed sophisticated ivory and bone technologies and hunted walrus, seals, and, in some cases, whales. Old Bering Sea art and tools on the Chukotkan and Alaskan sides of the strait, the large coastal community at Point Hope associated with the Ipiutak tradition, and the later Birnirk tradition, known from sites near Utqiaġvik, form the foundations of the story. Archaeologists generally argue that the Thule tradition emerged in northern Alaska out of Birnirk and related ancestral traditions during the early-to-mid second millennium CE, with recent high-resolution dating placing some securely defined Thule contexts in the thirteenth century. The tradition combined refined whale-hunting technology, skin boats, toggling harpoons, dog traction, and semi-subterranean winter houses.

From that base, Thule-tradition communities spread east across the Canadian Arctic. During the thirteenth century, Thule-tradition communities expanded rapidly eastward from Alaska through Arctic Canada toward Greenland. Older chronologies placed the movement around AD 1000–1200, while recent high-resolution dating has shifted important Alaskan and western Canadian contexts later within the thirteenth century: revised radiocarbon work argues that key western Canadian Arctic sites were not occupied before the thirteenth century, and a 2025 radiocarbon and tree-ring study of houses at Cape Espenberg, in northwest Alaska, places a clearly Thule-affiliated house in the second half of the thirteenth to early fourteenth century. The precise onset and regional sequence remain under active study. Within a few generations the tradition reached the central Arctic and the Foxe Basin region and then the Atlantic coast and Greenland. Radiocarbon dates, house remains, and toolkits mark the sequence, and the speed of the movement is striking by the standards of the archaeological record. Ancient DNA analyses indicate that the Thule-descended population was closely related across the whole region and genetically distinct from the earlier Dorset population, with only limited admixture. Beyond those points, specific timing, the number of waves, and the extent of interaction with earlier peoples are all debated.

It is essential to avoid two simplifications. First, the Thule tradition is an archaeological pattern and not a single "tribe" that marched east. The movement almost certainly involved many families, many decisions, and many generations, with regional adaptations arising along the way. Second, the Thule are not simply "the Inuit." Inuit peoples of today and of the past several centuries are widely considered the descendants of Thule-tradition communities, and Inuit tradition itself traces its history to this lineage, but a precise identity between a past archaeological tradition and named later peoples cannot be assumed without qualification.

Historical Vocabulary
Dorset, Birnirk, and Thule

Definition. Dorset is the archaeological name for a Paleo-Inuit tradition that flourished across much of the eastern Arctic and Newfoundland for roughly two thousand years. Birnirk names a maritime tradition of northern Alaska and Chukotka in the first and early second millennia CE, with regional timing varying. Thule names the maritime tradition, first defined by archaeologists in the early twentieth century and named after the Greenland trading station near which research was done, that spread across the North American Arctic, with its rapid eastward expansion occurring during the thirteenth century.

Historical context. The names derive from the places where early archaeologists first described the remains. They were not used by the people who made them.

Why it matters. These labels let archaeologists compare sites across thousands of kilometres. They should be read as descriptions of evidence, not as the names of peoples.

Potential misunderstanding. The Thule are sometimes described as "the first Inuit." The statement is shorthand for a more careful claim: Thule-tradition communities are widely considered ancestral to later Inuit, but the label belongs to archaeology and the name "Inuit" to the peoples themselves.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Thule Tradition Spread So Far?

The question: Why did Thule-tradition communities spread across thousands of kilometres of Arctic coastline within a few centuries, and how rapidly did they do so?

Interpretation A — Opportunity from favorable conditions: Some archaeologists connect the expansion to the Medieval Warm Period, arguing that reduced sea ice and changing bowhead whale movements opened new hunting opportunities in the Canadian Arctic, and that communities with specialized whaling technology followed the whales. Later dates for the migration make its relationship to the warm interval of the tenth to thirteenth centuries a matter of continuing discussion.
Interpretation B — Social and economic pull and push: Others emphasize social and economic motives: access to resources, such as meteoritic iron in northwest Greenland, exchange networks, population pressures, and the attraction of lightly inhabited coasts, as well as the cumulative effect of many families making local decisions over several generations.
Interpretation C — Reconsidering the chronology: Still others examine the dating itself. Radiocarbon dates on marine animals can be affected by the marine reservoir effect, which makes samples appear older than they are, and corrections vary by region. Depending on how dates are calibrated, the expansion may appear faster or more gradual.

Evidence: Radiocarbon dates, house and toolkit comparisons, faunal remains, climate proxies, and ancient DNA.

Points of disagreement: Chronology (how fast), causation (why), and scale (one wave or several).

Why it matters: The answers shape how historians understand Arctic peoples as decision-makers: as followers of environmental opportunity, as participants in social and exchange networks, or as both.

Student task: Explain what each interpretation would predict about the pattern of dates across the Canadian Arctic. What additional evidence could help distinguish among them?

Section 35.5

Unangan Worlds of the Aleutian Islands

The Unangan homeland, the Aleutian Islands and the western Alaska Peninsula, is among the oldest continuously documented maritime homelands in North America. The islands are volcanic, treeless, and exposed to storms, but the surrounding waters are rich in fish, shellfish, sea lions, seals, sea otters, seabirds, and occasionally stranded whales. Unangan villages were often located on narrow necks of land between the northern (Bering Sea) and southern (Pacific) shores, giving access to different resources and sea conditions, and near fresh water. Archaeological sequences such as those from Anangula and later middens on Umnak Island show long occupation and gradual changes in houses, tools, and diet.

Unangan houses, described in later accounts and known from excavation, were semi-subterranean structures supported by driftwood and whale bone and covered with sod, with entrances typically through the roof. Russian-derived "barabara" is now the common English word; Unangan terms differ by dialect. Several families might share a large house, which suggests that the household, and sometimes the whole village, was a basic social and economic unit. Unangan watercraft were light, skin-covered kayaks (iqyax̂), among the most seaworthy designs in the Arctic. Their makers used scarce driftwood frames covered with sea lion or seal skin, and hunters used them for sea-mammal hunting, fishing, and travel between islands. Fine grass weaving, bentwood hunting hats, and clothing of bird skin and fur show specialized craft traditions, and burial practices at some sites, including mummification of high-status individuals in caves on Kagamil Island, attest to elaborate beliefs about the dead.

Early written descriptions of Unangan society, made after Russian fur hunters reached the western Aleutians in 1745, describe a ranked society with hereditary or high-status household leaders, commoners, and captives, and they record conflict among villages and island groups. These sources were written by outsiders, often during a period of great disruption, and must be used with care. Population estimates for the pre-intrusion Unangan world vary widely, in the low tens of thousands, and rest on limited evidence. The central point for this chapter is that Unangan history was long, regionally varied, and autonomous, with its own traditions of leadership, craft, and relationship to the sea.

The islands form a long chain with distinct regions, and the evidence suggests that Unangan communities differed accordingly. Later accounts and linguistic work distinguish eastern, central, and western groupings, from the Fox Islands near the Alaska Peninsula through the Andreanof Islands to the Rat and Near Islands in the west, and Unangam Tunuu is spoken in several dialects, including varieties associated with Unalaska, Atka, and Attu. Archaeological sequences such as Anangula, a very early site on a small island off Umnak, and the Chaluka midden at Nikolski on Umnak, whose deposits record several thousand years of occupation, show long local continuity together with changes in house forms, tools, and the emphasis placed on particular animals. Archaeologists generally note that eastern islands had greater access to salmon and other fish, while western communities depended more heavily on sea mammals, seabirds, and shellfish, though such generalizations rest on a limited number of excavated sites.

Technology and craft were highly developed. Hunters in the iqyax̂ used throwing boards, harpoons with detachable heads, and lances, and later accounts describe the use of poison-tipped darts or spears, made with a preparation from aconite, in hunting large whales, a specialized technique probably particularly associated with the eastern Aleutians. Bentwood hunting hats and visors, often decorated and sometimes carved with ivory, protected the hunter's eyes and marked identity and status. Parkas were made of bird skin and of sea mammal skins, and waterproof outer garments were made from sea mammal intestine. Women made finely woven grass mats and baskets whose fineness was remarked on by later visitors and remains a living tradition. These crafts show that Unangan economic life rested on specialist knowledge held by women as well as by men, and that decoration and design carried meaning.

Social organization is more difficult to reconstruct. Houses sheltered multiple related families, and village communities were linked by kinship, marriage, and exchange among islands and with the Alaska Peninsula, where Unangan, Alutiiq, and Yup'ik neighbors met. Later accounts describe village leaders, differences in rank, hereditary privileges, captives taken in raids, and warfare, and archaeological evidence of defensive locations and weapons is consistent with conflict in some periods. Because the earliest detailed written descriptions come from a time of sudden demographic and political disruption, and were written by outsiders, historians use them as hints about earlier arrangements rather than as photographs of them, and they look to excavated houses, burials, and Unangan oral history for corroboration. Language revitalization and cultural programs among Unangax̂ communities today, discussed in Chapters 123 and 146, are part of the continuing process of recovering this history.

Section 35.6

Yupik Worlds of Western Alaska and the Bering Sea

The Central Alaskan Yup'ik and Cup'ik peoples lived on and near the Yukon–Kuskokwim Delta, one of the largest river deltas in the world, and Cup'ig communities lived on Nunivak Island. The delta's lakes, sloughs, and rivers supported salmon, whitefish, and other fish in great numbers, along with migratory waterfowl and their eggs, berries, and, on the coast, seals, beluga, and other marine mammals. Economic life followed a seasonal round: winter villages of sod houses, spring hunting and fishing, summer fish camps where families processed and dried salmon, and fall movements to harvest berries, birds, and caribou. Such a seasonal round was not a set of fixed routes; families adjusted to the condition of the ice, the size of fish runs, and local knowledge.

Later accounts and Yup'ik oral tradition describe a distinctive community architecture. The qasgiq, a large communal men's house, served as a center for work, instruction, council, and ceremony, while women and children lived primarily in household houses, and boys moved into the qasgiq as they grew. The qasgiq was also the setting for elaborate winter ceremonies, including the Bladder Festival, which honored the seals' spirits and returned their bladders to the sea, and the Messenger Feast (Kevgiq), in which one community invited another to a feast with reciprocal exchange of gifts. These ceremonial structures are known mainly from later descriptions, and their antiquity is inferred. Yup'ik concepts of ella, translated as weather, world, awareness, or universe, and yua, the person or spirit within beings and things, shaped relationships with animals and place, as discussed in Section 35.19.

St. Lawrence Island Yupik communities lived on Sivuqaq, a large island in the northern Bering Sea, within sight of Chukotka on clear days. Walrus, seals, and whales supported an economy that left spectacular archaeological records, including the Old Bering Sea and Punuk traditions known for carved ivory. Communities on St. Lawrence Island and in Chukotka, including Siberian Yupik and Naukan Yupik communities, remained in contact through kinship and exchange. Alutiiq (Sugpiaq) peoples of Kodiak Island, the Alaska Peninsula, and the Gulf of Alaska are also Yupik speakers, with ties to neighboring Northwest Coast peoples, whose worlds are examined in Chapter 33. Across all these communities, the Bering Sea was a shared space, and political relationships were typically organized by kinship, village, and region.

Archaeology in southwestern Alaska has been strengthened by community-partnered work. The site of Nunalleq, near the village of Quinhagak on Kuskokwim Bay, was excavated in partnership with the local village corporation and residents, and its waterlogged deposits preserved wooden, bone, grass, and skin objects that rarely survive elsewhere, including carved masks, dolls, and tools. Earlier radiocarbon estimates allowed occupation beginning in the late fourteenth century, but Bayesian modeling places the main dwelling-complex occupation largely in the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, ending amid conflict around AD 1645–1675, and evidence for the violent end of the village has been discussed by the excavators in relation to Yup'ik oral traditions of conflict. Nunalleq illustrates how archaeological evidence and Yup'ik knowledge can illuminate each other, and it shows ceremonial and artistic life, not just subsistence, in a pre-intrusion Yup'ik community.

The seasonal round can be described with more precision from later accounts and from oral history. Spring brought hunting of seals at the edge of the ice and the movement of families to camps for waterfowl, eggs, and, in some places, muskrat and other small game. Summer was dominated by fish camps on rivers and the coast, where salmon were caught, split, and hung to dry on racks, and where many families worked side by side. Autumn was a time for berries, for fall fishing, and, in some places, for caribou and for preparing food stores. Winter brought concentration in villages, where fish and stored foods were supplemented by fishing through the ice and hunting at the coast, and where the qasgiq hosted ceremonies, dances, and the exchange of stories. Households in the delta might move among several sites in a year, and the knowledge of which sites were productive in which conditions was passed on by older people.

On St. Lawrence Island and on the Siberian side, the later accounts of Yupik society describe patrilineal clans or lineages that organized whaling crews, houses, and ceremonial obligations, with captains who directed hunts and held ritual responsibilities. Archaeological evidence from the Punuk period shows bone and ivory slat armor, bows, and defensive sites, which many archaeologists associate with periods of conflict in the Bering Strait region, while the Old Bering Sea and Punuk traditions also produced remarkable carved ivory harpoon heads, winged objects, and decorated tools. These finds, along with oral histories of cross-strait kinship, show that Yupik societies were both artistically accomplished and politically active, and that the Bering Strait connected rather than divided them.

Section 35.7

Iñupiat Worlds of Northern and Northwestern Alaska

The Iñupiat occupy a long arc of coast and interior, from the Seward Peninsula and Bering Strait, across Kotzebue Sound and Point Hope, to the Chukchi and Beaufort Sea coasts of the North Slope, together with inland Brooks Range communities known as Nunamiut ("people of the land"). Their regional economies varied. In coastal communities, bowhead whales, walrus, ringed and bearded seals, and fish were central, while inland Nunamiut relied heavily on caribou, supplemented by fish and mountain sheep and by trade with the coast for sea-mammal oil and skins.

Spring whaling at the ice edge was, and remains, one of the most demanding cooperative undertakings in the Arctic. Crews, each associated with an umialik (often translated as whaling captain or boat owner), traveled across the ice to open leads, waited for bowhead whales passing north, and approached them in skin boats (umiaq). A successful harvest required many people to pull the whale onto the ice, butcher it, and distribute it according to customary rules, and it was followed by feasting and ceremony. Archaeological evidence shows large, long-lived villages of whale-bone and sod houses at Point Hope and near Utqiaġvik, and whaling in northwestern Alaska is evidenced over a very long span, with debate about how early it became a major economic basis.

Trade and travel connected Iñupiat communities with each other, with Siberian communities across the Bering Strait, and with interior peoples. Later accounts describe summer gatherings, including a major fair at Sisualik on Kotzebue Sound, where coastal and inland peoples exchanged sea-mammal products, hides, tools, and other goods, with goods from Siberia moving through Bering Strait intermediaries. The antiquity of specific fairs is uncertain, but their scale in the historical period points to older networks. Conflicts also occurred, including between Iñupiat groups and neighboring peoples, and oral histories remember both alliance and warfare. Chapter 36 places these networks in their continental context.

The deep archaeological record of northwestern Alaska is among the richest in the Arctic. At Point Hope, the Ipiutak tradition, dating broadly to the first millennium CE, left a large settlement with many house depressions and a cemetery famous for finely carved ivory objects, including elaborate pieces placed with the dead. Ipiutak sites are also notable for the apparent absence of the large-whale hunting equipment that characterizes later periods, a point archaeologists have discussed, and this reminds students that whaling was not simply a constant feature of the region. Later Birnirk and Thule-tradition communities developed the whaling economy, and at Point Hope and Utqiaġvik, large villages of whale-bone and sod houses from the thirteenth century onward show a dense population organized around the spring whale hunt.

The social organization of whaling communities is better known from later oral history and ethnography than from archaeology. Whaling crews were commonly linked by kinship and led by an umialik, whose household owned the boat and bore much of the cost of equipping the crew; success brought prestige, and the umialik was expected to host feasts and to distribute food widely. Men's houses (qargi) served as gathering places for crews and as centers for ceremony and instruction, and exchanges of gifts among communities were part of festivals such as the Messenger Feast (Kivgiq), a tradition that has been revived in modern times. Women were deeply involved in whaling through preparation of equipment and clothing, through the care of crew and of the stored harvest, and through ritual roles. The spring whaling festival now celebrated in several communities shows the continuity of the practice, but students should not assume that every detail of modern practice reproduces earlier forms.

Beyond the whaling villages, the Iñupiat world included a range of regional economies. On the Seward Peninsula and Bering Strait, communities such as those on the Diomede Islands and King Island hunted walrus and seals and served as intermediaries in cross-strait exchange, and later accounts describe houses on King Island built on stilts against steep slopes. On Kotzebue Sound and the Kobuk and Noatak rivers, communities combined seal and beluga hunting with salmon, whitefish, and caribou. In the Brooks Range, Nunamiut hunters followed caribou across the mountains and tundra, using drive lines, bows, and cooperative hunting, and they exchanged skins and meat for sea-mammal oil and coastal goods. These communities were linked by kinship and trade into a regional system, and relationships between coast and interior shaped both economic life and political alliance.

Section 35.8

Inuvialuit and the Western Canadian Arctic

The Inuvialuit homeland centers on the Mackenzie Delta and the Beaufort Sea coast and extends east and north across the western Canadian Arctic. The Mackenzie River carries enormous quantities of driftwood from the interior forest to the delta and coast, a resource unusual in the Arctic. Inuvialuit communities used timber for house frames and other construction, and later accounts describe large houses. Historical Inuvialuit groups, sometimes collectively called Siglit in the scholarly literature, included communities such as the Kitigaaryuit, associated with the large site of Kittigazuit.

Summer brought beluga whales into the shallow waters of the delta, and Kittigazuit was among the sites where large numbers of people gathered for the hunt, with families converging from different parts of the region. Bowhead whales, seals, fish such as whitefish and char, geese, and, on the Yukon North Slope and inland areas, caribou all contributed to a mixed seasonal economy. After the summer gathering, families dispersed to fishing, hunting, and trapping places, and winter brought different groupings. This pattern of seasonal aggregation and dispersal is common in the Arctic, and it shaped community life by alternating large gatherings with small household-based winters.

Inuvialuit communities were connected by kinship and trade with Iñupiat communities to the west and with Gwich'in and other Dene peoples to the south, whose histories are treated in Chapter 34. Relationships included exchange, intermarriage, and conflict; both Inuvialuit and Gwich'in oral traditions preserve memories of warfare as well as trade. The eve of sustained outside contact came relatively late for the Mackenzie Delta, with the first European contacts in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, and the later history of this region is taken up in Chapters 100, 115, and 116.

The archaeology of the Mackenzie Delta has been reconsidered in recent decades. Revised radiocarbon dating of sites in the western Canadian Arctic has led some archaeologists to argue that key Thule-tradition sites there were not occupied before the thirteenth century, which bears on the wider chronology of the eastward migration discussed in Section 35.4. Large settlement sites in the delta region, including Kittigazuit and Kuukpak, show timber-framed houses and extensive use of driftwood, and later accounts describe substantial dwellings and communal houses used for gatherings, dances, and feasts. Qikiqtaruk (Herschel Island) and other places along the Yukon coast were also important in Inuvialuit tradition.

The beluga hunt is among the best-known Inuvialuit practices. In summer, belugas entered the shallow waters of the delta, and hunters in kayaks and other boats worked to drive and take them in large numbers, with the harvest shared among the families who had gathered. The hunt brought together people from many parts of the region, and it was an occasion for exchange, for ceremony, for arranging marriages, and for the renewal of friendships. Later accounts mention influential leaders at such gatherings, though their authority depended on respect and skill rather than formal office. After the summer, families dispersed to fish, hunt caribou, and prepare for winter, and the large aggregations of summer gave way to smaller household-based groups.

Relations with neighbors were varied. Inuvialuit traded with Iñupiat communities to the west and with Copper Inuit to the east, and with Gwich'in and other Dene peoples to the south, exchanging sea-mammal oil, skins, and other coastal goods for caribou products, wood and other interior goods. Oral traditions among both Inuvialuit and Gwich'in recall periods of peaceful exchange and periods of conflict, and these accounts have been analyzed by scholars working with elders in both communities. As in other parts of the Arctic, the evidence for conflict comes largely from oral tradition and later records, and its scale and frequency are matters of continuing discussion.

Section 35.9

Inuit Worlds of the Central Arctic

The central Arctic, comprising much of what is now Nunavut and the adjoining mainland coast, was home to a number of regional Inuit groups known in the historical literature by names such as Copper Inuit, Netsilik, Iglulik, and the inland Caribou Inuit. These names reflect regional identities documented chiefly by later explorers and ethnographers, and communities themselves used and use their own terms. The environment ranged from the sea-ice-covered channels of the archipelago to the interior tundra of the mainland, and economies varied accordingly: sea-mammal hunting dominated on many coasts and islands, while caribou and freshwater and anadromous fish were central for inland groups and in summer for coastal families.

Winter sealing on the sea ice is one of the best-documented central Arctic practices. Hunters located seal breathing holes (aglu) in the ice and waited, often for long periods, to harpoon seals when they surfaced to breathe. Families congregated in winter camps on the ice, with snow houses (iglu, a word that simply means "house" and was applied to many kinds of dwellings) built of snow blocks as seasonal housing in many central Arctic and some Baffin and Greenland communities. In summer, families dispersed to inland and coastal places where they hunted caribou, fished for Arctic char at stone weirs (saputit), and gathered birds and eggs and plant foods. Caribou hunting included cooperative drives and crossings of rivers and lakes, using stone markers and lines, among them inuksuit, to direct animals and to mark routes. Copper Inuit used native copper from the Coppermine region for knives and harpoon heads and traded copper objects to neighbors.

Mobility, rather than permanent settlement, was the organizing principle. Families moved by sled, boat, and on foot, following seasonal opportunity, and they relied on kin and partners in other camps for information, assistance, and exchange. Because resources fluctuated, central Arctic communities developed practices of sharing, storage, and cooperation, and oral tradition and later ethnography also record periods of scarcity and hardship. Historians must avoid the pitfall of describing these communities solely in terms of risk, since the central Arctic was a homeland whose people had centuries of experience in managing variability. The next feature shows how historians connect environmental change and social reorganization.

The regional names used for central Arctic Inuit refer to differing environments and economies. Copper Inuit lived around Coronation Gulf and Victoria Island and had access to native copper; Netsilik, whose name is often glossed as people of the seal, lived on the Boothia Peninsula and King William Island and relied heavily on seals taken at breathing holes and on fishing; Iglulik lived on the Melville Peninsula and around Foxe Basin and hunted walrus, seals, and whales; and Caribou Inuit lived in the interior barren grounds west of Hudson Bay and depended on caribou, fish, and little sea-mammal hunting. Peoples of the Back River and other mainland areas combined caribou and char fishing, while Baffin Island communities hunted sea mammals along the coast. These differences show that the "central Arctic" is a convenient but not unitary term.

Fishing and caribou hunting required their own technologies and knowledge. Fish were taken with the three-pronged leister (kakivak), with nets, and at stone weirs, and caribou were taken with bows, lances, and drives, often at water crossings where hunters in kayaks could approach animals swimming in lakes and rivers. A large communal snow house, the qaggiq, is described in later accounts from Iglulik and neighboring regions as a place for gatherings, games, and ceremonies. Whale-bone houses at Thule-tradition sites in the High Arctic show that earlier communities there depended on whales, and the later abandonment or reduction of such settlements, in association with the cooling discussed below, is a major subject of archaeological work.

Evidence for the central Arctic comes from archaeology, from the extensive ethnographic work done by members of the Fifth Thule Expedition and others in the early twentieth century, and from oral histories collected by Inuit organizations and researchers. Because the best-known descriptions date from the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, a period of change, historians cannot assume that they reproduce earlier conditions. Many Inuit elders' accounts emphasize the extent of travel and knowledge across the region, and the oral record preserves information about specific places, families, and events that archaeology cannot recover.

Cause and Effect

Cooling Seas and Reorganized Arctic Economies, c. 1300–1700

Long-term causes

The Thule expansion had established whale-hunting communities across the Arctic, with technologies and household structures suited to the conditions of the Medieval Warm Period and to access to large marine mammals.

Short-term causes and context

From about 1300, cooler conditions associated with the Little Ice Age brought changes in sea ice, snow, and animal distribution that varied across regions and are known from ice cores, other climate proxies, and the archaeological record.

Immediate effects

Many archaeologists find that in parts of the Canadian Arctic, communities left some high-latitude sites, relied less on large whale hunts, and increasingly diversified into seals, caribou, and fish, with smaller and more mobile social groupings in some areas.

Long-term consequences

The regional groups, house types, and seasonal rounds described in later accounts of the central Arctic, including snow-house winter camps, are widely understood as products of this reorganization, though the dating of some practices remains uncertain.

Historical complexity: Climate did not determine what people did. Social networks, local knowledge, population movements, exchange, and decisions by families and communities shaped how each region responded, and archaeologists disagree about how much of the change was environmental and how much was social. The evidence also varies: snow houses, for example, leave little archaeological trace, so their antiquity is hard to establish.

Section 35.10

Nunavik and Nunatsiavut: The Eastern Canadian Arctic

The Inuit of Nunavik (northern Quebec), the Hudson Strait and Ungava Bay region, and Nunatsiavut (northern Labrador) lived along some of the longest Atlantic-facing coasts of the Arctic. Archaeological evidence indicates that Thule-tradition communities reached the Labrador coast after the thirteenth-century eastward migration and by the fifteenth century at the latest, though precise dates vary by site and are discussed by archaeologists. The region's economy combined sealing (ringed, harp, and bearded seals), walrus in the north, whales, fishing, caribou hunting, and the harvesting of birds and plants, with seasonal shifts between coastal winter sites and summer camps.

The Torngat Mountains and Labrador's rugged coast also preserve a long human presence that predates the Inuit, including the use of distinctive stone such as Ramah chert, whose quarrying and distribution over thousands of years show how places can carry layered histories. Later Inuit communities moved into this landscape and into relations with their neighbors to the south, notably the Innu, whose homelands and histories are treated in Chapter 34. Oral traditions on both sides recall periods of trade and cooperation and periods of conflict, and archaeologists and oral historians continue to work on how those relations developed.

Students should be cautious about reading later Labrador patterns back into earlier centuries. Large multi-family communal houses documented in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, for example, developed in contexts shaped by European trade, and earlier house forms were typically smaller. The eve of sustained outside intrusion in this region is therefore long and gradual, beginning with indirect contacts and fishing and whaling visits before sustained mission and trade settlements, and it is complex to date. Chapter 100 addresses twentieth-century administration, and Chapter 116 addresses modern land claims.

Dorset-tradition peoples had occupied the Hudson Strait and Ungava regions for centuries before the arrival of Thule-tradition communities, and Inuit memories of the Tuniit are widespread in Nunavik and Labrador. Thule-tradition sites in Nunavik show a mix of coastal winter houses and summer camps, and Hudson Strait walrus, seals, and whales were important resources, along with caribou and Arctic char in the interior. Archaeologists generally place the arrival of Thule-tradition communities in this region after about AD 1250, though the precise date and the number of movements are discussed.

In Labrador, the northern coast, with its fjords and offshore islands, offered access to seals, whales, fish, and caribou, and Inuit communities developed a distinctive regional identity, with their own dialect and customs. Their southern boundary lay in the Hamilton Inlet region, where Inuit and Innu met, and later accounts and oral histories on both sides describe trade, kinship, and conflict. At the southern edge of the region, in the Strait of Belle Isle area, sixteenth-century Basque whaling stations established a seasonal European presence, and archaeologists have discussed whether Inuit and Europeans had indirect contact there, a question treated in later chapters. The central point for this chapter is that Labrador Inuit were already established, with a long seasonal knowledge of the coast, before sustained mission and trade settlements began in the eighteenth century.

Section 35.11

Kalaallit Worlds of Greenland

Greenland's Inuit history descends from the Thule-tradition migrations that crossed from Arctic Canada, beginning in the northwest during the thirteenth century and spreading south and east along the coasts. The island's regional identities developed from that settlement: the Inughuit (Polar Inuit) of the northwest, the Kalaallit of the west, and the Tunumiit of the east. The north is shaped by the Pikialasorsuaq, which supports large concentrations of seabirds, seals, walrus, narwhal, and whales. West Greenland, particularly Disko Bay, is among the richest marine regions, and the east coast offers a different ice environment with its own seasonal patterns.

Greenlandic communities hunted ringed, harp, and other seals, walrus, narwhal, beluga, and whales, and they fished and harvested seabirds, eggs, and, in the south, plants. Kayaks, skin boats, and dog sleds made seasonal movement possible, and later accounts describe winter houses of stone and turf and summer tents of skin. One source of direct evidence is the Qilakitsoq burial site near Uummannaq, where bodies and clothing preserved by cold and dryness, dated to about 1475, show tailored sealskin and other skin garments, with signs of skilled sewing. The clothing demonstrates the specialized labor, skin preparation, and design knowledge discussed in Section 35.16.

Greenland is also the place where Inuit communities encountered the Norse, who had settled in the south and west beginning around 985 CE. That encounter, with its archaeological evidence and its limitations, is the subject of Chapter 37 and is deliberately not narrated here. It is enough to note that Kalaallit history cannot be reduced to the Norse episode: the Thule-tradition settlement of Greenland, and the long history of regional adaptation that followed, was an Indigenous process with its own logic.

The Greenland setting included earlier peoples. Saqqaq, Independence, and Dorset-tradition communities had inhabited parts of Greenland at different times, and the place called Sermermiut, beside the Ilulissat icefjord on Disko Bay, shows a long sequence of human use by successive peoples. Ancient DNA research on the Thule-descended population indicates that it was part of the same Neo-Inuit lineage that spread across the Arctic, and the arrival of this population in Greenland is placed in the thirteenth century, though an exact decade should not be implied. Over the following generations, regional groups developed, with the Inughuit in the far northwest relying on walrus, narwhal, and seabirds such as the little auk, and making use of meteoritic iron from the Cape York region, and the Tunumiit on the east coast adapting to a different ice regime and to relative isolation.

Qilakitsoq has provided exceptional insight. Eight individuals, six women and two children, were buried in two graves near Uummannaq around 1475, and the cold, dry conditions preserved their bodies and clothing. Examination of their clothing shows sealskin garments, boots, and other pieces carefully tailored and repaired, and analysis of the bodies shows diets rich in marine mammals, evidence of soot in the lungs from lamps, and, on several women, facial tattoos, a practice also described in later accounts. The finds make it possible to see individuals of the pre-intrusion Kalaallit world, and to link them to the technologies and labor discussed in Sections 35.15 and 35.16. Kalaallit oral tradition and place-names also preserve a detailed record of settlement and travel along Greenland's coasts.

Section 35.12

Sea Ice, Weather, Currents, and Environmental Knowledge

Sea ice is the central analytical subject of Arctic environmental knowledge. It is not a uniform frozen surface. Shorefast ice attaches to the coast and may be stable or unstable depending on tides, currents, and temperature. Beyond it, pack ice drifts and shifts, pressure ridges build where floes collide, leads open and close, and polynyas remain open or reopen where currents, winds, or upwelling prevent freezing. Snow cover, ice thickness, color, sound, and the pattern of cracks all carry information about safety and about where animals will be. Travel and hunting on this surface required a continual, practiced reading of conditions, since errors could be fatal.

Arctic peoples developed detailed vocabularies and practices for this knowledge. Hunters and travelers read the direction of snow-drift ridges, shaped by prevailing winds, as a means of orientation when visibility was poor. They watched the sky, stars, clouds, and the behavior of birds and animals, and they remembered landmarks, stone markers, and extremely dense systems of place names. They understood where seals would maintain breathing holes, when whales would pass through leads, where walrus would haul out, and where caribou would cross rivers. Knowledge was learned through experience and apprenticeship, through stories and instruction by elders, and through shared observation among hunters, and it was refined by continual testing against changing conditions.

This knowledge was not static, uniform, or merely practical. It varied by region, and it was held and applied differently by hunters, women who managed food, clothing, and shelter, elders, and others. It was also embedded in ideas about the relationship between people, animals, weather, and the world, as the vocabulary box below shows. Modern scientific and community-based research increasingly recognizes the value of this knowledge for understanding the Arctic, a subject returned to in Chapters 133, 138, and 145.

The skills involved were precise and context-dependent. A hunter seeking seals at a breathing hole needed to recognize signs of a hole's use, to place himself downwind and quiet, and to wait for the moment a seal surfaced. A traveler crossing the sea ice needed to judge the thickness and color of the ice, the sound of a probe or spear, the signs of weakening ice near currents and tidal cracks, and the probable path of drifting floes. A whaling crew at the ice edge needed to read the opening and closing of leads, the pattern of wind and current, and the timing of the whales' northward passage, and to decide where and when to place camps. These decisions were made collectively, with older and experienced members advising, and the knowledge of previous seasons and of other people's reports was woven into them.

Historical Vocabulary
Sila, Ella, and Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit

Definition. Sila (Inuit languages) and ella (Yupik languages) are words with wide meanings that include weather, air, the outdoors, the world, and, in some uses, awareness or intelligence. Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit, often translated as "that which Inuit have long known," is a modern term used in Nunavut for Inuit knowledge, values, and ways of knowing.

Historical context. The older words are known from linguistic records and from ethnographic and community discussion, and the modern term arose in the context of contemporary governance and research. Their usage and interpretation vary by community and speaker.

Why it matters. They show that environmental knowledge in Arctic societies was understood not as separate from social, ethical, and spiritual life, but as part of one understanding of how the world works.

Potential misunderstanding. Single-word English translations flatten these concepts. They should not be reduced to "weather" or to "traditional knowledge" as a fixed body of facts, since the knowledge was and is a living, practiced, and evolving system.

Section 35.13

Whales, Walrus, Seals, and Maritime Economies

Marine mammals were the foundation of many Arctic economies, though not all. Bowhead whaling was central in parts of northern Alaska and at times in the Bering Strait region, on St. Lawrence Island, and in other areas where the whales passed, and it was an important element of the Thule expansion; elsewhere it was less significant or absent. Walrus were hunted at haul-outs, at the ice edge, and from boats, and they supplied meat, hide, ivory, and bone. Ringed seals, the most widespread of Arctic seals, were hunted at breathing holes, at the ice edge, with nets under the ice in some places, and from kayaks in open water, while bearded, harp, ribbon, and other seals were taken where available. Beluga and narwhal were hunted where they gathered, as at Kittigazuit.

A single large animal yielded food, oil for lamps and cooking, skins for boats, clothing, and tents, sinew for thread, baleen, bone, and ivory for tools and structures. Harvests of this size required organization: crews, boats, and processing teams, along with storage in cellars, caches, and cold structures, and in some places fermentation and aging of meat and blubber. They also created questions of distribution. Customary rules assigned shares to crew members and to others in the community, and in many societies hunters were expected to share with kin and partners, which spread the benefits of a successful hunt and secured support for future ones. These expectations limited the extent to which individuals could accumulate wealth from a single hunt, although in some societies, such as whaling communities in northwestern Alaska, successful captains gained prestige and influence.

The scale of the harvests also supported larger and more settled communities than a purely mobile economy would. Large whaling villages in northern Alaska show that dense settlement could rest on marine resources. At the same time, marine economies were vulnerable to changes in ice, weather, and animal movements, and communities relied on knowledge, storage, and social ties to bridge lean periods. The feature below explores these relationships through the lens of a single whale.

The scale and timing of marine harvests varied by region. In Greenland and parts of the Canadian Arctic, ringed and harp seals were the mainstay, taken at breathing holes in winter, at the ice edge and in open water in spring, and in nets in some places. In the Foxe Basin and Hudson Strait, walrus were a major resource, and in the Bering Strait and Chukchi regions, walrus hunting from boats and at the ice edge supported both food and ivory production. In the high Arctic and in northern Alaska, bowhead whaling provided a very large harvest from each animal, but it was also uncertain and demanding. Communities relied on a combination of species and techniques, and they stored meat, blubber, and fish in caches, cellars, and frozen ground to bridge periods of scarcity. The ability to shift between resources, supported by knowledge and by sharing networks, was a central source of stability.

Section 35.14

Fish, Caribou, Birds, Plants, and Mixed Seasonal Economies

It would be a serious distortion to define Arctic peoples only as sea-mammal hunters. On the Yukon–Kuskokwim Delta and in Bristol Bay, salmon and other fish formed the backbone of the diet, harvested with weirs, traps, nets, and dip nets at summer camps, then dried and stored for winter. In the central Arctic and Nunavik, Arctic char was caught at stone weirs and in nets. In Greenland and parts of the Atlantic coast, cod and other fish were taken at different times, and in Aleutian waters fish and shellfish were basic foods. Fishing needed knowledge of runs, rivers, and tides, as well as the labor of processing and storing the catch, work done in large part by women and children in many communities.

Caribou were central to inland and mainland peoples, including the Nunamiut of the Brooks Range and the Caribou Inuit of the interior central Arctic, and caribou were also hunted seasonally by coastal peoples where herds were available. Hunters used drive lines, river crossings, and individual stalking, and caribou skins were prized for clothing because of their warmth and light weight. Birds and their eggs, gathered at nesting sites in spring and summer, were important sources of food and of skins and feathers, and sea-bird colonies were significant in the Aleutians, Bering Sea, and Greenland.

Plants were also important. Berries, roots, greens, seaweed, and, in some regions, edible tubers were gathered, often by women and children, and mixed foods such as akutaq (Yup'ik) or akutuq (Iñupiaq), made from fat and berries, show how animal and plant foods combined. Plants also supplied fuel, medicine, and materials for baskets and tools. Because the mix of foods shifted by region and season, no one "Arctic diet" existed, and economic life depended on knowing which resource to pursue at which time. This flexibility was a major source of resilience.

Seasonal gathering also shaped regional exchange. Fish, birds, and berries gathered in summer were dried, preserved in oil, or frozen for later use, and their processing was labor intensive, involving groups of women, children, and elders. Caribou hunting in autumn supplied skins for winter clothing, and the choice of when to hunt affected the quality of the skins. In regions with both fish and sea mammals, such as the Bering Sea coasts, communities could choose among resources depending on the year, and exchange with neighbors supplied missing resources. This diversity made households and communities more resilient, but it also required detailed knowledge of many resources and of the timing of their availability.

Section 35.15

Qayaq, Umiak, Harpoon, and the Technologies of the Sea

The kayak (spelled qayaq in Yup'ik and Iñupiaq, qajaq in Inuktitut and Greenlandic, and iqyax̂ in Unangam Tunuu) is a light, narrow, skin-covered boat with a frame of driftwood, bone, or antler and a covering of seal or sea lion skin stretched and sewn watertight. A hunter sat in a closed cockpit and propelled the boat with a double-bladed paddle. Designs varied by region: Unangan kayaks were long and low for open water; Greenlandic kayaks became renowned for stability and maneuverability, and Greenlandic hunters developed rolling techniques to recover from capsizing. The umiak (umiaq) was a much larger, open skin boat, framed with wood and covered with hides, used by crews for whaling, walrus hunting, transport of families and goods, and travel along coasts. In Greenland later accounts describe umiaks as women's boats rowed by women, a gendered pattern documented ethnographically. Neither boat was universal: where driftwood and skins were scarce, other solutions were used, and some inland communities relied on boats little or not at all.

The toggling harpoon was one of the most important Arctic inventions. Its detachable head, attached by a line, turned crosswise inside the animal's body after penetration so it could not pull out, and the line connected to a skin float (avataq in Inuktitut) that slowed and tired the animal and marked its position. Hunters also used lances, throwing boards, bows, nets, and a range of specialized tools, many made from bone, ivory, antler, baleen, and stone because wood was scarce. These materials required skills such as carving, drilling, sewing, and the careful repair of lines and seams. A hunter's equipment represented the work of many people: men who made and maintained gear, women who prepared skins and sinew and sewed waterproof coverings and clothing, and elders who passed down designs and techniques.

Arctic technology is best understood as knowledge made material. A kayak frame was fitted to its owner's body, and a harpoon head was shaped by the behavior of the animals it was intended for. Design decisions embodied understanding of buoyancy, tension, materials, and animal behavior, accumulated over generations and transmitted through practice. Such technologies spread and changed through exchange and imitation across regions, and they were never simply invented in response to cold. The following feature summarizes how historians read continuity and change in this world.

Craft knowledge was both technical and social. A kayak was typically built to its user's measurements by the hunter and his family, with the frame made of scarce driftwood or bone lashed with sinew and the covering sewn by women with watertight stitches. Parts were repaired and replaced, and the skill of maintaining a boat was part of a hunter's competence. Bone, ivory, and antler were carved and drilled with stone and, later, with imported metal tools, and Old Bering Sea and Punuk ivory carving shows great artistic skill in the decoration of functional objects. Iron, whether meteoritic or later obtained through trade, was valued for blades, and it moved through exchange networks, as discussed in Section 35.20. The skills were learned through observation and practice from childhood, and, because tools were made in households, the same families that used them passed on their design.

Section 35.16

Clothing, Lamps, Houses, Sleds, and Winter Technology

Clothing was a precise technology. Garments were tailored to fit the body and the season: caribou skin was favored for warmth, sealskin for water resistance, bird skin and, in some places, sea otter or fur for parkas, and fish skin or intestine for waterproof outer layers. Multiple layers trapped air, and seams were sewn tightly with sinew, with attention to waterproofing at the stitches. Boots, mittens, hoods, and snow goggles of wood or ivory with narrow slits completed the system. A parka for a woman who carried a child might be shaped differently from one for a hunter in a kayak. The preserved clothing at Qilakitsoq shows the care and skill involved.

Light and heat came from lamps. The stone lamp (qulliq), usually of soapstone in the central and eastern Arctic, burned oil or blubber with a wick of moss or similar material, and it provided heat, light, and a means of cooking and drying clothing. In western Alaska clay and stone lamps were also used. Tending the lamp was essential household work. Houses varied by region and season: semi-subterranean houses of stone, sod, driftwood, and whale bone served as winter dwellings in Thule-tradition communities, the Unangan barabara and the Yup'ik ena were sod houses, and skin tents were used in summer. Snow houses were built in winter in many central Arctic communities and some others, though not by all Inuit, and not as permanent homes. The word "igloo," from iglu, means "house" in general, and the common stereotype that all Inuit lived in snow houses misrepresents the range of dwellings.

Travel over snow and ice relied on dog teams and sleds (qamutiq in Inuktitut), which carried people, equipment, and game and extended the range of winter hunting and visiting. Dogs were also used in other ways, and their care was an important part of household economy. In regions without suitable ice or snow, other means of travel, such as boats and walking, predominated. Taken together, these technologies formed integrated systems of clothing, heat, shelter, transport, and food storage, each part dependent on the others, and each part dependent on knowledge, labor, and cooperation.

Regional differences were substantial. In the central Arctic, caribou-skin clothing with the fur worn inside and outside provided exceptional insulation, and women's parkas were designed to carry infants. In western Alaska, fish skin and sea mammal intestine were used for waterproof garments, and in the Aleutians, parkas of bird skin and gut rain garments were characteristic. In Greenland, sealskin clothing was common, and the Qilakitsoq garments show cut and stitching techniques of high skill. Houses likewise varied, from large driftwood-framed houses in the Mackenzie Delta to whale-bone houses in the High Arctic and sod houses in western Alaska and the Aleutians. Such variation shows how closely material life was tied to local resources and knowledge.

Section 35.17

Settlements, Households, Kinship, and Community Organization

The household was the basic unit of Arctic social and economic life. It usually consisted of a nuclear or extended family, sometimes with adopted children, relatives, or partners, living in a single dwelling or tent. Households were linked by kinship, marriage, and exchange into camps, villages, and regional networks. In many areas the size and composition of settlements changed with the seasons: large aggregations at whaling villages, fish camps, or winter sealing camps gave way to small dispersed households when resources were thin. Community organization was therefore flexible, with the same families living in different groupings at different times of year.

Leadership was generally based on skill, experience, kinship, and the ability to persuade, not on coercive power. Among the Iñupiat, whaling captains (umialgich) organized crews and hosted feasts and gained prestige from successful harvests. In Yup'ik communities, respected hunters and elders guided the qasgiq and community decisions. Ethnographic accounts from the Iglulik region record leaders sometimes described by a term glossed as "one who thinks" (isumataq), who held influence by judgment and respect. Unangan villages, according to early written accounts, had leaders with high status within ranked households. Communal buildings such as the qasgiq in Yup'ik communities and the qargi in Iñupiat communities were centers for work, meetings, instruction, and ceremony. The organization of these institutions varied by region, and the antiquity of specific institutions is often uncertain.

Disputes were managed through mediation, public pressure, avoidance, and customary practices such as song contests recorded in Greenland and the Canadian Arctic, in which opponents confronted each other with songs before an audience. Ethnographic accounts also record serious violence, including feuds and homicide, and, in some places, collective decisions to remove a person who endangered the community. Historians should neither romanticize Arctic societies as conflict-free nor reduce them to violence. Social inequality also existed. Rank and wealth were more developed in some societies, such as the Unangan and the whaling communities of northwestern Alaska, than in others, and captives and servitude are documented in some regions, a subject taken up more broadly in Chapter 53. Across the Arctic, though, obligations of sharing and reciprocity were central to how communities worked.

Regional differences in organization were substantial. Among the Unangan and the northwestern Alaskan whaling communities, rank and wealth were more developed, and leaders could command larger households, hold ceremonial privileges, and draw on the labor of kin and sometimes captives. In Yup'ik communities, the qasgiq structured relations among men and boys and provided a place for collective decisions, and women's houses and networks structured the lives of women and children. In the central and eastern Arctic, group size and composition shifted seasonally, and leadership was situational, with different people taking the lead in different tasks. In Greenland, later accounts emphasize the importance of skilled hunters, of spiritual specialists, and of kin networks. Recognizing these differences keeps the chapter from imposing a single political model on a very varied region.

Section 35.18

Gender, Labor, Childhood, and Everyday Life

Daily life in Arctic communities was organized around a division of labor that most accounts describe as complementary. Men commonly hunted, built and repaired boats, sleds, and weapons, and traveled for hunting and trade. Women commonly prepared skins, sewed clothing and boat covers, tended lamps, cooked, processed meat and fish, and cared for young children. These roles were interdependent: a hunter without well-made clothing could not hunt in winter, and a household without a hunter lacked food. Ethnographic accounts also record exceptions and flexibility, including women who hunted or acted as ritual leaders and men who did domestic work when circumstances required, and roles differed by region and over time, so students should not treat any pattern as universal.

Children learned by observation, participation, and stories. They watched adults work, played games that built skills and strength, and took on tasks gradually. Elders were valued as sources of knowledge, memory, and advice. Names carried particular importance in many Inuit communities: a child might be named for a deceased relative and be treated according to the relationship of the namesake, linking generations and kin across time. Customary adoption was common, moving children among households and strengthening ties. Games, storytelling, drumming, singing, and contests such as high kicks and the blanket toss were part of social life, particularly during gatherings.

Evidence for everyday life comes from several kinds of sources. Archaeological finds of toys, game pieces, sewing tools, and children's clothing show that childhood and domestic life are visible in the material record, and the Qilakitsoq burials include children whose clothing and care can be examined directly. Oral traditions and later ethnographic accounts describe the education of children through stories, games, and tasks, and they describe rituals marking a child's first catch or first steps. Seasonal rhythms structured daily life: long winter nights were times for storytelling, games, and handwork, and the return of light and the arrival of the first animals of spring were occasions for celebration. Historians should treat later accounts of these rhythms as guides to possibility and not as precise descriptions of earlier centuries.

Marriage, residence, and the life course also varied. Marriages were commonly arranged or negotiated between families and often joined people from different communities, strengthening exchange relationships, and couples might live with either spouse's family for periods. Adoption, naming, and fostering extended kinship beyond biological ties. Elders were respected for their knowledge and often had a role in teaching and in settling disputes, and care for the aged and disabled was a family and community responsibility, though accounts also record hardship during periods of scarcity. These patterns reflect general tendencies that appear in many sources, and not rules that applied uniformly.

Everyday Life and Social History

Making a Winter Parka: Skill, Labor, and Survival

Making a parka involved many stages. Skins had to be obtained by hunters, scraped, softened, and dried; sinew had to be prepared into thread; patterns had to be cut to fit a particular person; and seams had to be sewn so that they kept out wind and, for some garments, water. In many Arctic communities, much of this work was done by women, and ethnographic accounts emphasize that a hunter's safety and success depended on the quality of his clothing. A skilled seamstress was valued, and skills were taught from mother to daughter and among kin.

This example shows how everyday labor sustained the economy and the community. The knowledge of how to prepare skins and how to cut and sew a garment was as technical as the knowledge of how to find a seal. It also shows how the written record can mislead: accounts by outside observers often focused on hunting and travel, and may underemphasize the work that made them possible. Historians therefore read these sources critically, and attend to material evidence such as the clothing preserved at Qilakitsoq and to Indigenous oral accounts.

Section 35.19

Animals, Spiritual Power, Ceremony, and Oral Tradition

Across Arctic societies, relationships with animals were governed by moral and spiritual obligations. In many Inuit, Yupik, Iñupiat, and Unangan communities, animals were understood as persons or as beings with spirits that gave themselves to hunters who treated them properly, and failure to do so could cause the animals to withhold themselves. This understanding shaped practice: rules for butchering and sharing, taboos regarding the handling and mixing of land and sea animals in some regions, offerings of fresh water to sea mammals, and ceremonial returns of bones or bladders to the sea. Such practices linked economic life, ritual, and ethics.

The forms of religious life varied widely. In the central and eastern Arctic and Greenland, accounts describe a powerful sea figure, known by names including Nuliajuk and Sedna, who controlled the sea animals and whose displeasure required action by a spiritual specialist (angakkuq). Yup'ik communities held the Bladder Festival and the Messenger Feast and made elaborate masks used in winter dances, and Yup'ik concepts of ella and yua framed their understanding of persons and the world. Iñupiat whaling communities held ceremonies at the beginning and end of the hunt, and Unangan communities had their own mortuary and hunting practices. The word "shamanism" is sometimes applied to all of these, but it is a modern comparative label, and its use can obscure how distinct the systems were. This chapter therefore uses regional terms and describes practices on their own terms.

Oral tradition carried knowledge about origins, migration, place, relationships with animals, and moral life. Stories of Raven as a creator and trickster figure are important in many Alaskan communities, and Inuit stories of the sea figure, of the Tuniit, and of hunters and animals are widely told. These traditions are shaped by narrators, regions, and purposes, and they entered the written record mostly through outside collectors and, more recently, through Indigenous-led documentation. Missionary and administrative suppression of ceremonies and the changes of later centuries, discussed in later chapters, affected what survives, and revitalization efforts, treated in Chapters 123 and 146, are now restoring knowledge and practice.

Ritual life was closely tied to the seasonal cycle. In the Canadian Arctic and Greenland, accounts describe ceremonies at the return of the sun, at the start of the sealing season, and during periods of scarcity, when the specialist might travel in trance to the sea figure to restore relations. In Yup'ik communities, winter ceremonies, including masked dances in the qasgiq, dramatized relationships with animal and other beings, and the Bladder Festival ensured that the seals' spirits would return. In Iñupiat whaling communities, ceremonies at the start and end of the season expressed respect for the whale. Drums, songs, and dance were central to many of these events. The surviving evidence, including carved masks and ritual objects excavated at sites such as Nunalleq, shows that such practices were well established in the centuries before sustained intrusion, though their detailed meanings are known mainly from later accounts and from present-day knowledge-keepers.

Section 35.20

Trade, Diplomacy, Warfare, and Exchange Networks

Arctic peoples were connected by long-distance networks of exchange, kinship, and travel. At the Bering Strait, Iñupiat, Yupik, and Chukchi communities exchanged goods, including skins, ivory, tools, and materials from both continents, and the Diomede Islands and the shores on either side were meeting places. In northern Alaska, coastal peoples traded sea-mammal products to inland peoples for caribou skins and other goods. Materials that were scarce locally moved widely: copper from the Coppermine region, jade-like nephrite in northwest Alaska, soapstone for lamps and pots, driftwood, and meteoritic iron from northwest Greenland, which archaeological analysis shows reached sites in the Canadian Arctic. Such evidence of material movement demonstrates exchange over great distances, although it does not by itself reveal how the exchange was organized.

Trade relationships were often personal and kin-based. Partnerships among individuals and families in different communities provided hospitality, goods, and information, and marriage connected distant groups. Gatherings at seasonal sites provided opportunities for trade, ceremony, games, and the arrangement of marriages. These practices connected the Arctic to the Subarctic and, indirectly, to the Northwest Coast and beyond, a subject developed in Chapter 36.

Warfare is also documented, though its scale and frequency are debated and the sources are uneven. Oral traditions and early accounts describe raiding among Iñupiat and Yupik communities, between Iñupiat and neighboring Athabaskan peoples, between Inuvialuit and Gwich'in, among Unangan villages and island groups, and in Labrador between Inuit and Innu. Some of these accounts are historical memories of specific events and some are traditions that express relationships between peoples. Historians must be cautious in using later accounts, particularly those influenced by colonial-period conflict, to characterize earlier centuries. What the evidence supports is that Arctic peoples lived in a world of diplomacy, kinship, trade, and sometimes conflict, and that they managed these relationships actively.

The movement of iron deserves particular attention. Meteoritic iron from the Cape York region in northwest Greenland was worked into blades. Analyses have identified, or found compositions consistent with, Cape York meteoritic iron at Thule-tradition sites in the Canadian Arctic, which points to exchange over thousands of kilometres, although not every analyzed specimen can be securely sourced to Cape York. In the Bering Strait, iron and other goods from Siberia reached Alaska through intermediaries, and the introduction of iron tools affected technologies and trade. Because these movements occurred through many hands, they point to networks of relationship rather than to a single trader or route. The same networks carried news, songs, stories, and people, and they help explain the broad similarity of technologies and traditions across the Arctic despite regional differences.

Section 35.21

Arctic Worlds on the Eve of Sustained Colonial Intrusion

By the time sustained outside intrusion began, Arctic peoples had already built diverse societies with histories of migration, innovation, exchange, and adaptation extending over many centuries. The Unangan had an island-based maritime world with ranked households and elaborate craft traditions. Yupik peoples combined river, delta, and sea economies, with communal houses, ceremonies, and exchange across the Bering Strait. Iñupiat communities included large whaling villages and inland caribou hunters. Inuvialuit lived at the mouth of one of the continent's great rivers, and the Inuit of the central and eastern Arctic managed economies of extraordinary flexibility across huge areas. Kalaallit and related peoples had spread along the coasts of Greenland. Their technologies were sophisticated, their environmental knowledge was detailed, and their social institutions were adapted to seasonal and regional conditions.

The end of this pre-intrusion era came at different times. Danish–Norwegian mission and trade began in West Greenland in 1721. Russian fur hunters reached the western Aleutians in 1745, following Vitus Bering's 1741 expedition, with consequences for the Unangan that later chapters on Alaska Native history examine. European ships reached the Bering Strait in the late eighteenth century, and sustained commercial whaling and trade arrived in the nineteenth. Labrador saw mission settlement in the late eighteenth century, and for many peoples of the Mackenzie Delta and central Arctic, sustained contact came in the nineteenth and even the twentieth century. This chapter does not narrate those developments. They belong to later chapters, including Chapter 40 on epidemic disease and Chapters 99 and 100 on twentieth-century administration, and they should not be read back into the centuries described here.

Understanding these Arctic worlds on their own terms matters for what follows. Chapter 36 will place them within the continental system of exchange and communication, Chapter 37 will examine Norse Greenland, and later volumes will show how Arctic peoples engaged with, resisted, and adapted to colonial powers, and how they have since built modern political institutions, including land-claims agreements and Indigenous internationalism, in Chapters 115, 116, 143, and 148. The Arctic of these later chapters is not a blank slate on which outsiders wrote; it is a set of societies with their own histories.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Indigenous worlds of the Arctic and adjacent coasts, from the Aleutian Islands and the Bering Sea to Greenland, in the centuries before sustained colonial intrusion. Its central argument is that the Arctic was not a marginal environment to which people merely adapted. Unangan, Yupik, Inuit, and related peoples made it into a known, traveled, and named world, and their societies were built on knowledge of sea ice, currents, coastlines, and animal movements.

The chapter has stressed diversity. Unangan, Yupik, and Inuit peoples speak languages of one family, Eskimo–Aleut, but they belong to different branches, and their regional histories, economies, and institutions differ. Terms such as Iñupiat, Inuvialuit, and Kalaallit name distinct Inuit peoples, and Yupik names a related but separate grouping. The word "Eskimo" is an exonym that obscures these distinctions, and the Unangan are not Inuit.

The chapter has treated archaeology, oral tradition, linguistics, genetics, and later written sources as complementary and differently limited. Archaeological labels such as Dorset, Birnirk, and Thule describe patterns of material evidence and must not be confused with the names of peoples. Oral accounts of the Tuniit preserve a perspective on earlier inhabitants that archaeology cannot supply, and ancient DNA has contributed evidence about population relationships without settling questions of identity. Because most detailed written descriptions are later, the ethnographic present is a persistent hazard.

The migration of the Thule tradition from northern Alaska across the Canadian Arctic to Greenland, with its rapid eastward expansion during the thirteenth century, was one of the most extensive movements in North American archaeology. Historians disagree about its speed and causes, weighing environmental opportunity, social and economic motives, and the reliability of radiocarbon dating. The movement formed the base of the later Inuit world, though the Thule tradition is not identical with Inuit peoples.

Regional treatments showed varied economies. Unangan communities lived on volcanic islands rich in marine life, with ranked households and skilled kayak makers. Yup'ik, Cup'ik, and Yupik communities combined river, delta, and sea resources, with communal houses and ceremonies. Iñupiat communities included large whaling villages and inland caribou hunters. Inuvialuit communities made use of the Mackenzie's driftwood and the summer beluga hunt. The central and eastern Arctic peoples managed highly mobile economies, and Kalaallit communities spread along Greenland's coasts.

Environmental knowledge was at the heart of this history. Sea ice was a changing terrain, and its safe use required close reading of conditions and of animal behavior, learned through apprenticeship and practice. Concepts such as sila and ella show that this knowledge was part of a wider understanding of the world. Technologies, including the qayaq, umiak, toggling harpoon, tailored clothing, lamp, and house, embodied that knowledge and the labor and cooperation of many people.

Communities were organized around households, kinship, partnerships, and seasonal aggregation. Leadership rested on skill, experience, and persuasion, with greater rank and wealth in some societies, such as Unangan and northwestern Alaskan whaling communities. Gender roles were generally complementary, and children learned through participation. Sharing and reciprocity were central, and disputes were handled through mediation, public pressure, and, in some cases, violence, as the sources indicate.

Relationships with animals involved ethical and spiritual obligations, expressed through ritual, taboo, and ceremony, with distinct traditions among Inuit, Yupik, Iñupiat, and Unangan communities. Trade, kinship, and sometimes warfare connected Arctic peoples to each other and to their Subarctic neighbors, with materials such as copper, iron, jade-like stone, and soapstone moving over long distances.

Finally, the chapter has shown that the end of the pre-intrusion era came at different times, from the early eighteenth century in West Greenland and 1745 in the western Aleutians to the nineteenth and twentieth centuries elsewhere. This chapter deliberately does not narrate colonial contact. It sets the Arctic worlds of earlier centuries on their own terms, for the continent-wide synthesis of Chapter 36, the Norse chapter that follows, and the later volumes' treatment of colonization, resistance, and resurgence.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Eskimo–Aleut
A language family with two branches: Aleut (Unangam Tunuu) and Eskimoan (Yupik and Inuit languages).
Unangan (Unangax̂)
The Indigenous people of the Aleutian Islands and western Alaska Peninsula, speakers of Unangam Tunuu; a distinct people, not a subgroup of Inuit.
Yupik
A broad grouping of related languages and peoples in Alaska and Chukotka, including Central Alaskan Yup'ik, Alutiiq, and Siberian Yupik.
Yup'ik
The Central Alaskan Yupik people and language of the Yukon–Kuskokwim Delta and Bristol Bay region.
Cup'ik and Cup'ig
Varieties of Central Alaskan Yup'ik associated with coastal communities and Nunivak Island.
Iñupiat
Inuit peoples of northern and northwestern Alaska, speakers of Iñupiaq.
Inuvialuit
Inuit of the Mackenzie Delta and western Canadian Arctic, speakers of Inuvialuktun.
Kalaallit
The Inuit of West Greenland; related Greenlandic Inuit include the Inughuit of the northwest and the Tunumiit of the east.
Paleo-Inuit
A modern scholarly term for the early Arctic traditions, including Pre-Dorset, Dorset, and Saqqaq, that preceded Thule-tradition settlement in the eastern Arctic.
Dorset
An archaeological label for a Paleo-Inuit tradition of the eastern Arctic, not the name of a people.
Tuniit
Earlier people remembered in Inuit oral tradition; often, though not provably, associated with Dorset-tradition communities.
Birnirk
An archaeological tradition of northern Alaska and Chukotka in the first and early second millennia CE, ancestral to the Thule tradition.
Thule tradition
An archaeological tradition that emerged in northern Alaska from Birnirk and related traditions during the early-to-mid second millennium CE, with securely defined contexts dated to the thirteenth century; it spread rapidly across the Arctic during that century and is widely considered ancestral to later Inuit.
Marine reservoir effect
A dating complication in which radiocarbon samples from marine animals appear older than their true age, requiring regional correction.
Ethnographic present
The practice of describing a society as it was observed at one time and implying it was the same earlier.
Shorefast ice
Sea ice attached to the coast, used as a platform for travel and hunting.
Lead and polynya
A lead is a crack or channel of open water in sea ice; a polynya is a larger area that stays or becomes open through winter.
Qayaq (qajaq, iqyax̂)
A narrow skin-covered boat with a closed cockpit, used for hunting and travel; spelling varies by language.
Umiak (umiaq)
A large open skin boat used by crews for whaling, walrus hunting, and transport.
Toggling harpoon
A harpoon whose detachable head turns crosswise in the animal's body and is connected to the hunter by a line.
Umialik
An Iñupiaq term for the owner and organizer of a whaling boat and crew, often translated as whaling captain.
Qasgiq / qargi
A large communal house (Yup'ik qasgiq; Iñupiaq qargi) used for work, instruction, meetings, and ceremony.
Barabara
A Russian-derived English term for the semi-subterranean sod house of the Aleutians and nearby coasts.
Qulliq
An Inuit stone lamp burning oil or blubber for heat and light.
Sila / ella
Inuit and Yupik words with broad meanings including weather, air, world, and awareness.
Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit
A modern Nunavut term for Inuit knowledge, values, and ways of knowing.
Angakkuq
An Inuit term for a spiritual specialist; related forms occur in Yup'ik and other languages.
Kevgiq (Messenger Feast)
A Yup'ik ceremonial exchange in which one community invites another to a feast with reciprocal gifts.
Little Ice Age
A cooler interval, beginning about 1300 and varying by region, associated with changes in sea ice and animal availability.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it corrects a persistent habit of seeing the Arctic as the edge of the inhabited world. It shows instead a set of long-established, diverse, and sophisticated societies whose knowledge of ice, water, and animals allowed them to live and travel in places that outsiders long regarded as impassable. The chapter treats that knowledge as intellectual achievement as well as practical skill, built through generations of observation, teaching, and cooperation.

It also reveals larger processes. The Thule expansion shows how a technological and social tradition could spread over thousands of kilometres within a few centuries, and the debates over its causes show how historians weigh environment, society, and evidence. The regional reorganizations associated with changing climate show Arctic peoples responding to change long before colonial intrusion. And the staggered end of the pre-intrusion era reminds us, as Chapter 1 argued, that no single date marks the beginning of colonization across a continent.

Many of the continuities traced here carried forward. Languages, knowledge of the environment, ideas about reciprocity with animals, and the importance of kinship and sharing remained central to Arctic communities through later centuries, and they inform contemporary work in language revitalization, environmental stewardship, and self-government treated in Chapters 123, 133, 138, and 146. The history of Arctic peoples in the colonial and modern periods cannot be understood without the foundations laid here.

Within the larger textbook, this chapter completes the regional survey of Volume III's northern worlds, joining the Subarctic peoples of Chapter 34 with the Arctic coasts of this chapter. It prepares for Chapter 36, which will draw together the continental systems of exchange, migration, and communication that connected these and all the other regions of Indigenous North America before sustained colonization.
