Chapter Opening

On December 18, 1971, President Richard Nixon signed Public Law 92-203, the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act. In exchange for the extinguishment of aboriginal land claims, it conveyed roughly 44 million acres and $962.5 million to Alaska Native corporations, an arrangement with no precedent in the history of federal Indian policy.

The signing ended one chapter of a long struggle and opened another that continues today. For more than a century, the United States had claimed sovereignty over Alaska without ever negotiating with the great majority of its Indigenous inhabitants over who owned the land. The Treaty of Cession of 1867 was made between Russia and the United States; no Iñupiat, Yup’ik, Unangax̂, Athabascan, Tlingit, or other Alaska Native nation was a party to it. Congress’s 1884 act creating civil government for Alaska promised that Native people would not be disturbed in lands they actually used or claimed, but reserved to future legislation the terms on which they would obtain title. That promise remained unfulfilled for eighty-seven years.

What finally forced the issue was not a single event but a convergence. Alaska became a state in 1959 with the right to select more than 100 million acres of federal land. Native villages discovered that the lands they hunted, fished, and lived on were being chosen by the state, and they protested. In 1966 Native leaders from across the vast territory created the Alaska Federation of Natives (AFN). The Secretary of the Interior froze land transfers. In 1968 the discovery of oil at Prudhoe Bay on the Arctic coast raised the stakes enormously, because no pipeline could safely cross lands under unresolved claim. Congress, the State of Alaska, the petroleum industry, and Native organizations all came to want a settlement, though for very different reasons.

The settlement they produced was unusual in its structure. Rather than creating reservations held in federal trust, as in most of the United States, Congress directed land and money into state-chartered business corporations owned by Alaska Native shareholders. Twelve regional corporations and more than two hundred village corporations would own the land and manage the money. In return, the Act extinguished aboriginal title and claims based on use and occupancy, including aboriginal hunting and fishing rights. Congress described its purpose as a settlement made without creating a reservation system or lengthy wardship, language that reflected the termination-era assumptions still shaping federal thinking even as Chapter 114 shows policy turning toward self-determination.

This chapter treats ANCSA as the outcome of Indigenous political organization and also as a legal and institutional design with long consequences. Alaska Native people achieved something no other Indigenous people in the United States had: title to tens of millions of acres and a large capital endowment, won by their own coordinated advocacy. Yet the corporate form raised enduring questions. Who belongs to a corporation, and who belongs to a tribal nation? What happens to the stock, and the land behind it, as generations pass? Can a business with fiduciary duties to shareholders protect ancestral places and subsistence resources? And what became of hunting and fishing rights that Congress declared extinguished?

Historians draw on a rich body of evidence for these questions: the text of the Act and its amendments, congressional hearings and committee reports, Interior Department records, the 1968 report of the Federal Field Committee for Development Planning in Alaska, newspapers such as the Tundra Times founded by Howard Rock in 1962, the records of the AFN and regional associations, court decisions, and the testimony and memoirs of participants. The Alaska Native Review Commission’s 1985 hearings in villages added a body of testimony on how the settlement was lived. Most of these sources were produced by institutions of government and business; the voices of village residents and elders must often be recovered from hearings, oral histories, and Native-owned publications.

The chapter’s central argument is that ANCSA secured an unprecedented land and financial settlement, but did so through a corporate ownership system that created lasting tensions among Indigenous nationhood, commercial development, subsistence practices, and ancestral relationships to land. It should be read as neither an unqualified triumph nor an unqualified failure. It follows Chapter 114’s account of self-determination in program administration with an account of how Alaska Native peoples pursued control of their territory, and it prepares for Chapter 116’s very different Arctic settlement history in Canada.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the diversity of Alaska Native peoples, homelands, and territorial systems that existed before and independently of Russian and American claims.
  2. Explain why the 1867 purchase of Alaska was not an Indigenous cession of land and how the Organic Act of 1884 left Native land rights unresolved.
  3. Trace Alaska Native political organizing from the Alaska Native Brotherhood and Sisterhood through the Tlingit and Haida litigation to the founding of the AFN in 1966.
  4. Analyze how statehood, the federal land freeze, the Prudhoe Bay discovery, and congressional negotiation interacted to make a settlement possible in 1971.
  5. Identify the principal provisions of the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act, including its financial terms, land entitlements, and corporate structure.
  6. Distinguish the extinguishment of aboriginal title and aboriginal hunting and fishing claims from the continuing political existence of Alaska Native tribes.
  7. Distinguish Alaska Native corporations from tribal governments in authority, obligations, membership, and landholding.
  8. Compare how ANCSA’s land, revenue, and corporate arrangements worked in different regions, including Southeast Alaska.
  9. Explain why subsistence rights became one of the settlement’s most contested unresolved questions, and evaluate ANILCA’s rural priority as a response.
  10. Assess the 1991 stock question and the 1988 amendments as attempts to protect Native ownership across generations.
  11. Evaluate competing interpretations of ANCSA as empowerment and as a settlement with structural limitations, using historical evidence.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a land question that was legally open from 1867 remain unresolved until 1971, and what finally changed the political conditions?
  2. How did Alaska Native leaders build statewide unity across profound differences of language, region, economy, and priorities?
  3. In what ways did oil development accelerate the settlement, and why would it be misleading to call it the origin of Native land-claims activism?
  4. What does it mean that ANCSA extinguished aboriginal title but did not extinguish Alaska Native tribes?
  5. Why does it matter historically that land and money were placed in corporations rather than in tribal governments or a trust-land system?
  6. Why does owning land not automatically secure the right to hunt and fish on it, and how did the subsistence controversy expose that gap?
  7. How did the experience of the settlement differ among the North Slope, western Alaska, the interior, and Southeast Alaska?
  8. What evidence is needed to judge ANCSA, and why might Alaska Natives of different regions and generations reach different judgments?

Historical Context

Alaska covers about 586,000 square miles, roughly 375 million acres, and spans Arctic tundra, boreal forest, river deltas, mountain ranges, volcanic islands, and temperate rainforest. Its Indigenous peoples developed economies suited to each setting: Arctic marine mammal hunting, salmon fishing, caribou and moose hunting, and trade among regions. Chapters 10, 11, 33, 34, and 35 treat these societies and their antiquity. Chapter 99 treats Alaska Native communities under American territorial administration in the first half of the twentieth century, and this chapter summarizes that administration only as needed.

The legal inheritance matters. United States law had long recognized that Indigenous peoples held a right of occupancy, often called aboriginal or Indian title, which only the federal government could extinguish. In the lower forty-eight states, extinguishment typically occurred through treaties, which ended as a form of diplomacy with Indian nations in 1871. Alaska was acquired in 1867, and no treaties were ever negotiated with its Indigenous peoples. The result was a legal vacuum: Native title was acknowledged in principle by the 1884 Organic Act, yet no process existed to define, protect, or extinguish it. For decades this mattered little to officials, because few non-Natives wanted Alaska land. Statehood and oil changed that.

The Alaska land question also developed against a national background. Postwar federal policy had swung toward termination, as Chapter 104 explains, and then toward self-determination, treated in Chapter 114. The Indian Claims Commission Act of 1946 expressly included tribal claims arising within the United States or Alaska, and some Alaska Native claims were before that tribunal. Yet the commission’s principally monetary claims process did not resolve the comprehensive Alaska-wide title and land-selection problems confronting villages after statehood. That distinction helps explain why Alaska Native leaders sought an acreage-and-capital settlement through Congress rather than relying solely on litigation. The civil rights and Red Power movements, treated in Chapters 108 and 109 through 112, supplied political models and allies. Alaska Native leaders drew on these currents while pursuing a distinctive strategy shaped by their particular circumstances.

Division I · Section 115.1

Alaska Native Peoples, Homelands, and Territorial Systems

Alaska was never an empty frontier awaiting settlement. At the time of Russian arrival in the eighteenth century, and for thousands of years before, it was home to dozens of distinct peoples speaking languages from several unrelated families. Along the Arctic and Bering Sea coasts lived the Iñupiat, whose hunters pursued bowhead whales, seals, walrus, and caribou from permanent coastal villages. On St. Lawrence Island and parts of the Bering Strait region lived Siberian Yupik peoples with close ties across the strait. The Yup’ik and Cup’ik peoples of the Yukon-Kuskokwim Delta and Nunivak Island combined fishing, sea mammal hunting, and seasonal movement between camps and villages. The Unangax̂ (Aleut) occupied the Aleutian Islands and western Alaska Peninsula, and the Alutiiq or Sugpiaq peoples lived around Kodiak, the Kenai Peninsula, and Prince William Sound.

The interior was the homeland of Athabascan (Dene) peoples, whose roughly eleven languages in Alaska include Gwich’in, Koyukon, Tanana, Ahtna, and Deg Hit’an. Around Cook Inlet lived the Dena’ina, an Athabascan people oriented toward salmon and marine resources, and the Eyak occupied the Copper River Delta. In Southeast Alaska, the Tlingit, the Haida (whose Kaigani Haida communities were on Prince of Wales Island), and, from the late nineteenth century, a Tsimshian community at Metlakatla, built societies organized around clans, house groups, and crests. Chapters 33, 34, and 35 examine these societies in depth.

These peoples differed profoundly in how they organized land. Tlingit and Haida clans and house groups held rights to specific salmon streams, hunting territories, berry grounds, and intertidal areas, and these rights were validated through ceremony and the display of crests and ancestral narratives. Iñupiaq whaling crews, led by captains, organized around ocean ice and seasonal movements, and communities shared access to hunting areas through kinship, reciprocity, and negotiated agreements with neighbors. Athabascan bands of extended families moved across large territories following fish runs and caribou, with recognized areas associated with particular groups. Yup’ik villages managed fishing camps along rivers and the coast, with ceremonial and communal life centered on men’s houses. None of these systems corresponded to a European-style survey of parcels held in fee simple. They were territorial systems based on use, kinship, ceremony, and recognized authority.

Historians reconstruct them from archaeology, oral tradition, linguistics, early Russian and American accounts, and, importantly for this chapter, twentieth-century land use and occupancy studies, in which elders mapped where they and their forebears had hunted, fished, and camped. These maps became evidence in the claims process and are discussed in Sections 115.5 and 115.8. They also carry limitations: they record twentieth-century memory and use, and the line between a territory used and a territory owned did not mean the same thing in Native and American legal thought.

A note on terminology is necessary. ANCSA and other federal statutes use the phrase “Indians, Aleuts, and Eskimos,” and “Alaska Native” is the common contemporary umbrella term, as discussed in Chapter 1. This chapter uses specific peoples’ names wherever possible, because the settlement’s consequences depended heavily on region and people.

Division I · Section 115.2

Russian Colonization, American Acquisition, and Indigenous Land Rights

Russian contact began after Vitus Bering’s 1741 voyage revealed the abundance of sea otters. Promyshlenniki, fur hunters operating from Siberia, moved eastward through the Aleutians in the following decades, compelling Unangax̂ hunters to harvest sea otters under threat of violence and the seizure of hostages. Conflict, forced labor, and introduced disease devastated the Unangax̂, and estimates of their population decline in the first decades of contact run to the great majority of the people, though figures at contact are uncertain. The Russian-American Company, chartered in 1799, consolidated this system and extended it to Kodiak and the Alutiiq people, and eventually to Sitka. Russian Orthodox missionaries, including Ivan Veniaminov from 1824, established a church that many Alaska Native communities adopted and adapted, giving Orthodoxy a lasting place in Native life.

Russian power was narrower than later American law assumed. Russian settlements were few, and the Company’s control of the interior and of much of the coast was slight. The Tlingit resisted: in 1802 they destroyed the Russian post near present-day Sitka, and Russians returned to retake the site in 1804 only after a hard campaign. The Tlingit continued to trade with and fight Russian colonists on their own terms. The Russian state also divided people by legal category, including the “creole” estate of people of mixed Russian and Native descent, in ways that shaped later American classifications.

In the Treaty of Cession, concluded March 30, 1867, ratified by the Senate that April, and implemented in a ceremony at Sitka on October 18, Russia transferred its claims to the United States for $7.2 million. The treaty’s provisions on inhabitants distinguished those who would become citizens from “uncivilized native tribes,” who would be subject to whatever laws the United States adopted for Indigenous tribes of the country. That language indicated a presumption of federal authority over Alaska Natives, but it did not constitute a cession by them. Russia could transfer only what it held, and Russia had not purchased, conquered, or negotiated for most of the lands it was selling.

Historical Vocabulary
Aboriginal Title (Indian Title)

Definition: In United States law, the right of an Indigenous people to occupy and use lands they have inhabited from time immemorial, a right that is distinct from fee ownership and that could be extinguished only by the federal government.

Historical context: The doctrine descended from nineteenth-century Supreme Court decisions, which recognized Indigenous occupancy but placed the power to extinguish it in federal hands. In Alaska, the 1884 Organic Act acknowledged Native possession but left the terms for acquiring title to Congress.

Why it matters: ANCSA’s central bargain was the extinguishment of aboriginal title in exchange for land and money.

Potential misunderstanding: Aboriginal title is not the same as tribal sovereignty. Title concerns property rights in land; sovereignty concerns inherent political authority. ANCSA extinguished specified claims of the first without abolishing the second.

After 1867 the United States did not create a process to resolve these questions. Alaska was governed by the army and then briefly by customs officials, and from 1884 as a “district.” Missionaries and officials, including the Presbyterian educator Sheldon Jackson, promoted mission schools, and federal policy aimed at assimilation, as Chapter 99 shows. Native people increasingly participated in the cash economy, fishing for canneries, working in mines, and trapping. Congress did pass some limited measures, such as the Native Allotment Act of 1906, permitting individuals to claim up to 160 acres, but the process was slow and burdensome, and most Native land use fell outside it. The aboriginal title question was deferred rather than resolved.

Division I · Section 115.3

Early Alaska Native Land Claims and Political Organizing

On November 5, 1912, Tlingit and Tsimshian Native men met in Juneau and founded the Alaska Native Brotherhood (ANB), whose early organization and influential Camp No. 1 subsequently developed in Sitka. Women established the Alaska Native Sisterhood (ANS) during the following years; histories commonly date it to 1915, while some archival accounts identify organizing in 1914. Many founders were products of Presbyterian mission schools who favored citizenship, education, and equal treatment, and the organizations initially stressed assimilation and civil rights. Over time they became vehicles for broader political action, including challenges to discrimination in schools and public accommodations. Elizabeth Peratrovich, a leader in the Sisterhood, testified in 1945 in support of the territorial Equal Rights Act, a landmark law against discrimination. William Paul, a Tlingit lawyer who sat in the territorial legislature, became a key advocate of the land claim.

The Tlingit and Haida claim illustrates both the possibilities and limits of the legal route. Native leaders protested that creation of the Tongass National Forest in 1907 and Glacier Bay National Monument in 1925 had taken land that Tlingit and Haida people owned. Congress in 1935 passed a Jurisdictional Act allowing the Tlingit and Haida Indians of Alaska to sue the United States in the Court of Claims. The suit, brought in 1947, took more than two decades. In 1959 the court held that the Tlingit and Haida had held title to certain lands in Southeast Alaska that were taken by the federal reservations, and in 1968 it awarded approximately $7.55 million based on valuations tied to different dates when particular lands were taken, rather than a single 1867 acquisition date. The relevant dates included 1891 for Annette Islands lands, various 1902–1909 Tongass withdrawals, and 1925 for Glacier Bay, along with dates of individual patents. The award did not restore the lands and did not use their later replacement values. Money had been awarded, but the land had not been returned, and the compensation, calculated at nineteenth-century values, seemed small.

A different judicial decision demonstrated why litigation alone was risky. In Tee-Hit-Ton Indians v. United States (1955), the Supreme Court held that the Tee-Hit-Ton, a Tlingit group suing over timber taken in the Tongass, had aboriginal title that Congress had not recognized, and that the taking of such title required no Fifth Amendment compensation. In the Court’s reading, Congress could extinguish unrecognized aboriginal title at will. For Alaska Natives, the ruling meant that the safest route to securing land and compensation was legislative, through a negotiated settlement approved by Congress, and not a lawsuit that the courts might decline to enforce.

Division I · Section 115.4

Alaska Statehood and the Intensification of Competing Land Claims

The Alaska Statehood Act, signed July 7, 1958, and Alaska’s admission on January 3, 1959, created the conditions for open conflict. Because a new state without land would be poor, Congress authorized Alaska to select about 102.5 million acres from vacant, unappropriated, and unreserved federal land over twenty-five years, with additional smaller grants bringing the total entitlement to roughly 104 million acres. The act also contained a disclaimer, in which the state and its people renounced right and title to lands held by Natives or held by the United States in trust for them. The disclaimer implicitly acknowledged that Native claims existed, but it left their extent undetermined, and nothing in the statute defined how those claims would be settled.

The tension was immediate because state selections often targeted just the lands that Native communities used. The state chose places for their resource value, access, and strategic location, many close to villages and traditional hunting and fishing grounds. Villagers who learned of selections filed protests with the Bureau of Land Management. In the early 1960s residents of Minto in the Interior objected when the state moved to select lands including Minto Flats, an important area for waterfowl, fish, and game. Other protests followed from regions across the state. By the mid-1960s the claims filed by Native communities, added together, covered more land than the entire state.

The interests were divided. Alaska’s state government, led by statehood leaders, saw land as the foundation of fiscal independence and development, and many officials considered the Native claims a threat to growth. Federal agencies had to administer the transfers and faced pressure from both sides. Commercial interests wanted clear title to resource lands. Alaska Native communities, whose economy and culture remained dependent on hunting, fishing, and gathering, saw the selections as a direct threat to a way of life that had survived a century of American rule. The Native population, roughly one in six Alaskans in 1970, was geographically dispersed in small villages and politically underrepresented in a state dominated by Anchorage and Fairbanks. The question was how a scattered, diverse population could respond to a coordinated challenge to its homelands. The answer came in the 1960s with the creation of statewide Native political organization.

Division II · Section 115.5

The Rise of the Alaska Federation of Natives

By the 1960s a generation of Native leaders, many educated in boarding schools, universities, or military service, had entered politics while remaining rooted in villages. Several local struggles prepared the ground. In 1958 the Atomic Energy Commission proposed Project Chariot, a plan to use nuclear explosions to carve a harbor near Point Hope. Residents and their allies questioned its safety and the lack of consultation, and the project was abandoned by about 1962. In 1962 Howard Rock, an Iñupiaq artist from Point Hope, founded the Tundra Times, which linked Native communities across the state and published news and commentary that mainstream papers ignored. Proposals such as the Rampart Dam on the Yukon River, which would have flooded Athabascan homelands, reinforced the sense that decisions affecting Native lands were being made without Native consent. Regional associations, including the Association of Village Council Presidents in the Yukon-Kuskokwim region, the Arctic Slope Native Association, the Tanana Chiefs Conference, and Cook Inlet and Fairbanks Native associations, emerged or grew in these years.

In October 1966, more than 400 Alaska Native delegates representing regional organizations assembled in Anchorage for a statewide land-claims conference and created a predecessor federation. The organization adopted the Alaska Federation of Natives name and completed its formal nonprofit incorporation in early 1967; Emil Notti, an Athabascan engineer, became its first full-time president. Distinguishing the 1966 coalition-building conference from the 1967 formal organization avoids making institutional change seem instantaneous. Willie Hensley, an Iñupiaq from Kotzebue who entered the state legislature in the late 1960s, articulated the case for land claims in an influential intellectual and political role. Howard Rock provided a platform through the Tundra Times, and Flore Lekanof, an Unangax̂ leader, was among the organizers who brought Aleutian and Pribilof communities into the coalition. Women leaders, village council members, elders, and young activists also shaped the movement through regional associations, village delegations, and the ANS and ANB networks, although the historical record has often foregrounded a few male spokesmen.

Unity was hard won. The AFN encompassed Arctic whaling communities, Yup’ik fishing villages, Athabascan hunters, Southeast clans with a century of organization, and urban Natives in Anchorage and Fairbanks. Their priorities differed. Some stressed protection of subsistence lands; some sought capital for economic development; some favored large village-based land holdings; some wanted regional institutions. Southeast leaders, who already had the Court of Claims case, weighed whether a statewide settlement served them. The AFN’s strength lay in its ability to negotiate these differences into a common position, while its weakness was that compromise would leave some communities dissatisfied. The Federal Field Committee’s 1968 report Alaska Natives and the Land compiled evidence on Native land use, income, housing, and health and supported the claim that the Native economy depended on land.

Division II · Section 115.6

The Federal Land Freeze and the Defense of Aboriginal Title

Federal control of the land transfer process gave Native claimants their strongest early leverage. State selections could not become final without approval from the Interior Department, and the Secretary of the Interior had discretion over whether to grant it. Beginning in 1966, Secretary Stewart Udall declined to approve further transfers on lands subject to Native protest. Udall’s reasoning combined law and prudence. Aboriginal claims, whatever their ultimate extent, had been acknowledged by Congress, and approving transfers that might later prove invalid would invite litigation and prejudice a future settlement. In January 1969, shortly before leaving office, Udall formalized the policy in a broad withdrawal of unreserved federal land in Alaska from disposition, which his successor Walter Hickel, a former governor of Alaska, kept in place and extended.

The freeze angered the State of Alaska, which contended that the Statehood Act’s land grant was a promise Congress had to honor and that the freeze stalled development. It also frustrated mining and oil interests. But for Native claimants, the freeze was essential. Without it, selection and patenting of lands could have continued until little remained to settle. By stopping the clock, Interior ensured that any settlement would involve the lands themselves, not merely compensation for lands already gone, as had happened in the lower forty-eight under the Indian Claims Commission.

The freeze did not decide the content of a settlement. It created a political equilibrium in which everyone with an interest, the state, the Native claimants, the federal government, and eventually the oil industry, had reason to want resolution but not on identical terms. Native leaders understood that the freeze depended on executive discretion and could be reversed. They therefore pursued legislation actively, aware that time and political support were limited.

Division II · Section 115.7

Prudhoe Bay, Oil Development, and the Pipeline Question

In 1968, Atlantic Richfield and Humble Oil announced a major oil discovery at Prudhoe Bay on Alaska’s North Slope, on lands the state had selected in the mid-1960s. It proved to be the largest oil field in North America. In September 1969 the state’s lease sale brought in nearly $900 million in bonus bids, a sum that dwarfed the state’s annual budget. The discovery also posed a technical and legal problem: oil had to reach tidewater, and the route proposed ran roughly 800 miles south across federal and state land to the ice-free port of Valdez. Crossing that land required federal rights-of-way, and much of the route crossed territory subject to Native claims.

Oil did not create the land-claims movement, which was already well under way. It altered the political economy of the negotiation. In 1970, litigation, including a suit brought on behalf of Native villages and one by environmental organizations, blocked pipeline permits, and a federal court issued an injunction that included the unresolved Native claims among its obstacles. Companies could not build without clear title to rights-of-way, and the state wanted revenue flowing. Congress’s Trans-Alaska Pipeline Authorization Act of 1973 eventually cleared the way, and the pipeline began carrying oil in 1977. The petroleum industry, which had no particular interest in Native rights, now had a strong interest in settling Native claims quickly.

For Native leaders, oil was both opportunity and danger. It gave them negotiating leverage and suggested that Native lands might contain valuable resources, which supported demands for revenue sharing. It also meant that the settlement’s terms would be pressed by powerful interests wanting speed. The leaders recognized that pipeline urgency could lead Congress to a settlement that was fast but not generous. Historians therefore treat oil as an accelerant of the settlement, not as its cause, and consider the negotiation as the point where Indigenous organization and economic pressure converged.

Division II · Section 115.8

Negotiating a Settlement: Indigenous Demands and Congressional Compromise

From 1967 to 1971, Congress, the Interior Department, the White House, the State of Alaska, the oil industry, and the AFN negotiated a settlement in a series of bills, hearings, and drafts. Interior officials drew up proposals, state leaders sought to protect state land selections and revenue, and the AFN formulated demands that evolved as the process advanced. The AFN pressed for a large land base of about 40 million acres, a substantial cash payment, and a continuing royalty on mineral production on the lands that would be taken for the state and the federal government. It also sought Native control over the institutions that would receive the settlement. Congress was divided over acreage, compensation, administration, and the question of whether to settle at all.

Key figures in Congress shaped the result. Senator Henry Jackson of Washington, as chairman of the Senate Interior Committee, advanced settlement legislation, and in 1970 the Senate passed a bill, but the House did not act on it before the Congress ended. Representative Wayne Aspinall of Colorado, who chaired the House Interior Committee, favored smaller land awards and quicker closure, and his support was necessary. Alaska’s congressional delegation, including Senators Ted Stevens and Mike Gravel, worked to balance state and Native interests. The Nixon administration, wanting the pipeline built, sent its own proposals and supported a settlement. In 1970 Nixon removed Hickel from the Interior Department, and his successor Rogers Morton carried the department’s position into the final negotiations.

The central disagreements shaped the final law. On acreage, the AFN’s large land request ran against pressure from Congress and the state to limit the amount of land leaving federal and state control. On compensation, the AFN sought a perpetual royalty, while Congress preferred a capped payment. On the form of ownership, many Natives favored village-based holdings, while others favored regional organization, and the Act eventually adopted a two-tier corporate structure. On subsistence, Native leaders sought guarantees of hunting and fishing rights, but Congress resisted writing detailed protections into the settlement and instead expressed an expectation that the Interior Secretary and the state would protect Native subsistence needs. On sovereignty, the Act’s sponsors explicitly sought to avoid a reservation system and permanent special status.

The Native coalition did not control the pace of congressional action. Committees could advance bills containing fewer acres or different cash arrangements than delegates wanted, while the state defended its land selections and industry representatives sought a predictable pipeline corridor. Native advocates therefore had to decide whether a proposal gained enough secure title to justify surrendering claims that, as a matter of community history, had never been voluntarily relinquished. That calculation was particularly difficult where subsistence areas did not coincide with commercially valuable lands or with tracts likely to be patented to corporations. Neither the state nor the federal government was negotiating as a single-minded actor: individual legislators, Interior officials, and federal planning agencies approached the competing objectives differently.

An important feature of the final bargaining was the separation of three questions that earlier discussions often combined: which lands could be conveyed, how much money the federal government and resource revenues would supply, and who would govern the resulting assets. Congress could enlarge one part of the offer while narrowing another. The two-tier corporate model sought to keep some lands close to villages and to spread financial and mineral interests across larger regions; it also meant that corporations would make decisions according to legal duties not identical with customary territorial obligations. The resulting compromise could command organized support without ending disagreement about the consequences of extinguishment.

The final bill represented compromise, not consensus. Native delegates debated its terms and ultimately endorsed it as the best attainable settlement, but not all agreed. Some leaders, especially in the North, objected to extinguishment of aboriginal title and to the corporate structure; others feared that their communities would receive too little. The compromise left questions unresolved, including subsistence and the relationship of corporations to tribal governments, that dominated the following decades.

Cause and Effect

Why ANCSA Became Law in 1971

Long-term causes

Unresolved Native title since 1867 and 1884; no treaty process in Alaska; the Tee-Hit-Ton decision, which pushed claimants toward Congress; decades of Native organizing from the ANB and ANS onward.

Short-term causes

Statehood land selections; protests from villages; the founding of the AFN in 1966; the Interior land freeze; Federal Field Committee documentation.

Immediate trigger

The Prudhoe Bay discovery and the pipeline’s need for rights-of-way across lands under claim, which gave oil companies, the state, and the Nixon administration reasons to seek resolution.

Immediate effects

A negotiated compromise: land and money for extinguishment of aboriginal claims, delivered through corporations rather than reservations.

Long-term consequences

A new Native corporate landholding system, continuing disputes over subsistence and sovereignty, the 1980 ANILCA, the 1988 amendments, and a model that influenced debate elsewhere in the Arctic.

Historical complexity

No single cause sufficed. Without Native organization there would have been no negotiating counterpart; without the freeze there would have been little land left to settle; without oil, Congress might have waited longer.
Division III · Section 115.9

The Enactment of ANCSA on December 18, 1971

Congress passed the compromise measure in mid-December 1971, and President Nixon signed it as Public Law 92-203 on December 18. The Act’s declaration of policy stated that there was an immediate need for a fair and just settlement of all claims by Natives and Native groups based on aboriginal land claims, and that the settlement should proceed rapidly, with certainty, and in conformity with the real economic and social needs of Natives. It specified that this be done without creating a reservation system or lengthy wardship or trusteeship. This statement was the legislative expression of a policy preference that distinguished ANCSA from earlier Indian settlements.

The Act’s financial terms provided $962.5 million. Of this sum, $462.5 million was to be appropriated from the federal Treasury over eleven years, and $500 million was to come from specified mineral-related receipts under Section 9: generally a two-percent royalty calculated on the gross value of designated mineral production and a two-percent share of relevant mineral lease rentals and bonuses from federal and state sources, subject to statutory exceptions. In particular, the original Act excluded specified payments associated with Alaska's September 1969 North Slope lease sale. Receipts flowed into the Alaska Native Fund until the statutory $500 million target was met; this was not simply an indefinite production royalty. The Act also conveyed title to about 44 million acres, composed of roughly 40 million acres selected by village and regional corporations and additional acreage for Natives of former reserves who chose to take title to those lands in place of other benefits. The Act created the Alaska Native Roll, a list of eligible Natives born on or before December 18, 1971 and ordinarily meeting a one-quarter Alaska Native ancestry definition, with an express alternative where the minimum ancestry could not be proved but the person and a parent were recognized as Native by an appropriate village or group, and with provisions concerning adopted persons. Enrollment produced a roll of around 80,000 people, including Alaska Natives living outside the state.

The headline amount should not be confused with an immediate cash transfer. The federal appropriation component was scheduled over years, mineral receipts depended on the relevant revenues, and corporations needed to form and establish accounts before receiving and administering funds. The land figure likewise described a statutory entitlement rather than 44 million fully surveyed acres delivered on signing day. These delays made administrative capacity, legal advice, land-selection deadlines, and knowledge of local geography consequential components of the settlement, not mere technical details.

The Act established a two-level corporate system, discussed in Section 115.11, and provided for selection and conveyance of land through a process that took decades. It also created the Joint Federal-State Land Use Planning Commission and authorized the Secretary of the Interior to study and set aside up to 80 million acres of federal “public interest” land under Section 17(d)(2) for possible parks, forests, wildlife refuges, and wild and scenic rivers. This provision became the basis of the 1980 Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act, discussed in Section 115.16. It is important to distinguish what the statute promised on the day of signing from the administrative work required to implement it. Enrollment, incorporation, selection, survey, and conveyance occupied federal agencies and Native corporations for decades, and some conveyances were incomplete at the end of this chapter’s principal period.

Division III · Section 115.10

The Extinguishment of Aboriginal Title and Traditional Claims

ANCSA’s most consequential and controversial provisions concern extinguishment. Section 4 declared the settlement of Native claims and extinguished aboriginal titles and claims of aboriginal title in Alaska based on use and occupancy, including submerged lands underneath inland and offshore waters, and including any aboriginal hunting or fishing rights that might exist. It also extinguished claims against the United States, the state, and other persons based on aboriginal right, title, use, or occupancy. Congress also repealed the 1906 Native Allotment Act, while preserving applications already pending, and revoked most of the existing reserves except the Annette Island Reserve of the Metlakatla community, discussed in Section 115.15.

Extinguishment meant that Alaska Natives could no longer assert, in court or administrative proceedings, that they held aboriginal title to lands outside the lands conveyed to them or that ancestral use gave them an independent legal right to hunt or fish. This was the price of the settlement. The AFN’s negotiators understood that without extinguishment the settlement would not pass, because the state and the oil industry needed clear title. Yet the provisions raised profound questions about ancestral authority. Many Native people regarded their relationships to lands and waters as not capable of being bought and sold or legislated out of existence, and elders at later hearings spoke of the losses in those terms.

It is essential to be precise about what extinguishment did not do. It extinguished specified property-based claims: aboriginal title, claims based on use and occupancy, and aboriginal hunting and fishing rights that might exist. It did not abolish Alaska Native tribes as political entities or terminate their government-to-government relationship with the United States. Federal Indian policy continued to recognize Alaska Native tribes and villages, as the 1993 Interior Department list of recognized Alaska Native entities confirmed, and Chapter 118 shows how courts have treated their governmental powers. Aboriginal title and tribal sovereignty are different legal concepts, and ANCSA addressed the first more directly than the second.

Division III · Section 115.11

The Creation of Regional and Village Corporations

ANCSA divided Alaska into twelve geographic regions, each with a regional corporation: Ahtna, the Aleut Corporation, Arctic Slope Regional Corporation, Bering Straits, Bristol Bay, Calista, Chugach Alaska, Cook Inlet Region (CIRI), Doyon, Koniag, NANA, and Sealaska. A thirteenth regional corporation was provided for eligible Alaska Natives living outside Alaska; it shared in the settlement money but, unlike the other twelve, received no land. Native villages meeting the Act’s criteria, generally having at least twenty-five Native residents and not being modern and urban in character, formed village corporations, which numbered more than two hundred. Many Natives in larger towns such as Anchorage and Fairbanks were represented through regional corporations and, in some cases, urban corporations.

These were corporations in the legal sense, incorporated under Alaska law, with boards of directors, shareholders, bylaws, and fiduciary duties. Each enrolled Native received 100 shares of stock in a regional corporation and, if eligible, in a village corporation. The original statute required regional corporations to organize as for-profit entities but expressly permitted eligible village corporations to organize under Alaska law as for-profit or nonprofit corporations. Business development became central to the settlement, while regional corporations were also expected to use their resources for broader community needs, and regional nonprofit associations continued to administer many health and social programs. The relationship between the two tiers was designed to share power: village corporations held surface rights to land near villages, and regional corporations held subsurface rights and further lands, as described in the next section.

Historical Vocabulary
Native Corporation and Tribal Government

Definition: A Native corporation is a business entity chartered under state law, owned by Alaska Native shareholders, and governed by a board accountable to those shareholders. A tribal government is a political entity possessing inherent governmental powers, recognized by the federal government, and governing citizens or members under its own constitution or customary authority.

Historical context: ANCSA created corporations, not tribal governments. Alaska Native tribes and villages continued to exist as governmental entities, often with the same people involved in both. Many villages therefore had both a tribal council and a village corporation, with different powers, memberships, and legal obligations.

Why it matters: A corporation may own land and operate businesses without possessing governmental powers such as law-making, taxation, or regulation of members. Questions of land and authority depend on which entity is involved.

Potential misunderstanding: Shareholding is not citizenship. Shares were issued to those on the 1971 roll, and later generations did not automatically become shareholders, while tribal membership follows each tribe’s own criteria.

The two organizational levels did not map neatly onto a political nation. A village might have a village corporation, a separate federally recognized tribal council, a municipal government, and a regional corporation in which its residents held shares. Those bodies could cooperate and share leaders, but their decision rules were not interchangeable. A regional board was accountable to shareholders across many communities, whereas a village corporation ordinarily concentrated on its own land and members. Tribal councils drew authority from governance structures and federal recognition rather than from ownership of ANCSA stock. Understanding these parallel institutions is necessary before evaluating later disputes about mining, environmental protection, enrollment, and the transfer of land to tribal governments.

The ANCSA structure was shaped by assumptions of its drafters. Corporations were seen as vehicles for economic development and as a way to avoid permanent federal trusteeship. Native leaders accepted them partly because the alternative, a federal trust system over Native land, seemed to many to imply paternalism and limited control, and partly because corporations offered flexibility and the possibility of wealth. Critics later argued that corporations placed commercial pressures on institutions that held land for cultural as well as economic reasons. Section 115.17 returns to this problem.

Division III · Section 115.12

Land Selection, Surface Ownership, and Subsurface Rights

The mechanics of land transfer shaped the settlement’s effects. Lands near each eligible village were withdrawn so that the village corporation could select its entitlement. The amount depended on the village’s enrolled population, ranging in five statutory tiers from 69,120 acres for villages with 25–99 Alaska Native residents to 92,160 acres for 100–199, 115,200 for 200–399, 138,240 for 400–599, and 161,280 for 600 or more, based on the applicable 1970 count. The figures correspond to multiples of the 23,040-acre survey township; the distinctive Southeast Alaska provisions are discussed in §115.15. Village corporations generally received title to the surface estate of the lands they selected. The regional corporation received the subsurface estate, meaning minerals and oil, beneath those village lands, and in addition received both surface and subsurface title to a separate allotment of roughly 16 million acres, selected within the region. Section 14(h) set aside land for historical places and cemeteries and for Native groups and individuals, and Section 14(c) required village corporations to reconvey land to individuals and families already occupying it for homes, businesses, and subsistence camps, and to local municipalities for community growth.

The surface and subsurface division produced practical consequences. A village corporation that owned the surface of its land could not alone authorize mining beneath it, and a regional corporation owning minerals needed access across surface lands owned by others. The two tiers were therefore interdependent, and cooperation or conflict between them affected development. The Act also reserved public easements across Native lands for access to public lands and waters, and required that selections be made in compact tracts within withdrawn areas, constraints that limited choice. Regions with extensive resource wealth, such as the North Slope, received subsurface interests of enormous value; other regions held lands with fewer commercially developable resources.

Consider a simplified case. If a qualifying village received a patent to 92,160 acres of surface land, the village corporation would generally decide questions such as surface leases and the use of timber, subject to law and other holders' rights. The regional corporation's subsurface estate could include minerals under those acres, so a prospective mineral project would involve distinct land interests. Neither owner could disregard the other's legally protected estate, and neither automatically received unrestricted access to nearby state or federal land. This example makes clear why saying that a village “received land” does not answer who could authorize a particular project or which residents could continue customary activities there.

Section 14(c) also prevented the selected land from functioning simply as one undivided private estate. Occupants of qualifying homes, businesses, and subsistence camps had specified reconveyance protections; municipal lands and areas needed for community growth were to be addressed; and existing claims and rights-of-way had to be considered. These transfers sometimes became contentious when boundaries, occupancy, or future local government needs were disputed. A selection map described a proposed landholding. Survey, interim conveyance, patent, and subsequent reconveyances determined the practical pattern of title, a sequence that could extend long beyond the original settlement date.

Conveyance was slow. After selection, the Bureau of Land Management had to survey boundaries, adjudicate conflicting claims including individual allotments, resolve state selections, and issue interim conveyances and patents. Decades after 1971, a considerable amount of land had still not been fully conveyed. The distinction between a corporation’s selection and its legal title mattered because title, not entitlement, determined whether it could develop, lease, or borrow against land. Legal title and resource ownership thus became central questions of the settlement’s implementation, shaping early corporate strategy and disputes.

Division IV · Section 115.13

Settlement Payments, Shareholders, and Corporate Membership

The money in the Alaska Native Fund moved through the corporate system. Federal appropriations and mineral royalties were paid into the Fund over time and distributed to the regional corporations under formulas tied to the number of enrolled Natives, with regional corporations required to pass portions to village corporations and, in specified amounts, directly to individual shareholders. These payments gave corporations capital for investment and operations. The early years were marked by inexperience: corporations hired managers, bought businesses and real estate, and sometimes invested poorly. Some succeeded and some struggled, and the variation across regions is treated in Sections 115.14, 115.15, and 115.19.

The shareholder system created a form of membership that was new to most Native communities. Each person on the roll received stock, and dividends depended on corporate performance. Initial eligibility generally required birth on or before December 18, 1971 and one-quarter Alaska Native ancestry, but the original law also recognized certain applicants who lacked proof of the threshold yet were regarded as Alaska Native by a village or group and whose parent had been so regarded. Persons born after the statutory date did not qualify for the original allotment solely by being born Alaska Native. They might subsequently inherit shares, and later legislation permitted additional classes of shares in specified circumstances, subject to corporate authorization; inheritance and newly issued shares are legally distinct routes into shareholding. This produced a generational question that grew over time. Families came to include shareholders and nonshareholders, and some Native people who were fully members of their tribal communities had no corporate shares. Chapter 144 examines blood quantum and citizenship more broadly.

Enrollment generated more than one kind of connection. A person enrolled to a particular village ordinarily held an interest in that village's corporation and its regional corporation, while an eligible non-village resident might hold regional shares without membership in a village corporation. The thirteenth-region arrangement was different again because it supplied a cash interest without an associated regional land entitlement. These classifications affected distribution formulas and participation in corporate elections, but they did not determine whether a person belonged to an Indigenous nation in the political or cultural sense. Conversely, an Alaska Native born after the cutoff could be fully recognized in a tribal community while lacking original ANCSA shares.

The practical problem was not only whether an individual received dividends. Decisions about selecting directors, approving changes to stock restrictions, and creating new classes of shares depended on shareholder voting. A system intended to prevent non-Native acquisition therefore also gave the initial enrolled generation unusually strong authority over how later-born descendants would participate. Families could reasonably disagree: issuing new shares might broaden inclusion but alter existing shareholders' economic interests, while preserving the original share base could exclude people who lived and worked in the same community. Section 115.18 explains why Congress later changed the available options without requiring one uniform solution.

Corporate membership and community belonging overlapped but did not coincide. A shareholder might live in a distant city or in another state, while a tribal member living in the village might not own stock. Corporations’ obligations ran to shareholders as a body, whereas tribal governments’ responsibilities ran to residents and citizens in a community. This distinction shaped debates over how to use corporate profits, whether for dividends, jobs, scholarships, cultural programs, or reinvestment.

Division IV · Section 115.14

Resource Revenue Sharing and the Corporate Economic System

Congress recognized that the settlement’s regions held unequal resources. Section 7(i) therefore required the twelve regional corporations to share 70 percent of the revenues they received from timber and subsurface resources, distributing them among all twelve in proportion to the number of enrolled Natives in each region. Original Section 7(j) required the regional corporations, during the first five years after enactment, to distribute at least 45 percent of funds from the specified sources—receipts under Section 6 (the Alaska Native Fund), Section 7(i), and other net income—to village corporations and the class of shareholders not residing in villages. The original percentage increased to at least 50 percent thereafter. A separate original provision required at least 10 percent of those specified sources to be distributed among regional shareholders during the first five years. Thus the law did not simply direct 50 percent of each corporation's Section 7(i) receipts to its villages from day one. The system was meant to ensure that resource wealth benefited Native Alaskans broadly and not only those who happened to live above oil, timber, or mineral deposits.

Revenue sharing was historically significant and politically contentious. Regions with substantial resource revenue, including the North Slope and regions with timber or major mines, contributed to the pool, while regions with little commercial resource wealth drew from it. The formula for calculating contributions led to disputes and litigation among regional corporations in the 1970s and afterward, over what counted as a revenue and which expenses could be deducted, and negotiated agreements eventually established accounting rules. The Government Accountability Office and other federal bodies later reviewed the system’s operation.

The two statutory mechanisms answered different distribution problems. Section 7(i) was interregional: it pooled 70 percent of specified natural-resource revenues received by each landholding region and allocated the pool among the twelve according to enrolled population. Section 7(j) was intraregional: it distributed minimum shares of designated funds from each regional corporation to village corporations and non-village resident shareholders within its own region. Section 7(k) divided the village portion according to the relevant village-resident shareholdings, while Section 7(m) set a proportionate relationship for payments to non-village resident shareholders. The formulas should not be confused with an equal payment to every Alaska Native person or with a distribution of the land itself.

For illustration, a region that received a qualifying allocation from the 7(i) pool still had to consider its other receipts and the applicable 7(j) requirements in deciding what portion to distribute within the region. Some villages were able to invest cash receipts in local enterprises or community facilities; others had fewer commercially viable opportunities. The rules generated intercorporate disputes over which resource proceeds were shareable and how costs were treated, and debates within regions over dividends versus investment. They were therefore a form of negotiated distributive policy embedded in corporate law, not simply an accounting formula.

Section 7(i) expressed a particular understanding of the settlement: the land and money belonged to all Alaska Natives, not only to the local community that happened to hold a resource. It also created tensions between profitability and obligation. A corporation that discovered a valuable deposit had to share most of the proceeds, which reduced the incentive for aggressive development and raised questions about fairness. At the same time, the system helped resource-poor regions, such as the Yukon-Kuskokwim, where most villages had little commercially exploitable land, remain participants in the settlement’s benefits.

Division IV · Section 115.15

Regional Differences and the Southeast Alaska Exception

The settlement did not apply uniformly. The twelve regions differed enormously in geography, population, resources, and prior history, and their land and corporate arrangements reflected those differences. On the North Slope, Arctic Slope Regional Corporation, organized under Iñupiat leadership, received subsurface rights near Prudhoe Bay and other oil and gas prospects. Iñupiat leaders, notably Eben Hopson, helped create the North Slope Borough in 1972, a public local government with taxing power over the oil infrastructure, giving the region a distinct institutional base. In the Northwest Arctic, NANA later developed the Red Dog zinc mine in partnership with a mining company. In the Interior, Doyon, covering Athabascan homelands, received the largest land entitlement of any regional corporation, approximately 12.5 million acres in entitlement, the largest regional total, although entitlement was not identical to acreage already patented at any given time; much of the region had fewer readily marketable resources. In the Yukon-Kuskokwim region and Bristol Bay, Calista and Bristol Bay Native Corporation represented large Yup’ik, Cup’ik, and Athabascan populations with limited commercial lands but extensive subsistence economies.

Southeast Alaska was the most distinctive case. The Tlingit, Haida, and Tsimshian people had pursued their claim through the Court of Claims since 1935, and the 1968 judgment had already addressed compensation for lands within the Tongass National Forest and Glacier Bay. Most Southeast land was federally owned within the Tongass, which Congress was reluctant to convey. Scholars have pointed to these factors in explaining why ANCSA gave Southeast villages far smaller land entitlements than villages elsewhere, with Section 16 generally specifying 23,040 acres—one survey township—for a qualifying Southeast village, rather than the larger five-tier Section 14(a) scale used elsewhere, and why Sealaska’s land base was modest compared with other regional corporations. Sealaska nonetheless developed substantial timber operations and revenue, and its contributions to the Section 7(i) pool became significant. The Tsimshian community of Metlakatla, whose Annette Island Reserve dated from 1891, remained outside the ANCSA land system, retaining its reserve. Section 19(b) created another special route: seven village corporations initially elected to take title to five former reserves rather than receive the usual package of ANCSA benefits. They were the corporations associated with Elim, Klukwan, Tetlin, Gambell and Savoonga on St. Lawrence Island, and Venetie and Arctic Village on the former Venetie reserve. Klukwan subsequently changed course under amended statutory provisions, conveying former-reserve lands to the Chilkat Indian Village tribal government and receiving alternative settlement land and benefits. The seven original elections are thus not the same as a permanent list of seven corporations retaining former-reserve title.

These exceptions mattered because their legal consequences differed even when the land lay near Native homes. The federally recognized Metlakatla community retained the Annette Island Reserve, whose status was expressly protected by the 1971 Act. Communities making former-reserve elections received a different estate and relinquished specified conventional ANCSA distributions. The ordinary village corporation in Southeast Alaska generally received a smaller Section 16 entitlement, whereas elsewhere the applicable tier depended on Native population. A map showing all these areas as equally “Native land” conceals the differences among federal trust arrangements, selected fee lands, surface-only estates, and former-reserve holdings.

Taken together, these differences show that ANCSA is better understood as a framework applied differently across regions than as a uniform settlement. A statement that Alaska Natives received 44 million acres conceals the variation in who held it, what lay beneath it, and what restrictions applied.

Division IV · Section 115.16

Subsistence Rights and the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act

For most Alaska Native households, hunting, fishing, and gathering were not recreation but the foundation of food, culture, and community life. Salmon, caribou, moose, whale, seal, walrus, waterfowl, berries, and many other resources provided food and were shared through kinship and community networks. ANCSA’s extinguishment of aboriginal hunting and fishing rights therefore struck at a central part of Native life. Congress did not place subsistence protections in the Act. Instead, the committee report accompanying the final bill expressed an expectation that the Secretary of the Interior and the State of Alaska would take action to protect Native subsistence needs. That expectation was not an enforceable right, and it left the question to later administrative and legislative decisions.

Historical Vocabulary
Subsistence

Definition: In Alaska usage, customary and traditional hunting, fishing, and gathering to provide food, materials, and cultural continuity, including sharing and exchange within communities.

Historical context: In law, “subsistence” became a category through federal and state statutes. The term does not imply poverty; it describes a mixed economy in which wild foods and cash income coexist.

Why it matters: Subsistence connects food, culture, and land relationships, and its legal status after 1971 became a central unresolved question.

Potential misunderstanding: Subsistence rights are not identical to landownership, to treaty rights in the lower forty-eight, or to aboriginal rights. They depend on the particular statute or regulation that creates them.

Other federal laws and institutions partially addressed subsistence in specific sectors. The Marine Mammal Protection Act of 1972 included an exemption for Alaska Native subsistence harvests of marine mammals. When international whaling regulators moved in 1977 to ban the Native bowhead whale hunt, Iñupiat whaling captains formed the Alaska Eskimo Whaling Commission to negotiate and later co-manage the hunt with federal authorities. These arrangements protected particular harvests but did not provide a general guarantee.

The central legislative response was the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act (ANILCA), signed by President Jimmy Carter on December 2, 1980. It arose from ANCSA’s own provision allowing the withdrawal of public interest lands, and it set aside more than 100 million acres in new or expanded parks, refuges, forests, and other conservation units. Title VIII of ANILCA established a priority for subsistence uses by rural Alaska residents on federal public lands when restrictions are necessary. Its congressional findings also recognize the importance of Alaska Native subsistence on Native lands; that acknowledgement is not the same thing as extending the operative federal rural priority to every acre held by an ANCSA corporation. Congress based the priority on rural residence, not Native ancestry, in part because of concerns about constitutional equal-protection limits and the political difficulty of creating a Native-specific preference. The rural priority therefore protected many Native communities, since most Native people lived in rural areas, but it also covered non-Native rural residents and excluded Natives living in urban areas.

The framework remained contested. Alaska’s own subsistence law was tested when, in 1989, the Alaska Supreme Court in McDowell v. State held that the state’s rural preference violated the state constitution’s provisions on equal access to fish and wildlife. Because the state could not maintain a rural preference, federal officials assumed management of subsistence on federal public lands in 1990, creating a dual system of state and federal management that persisted. Native leaders argued that a rural priority was no substitute for Indigenous rights, while many non-Native Alaskans resisted any preference. Subsistence thus remained one of ANCSA’s most consequential unresolved questions. Chapter 135 treats modern wildlife and fisheries management more fully.

Everyday Life and Social History

Village Life, Shared Food, and the Cash Economy

The settlement’s abstract provisions were experienced in the rhythm of village life. Families moved between summer fish camps, fall hunting trips, and winter villages. Households processed salmon, dried and stored meat, and shared food with relatives and elders in networks that carried obligations of reciprocity. At the same time, village residents depended on cash from wages, fishing, construction, and, after 1971, corporate employment and dividends, which they used for fuel, equipment, and store-bought goods. Gasoline, snowmachines, boats, and ammunition made subsistence expensive and tied it to the cash economy.

The distinction between hunting on a corporation's land and harvesting a migratory resource under state or federal rules made the problem especially difficult. An Alaska Native family might hold shares in a corporation that owned a stretch of riverbank, yet fishing in that river could still be subject to separate allocation and conservation rules. A village's long-standing use of adjacent federal land did not automatically become a private right merely because nearby acreage was conveyed to its corporation. Subsistence was therefore a problem of access, law, ecology, and governance at once; it could not be answered by the acreage settlement alone.

The Federal Field Committee’s 1968 report documented harsh conditions: limited employment, crowded housing, and inadequate sanitation in many villages. ANCSA corporations brought new jobs and dividends, but also new decisions about land use, new meetings and annual shareholder votes, and new tensions within communities. Elders, who held knowledge of land and tradition, sometimes found themselves outside corporate decision-making, while younger people left for cities or faced uncertain prospects as nonshareholders. Women played important roles as board members, shareholders, and cultural leaders, though sources have more often recorded male corporate executives.

Division V · Section 115.17

Alaska Native Corporations and Tribal Governments

After 1971 two kinds of Native institution coexisted in most Alaska communities. Federally recognized tribal governments, in the form of village councils organized under customary authority or, in many cases, under the 1936 extension of the Indian Reorganization Act to Alaska, exercised governmental powers over members and, to the extent recognized, over territory. State-chartered corporations owned land and conducted business. The two often overlapped in personnel and purpose, but they differed in authority, accountability, and legal status. A corporation’s board owed fiduciary duties to shareholders; a tribal council answered to the community’s citizens or members. A corporation could own land in fee simple, but its land was not reservation or trust land, and its owners did not by that fact gain governmental powers over it.

The choice of corporations over a conventional trust-land system had consequences. Reservation land in the lower forty-eight is generally held in trust by the United States, protected from sale and taxation, and tied to tribal jurisdiction. ANCSA lands were held in fee by corporations, which could in principle be taxed, sold, or lost to creditors once statutory protections lapsed. The Act’s drafters considered this a feature, since it would free Native land from federal wardship. Critics considered it a danger, since land that could be alienated could be lost. Section 115.18 shows how Congress responded to that danger.

The consequences for governance became especially clear in the case of Venetie. Residents of Venetie and Arctic Village, whose former reserve had been established in 1943, chose under Section 19(b) to take title to roughly 1.8 million acres in place of other ANCSA benefits. They then transferred the land from the village corporation to their tribal government. When the tribe tried to tax a non-Native contractor, the Supreme Court held in Alaska v. Native Village of Venetie Tribal Government (1998) that the ANCSA-conveyed lands were not “Indian country,” because they had not been set apart for Indian use under federal superintendence, which defeated the particular asserted territorial taxing power on that land. The Court's conclusion about those lands did not mean that the Native Village of Venetie ceased to exist as a tribe or that every form of tribal authority throughout Alaska disappeared. Chapter 118 analyzes the decision and its jurisdictional implications; for this chapter, its importance is that it showed how the corporate settlement design reduced the territorial authority of tribal governments. Another institutional outcome was the North Slope Borough, a public local government shaped by Iñupiat leadership and not a tribal government or corporation.

Multiple Perspectives

One Settlement, Different Priorities

Note: the perspectives below are analytical syntheses of positions documented in the historical record, not quotations from named individuals. The groups held unequal power.

Regional Native leaders: Many saw the settlement as a historic achievement that secured land and capital and gave Alaska Natives a basis for economic independence and political influence. They also worried about unequal regional resources and the pressures of corporate management.
Village residents and subsistence users: Many valued the land and money but emphasized the continued importance of hunting, fishing, and community life. They were concerned about the loss of aboriginal hunting and fishing claims and about decisions made in distant corporate offices.
Corporate shareholders and directors: Shareholders sought dividends, jobs, and security for their inheritance, while directors faced fiduciary duties, market risk, and competing demands. Their priorities could differ from those of residents who relied on land for subsistence.
The State of Alaska: State officials sought resolution of title so that development and state land selections could proceed, and they were wary of Native-specific hunting and fishing rights that would conflict with state law.
Federal policymakers: Congress and the executive branch sought a final, rapid settlement that would clear the pipeline route and avoid a reservation system, while supporting Native participation in the economy.
Division V · Section 115.18

The 1991 Stock Question and the ANCSA Amendments

ANCSA originally restricted the sale of corporate stock for twenty years, until December 18, 1991, and during that time undeveloped Native corporation land was exempt from state and local taxation and protected from certain claims. As 1991 approached, Native leaders and shareholders feared that when the restrictions ended, stock could be sold to outsiders, corporations could be taken over by non-Natives, or corporate land could be taxed, mortgaged, or lost to creditors. Because shares were tied to land and capital, many saw the 1991 date as a threat to the settlement’s central purpose of preserving Native ownership.

The Alaska Native Review Commission, led by Canadian jurist Thomas Berger and sponsored by the Inuit Circumpolar Conference, held hearings in dozens of villages in 1983–1985 and published its report, Village Journey, in 1985. It recorded widespread anxiety about the corporate structure and the fate of land, and it recommended strengthening tribal governments and securing land in tribal ownership. The commission’s hearings gave villagers a venue to speak that the legislative process had often denied them, and they remain an important source for the settlement’s lived history.

Congress responded with the 1987 amendments, enacted in February 1988. They continued the restrictions on selling stock indefinitely unless shareholders of a corporation voted to lift them, and they protected undeveloped corporate land from taxation, adverse possession, and certain creditors’ claims. The 1988 amendments also authorized, at corporate option and subject to specified approvals, the issuance of additional shares to eligible Natives born after 1971 and other designated groups, created a statutory framework for settlement trusts to hold assets for beneficiaries, and facilitated certain protected transfers of land to tribal governments and other Native entities. In 1992, Public Law 102-415 expressly enlarged the optional additional-share category to include descendants of Alaska Natives born after December 18, 1971; this later amendment should not be silently attributed to the 1988 enactment. These reforms attempted to protect Native ownership while leaving key decisions to shareholders.

Stock protections, land protections, and inheritance addressed different vulnerabilities. Restrictions on alienating settlement common stock guarded against a takeover through ordinary purchases; undeveloped-land protections guarded against certain tax and creditor risks; inheritance rules determined who could receive existing shares on an owner's death. None of these rules guaranteed that every Native person born later would obtain a voting interest. A corporation's decision to issue shares to new groups could require shareholder approval and might affect dividend expectations. Settlement trusts offered another way to protect assets for beneficiaries, but a trust established by a corporation was not simply the same legal institution as a tribal government or federal Indian trust land.

The amendments also exposed enduring questions. Corporations were not required to enroll new shareholders, so a generation of Native people could remain outside the corporate system. Inheritance affected shareholding, as shares passed to heirs, and some heirs were not themselves Alaska Native. And the issue of whether land should be held by corporations or tribal governments remained unresolved, a question Chapters 118 and 139 take up.

Division V · Section 115.19

Development, Environmental Protection, and the Contradictions of Corporate Ownership

Native corporations faced the dilemma of managing land and money to meet several goals at once: generating revenue for shareholders, providing jobs, protecting ancestral places, conserving resources, and maintaining community access. The outcomes varied widely. Sealaska and a number of village corporations in Southeast Alaska and Prince William Sound harvested timber heavily in the 1980s to generate income, drawing criticism from residents who valued forests for subsistence and culture. Cook Inlet Region developed oil and gas and urban real estate around Anchorage. NANA’s Red Dog mine, operating under an agreement signed in 1982 with a mining company, provided jobs and royalties in the Northwest Arctic but also raised concerns about environmental impact on the lands and waters used for hunting and fishing. Some village corporations with little commercial land struggled financially, and some corporations incurred losses through poor investments in the early years.

Environmental events highlighted the tensions. The Exxon Valdez oil spill in March 1989 damaged Prince William Sound, where Chugach villages such as Chenega Bay and Tatitlek depended on fish, shellfish, and marine mammals. The spill affected subsistence harvests and community life, and it showed that corporate landholdings did not insulate Native communities from environmental risks created by outside development. Later controversies over oil and gas development in the Arctic National Wildlife Refuge, which involved Iñupiat corporations and Gwich’in communities with opposing interests, extended these tensions, as Chapters 136 and 137 discuss.

The financial and environmental goals could collide within the same household. A shareholder might welcome a dividend or employment generated by a development project while depending on the same watershed for seasonal food and cultural practices. A corporation had responsibilities under corporate law, while the nearby tribal government or community council might judge a project by different standards. Some disputes therefore took place among Alaska Native institutions and citizens, not only between Native communities and outside businesses. These tensions do not invalidate the settlement's achievements; they reveal why commercial ownership was an imperfect institutional substitute for the full range of relationships that communities maintained with land.

Assessing success is therefore difficult. Total acreage, revenue, and dividends are useful measures, but none alone measures the settlement’s consequences for communities. A region with large land holdings might have modest income; a profitable corporation might have little effect on village poverty; a corporation that protected land might forgo revenue. The settlement also created institutions that Native people could use for their own purposes: corporations funded scholarships, supported cultural programs, hired Native workers, and became political actors in state and national affairs. Chapter 121 considers the broader history of Indigenous economic development.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Indigenous Landownership Before and After ANCSA

What Changed

  • Land relationships based on customary use, clan or family territory, and recognized authority were replaced, for specified lands, by fee-simple corporate title.
  • The unresolved aboriginal-title regime of 1867–1971 gave way to a statutory settlement that extinguished aboriginal claims.
  • Wealth and decision-making moved into boards, shareholder meetings, and business plans.
  • Membership in the settlement institutions was defined by enrollment and blood quantum as of 1971.

What Stayed the Same

  • Native communities remained in their homelands and continued subsistence practices and sharing networks.
  • Tribal governments and village councils continued to exist and to be recognized by the federal government.
  • Native people continued to assert claims to land, resources, and political voice.
  • Relationships to land remained cultural and spiritual as well as economic.

Pattern of change: The change was abrupt in law, with the enactment of 1971, but uneven in practice, since conveyance of land took decades and customs of land use persisted. Those who held corporate leadership experienced the change most directly; subsistence users experienced greater continuity in daily life but new uncertainty in legal rights. The pattern matters because it shows that legal transformation did not erase Indigenous relationships to land, and that settlement design shaped how those relationships could be defended.

Division V · Section 115.20

The Historical Legacy of Alaska Native Land Claims

The history of Alaska Native land claims shows how Indigenous political organization can reshape the terms of a colonial settlement. Native leaders built a statewide coalition from scattered villages and diverse peoples, used the land freeze and the political pressure of oil development, and won title to tens of millions of acres and a large capital fund, an achievement without parallel in the history of federal Indian policy. At the same time, the settlement was shaped by a Congress that sought rapid closure, by an oil industry that wanted certainty, and by assumptions that corporations and individual ownership would replace trusteeship. Native negotiators accepted extinguishment and the corporate form as the price of land they could control.

The legacy is therefore mixed in ways that depend on perspective. The settlement produced Native-owned institutions with economic and political weight, and it gave Alaska Natives a land base that no other Indigenous group in the United States secured through legislation of this kind. It also left subsistence unprotected by the settlement itself, created a generational divide in membership, and set corporations alongside tribal governments in ways that complicated questions of sovereignty. Alaska’s model differs from the reservation system found across much of the United States, and from the modern treaty agreements negotiated in Arctic Canada, which Chapter 116 examines, including the creation of Nunavut. Chapter 119 treats later land-recovery movements and sacred landscapes, and Chapter 148 returns to sovereignty in a changing North America.

Historical Debate

Was ANCSA a Model of Indigenous Empowerment or a Settlement with Structural Limitations?

The question: Historians, legal scholars, and Alaska Natives themselves disagree about how to judge the settlement. Did it empower Native people by giving them land, capital, and institutions, or did its structure, especially extinguishment, corporate ownership, and the absence of subsistence protections, impose limits that undermined Indigenous self-determination?

Interpretation A — Empowerment: Supporters emphasize the scale of the land and money, Native control of the corporations, the creation of Native-owned businesses that became major regional economic forces, and Native political influence in Alaska. They argue that the settlement was the best outcome attainable given political constraints and that the corporations provided a durable economic foundation.
Interpretation B — Structural limitations: Critics, including the Alaska Native Review Commission in 1985, emphasize extinguishment of aboriginal title and hunting and fishing claims, the risk that corporate land could be lost, the focus on shareholders rather than communities, and the failure to secure subsistence rights. They argue that the corporate model reflected federal assimilationist assumptions and weakened tribal governments.

Evidence: Both sides draw on the text of the Act and its amendments, corporate financial records, subsistence harvest data, court decisions, and village testimony. Evidence of corporate profits, jobs, and acreage supports the first view; evidence of subsistence conflict, shareholder exclusion, and limits on tribal jurisdiction supports the second.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and weighting of evidence, not the basic facts: how to compare economic gains with legal concessions, whether the settlement’s flaws were inherent or correctable through amendment, and what standard to apply. It also reflects real differences among Alaska Natives by region, generation, and economic circumstance, so neither view should be assigned to all Native people.

Why it matters: How historians answer shapes whether ANCSA is treated as a model for other Indigenous settlements or a cautionary example, and how its unresolved issues, such as subsistence and tribal authority, are understood.

Student task: Select two categories of evidence from this chapter and explain how each could support either interpretation. Then identify one further source, such as regional corporation records or village testimony, that could help clarify the question.

For students, the decisive comparison is among land entitlement, corporate governance, and political sovereignty. ANCSA enlarged the first and created a distinctive institutional framework for the second, but it did not define the third in a comprehensive way. The same village could experience a stronger economic position while still seeking recognition of governing powers or subsistence protections that the original settlement did not provide. That tension explains why Alaska Native politics continued after 1971 and why later debates about the status of land, shares, descendants, and public resources were intrinsic to the settlement's history rather than evidence that the land-claims movement had ended unsuccessfully.

Finally, ANCSA belongs to the continental story told in this textbook. Alaska’s Iñupiat share language, kinship, and hunting traditions with Inuit communities across the Canadian Arctic and Greenland, and the Inuit Circumpolar Conference, founded at Utqiaġvik (then Barrow) in 1977, joined these peoples in an international movement, as Chapter 143 explains. The Alaska settlement was one response to a common question facing Arctic peoples: how to secure land, resources, and political voice within settler states. The answers differed across borders, and understanding why requires seeing both the achievements and the limits of the Alaskan path.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how Alaska Native peoples organized to defend their ancestral lands and negotiated the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of 1971. Its central argument is that ANCSA secured an unprecedented land and financial settlement, but did so through a corporate ownership system that created lasting tensions among Indigenous nationhood, commercial development, subsistence practices, and ancestral relationships to land.

The settlement’s foundations lay in Indigenous homelands. Alaska was home to Iñupiat, Yupik, Yup’ik, Cup’ik, Unangax̂, Alutiiq, Athabascan, Eyak, Tlingit, Haida, and Tsimshian peoples, each with territorial systems based on use, kinship, and recognized authority. These systems predated and differed from any European concept of land tenure, and the Indigenous presence meant that Alaska could not be treated as empty.

Russian colonization, which bore most heavily on the Unangax̂ and Alutiiq and met sustained Tlingit resistance, produced claims that Russia transferred to the United States in 1867. Because Alaska Natives were not parties to the Treaty of Cession, the transfer was not an Indigenous cession. The Organic Act of 1884 acknowledged Native possession but left the terms of title to Congress, and no treaty process ever resolved the question.

Early organizing, through the Alaska Native Brotherhood and Sisterhood and the Tlingit and Haida litigation, demonstrated both political capacity and the limits of the courts. The 1968 judgment awarded compensation calculated from several specific dates of federal takings between 1891 and 1925 rather than uniformly from 1867, and the Supreme Court’s Tee-Hit-Ton decision in 1955 showed that unrecognized aboriginal title could be extinguished without compensation. These experiences pointed claimants toward a legislative settlement.

Statehood in 1958–1959 transformed the situation. The state’s right to select roughly 104 million acres collided with Native land use, and villages filed protests. The Alaska Federation of Natives, founded in 1966, united diverse regions; Interior Secretary Udall’s land freeze preserved the lands at issue; and the 1968 Prudhoe Bay discovery gave the state, the oil industry, and the federal government strong reasons to settle. These conditions interacted: none alone was sufficient.

ANCSA, signed on December 18, 1971, conveyed roughly 44 million acres and $962.5 million through twelve regional and more than two hundred village corporations, with a thirteenth regional corporation for nonresident Natives. It extinguished aboriginal title and claims based on use and occupancy, including aboriginal hunting and fishing rights, but it did not extinguish Alaska Native tribes. Land was divided into surface and subsurface estates, and revenue-sharing provisions in Sections 7(i) and 7(j) redistributed resource income among regions and villages.

Implementation revealed the settlement’s complexity. Regions differed in land, resources, and prior history, and Southeast Alaska received a distinctive and more limited land arrangement. Corporate shareholding, based on a 1971 roll with a blood-quantum threshold, created a form of membership distinct from tribal citizenship and excluded those born later unless corporations chose to include them.

Subsistence became the settlement’s most contested unresolved issue. Congress left it to future action, and ANILCA’s 1980 rural priority on federal lands provided partial protection that was based on residence rather than Indigenous status. State-federal conflicts followed, including the 1989 McDowell decision and federal management beginning in 1990.

The question of how corporate land would be protected led to the 1991 stock debate and the 1988 amendments, which kept restrictions on stock sales unless shareholders voted otherwise and protected undeveloped land. Meanwhile, the coexistence of corporations and tribal governments, illustrated by Venetie, left the relationship between land ownership and sovereign authority unsettled, a subject Chapter 118 takes up.

ANCSA should be assessed neither as unqualified triumph nor as failure. It transferred land and capital on a scale unmatched elsewhere in the United States and created durable Native-owned institutions, while extinguishing claims, adopting a corporate form that critics found vulnerable, and leaving subsistence and sovereignty unresolved. Its history shows Indigenous peoples shaping, and being constrained by, the terms of a colonial settlement.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 115 matters because it shows how an Indigenous political movement, working within the constraints of a settler state, won a settlement that changed landownership across an entire state. It reveals that the terms of such settlements are shaped by Indigenous organization, by the interests of governments and industries, and by assumptions about what Indigenous institutions should look like.

It also reveals the importance of distinctions that the settlement blurred. Aboriginal title, tribal sovereignty, corporate ownership, shareholder membership, and subsistence rights are different things, and ANCSA addressed them unevenly. Understanding the settlement requires keeping them apart, as later chapters on tribal jurisdiction, economic development, and wildlife management will show.

The chapter’s developments shaped what followed. They informed the debates of the 1980s and 1990s over corporate land and tribal governments, the continuing conflict over subsistence management, and the way Native corporations became major economic and political actors in Alaska. They also entered a wider Arctic conversation, as Inuit in Canada pursued comprehensive land-claim agreements of their own, the subject of Chapter 116.

Finally, the chapter reinforces one of this textbook’s governing claims: Indigenous nations are continuing political and historical communities. Alaska Native peoples used the instruments available to them to protect their homelands, and the questions they raised about land, law, and nationhood remain open.
