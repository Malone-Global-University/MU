Columbus and the Opening of the Atlantic Invasion

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

In the early morning of 12 October 1492, by the Julian calendar then in use, people living on a small island in the Lucayan Archipelago saw three European ships approach their shore. Within hours the newcomers had gone ashore, performed a ceremony of possession that nobody on the beach could have understood, and begun to treat a populated landscape as if it were an empty prize. The Norse had come and gone. These ships would be followed by thousands more.

Chapter 37 ended with a crossing that did not last. Norse settlers lived in Greenland for roughly four centuries and left a brief, archaeologically documented outpost at L'Anse aux Meadows, but neither presence generated a continuous stream of ships, colonists, soldiers, and institutions moving between Europe and the Americas. After 1492 that stream began and did not stop. The first voyage of Christopher Columbus was a reconnaissance carried out with three small ships. By 1493 a second expedition of some seventeen ships carried settlers, livestock, seeds, clergy, and soldiers. By the first decade of the sixteenth century a Spanish governor presided over a colonial capital on Hispaniola, and by the 1510s Spanish ports, ships, interpreters, and armed companies stood ready on several Caribbean islands to move farther west.

This chapter asks how that transition happened and what it meant for Indigenous peoples. It begins not with Columbus but with the Caribbean, an archipelago that had been inhabited for millennia by societies with agricultural economies, hereditary leadership, long-distance canoe networks, ceremonial landscapes, and their own political rivalries. Those societies did not receive the Spanish as strangers arriving in an empty space. They interpreted the newcomers through their own diplomatic conventions, tested them through gift exchange, allied with or resisted them according to local interests, and shaped the early course of colonization in ways that Spanish sources only partly reveal.

The chapter then follows the four voyages of Columbus between 1492 and 1504, but it treats him as one actor in a larger process. Spanish monarchs, Iberian shipowners, royal officials, clergy, settlers, and soldiers all shaped what happened, and so did the Indigenous leaders who sheltered, fed, resisted, deceived, or fought them. The chapter traces how a voyage of exploration became a program of colonization, how tribute and forced labor emerged on Hispaniola, how enslavement and the Atlantic transport of captives began, how European rulers and church authorities claimed sovereignty over an ocean and the lands beyond it, and how the Caribbean became a network of Spanish bases by about 1513.

The evidence for this history is uneven and often troubling. The surviving written record was produced almost entirely by Spaniards, and the principal account of the first voyage, Columbus's journal, survives only through a later summary by Bartolomé de las Casas. Archaeology, ethnohistory, linguistics, genetics, and the continuing knowledge of Indigenous Caribbean descendants provide essential correctives. This chapter makes the problem of evidence explicit, in Section 38.11 and throughout, because students should be able to distinguish what Columbus believed, what Spanish writers reported, what archaeology can show, and what remains unknown.

This chapter also observes the boundaries the textbook has set. It introduces disease because mortality among Indigenous Caribbean people began early and because the biological exchange was part of the new Atlantic system, but the full analysis of epidemic catastrophe belongs to Chapter 40. It describes the Caribbean as a launching ground for mainland expeditions but stops at the threshold; the conquest of Mesoamerica belongs to Chapter 39, Florida and the Southeast to Chapter 41, and the northern frontier of New Spain to Chapter 42. Its task is to establish the machinery and logic of sustained colonization, the ships, institutions, legal claims, labor systems, and bases, without narrating what those later chapters own.

The chapter's central argument is that 1492 mattered not because Europeans discovered an unknown continent, since the continent was known and inhabited by its own peoples, but because Columbus's voyages opened a sustained Atlantic corridor through which ships, settlers, soldiers, animals, plants, pathogens, institutions, and imperial claims could repeatedly cross. Indigenous Caribbean peoples first met the newcomers through their own systems of diplomacy, exchange, and alliance. The repeated arrival of armed colonists, backed by a state that claimed sovereignty over the land, converted encounter into invasion, settlement, forced labor, enslavement, and conquest.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the post-1492 Atlantic crossing differed in kind from the earlier Norse presence, using the concept of a sustained Atlantic corridor.
  2. Describe the political, economic, religious, and maritime organization of Indigenous Caribbean societies before 1492, and explain why they cannot be treated as an undifferentiated population.
  3. Distinguish among the labels Taíno, Lucayan, Kalinago, and "Carib," explaining which are Indigenous, which are colonial, and which are modern scholarly conventions.
  4. Identify the Iberian institutions, technologies, and precedents, including Atlantic island colonization, sugar, slavery, and papal authorization, that Columbus's expeditions carried westward.
  5. Trace the four voyages of Columbus and the founding of La Navidad, La Isabela, and Santo Domingo, distinguishing exploration from colonization.
  6. Analyze the first encounters on Guanahaní and Hispaniola as diplomatic events interpreted differently by Indigenous leaders and Spanish observers.
  7. Evaluate the evidentiary basis for the first voyage, including the transmission of Columbus's journal through Las Casas, and compare it with archaeological and Indigenous evidence.
  8. Explain how tribute, repartimiento, encomienda, and enslavement emerged on Hispaniola as evolving institutions rather than a finished system.
  9. Assess the legal and ideological claims, including papal bulls, the Treaty of Tordesillas, and the Requerimiento, by which Iberian powers asserted sovereignty without Indigenous consent.
  10. Analyze Indigenous resistance, alliance, flight, and survival as historical agency, avoiding narratives of inevitable disappearance.
  11. Evaluate competing terms for the period, including encounter, contact, conquest, and invasion, and explain what each emphasizes or conceals.
  12. Explain how the Caribbean became the base for later mainland expeditions while recognizing that those outcomes were not predetermined.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the Norse crossing remain episodic while the crossings after 1492 became continuous, and which differences were most important: technology, state backing, motives, geography, or the Indigenous response?
  2. What did Indigenous Caribbean societies look like in 1492, and how does beginning with them rather than with Columbus change the story?
  3. How did Indigenous leaders and Spanish observers interpret the same acts of gift-giving, hospitality, and exchange, and what consequences followed from the differences?
  4. How do historians reconstruct the first voyage when the principal document survives only through a later intermediary and no Indigenous account of the event survives in comparable form?
  5. At what point, and through what decisions, did Spanish activity on Hispaniola shift from trading and exploration to settlement, tribute, and forced labor?
  6. How could European monarchs and church authorities claim lands and peoples that had never been party to their agreements?
  7. In what ways did Indigenous Caribbean peoples resist, adapt, and survive, and why is a purely demographic narrative of decline inadequate?
  8. Which terms, among encounter, contact, conquest, and invasion, best describe the years 1492 to 1513, and why might different terms suit different moments and places?
  9. How did the Caribbean become a launching ground for later mainland expeditions, and what was contingent rather than inevitable in that outcome?

Historical Context

Three earlier lines of development converge in this chapter. The first is the long Indigenous history of the Caribbean. Human communities had lived on the Antillean islands for several thousand years, and by 1492 the Greater Antilles supported farming societies organized around hereditary leaders known in Spanish sources by the Taíno word cacique. These societies were connected by sea to one another, to the Lesser Antilles, and to the northern coast of South America. The agricultural background is examined in Chapter 13, the broader pattern of exchange networks across the continent in Chapter 36, and the distinct civilizations of Mesoamerica, which lay to the west and which Columbus never knew, in Chapter 14.

The second line is Iberian. Castile and Aragon, joined by the marriage of Isabel I and Fernando II but retaining separate institutions, completed the conquest of the Muslim kingdom of Granada in January 1492. Castile had already conquered several of the Canary Islands, including Gran Canaria in 1483, and was still completing its conquest of the archipelago when Columbus sailed; the Guanche inhabitants resisted for decades, and Portugal had established sugar-producing colonies on Madeira and traded for enslaved Africans along the West African coast. These experiences gave Iberian rulers, merchants, and soldiers a practical toolkit for overseas expansion: ship types suited to Atlantic sailing, a legal vocabulary of conquest and conversion, papal grants, and an economic model that tied overseas land to commodity production and coerced labor.

The third is the legacy of Chapter 37. The Norse experience shows that the North Atlantic could be crossed centuries before Columbus, and that Indigenous peoples in the Arctic and northeastern North America were capable of both trade and armed resistance. It also shows how much depended on the infrastructure behind a crossing. Norse Greenland was thinly supported, and no European state claimed the lands to the west or sent repeated fleets. The state-backed Atlantic system that emerged after 1492 was different in organization even where individual voyages looked similar.

Historians should also guard against two errors. The first is to treat Columbus as the sole author of what followed, a personal narrative that hides the roles of the Crown, of other captains and settlers, and above all of Indigenous peoples. The second is to treat the Indigenous Caribbean as a passive background that disappears from history at the moment of contact. Indigenous leaders made decisions, Indigenous communities adapted and resisted, and Indigenous descendants and cultural legacies persist in the Caribbean today. This chapter follows both the machinery of invasion and the strategies of those who confronted it.

Section 38.1 · Division I

1492: From Encounter to Sustained Atlantic Invasion

The chapter's organizing contrast is between an episode and a system. Chapter 37 examined Norse Greenland and the brief site at L'Anse aux Meadows, which show that European crossings were possible centuries earlier. What they did not produce was an enduring line of communication. After 1492 Spanish ships crossed the Atlantic every year, and each crossing brought information, goods, people, and claims that made the next crossing easier to organize and justify. The difference lay less in the ships than in the institutions behind them: a Crown that asserted sovereignty, a legal structure for claiming land, financiers and shipowners who expected returns, a Church that supported conversion, and a pool of soldiers and settlers willing to go.

This textbook uses the phrase Atlantic invasion as an analytical term, not as a slogan. It refers to the process, beginning with the 1492 voyage and consolidating after 1493, by which armed Europeans repeatedly crossed the Atlantic, established fortified bases on Indigenous land, asserted sovereignty on behalf of European rulers, and extracted tribute, labor, and captives. The term is deliberately paired with other descriptions. The earliest meetings on Guanahaní and Hispaniola were, in important respects, encounters in which Indigenous people extended hospitality and tested the newcomers through exchange. The Historical Debate in Section 38.21 asks students to weigh what the terms encounter, contact, conquest, and invasion each reveal and conceal.

The chapter's chronology runs from 1492 to about 1513, a span of two decades during which the Spanish presence moved from a single island to a network, and its geography is the Caribbean basin and its immediate edges. This is a limited part of North America, but it is the part where the invasion's methods were first worked out. Many later practices on the mainland, including the use of tribute, the grant of Indigenous labor to Spanish settlers, the employment of interpreters and Indigenous allies, and the legal formulas for claiming land, were first improvised or institutionalized in the Antilles.

Students should keep three questions in view. What were the Indigenous societies that the Spanish entered, and how did they interpret and respond to the arrivals? How did expeditions become a colonial system, and what decisions and conditions, European and Indigenous, shaped that change? And what were the consequences, short and long term, for Indigenous people in the Caribbean and for the later history of the continent? The answers occupy the five divisions of the chapter: Indigenous Caribbean worlds (Sections 38.2 through 38.5), the first voyage (38.6 through 38.12), encounter becoming colonization (38.13 through 38.15), the construction of a Caribbean colonial system (38.16 through 38.20), and the transition toward continental conquest (38.21).

Section 38.2 · Division I

Indigenous Caribbean Worlds Before Columbus

The Caribbean is an archipelago of several hundred islands and cays arcing from the Bahamas and the Greater Antilles, Cuba, Hispaniola, Jamaica, and Puerto Rico, through the Lesser Antilles to the northern coast of South America. Its environments range from the dry limestone Bahamas to the mountainous, well-watered interiors of Hispaniola and Puerto Rico. Hispaniola in particular combines high cordilleras, river valleys such as the fertile plain later called the Vega Real, coastal lagoons, and salt flats, and it supported dense settlement.

Human history in the region is deep. Archaeological evidence indicates that people were living on the Greater Antilles by roughly six thousand years ago and perhaps earlier, though the dating of the earliest sites is debated. These early communities relied on fishing, shellfish, small land animals, and wild plants. Around the final centuries before the common era, ceramic-making farming peoples spread from the lower Orinoco region of northeastern South America into the Lesser Antilles and Puerto Rico, a movement archaeologists associate with the Saladoid tradition. Over the following centuries their descendants and neighbors developed regionally distinctive societies. In the Greater Antilles, population grew and political organization became more complex, and by about the thirteenth century the societies that Spanish observers would meet in 1492 were taking recognizable form.

Agriculture was central. The principal staple was cassava, made from bitter manioc, a root whose poisonous juices were pressed out with woven tubular presses before the grated pulp was baked into flat bread on ceramic griddles. Other crops included sweet potato, maize, beans, squash, peppers, peanuts, and tobacco. Fields were commonly planted in mounds called conucos, a method that drained soil, resisted erosion, and allowed several crops to be grown together. Fishing, turtle and manatee hunting, and the taking of small mammals such as hutia and of iguanas and birds supplemented farming. Archaeologists reconstruct this economy from plant remains, grinding and baking implements, animal bones, and settlement patterns, and early Spanish descriptions confirm and elaborate it.

Settlements ranged from small hamlets of a few houses to larger towns with central plazas. Houses, called bohíos, were built of wood and thatch around a post frame, and caciques lived in larger rectangular buildings. Many towns included plazas and stone-lined ball courts, which Spanish writers called bateyes, used for a team ball game and for gatherings and ceremonies. Elites sat on carved wooden seats called duhos. Religious life centered on spirit beings and ancestors represented by carved or modeled objects known as zemís, with ritual specialists, healers, and singers mediating between the human and spiritual worlds. A friar named Ramón Pané, who lived among Indigenous communities on Hispaniola in the 1490s at Columbus's request, recorded stories of the origin of the sun and moon, the origins of people, and the role of the zemís, producing the earliest European account of Indigenous religion in the Americas, though it is filtered through his own interpretation and the limits of translation.

Historical Vocabulary
Cacique and Cacicazgo

Definition. Cacique is a Spanish adaptation of a word from the island Arawakan language of Hispaniola for a hereditary leader. A cacicazgo is the Spanish-derived term for the territory and following under one cacique. Spanish authors later applied both words to Indigenous leaders across the Americas, including Mesoamerica and the Andes.

Historical context. On Hispaniola at the time of Columbus, colonial chroniclers and later historians commonly reconstructed five major cacicazgos: Marién in the northwest, Maguá in the northeast and the Vega Real, Maguana in the interior south, Jaragua in the southwest, and Higüey in the east. Modern scholars debate whether these five were fixed, exhaustive political divisions; the familiar map partly reflects later interpretation of early sixteenth-century Spanish regional descriptions, and Indigenous authority was probably more locally and dynamically organized than it implies. Authority passed through lineages, and women could hold leadership: Anacaona exercised power in Jaragua, and Spanish sources attest to women who headed communities. Caciques organized agricultural production, redistributed goods, hosted ceremonies, and negotiated alliances and marriages with other caciques. Below them stood a noble class often called by Spanish writers nitaínos, ritual and medical specialists called behiques, and the majority of commoners.

Why it matters. These political structures were what Spanish leaders encountered. They shaped who could offer hospitality, who could demand labor, and who could organize resistance. Later Spanish administration often adapted the cacique's authority to extract tribute and labor, so that Indigenous political forms were both a resource for the colonists and a source of resistance.

Potential misunderstanding. Spanish sources sometimes describe caciques as kings and cacicazgos as kingdoms, language that served Spanish ideas of rank and sovereignty. Chiefdom-like authority was real but was embedded in kinship, ritual, and reciprocal obligation rather than in a bureaucratic state. Population estimates for the pre-contact Caribbean are also strongly disputed, ranging widely depending on method; the point to retain is that the islands were far more populous and politically differentiated than Columbus's early descriptions suggest.

Section 38.3 · Division I

Taíno, Lucayan, Kalinago, and the Problem of Colonial Labels

The names by which the Indigenous peoples of the Caribbean are known carry layers of history. The word Taíno, now the standard scholarly label for the Arawakan-speaking peoples of the Greater Antilles and the Bahamas, was not a general self-designation in 1492. Spanish sources reported that people in the Lesser Antilles used a term like taíno in the sense of "good" or "noble" when speaking to Columbus's men on the second voyage, and nineteenth-century scholars generalized the word as an ethnic label. In the fifteenth century communities most likely identified by their cacicazgo, region, and kinship ties. The label remains useful because it names a shared language family and cultural pattern, and it has been taken up by many descendants and cultural revival movements, but it should be used with an awareness of its history.

Within the Greater Antilles, languages and cultural traditions varied. On Hispaniola, Spanish sources distinguished the Arawakan speakers of the main cacicazgos from peoples called Macorix and Ciguayo in the northeast and northern coast, who spoke languages recorded by Pané and others as different, and whose relationships to the dominant Taíno-speaking groups remain imperfectly understood. In western Cuba, Spanish writers described communities they called Guanahatabey and others labeled Ciboney, usually understood as groups with a more mobile, fishing-and-gathering way of life, though archaeologists continue to refine how such labels correspond to the material record. In the Bahamas lived the Lucayans, whose name derives from a term meaning "island people" and who had settled the archipelago from Hispaniola and Cuba during the first millennium of the common era and the centuries immediately after. Puerto Rico, called Borikén by its inhabitants according to Spanish sources, had its own cacicazgos and a long ceremonial tradition.

In the Lesser Antilles lived peoples who in later colonial usage were known as Kalinago, or Island Carib. Their language shows Arawakan structure with a distinctive layer of vocabulary of Cariban origin, a pattern that linguists explain in several ways, and their societies were organized around villages and kin groups, with mobile maritime networks stretching to the South American coast. Their relationship to Taíno-speaking neighbors included trade, intermarriage, rivalry, and raiding. Kalinago communities resisted European colonization for generations in the Lesser Antilles, and descendants live today in Dominica's Kalinago Territory and elsewhere in the region.

Historical Vocabulary
"Carib" and "Cannibal"

Definition. "Carib" and the related word "cannibal" both derive from forms of a name that Columbus heard, probably an Indigenous word for the people of the Lesser Antilles, which he recorded in forms such as Caniba and associated with the idea of man-eating warriors and also, at first, with the subjects of the Great Khan of Asia. "Cannibal" entered European languages through this report.

Historical context. Spanish colonial law made the label a legal tool. A royal decree issued in 1503 distinguished peaceful Indigenous people who were to be treated as free vassals from those described as "Caribs" who resisted Spanish rule and were alleged to practice cannibalism, who could be enslaved. Spanish officials and slave raiders applied the label broadly, including to people who were not Kalinago and in places far from the Lesser Antilles.

Why it matters. The distinction shows how colonial categories functioned. Accusations of cannibalism were not neutral ethnography; they justified enslavement and war. Historians and archaeologists continue to debate whether and to what extent some Indigenous Caribbean groups practiced ritual consumption of enemies, and the surviving evidence is thin and heavily shaped by Spanish interests.

Potential misunderstanding. The modern scholarly name Kalinago, and the older term Island Carib, designate a people who lived in the Lesser Antilles. They are not equivalent to the legal category "Caribs" used by Spanish officials, and the Spanish category was sometimes imposed on peoples who had never used the name.

The point of this brief survey is methodological. Columbus's categories, simplified into friendly "Arawaks" and hostile "Caribs," do not describe the real variety of Indigenous Caribbean societies, and a history that adopts them uncritically reproduces a colonial map. Where this chapter uses Taíno, Lucayan, and Kalinago, it uses them as convenient scholarly labels for language-and-culture groupings, while recognizing that the people involved belonged first to specific communities and cacicazgos.

Section 38.4 · Division I

Islands Connected by Sea

The sea was not a barrier for Indigenous Caribbean peoples but an infrastructure. They traveled in dugout canoes, a word Europeans took from the Caribbean, hollowed from single trunks and in some cases large enough to carry dozens of people, according to Spanish accounts. These vessels connected islands separated by tens or hundreds of kilometers and allowed travel along the South American coast. Seasonal winds, currents, and the visibility of neighboring islands from the water made inter-island travel routine, and canoe-building was a specialized craft.

Exchange followed these routes. Ceramics, stone tools and ornaments, shell, cotton, feathers, foods, and ritual objects moved among islands, and elites exchanged prestige goods that conveyed status and cemented alliances. A particularly significant item was guanín, an alloy of gold and copper that circulated in the Caribbean and that, according to chemical studies of artifacts, was partly made from metals originating on the South American mainland. Gold itself carried ceremonial and social meaning as an object of rank and spiritual power, not as a commodity measured against European coin. This difference of valuation will matter in Section 38.8 and again in the following sections: Columbus's hunger for gold met Indigenous gold that was embedded in social relationships.

Political relationships crossed the sea as well. Caciques arranged marriages with the families of distant leaders; communities hosted visitors under conventions of hospitality; and rivalries could erupt into raids and warfare. Songs and dances called areítos, described by Spanish writers, preserved genealogies and historical memory and were occasions for gathering and for reaffirming relationships. Spanish observers recorded these performances without fully understanding them, and the genealogical knowledge they carried was disrupted by the catastrophe that followed.

Students should connect this picture to Chapter 36's argument that Indigenous North America operated as a continental system. The Caribbean was part of such a system, linked to South America by canoe travel and perhaps more tenuously to Mesoamerica and the southeastern mainland. The strength of the evidence varies. Contact between the Caribbean and the Yucatán coast is suggested by later accounts, including a large trading canoe encountered by Columbus's fourth voyage that carried goods of Mesoamerican character (Section 38.17), but the extent and regularity of such contact before 1492 remain debated. What is clear is that the Spanish entered a region already woven into regional networks of movement, and that they used that fact. They relied on Indigenous knowledge of routes and winds, on Indigenous interpreters, and on the willingness of local leaders to supply food and guides.

Geography Study

The Sea-Lanes of the Caribbean

Using a map of the Caribbean basin that shows modern coastlines but no modern political boundaries, students should consider the following:

  • The Bahamas (the Lucayan Archipelago), a chain of low limestone islands north of Cuba and Hispaniola where Columbus made his first landfall; its inhabitants were linked by short canoe passages to Cuba and Hispaniola.
  • The Greater Antilles of Cuba, Hispaniola, Jamaica, and Puerto Rico, the largest and most populous islands, with mountainous interiors and extensive river valleys that supported dense settlement.
  • The Lesser Antilles, running southeast toward South America, the homeland of Kalinago communities and the route Columbus used on his second voyage.
  • The northern coast of South America and the lower Orinoco region, the source of earlier migrations and a continuing zone of exchange.
  • The Caribbean coasts of Central America and the Yucatán, the western horizon of the Spanish advance and the edge of Mesoamerican exchange networks, examined in Chapters 14 and 39.
  • The Atlantic wind system: the northeast trade winds that carried Columbus westward at lower latitudes, and the prevailing westerlies of higher latitudes that carried him home by way of the Azores.

Section 38.5 · Division I

Iberian Expansion Before 1492

The expedition of 1492 was possible because of what Iberians had learned in the preceding century. Portuguese captains had navigated down the Atlantic coast of Africa from the early fifteenth century, colonized the uninhabited islands of Madeira and the Azores, and from the 1440s taken enslaved Africans by purchase and raid. Madeira's settlers developed sugar cane plantations with imported labor, establishing a link between Atlantic islands, a high-value crop, and coerced workers that would later define Caribbean colonization. The Portuguese Crown and the papacy supported these efforts with grants and bulls that connected claims to trade and territory with the aim of Christian expansion.

Castile developed its own Atlantic experience. Castilian forces and adventurers, some acting under royal licenses, conquered the Canary Islands in stages across the fifteenth century. The Guanche inhabitants of the western and central islands fought, died in large numbers, were enslaved, and were gradually incorporated or displaced as Castilian settlers established sugar and other agriculture. The conquest of Tenerife was completed in the 1490s, concurrent with Columbus's first voyages. The Canaries were also the staging point from which Columbus began his Atlantic crossing, and from which he would organize later fleets. They had been a laboratory for conquest, enslavement, missionary activity, and the establishment of Castilian authority over an Indigenous population, and their legal treatment shaped later practice.

On the Iberian mainland the Reconquista, the centuries-long Christian campaign against Muslim-ruled territories, created the habits of mind and the military institutions that supported overseas expansion. It rewarded soldiers with land and offices, made frontier warfare an avenue of social advancement, and gave the monarchy experience governing newly conquered populations. The fall of Granada in January 1492 and the order that spring requiring Jews to convert or leave Castile and Aragon show the Crown asserting religious uniformity at home in the very months when it sponsored the Atlantic voyage. These developments should not be treated as a single plan leading to the conquest of the Americas. Rather, they supplied the means, the models, and the motives that expedition leaders and officials drew upon as events unfolded.

Technology mattered too. The Portuguese-developed caravel, a light, maneuverable vessel with lateen sails, and the larger, square-rigged nao suited different needs, and navigators combined dead reckoning with astronomical observation to sail beyond sight of land. Equally important was the discovery, worked out by Portuguese pilots in the fifteenth century, that ships could return from the open Atlantic by sailing north and then east on the westerlies, a maneuver known as the volta do mar. Without this knowledge a westward voyage might have been a one-way trip. Crossing, return, and repetition formed the technical core of the Atlantic corridor.

Section 38.6 · Division II

Columbus's Project and the First Atlantic Crossing

Christopher Columbus, born in the Genoese Republic, came to Iberia as a mariner and chart-maker. He proposed to sail west from Europe to reach the East Indies, the rich trading lands of Asia that European merchants reached with great cost and difficulty through intermediaries. His argument depended on a smaller estimate of the Earth's circumference and a greater estimate of Asia's eastward extent than most contemporary experts accepted, and the Portuguese court, advised by specialists, declined to support him. After years of lobbying, and after the conquest of Granada freed royal attention, Isabel and Fernando agreed to sponsor the venture. In April 1492 the monarchs and Columbus concluded the Capitulations of Santa Fe, which granted him the hereditary title of Admiral of the Ocean Sea, appointments as viceroy and governor of lands he claimed, and a share of the revenues.

Columbus's geographical assumptions must be distinguished from the geography that modern knowledge establishes. He believed that a relatively short ocean separated Europe from Asia, and he interpreted what he found, on this and later voyages, in light of that belief. In fact the Atlantic is far wider than he supposed, and between Europe and Asia lay two continents, long known to their inhabitants, of which Europeans had no secure knowledge. This is why "discovery" is an inadequate description from an Indigenous or a global perspective. Columbus's achievement lay in repeatedly crossing the ocean and returning, which opened a route for others, not in finding lands previously unknown to human beings.

The expedition consisted of three vessels, the larger Santa María and the smaller caravels Pinta and Niña, with a combined crew usually estimated at around ninety men. Mariners from Palos and neighboring towns, including members of the Pinzón family who contributed ships and leadership, made up much of the company. The ships left Palos on 3 August 1492, stopped at the Canary Islands for repairs and supplies, and in early September sailed west into the open Atlantic, following the trade winds. After more than a month with no sight of land, and with increasing unease among the crew, the lookouts sighted an island on the night of 11 and 12 October.

Several features of the voyage deserve emphasis. The Crown financed it largely through Castilian sources and loans, and it expected returns. The expedition carried notaries and officials who could document possession, interpreters of Arabic and Hebrew who might communicate with Asian courts, and trade goods such as beads and small bells. Columbus carried a letter of introduction to the Great Khan. All of this reflects expectations of finding organized states in Asia with which diplomatic and commercial relations were conducted by established means, a framework that shaped his readings of what he saw and kept him, throughout his life, from fully recognizing that he had reached unfamiliar continents.

Section 38.7 · Division II

Guanahaní, 12 October 1492

The island where Columbus landed, which its inhabitants according to his journal called Guanahaní, lay in the Lucayan Archipelago. Columbus renamed it San Salvador, and the modern identification of the island is disputed. For much of the twentieth century the island now officially called San Salvador, formerly Watling Island, was accepted, but other candidates, including Samana Cay and islands farther south, have been proposed on the basis of readings of the journal's descriptions and distances. The dispute shows how much interpretation rests on a single document read against a changing landscape. For the history of the encounter what matters is that it occurred among Lucayan communities, whose settlements, canoes, and relationships with neighboring islands stretched across the archipelago.

According to Columbus's journal as preserved by Las Casas, the inhabitants approached the Spanish ships and boats and brought food, water, cotton thread, parrots, and spears, and the Spaniards gave them in return small gifts of red caps, glass beads, and bells. Columbus wrote that the people went naked or nearly so, had no iron weapons, and carried scars that they indicated were caused by raiders from other islands. From these observations he drew conclusions that shaped his plans: that they were gentle, would be easy to convert, would make good servants, and could be subjugated with a small force. These inferences came from a European expectation, not from any adequate understanding of the people he had met, who had their own political institutions, histories of regional conflict, and knowledge of the surrounding waters.

On landing, Columbus, accompanied by the officials of the expedition, took formal possession of the island for the Crown of Castile before a notary, an act documented in the journal. The possession ceremony was a legal ritual intended to create a record for European audiences and courts. It had no meaning for those on the beach, who saw bearded men in unfamiliar clothing speaking an unknown language and performing an unfamiliar ritual. From the Spanish side, however, the act established a claim that Castilian authorities would later cite. This is a central asymmetry of the whole period: a legal claim was created in a language and a system that the people concerned did not share and to which they had not consented.

Columbus also seized several local men, reported as a handful, whom he intended to use as guides and interpreters and to take to Spain. This act, recorded by him as practical and unremarkable, was the first in a long series of Spanish captures of Indigenous people for similar purposes. It also reveals the unequal terms of the encounter. The Lucayans who greeted the ships as visitors discovered within hours that some among them were to be carried away. We do not know how the people of Guanahaní understood the strangers, since no Lucayan account of the day survives. The best we can do is to read Columbus's record critically, compare it with archaeological evidence of Lucayan life, and treat his inferences about Indigenous character, institutions, and intentions as claims that require testing, not as description.

Section 38.8 · Division II

Diplomacy, Reciprocity, and Misinterpretation

The earliest days of the encounter can be read as diplomacy conducted without a common language. Indigenous communities across the Caribbean approached the ships bearing food, water, cotton, and objects, and they accepted Spanish gifts in return. In many societies, including those of the Greater Antilles, exchange of gifts established relationships and obligations; to receive hospitality without reciprocating, or to give without an expectation of return, would have been a social matter, not a purely economic one. Spanish chroniclers record that Hispaniolan leaders formed pacts by exchanging names, a practice they called guaitiao, and the surviving accounts of Guacanagarí's reception of Columbus show him extending hospitality in ways consistent with a cacique's role.

Columbus read these gestures differently. In his journal he repeatedly interpreted generosity as simplicity, an absence of religion, or readiness for subjugation, and he described gift-giving as evidence that the people would easily accept Spanish rule. His own gifts, beads and caps and bells, were meant to win goodwill and to obtain information about gold. For the Indigenous givers, goods such as cotton thread and parrots were part of an economy of reciprocity, while gold ornaments and guanín carried ritual and status meaning. The Spanish concept of trade, in which commodities have comparable monetary value, did not match the Indigenous concept of exchange as relationship. The mismatch was a source of confusion on both sides and later of violence.

Historians have reasoned about Indigenous motives with caution. Some Lucayan and Hispaniolan communities may have seen the newcomers as powerful visitors from a distant place whose goods and potential alliance were worth cultivating. Others, particularly those with rivals, may have sought allies against neighbors. Spanish sources report that people on several islands pointed to other islands as sources of gold or as homes of enemies, which Columbus interpreted as directions to wealth, though they may sometimes have been a way of redirecting the strangers away from their own communities. Columbus's inferences are reliable evidence of what he believed and of how Indigenous people answered his questions, but they are poor evidence of what Indigenous people meant.

The key analytical point is that misinterpretation carried consequences. Columbus's conviction that the Indigenous people were submissive encouraged him to leave a garrison on Hispaniola, to demand gold, and to treat captives as a resource. Indigenous hosts who believed they had entered a reciprocal relationship found the Spaniards acting as though they had been granted authority. Misunderstanding did not cause the invasion, which rested on Spanish interests and power, but it helped shape its early course.

Multiple Perspectives

The Guanahaní Encounter: Three Ways of Seeing

Note: the viewpoints below are analytical reconstructions based on the kinds of evidence discussed in this chapter. No Lucayan account of the encounter survives, and none of the statements below is a quotation from any individual.

From the perspective of Columbus and the officers of the expedition: The voyage had reached islands off the coast of Asia. The people were potential subjects, trading partners, and converts, and the landing created a legal claim for the Crown. The most important questions were where gold and spices could be found and how to communicate with the powerful rulers they expected to meet.
From the perspective of Lucayan residents of the island, as reconstructed from archaeology and later accounts of Caribbean diplomacy: Large vessels with unfamiliar people had appeared. Customary hospitality and exchange offered a way to learn the strangers' intentions. Some may have regarded them with awe or suspicion, and the seizure of local men would have changed the meaning of the visit, since it showed that the newcomers did not observe reciprocal obligations.
From the perspective of the Spanish Crown and its legal advisers, who received reports after the return: The landfall created a basis for claiming sovereignty and for authorizing further voyages, especially when linked to papal authority. The primary concerns were competition with Portugal, the economic promise of the new lands, and the obligation to spread Christianity.
From the perspective of Indigenous leaders on neighboring islands who would soon hear of the strangers: News traveled along canoe routes. Caciques on Cuba and Hispaniola had to decide whether the visitors were a threat, a source of useful goods, or a potential ally in local rivalries, and their decisions would differ with their circumstances.
Section 38.9 · Division II

Gold, Captivity, and the Turn Toward Coercion

From the first days Columbus questioned the people he met about gold. The journal records that he noted gold ornaments, usually small pieces worn in the nose, and that he asked where they came from, interpreting gestures toward the south and west as indications of a rich land and a powerful king. These exchanges established a pattern. Columbus assumed that islands or regions identified by Indigenous speakers were sources of the metal he sought, and he adjusted his route accordingly. The Spanish interest in gold structured nearly all of their decisions in these years.

Captivity was part of the same logic. On Guanahaní and later on other islands Columbus seized Indigenous men, and in some cases women and children, to serve as guides and interpreters and to take back to Spain as evidence and as subjects for conversion. Some of those taken escaped; others stayed with the expedition. The practice anticipated the later enslavement of Indigenous people and showed the Spanish treating Indigenous persons as resources even in the absence of war. It also had an unintended effect: captives taught the Spanish names, geographical information, and elements of Indigenous languages, and became the first of many Indigenous intermediaries between the two sides.

The expedition moved from island to island through the Lucayan Archipelago before reaching the northeastern coast of Cuba at the end of October. Columbus identified Cuba as part of the Asian mainland and sent a small party, including Luis de Torres, who spoke languages of the eastern Mediterranean, inland to find the court of the Great Khan. They found instead agricultural villages and a large settlement, were received with hospitality, and observed local people smoking rolled tobacco leaves, a practice recorded here for the first time by Europeans. The episode, which showed Indigenous Cubans offering hospitality and information, also shows the Spanish expectation of encountering a centralized Asian state. When the envoys found none, Columbus turned east again in search of the gold that Indigenous informants had described.

These episodes reveal how the objectives of the expedition were incompatible with relations of equal exchange. Columbus sought gold, captives, and information, and he was prepared to use force to obtain them. The fact that violence did not yet characterize most encounters should not obscure the structure of the situation. A small force with ships and weapons, backed by a distant state, was assessing a population as a source of labor and wealth. The next stage, the establishment of a permanent garrison, would turn that assessment into a presence on Indigenous land.

Section 38.10 · Division II

Cuba, Hispaniola, and La Navidad

In November 1492 Martín Alonso Pinzón took the Pinta and separated from Columbus, pursuing his own search for gold, and the two ships did not meet again until January. Columbus meanwhile sailed east along the northern coast of Cuba and crossed to Hispaniola in early December, naming it La Española. There he encountered communities of the northwestern coast, within the cacicazgo of Marién, whose leader was Guacanagarí. Columbus's journal records exchanges of gifts, descriptions of fertile land, and reports of gold in the interior region called Cibao.

On the night of 24 to 25 December the Santa María ran aground on a reef off the northern coast. According to Columbus's journal, Guacanagarí sent canoes and men to help salvage the cargo and ship's fittings and provided housing for the Spanish crew. Columbus interpreted the response as evidence of loyalty and friendship. It is equally consistent with Indigenous conventions of hospitality and with a calculation that a relationship with the strangers might benefit Guacanagarí in a region where he had rivals. Whatever his motives, the help he gave allowed Columbus to salvage timber and guns from the wreck and to build a small fortification.

That fort, named La Navidad, was garrisoned by thirty-nine men under the command of Diego de Arana, equipped with provisions, trade goods, and weapons, and charged with trading for gold and exploring until Columbus returned. The decision was probably practical, since the Spanish had lost a ship and lacked room for the whole crew, but its effect was decisive. It placed an armed Spanish group in permanent residence in an Indigenous community, outside the diplomatic conventions of a visit. The garrison would be dependent on its hosts for food, would be tempted by the pursuit of gold and by contact with women, and would lack effective supervision. These pressures, rather than Columbus's intentions, determined what happened next.

In January 1493 Columbus left La Navidad on the Niña, rejoined Pinzón, and sailed east along the northern coast of Hispaniola. At Samaná Bay he met people he called Ciguayos, who were not part of the Taíno-speaking cacicazgos the Spaniards had so far seen, and the encounter turned violent; Spanish sources report a brief clash in which several Indigenous men were wounded. It is the first recorded armed conflict of the voyage and, though small, shows that Indigenous responses varied among communities and that Spanish assumptions of universal peaceable welcome were mistaken. Columbus departed for Spain in mid-January, carrying a handful of Indigenous captives, samples of gold, and plants and objects as evidence.

The exact location of La Navidad has not been securely established. Archaeologists have investigated sites in the area of northern Haiti, including En Bas Saline, a large Indigenous town excavated by Kathleen Deagan and colleagues that has been associated with the cacicazgo of Marién, but no site has yet been unambiguously identified as the fort. The uncertainty is a reminder that the physical traces of the first European settlement in the Americas since the Norse are more elusive than the documentary record suggests.

Section 38.11 · Division II

How Do We Know? Columbus, Las Casas, Archaeology, and Indigenous Evidence

The documentary base for the first voyage is surprisingly narrow. Columbus kept a daily log, and a copy was prepared for the Crown, but neither Columbus's original nor the royal copy is known to survive. What historians use is an abstract and partial transcription made by Bartolomé de las Casas decades later, in which Las Casas copied some passages directly, summarized others in the third person, and occasionally added comments. The principal manuscript, discovered in the nineteenth century, is sometimes called the Barcelona manuscript. A reader of the "journal of Columbus" is therefore reading Las Casas's editorial selection, made by a man who by then had become a fierce critic of Spanish treatment of Indigenous peoples and who may have emphasized or shaped what he found, even while generally trusted by scholars as a faithful transcriber. This limitation should always be stated when the journal is used.

Other contemporary documents supplement the journal. Columbus's own letter reporting the first voyage to royal officials, printed in several editions in 1493, is a polished, promotional account designed to win support. Later chroniclers and eyewitnesses add layers: the physician Diego Álvarez Chanca wrote a letter about the second voyage; Ramón Pané compiled his account of Indigenous beliefs; Peter Martyr of Anghiera composed a humanist account of the new lands from conversations with participants; Gonzalo Fernández de Oviedo and Las Casas wrote large histories decades afterward, drawing on memory, documents, and their own experience. Ferdinand Columbus, the admiral's son, wrote a biography. Each of these authors had interests and limitations, and none was Indigenous.

Archaeology provides independent evidence. Excavations at En Bas Saline, La Isabela, and other sites document Indigenous settlement, craft production, food remains, and the introduction of European objects, animals, and architectural forms. Studies of pottery, shell, stone, and metal show networks of exchange; analyses of animal bones and plant remains show diets and agriculture; and burials provide evidence of health and social organization. Where documentary and archaeological evidence agree, interpretation is strong; where they diverge, historians must consider the biases of each. Linguistic evidence preserves Taíno words in Spanish and English, including canoe, hammock, hurricane, barbecue, tobacco, cassava, and maize. Genetic studies of modern and ancient individuals have identified Indigenous Caribbean ancestry in present populations, but, as the Historical Context cautioned, genetic ancestry is not the same as cultural identity.

The most important absence is Indigenous testimony. The Taíno and Lucayan societies left no surviving alphabetic writing, and the traditions carried by song and ceremony were disrupted by mortality and displacement. Indigenous perspectives therefore have to be reconstructed from fragments: Spanish records of Indigenous statements, the testimony of Indigenous witnesses in later legal proceedings, archaeological and linguistic evidence, and the knowledge and oral traditions of present-day Indigenous and Indigenous-descended communities. Historians use the term ethnohistory for the method of combining such sources, with attention to how each source's author and purpose shaped what it records.

Primary Source Window

Columbus's First-Voyage Journal, Entry for 12 October 1492

Creator: Christopher Columbus, as abstracted by Bartolomé de las Casas · Date: written 1492; abstract prepared decades later · Type: Shipboard log kept in Spanish for the Crown, preserved only in a later summary and partial transcription

Context: The entry records the landfall and the first meeting with the inhabitants of Guanahaní. Columbus wrote it to document the voyage for Isabel and Fernando, and it was shaped by his hopes for royal reward and by his belief that he had reached the edge of Asia. The wording in the surviving manuscript reflects Las Casas's mediation.

Paraphrase, not a quotation: the entry reports that the ships sighted land in the early morning and that the officers went ashore and claimed the island for the Crown before the expedition's notary. It then describes the inhabitants, who came to meet the Spanish, as unarmed and without clothing, notes scars that Columbus understood to come from raiders, records the exchange of beads and caps for parrots, cotton thread, and spears, and concludes that the people would be easy to convert and could be made servants by a small force.

This is a summary of the content of the entry in the editorial tradition, not a verbatim translation. Students should consult a published scholarly translation of the journal when analyzing exact wording.

Analysis: The entry is valuable as a contemporary Spanish record of the landing, the possession ceremony, the exchange of goods, and Columbus's first impressions. It shows that the Spanish immediately interpreted the landfall in terms of conversion, labor, and sovereignty. It also records, almost in passing, indications of Indigenous political life, such as the scars that Columbus linked to attacks from neighboring islands.

Limitations: The entry cannot tell us what the Lucayans thought, said, or intended. Columbus and the people he met shared no language, so his accounts of speech and gesture are interpretations. The text we have is filtered through Las Casas. The inference that the people were passive or easily subjugated reflects Columbus's expectations and aims.

Perspective: The perspective is that of a European commander seeking commercial and political reward. Absent are the perspectives of the Lucayans, of Spanish ordinary sailors, and of women and children on the beach.

Section 38.12 · Division II

The Return to Europe and the Transformation of a Voyage into a Program

The return voyage in early 1493 was hazardous. A severe storm separated the ships and forced the Niña to put into Lisbon in early March, where Columbus was received by the Portuguese king, João II, who took an interest in the claims and in the proximity of the new lands to areas he regarded as within the Portuguese sphere. Columbus reached Palos in mid-March and went to the court, then at Barcelona, to present his report. He brought with him a number of Indigenous people, samples of gold and cotton, parrots, and other items. The court received the party with great interest, and several of the Indigenous visitors were baptized with the monarchs as sponsors, an event that Spanish officials treated as a sign of the mission's promise.

Columbus's report and the letter printed in 1493 stressed the fertility of the islands, the gentleness of the inhabitants, and the prospect of gold and spices, and downplayed the uncertainty about whether he had reached Asia. These claims were persuasive not because they were accurate but because they promised returns on investment. The Crown responded within weeks. It sought papal confirmation of its rights, secured the bulls discussed in Section 38.18, and began organizing a second, far larger expedition.

The scale of the second expedition marks the change in the nature of the project. The first voyage had been an experiment with three ships. The second was designed as a colonizing enterprise, with a fleet, settlers, officials, priests, livestock, seeds, and instructions for establishing a permanent presence. Its purpose was no longer only to confirm a route but to claim territory, convert inhabitants, and extract wealth. The fact that the Crown could mobilize these resources within months demonstrates the institutional backing that the Norse venture had lacked.

The Indigenous captives who crossed the Atlantic provide a glimpse of another dimension. Taken without consent, they became early participants in the circulation of people and information between worlds. Some were baptized and instructed in Spanish, and a few returned with the second expedition as interpreters. Their experiences are poorly documented, and many died. Their presence at court and their return to the Caribbean show that the Atlantic corridor carried Indigenous people as well as Europeans, a pattern that would expand dramatically with enslavement.

Section 38.13 · Division III

The Second Voyage, 1493: Colonization Arrives

The fleet that left Cádiz in late September 1493 comprised some seventeen ships and, by common estimates, well over a thousand people, with figures ranging from about 1,200 to 1,500 in different sources. It carried soldiers, farmers, artisans, officials, and clergy, including a papal vicar, Bernardo Buyl, and Ramón Pané. It also carried horses, pigs, cattle, sheep, goats, and chickens, wheat and other European seeds, and sugar cane cuttings from the Canary Islands. For the first time an Atlantic crossing carried the elements of a transplanted European agricultural and social system.

The route took the ships farther south than in 1492, to the Lesser Antilles. In early November the fleet reached Dominica, followed by Guadeloupe and other islands, where Spanish landing parties encountered Kalinago communities and, on some islands, found captives from other islands living among them. Spanish authors, including Chanca, described these encounters and framed them in terms of cannibalism and warfare, accounts that must be read in light of the legal and propaganda uses described in Section 38.3. The fleet then sailed along the coast of Puerto Rico and arrived off Hispaniola in late November, where the Spanish found La Navidad destroyed and the garrison dead.

Spanish accounts of what had happened conflict. Some reported that Guacanagarí had been hostile or had failed to protect the garrison, while others said that a rival cacique, Caonabo of Maguana, had attacked the fort after the Spanish had provoked conflicts by seizing gold, taking women, and quarreling among themselves. The explanation that the garrison's own behavior and the reaction of Indigenous leaders to it caused the destruction is widely accepted by historians, but the evidence is thin. What is clear is that within a year the first European settlement on Hispaniola had collapsed because of its relations with its Indigenous neighbors.

The fate of La Navidad did not deter the Spanish. Instead, Columbus founded a new settlement, La Isabela, on the north coast in early 1494, and the Spanish presence became permanent. The decisive change was that the Atlantic crossing was now repeatable and supported. Ships returned to Spain for supplies, and the Crown's officials expected a regular flow of goods and people. From this point the question was not whether Spanish colonists would remain but on what terms.

Cause and Effect

Why the Crossing Became Continuous After 1492

Long-term causes

Iberian experience with Atlantic islands, sugar, slavery, and conquest; Portuguese navigational knowledge, including the return route across the Atlantic; the Reconquista's tradition of rewarding conquest; and papal support for expansion and conversion.

Short-term causes

The completion of the conquest of Granada, the Capitulations of Santa Fe, competition with Portugal, the availability of financing, and Columbus's persuasive report of gold, fertile land, and a compliant population.

Immediate trigger and event

Columbus's return in 1493 with captives and gold, which convinced the Crown to organize a colonizing fleet and to seek papal confirmation of its claims. The second voyage established permanent settlement.

Immediate effects

A regular line of ships between Spain and the Caribbean, the introduction of livestock, plants, and pathogens, the founding of fortified settlements, and the beginning of tribute and war on Hispaniola.

Long-term consequences

The expansion of Spanish bases across the Caribbean, the creation of institutions that would be extended to the mainland, the transfer of large numbers of people across the Atlantic, and profound transformation of Indigenous societies.

Contrast with the Norse

The Norse had navigational skill but lacked a state claiming and supporting the lands, a commodity or labor system to justify repeated voyages, and a continuing flow of settlers and supplies. The difference lay in institutions and incentives, not in individual seamanship.

Historical complexity: No single factor explains the continuity of crossings. Indigenous responses also mattered: where Indigenous leaders provided food, guides, and alliances, as in Marién, the Spanish could establish themselves, and where Indigenous resistance was effective, as at La Navidad, they were set back. The outcome was not fixed in advance.

Section 38.14 · Division III

La Isabela and the Problems of Colonial Settlement

La Isabela, founded in January 1494 on the north coast of Hispaniola in what is now the Dominican Republic, was the first European town in the Americas intended as a permanent colonial settlement. Archaeologists have excavated its remains, including the foundations of buildings, a fortified storehouse, and cemetery, and these sites provide evidence of its layout and the hardships of its inhabitants. The site proved unhealthy, short of fresh water and good farmland, and poorly located for reaching the interior gold fields. Within a few years the main Spanish settlement moved to the south coast, to the place that became Santo Domingo.

The settlers' difficulties reveal how dependent the early colony was on Indigenous people. Many of the Spaniards fell ill soon after arrival, and food supplies from Spain were inadequate and spoiled. European crops did not thrive at first, and the colonists, many of whom expected rapid wealth and had no desire to farm, relied on Indigenous communities to feed them. When they obtained food by demand or seizure rather than exchange, they provoked resistance. Columbus's decision to send expeditions into the interior to find gold and establish forts, including Fort Santo Tomás in the Cibao, extended the Spanish presence into Indigenous territory and generated conflict. Disease among the Spanish, whose cause is debated and is discussed further in Chapter 40, killed many of the settlers and also began to affect Indigenous populations.

Social tensions among the Spaniards worsened the situation. Hidalgos and officials resented orders to do manual labor; royal officials quarreled with Columbus; and rival factions accused one another of mismanagement. In the spring of 1494 Columbus sailed to explore the southern coast of Cuba and Jamaica, leaving the colony under a council headed by his brother Diego. His brother Bartolomé arrived later that year, and the elevation of Columbus's Genoese-born relatives caused resentment among Spaniards who objected to being governed by foreigners. When Columbus returned the situation had deteriorated, with Spanish groups plundering Indigenous communities and Indigenous leaders organizing opposition.

The experience of La Isabela illustrates a central feature of the early invasion: a European settlement could not sustain itself by Spanish resources alone, and its survival depended on extracting food, labor, and information from Indigenous communities. This dependency was a source of Indigenous leverage, but the Spanish response was to convert it into coercion.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Fields, and the Disruption of Daily Life

Ordinary life in a Hispaniolan community in 1492 revolved around the household, the conuco, and the plaza. Women and men shared agricultural work, with women playing a central role in processing cassava, making pottery, and caring for children, while men fished, hunted, and cleared fields. Communities held ceremonies, ball games, and areíto performances that structured the year. Food was abundant by the testimony of early Spanish observers, though their descriptions mixed admiration with interest in exploiting what they saw.

Spanish arrival disrupted these routines in several ways. Demands for food strained local supplies; tribute and labor requirements drew people away from their fields; and free-ranging pigs and cattle, introduced by the Spanish, damaged unfenced conucos, a problem noted by contemporaries. Sexual violence against Indigenous women is recorded in Spanish sources and was part of the coercive environment. Families were separated by captivity and forced labor. Spanish colonists, for their part, faced shortages, disease, and hardship in an unfamiliar environment, and many lived in precarious conditions, though they held power over Indigenous people. The social history of these years thus includes both shared struggle for survival and sharply unequal burdens.

Cultural exchange occurred alongside coercion. Spanish settlers adopted hammocks, cassava bread, and tobacco, and learned Indigenous words for plants and places. Indigenous people incorporated European goods and animals. Such exchanges did not make relations equal, but they show that daily life involved adaptation on both sides.

Section 38.15 · Division III

Hispaniola: Alliance, Resistance, and War

Hispaniola was not a single political unit, and Indigenous responses to the Spanish varied accordingly. Guacanagarí of Marién remained generally friendly to the Spanish, a stance that earned him the hostility of other caciques who regarded him as a collaborator. Caonabo of Maguana, whose territory included the Cibao gold region, opposed the Spanish presence and became the best-known early leader of resistance. His wife Anacaona, sister of the Jaragua cacique Behecchio, was a political figure in her own right. Guarionex, the cacique of Maguá in the Vega Real, was drawn into conflict as Spanish tribute demands fell on his people. These leaders held differing interests and acted according to them, so that "the Taíno" never confronted the Spanish as a single actor.

In 1494 Spanish forces under Alonso de Ojeda captured Caonabo, reportedly by trickery, and sent him toward Spain, though he died on the way. The capture did not end resistance. In March 1495 Columbus, his brother Bartolomé, and a force of roughly two hundred infantry and a small number of horsemen, with war dogs and Indigenous allies including Guacanagarí's people, defeated a large Indigenous force at the battle of the Vega Real. The size of the opposing force is reported in Spanish sources in large but unreliable numbers. The Spanish victory was not simply a product of technology, since the Indigenous coalition was divided, but horses, dogs, steel weapons, and cohesion gave the Spanish important advantages.

Violence extended beyond formal battles. Spanish parties raided communities for food, gold, and captives, and Indigenous groups retaliated against isolated Spaniards and posts. Some Indigenous communities withdrew to the mountains and abandoned planting in order to avoid Spanish demands, a strategy that also caused hunger. Guarionex, who tried initially to accommodate Spanish demands, later fled and sought refuge with Mayobanex of the Ciguayos, who refused to surrender him and was captured along with him in 1498. The incident shows both the persistence of political solidarity and the Spanish capacity to hunt down leaders.

The wars of 1494 through 1498 set the pattern for what followed. They demonstrated that Spanish power could be projected across the island from fortified bases; they weakened some of the strongest caciques; and they cleared the way for a systematic tribute and labor demand. Yet the conquest of Hispaniola was not complete in 1498. Jaragua and Higüey remained largely independent until 1503 and 1504, and Indigenous resistance would continue in various forms for decades.

Section 38.16 · Division IV

Tribute, Forced Labor, Enslavement, and the Emerging Colonial Economy

The Spanish colony needed a way to pay for itself, and the Crown expected returns. Columbus's solution after the wars of 1494 and 1495 was tribute. According to Las Casas and other sources, Indigenous people in the gold-bearing districts were required to deliver a fixed measure of gold at intervals, a quantity associated with a small hawk's bell filled with gold dust, while people in other regions were to supply cotton. These demands bore no relation to Indigenous production, since gold mining was not an established economic activity and the amounts required were beyond the capacity of most people. Failure to deliver was punished. The system was an extractive measure, not a negotiated exchange, and it displaced the reciprocal relationships that had earlier characterized relations between caciques and their communities.

Enslavement developed alongside tribute. In 1494 Columbus proposed to the Crown that captives taken in war, and in particular those designated as Caribs, could be sold in Spain to help finance the colony. In early 1495 Spanish forces rounded up a large number of Indigenous people on Hispaniola, and several hundred were shipped across the Atlantic, with many dying on the voyage. The Crown's response was mixed: Isabel questioned the legitimacy of enslaving people she regarded as her subjects and in 1500 ordered that some Indigenous people brought to Spain be returned, but the Crown also allowed enslavement of those classified as hostile or as Caribs, culminating in the 1503 decree discussed in Section 38.3. Indigenous slavery thus entered Spanish practice through exceptions that proved elastic. Chapter 53 examines the long history of Indigenous slavery, captivity, and adoption across the continent.

The institutions of forced labor took shape through improvisation. During the rebellion led by the Spanish official Francisco Roldán in 1497 to 1499, Columbus granted some rebels land and the services of Indigenous workers in settlement of their demands, an early form of what Spanish sources called repartimiento. When Nicolás de Ovando arrived as governor in 1502, he organized the distribution of Indigenous laborers among Spanish settlers more systematically, and in 1503 the Crown issued instructions allowing Indigenous people to be compelled to work for wages while being instructed in Christianity. The arrangement, in which a Spaniard held a grant of the labor and tribute of a group of Indigenous people, became known as the encomienda. The wages promised were often small or unpaid, and the conditions, especially in the gold mines, were severe.

Historical Vocabulary
Repartimiento and Encomienda

Definition. Repartimiento, "distribution," referred in the early Caribbean to the assignment of Indigenous people to Spanish colonists for labor. Encomienda, from the Spanish verb for "to entrust," named a grant by which a Spaniard, the encomendero, received the right to the labor or tribute of a group of Indigenous people in return for an obligation to protect and instruct them in Christianity.

Historical context. The institution drew on medieval Iberian precedents for entrusting populations to lords, but in the Caribbean it was adapted to a situation of conquest, a rapidly shifting population, and an extractive economy. Its forms changed over time, and it was reformed by laws such as the Laws of Burgos of 1512 and later legislation, which professed to regulate treatment but were poorly enforced.

Why it matters. The encomienda became the principal means of organizing Indigenous labor in much of Spanish America. It tied the interests of colonists to control over Indigenous people and created a class of settlers with a stake in the continuation of conquest.

Potential misunderstanding. Encomienda was not a grant of land and did not formally make Indigenous people slaves, but in practice, especially in the early Caribbean, its labor demands were often indistinguishable from forced servitude, and the legal distinction coexisted with enslavement of people classed as outside Crown protection. The mature system of the mainland differed from the improvised Caribbean version.

The consequences for Indigenous communities were severe. Mining and tribute drew men away from agriculture; women were taken for domestic and sexual labor; families were separated; and malnutrition and overwork increased vulnerability to disease. Contemporary observers, notably Las Casas, described these conditions in terms of devastation, and Spanish enumerations of the early 1500s record a steep and continuing fall in the number of Indigenous people on Hispaniola. Historians agree that the population collapsed within a few decades, but they continue to debate the proportions, the starting figure, and the relative roles of disease, violence, forced labor, famine, and displacement, a question that Chapter 40 takes up. What the evidence does not support is the idea that the decline resulted from a single cause or was inevitable; it flowed from decisions about labor, food, and violence made by colonists and officials.

Section 38.17 · Division IV

The Third and Fourth Voyages: The Geographic Horizon Widens

Columbus's third voyage, begun in 1498, took a more southerly route and in late July reached the island he named Trinidad. Passing through the Gulf of Paria, he encountered the fresh water pouring from the Orinoco and recognized, from the volume of water, that he was near a large landmass. He landed on the coast of what is now Venezuela, observed Indigenous people wearing pearls, and concluded that he might be near the Earthly Paradise. The voyage thus revealed a mainland, South America, but Columbus continued to interpret it within his framework of Asia. It also showed that the Caribbean was linked by sea to a continental coast, extending the geographical reach of Spanish contact.

Other Spanish and Portuguese navigators soon followed. Several captains, some with royal licenses and some in violation of Columbus's monopoly claims, explored the northern coast of South America in 1499 and 1500, finding pearl fisheries that attracted slave raiders. In 1500 a Portuguese fleet under Pedro Álvares Cabral reached the coast of present-day Brazil. The pace of discovery shows that the Atlantic corridor was no longer Columbus's private route but an open pathway used by rivals. This competition drove Spain to extend its own system of claims and licenses and made the Crown wary of leaving control in the hands of a single man.

Columbus's administration of Hispaniola ended in 1500 when the royal commissioner Francisco de Bobadilla, sent to investigate complaints, arrested Columbus and his brothers and sent them to Spain in chains. The Crown restored some of his privileges but ended his governorship, appointing Ovando. The episode illustrates the Crown's determination to assert control over a colony that had become valuable and turbulent. It also shows that Columbus's rule had produced as much conflict among Spaniards as among Indigenous people.

On his fourth voyage, from 1502 to 1504, Columbus sailed to the western Caribbean and along the coast of Central America, from what is now Honduras to Panama, searching for a strait to the Indian Ocean. Off the Bay Islands of Honduras his ships met a large trading canoe, described by his son Ferdinand and others, carrying cotton textiles, ceramics, copper objects, and other goods that Spanish observers recognized as the products of a more elaborate culture than those of the islands. The encounter was among the first Spanish glimpses of the Maya and Mesoamerican trading world, on whose networks Chapter 14 provides background. Columbus did not pursue it. His ships, damaged by storms and shipworm, were stranded on the north coast of Jamaica for about a year before rescue. He returned to Spain in late 1504, shortly before Isabel's death, and died in 1506 without having understood the continental scale of what he had opened.

Section 38.18 · Division IV

Claiming an Ocean: Papal Bulls, Tordesillas, and Imperial Sovereignty

European rulers justified their claims to the Americas through a set of legal and religious arguments that did not involve the consent of the peoples who lived there. In May 1493 Pope Alexander VI, a Valencian who was well disposed toward the Spanish monarchs, issued bulls, the best known beginning Inter caetera, that granted Castile rights over lands discovered to the west of a line in the Atlantic, on condition that the Crown work to convert the inhabitants. Papal authority was invoked as a way of resolving competition among Christian powers and of linking sovereignty to the missionary obligation. The bulls reflected medieval ideas of papal jurisdiction over non-Christian lands that were increasingly contested but still influential.

Portugal, which held earlier papal grants for the African coast, objected, and in June 1494 the two Iberian powers concluded the Treaty of Tordesillas, which moved the dividing line to a meridian 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands, reserving lands to the east for Portugal and to the west for Castile. The treaty was an agreement between two European monarchies and was later confirmed by the papacy. It created a legal framework for dividing the world among Christian rulers without reference to the inhabitants. Other European powers, notably France and later England, did not recognize its exclusivity, and Indigenous peoples were never party to it.

Spanish jurists and theologians also developed practical instruments for dealing with Indigenous peoples. As the colony expanded, critics emerged. In December 1511 a Dominican friar, Antonio de Montesinos, preached in Santo Domingo a sermon denouncing the colonists' treatment of Indigenous people and questioning their right to hold them in servitude, a protest that provoked royal discussions and led to the Laws of Burgos of 1512 to 1513, which regulated labor, housing, and religious instruction while affirming the encomienda. In 1513 a text known as the Requerimiento, drafted by Crown jurists, was prepared to be read aloud to Indigenous groups before the Spanish attacked them. It declared the authority of the pope and the Crown and demanded submission, threatening war and enslavement for refusal. It was usually read in Spanish to people who could not understand it, sometimes from a distance, and it has been seen by historians as a legal formality that justified conquest more than a genuine attempt to communicate.

These instruments should be understood as parts of a developing structure, not as a complete system in 1492. They show how claims of sovereignty were made and defended, how some Spaniards criticized the consequences, and how legal forms could coexist with brutality. For Indigenous peoples the issue was not legal theory but the practical effect: lands were claimed, communities were assigned to colonists, and refusal could justify violence. The long debates that followed, including those about the status of Indigenous people and the legitimacy of conquest, were responses to this reality. Chapter 54 considers how Christian missions and Indigenous religious life intersected with these claims.

Section 38.19 · Division IV

From Hispaniola to a Caribbean Colonial Network

Under Ovando, who arrived in 1502 with a large fleet carrying some thousands of settlers according to contemporary estimates, Hispaniola became the Crown's administrative center. Santo Domingo was rebuilt after a hurricane in 1502 and developed as a seat of government, church, and trade. Ovando pursued the final conquest of the western and eastern districts. In 1503 at Jaragua, he and his forces killed a number of caciques and nobles who had assembled, and executed Anacaona, in an episode Spanish sources describe as a response to an alleged conspiracy, and which Las Casas condemned as a massacre. In 1503 and 1504 the Spanish also fought a war in Higüey, where the cacique Cotubanamá was captured and executed. By the end of Ovando's term, effective Indigenous political independence on the island was broken, though resistance and flight continued.

From Hispaniola, Spanish activity spread. In 1508 and 1509 Juan Ponce de León, who had fought in the Higüey war, explored and then settled Puerto Rico, establishing a settlement and organizing Indigenous labor for gold. A Taíno uprising in 1511, in which a leader called Agüeybaná II is named in Spanish sources, was suppressed. In 1509 Juan de Esquivel began the Spanish colonization of Jamaica, and in 1511 Diego Velázquez led an expedition to Cuba, founding settlements, including Baracoa, and conquering the island over several years. Slave raiders from Hispaniola took thousands of Lucayans from the Bahamas to work in mines and fields, so that the archipelago was largely depopulated within about two decades. Spanish pearl fisheries off the northern coast of South America relied on enslaved Indigenous divers. On the mainland, Spanish settlers established a base at Darién in Panama in about 1510, from which Vasco Núñez de Balboa would cross the isthmus to the Pacific in 1513.

The network depended on several kinds of movement. Ships carried soldiers, officials, livestock, and supplies from Spain and between islands. Cattle and pigs multiplied on the islands and formed the basis of ranching and a food supply for expeditions. Sugar cane planted from Canary stock became an export crop on Hispaniola in the early sixteenth century, with mills appearing by the 1510s and 1520s. Small numbers of enslaved Africans began to be brought to the islands in the early 1500s, and as Indigenous labor declined, African slavery expanded, a development outside this chapter's scope but linked to its story. Information also moved: pilots, interpreters, and Indigenous captives carried knowledge of coasts, peoples, and rumors of rich lands, and Spanish officials collected this knowledge for the next expeditions.

Dependence on Indigenous knowledge persisted. Spanish forces relied on Indigenous guides, porters, and allies, and on captives trained as interpreters. The combination of ships, supply bases, military experience, and Indigenous intermediaries gave Spanish forces capabilities that did not exist in 1492. It is this accumulated capacity that the next section identifies as the Caribbean's role in the larger invasion.

Section 38.20 · Division IV

Indigenous Resistance, Flight, Survival, and Adaptation

A history of the early Caribbean that records only demographic collapse omits the ways in which Indigenous people acted. Resistance took many forms. Armed resistance included the actions of Caonabo and Guarionex, the Higüey war of 1503 to 1504, the Puerto Rican uprising of 1511, and, in Cuba, the resistance of the cacique Hatuey, who had fled Hispaniola and warned Cuban communities of Spanish behavior. Spanish forces captured Hatuey in 1512 and burned him, an episode recorded by Las Casas, who reported that he rejected baptism when told it would lead to a Christian heaven where Spaniards also went. The detail comes through a Spanish critic and cannot be verified, but it was preserved as an emblem of Indigenous defiance.

Flight was another strategy. People withdrew to mountain and forest zones, abandoned villages, and established hidden communities. The Spanish used a word, cimarrón, first for escaped livestock and then for Indigenous and later African people who fled bondage. Escape was dangerous but could produce enduring communities, and on Hispaniola such refuges allowed some groups to continue their lives beyond Spanish reach. Some Indigenous leaders also used negotiation. Guacanagarí's alliance with the Spanish, whatever his motives, preserved his community for a time, though it did not save it from later demands. In 1519 the cacique Enriquillo, who had been raised in a Franciscan mission and wronged by an encomendero, began a long guerrilla resistance in the mountains of Hispaniola that ended in 1533 with a negotiated settlement recognizing a community of Indigenous people, a case beyond this chapter's chronology but showing the persistence of resistance.

Survival also took the form of cultural adaptation and demographic mixing. Many Indigenous women bore children with Spanish settlers or enslaved Africans, creating mixed families that blended traditions; Indigenous words, foods, agricultural practices, and craft techniques entered Caribbean life; and Indigenous communities, even when reduced, continued to exist in parts of Cuba, Hispaniola, and Puerto Rico into later centuries. The claim, long repeated in textbooks, that the Taíno were extinct by the mid-sixteenth century reflects Spanish administrative records that stopped counting Indigenous people as a separate category and ignores the persistence of Indigenous ancestry, language traces, and community identities. In the Lesser Antilles, Kalinago communities resisted European settlement for much longer and survive today in Dominica and elsewhere. Contemporary Taíno cultural revival movements and the research of Indigenous Caribbean descendants have challenged the extinction narrative, and Chapter 149 returns to the problem of narratives of disappearance.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Indigenous Caribbean Societies, c. 1400–1513

What Changed

  • Political independence on Hispaniola ended within about a decade after sustained war and the destruction of leading cacicazgos.
  • Labor was reorganized from reciprocal and communal forms to tribute, repartimiento, and enslavement.
  • The Lucayan Archipelago was largely depopulated by slave raids and removals.
  • New animals, crops, diseases, and settlements altered the landscape and food systems.
  • Population declined sharply, with the proportions and causes the subject of continuing research.

What Stayed the Same

  • Cassava, maize, sweet potato, and other crops remained basic foods, and Indigenous agricultural knowledge supplied the colonists.
  • Canoe travel and knowledge of coasts and routes continued to be used by Indigenous people and borrowed by the Spanish.
  • Language, place-names, and elements of belief and practice survived in the Caribbean, in new forms.
  • Indigenous communities continued to assert themselves politically through resistance, negotiation, and flight.

Pattern and timing. Change was abrupt in political and economic terms and uneven in demographic and cultural terms. It was experienced most severely by communities in the Greater Antilles near Spanish centers and the Bahamas; greater continuity occurred in remote areas and in Kalinago communities of the Lesser Antilles. Recognizing both change and continuity prevents two errors: treating Indigenous Caribbean societies as static before 1492, and treating them as having vanished afterward.

Section 38.21 · Division V

The Caribbean Becomes the Launching Ground for Continental Conquest

By about 1513 the Spanish Caribbean had acquired what earlier expeditions lacked. It had ports and shipyards on several islands, a Crown administration in Santo Domingo that included a high court established in 1511, and commercial links to Seville. It had a body of soldiers and settlers with experience of fighting Indigenous peoples and of exploiting them for labor, and a set of leaders who had learned how to combine alliance, trickery, and force. It had interpreters, some of them Indigenous captives, and information about coasts and peoples. It had livestock and food supplies to provision expeditions. And it had an incentive: as the Indigenous population of the islands declined and gold deposits proved limited, colonists and officials looked for new sources of labor, captives, and wealth.

Expeditions began to push outward. Ponce de León sailed from Puerto Rico to the coast of Florida in 1513, a development examined in Chapter 41. Balboa reached the Pacific from Panama in the same year. In 1517 and 1518 expeditions sent from Cuba reached the Yucatán coast and encountered Maya communities with towns, architecture, and trade goods that suggested far greater wealth and complexity than the islands. These contacts led to further preparations, and the Spanish governor of Cuba began to organize a larger expedition. The history of that expedition and of the conquests that followed belongs to Chapter 39, and this chapter ends at the threshold.

It would be wrong to describe the invasion of the mainland as the inevitable culmination of 1492. The Spanish in 1513 did not know what lay west of the Caribbean, and they met Indigenous states and societies, including large and politically sophisticated Mesoamerican polities, whose capacities and alliances were unlike those of the Caribbean islands. The outcome of Spanish expansion on the mainland depended on contingent factors, including Indigenous political divisions, alliances, epidemics, and the choices of both Indigenous and Spanish leaders. What the Caribbean experience provided was the toolkit, the bases, and the habits that made certain choices more likely.

What was established in the Caribbean was thus a method and a framework, not a destiny. The method combined fortified bases, tribute, forced labor, enslavement, alliance with some Indigenous groups against others, and legal claims of sovereignty and conversion. The framework linked Spain and its colonies across the ocean through ships, institutions, and commercial networks. For Indigenous peoples of the Caribbean the cost had been immense. For Indigenous peoples of the mainland, who would confront these methods in turn, the Caribbean had provided a warning that some would heed and others, in different circumstances, would not.

Historical Debate

Encounter, Contact, Conquest, or Invasion?

The question: What term best describes the events beginning in 1492? Historians have used several, and the choice is not merely stylistic, because each term carries assumptions about who acted, what happened, and how it should be judged.

"Encounter" and "contact": These terms emphasize interaction between peoples and have been favored by historians seeking to include Indigenous actions and perspectives and to avoid a one-sided narrative of European triumph. They fit the first days on Guanahaní and Hispaniola, when exchange and hospitality predominated. Critics argue that they can make a violent process appear symmetrical and neutral, concealing the imbalance of power and the coercion that quickly followed.
"Conquest": This term emphasizes the acquisition of territory and political control and is well suited to the military campaigns on Hispaniola and elsewhere. It is traditional in Spanish-language historiography. Critics note that it can suggest a completed victory and a passive Indigenous population, whereas control was partial and contested for decades, and that it centers the conqueror's aims.
"Invasion": This term emphasizes the armed entry of outsiders into inhabited land and the claim of sovereignty without consent, and it centers the Indigenous experience of being invaded. Critics point out that it can obscure the early, cooperative phases of contact and the role of Indigenous allies, and that it may be read as a moral judgment rather than an analytical category.

Evidence and points of disagreement: All interpretations rely on the same sources, Spanish documents, archaeology, and Indigenous testimony, but they differ in the weight they give to different phases and actors. The disagreement is partly terminological and partly about scale and chronology: whether the key transformation was the first landing, the establishment of La Navidad, the second voyage, or the imposition of tribute and enslavement.

Why it matters: The term chosen frames how students understand responsibility, agency, and legitimacy. This textbook uses "Atlantic invasion" for the sustained process after 1492 because it captures the structure of armed arrival, fortification, and claims of sovereignty, while using "encounter" and "contact" for particular moments of exchange and acknowledging that the process changed over time and varied by place.

Section 38.22 · Review

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has traced the movement from a single landfall to a Caribbean colonial network. Before proceeding to Chapter 39, students should be able to explain what distinguished the post-1492 Atlantic corridor from earlier crossings, to describe Indigenous Caribbean societies on their own terms, to analyze the first encounters as diplomatic events interpreted differently by each side, to evaluate the evidence for the first voyage, to trace how tribute, repartimiento, encomienda, and enslavement emerged, and to assess the role of Indigenous resistance and survival.

The core insights of the chapter are these. The Caribbean was a populous, politically organized, sea-connected region before 1492. The labels "Taíno" and "Carib" are partly colonial constructs. Columbus's voyages mattered because they opened a repeatable, state-backed Atlantic corridor. Early meetings were shaped by Indigenous diplomacy and by mutual misunderstanding. The shift from encounter to invasion occurred through the establishment of fortified bases, tribute, forced labor, and enslavement. European claims of sovereignty were made without Indigenous consent. Indigenous peoples resisted, adapted, and survived. And by about 1513 the Caribbean had become a base for expansion to the mainland, a development narrated in Chapters 39 through 42. The review materials that follow are organized to help students consolidate these points.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that 1492 mattered not because Europeans discovered an unknown continent but because Columbus's voyages opened a sustained Atlantic corridor. Ships, settlers, soldiers, animals, plants, pathogens, institutions, and imperial claims could cross it repeatedly. The Norse crossings examined in Chapter 37 showed that the Atlantic could be crossed, but they lacked the state sponsorship, commercial incentives, and flow of people and supplies that made the Spanish crossings continuous.

The chapter began with the Caribbean itself. For several thousand years the islands had been inhabited by peoples who developed fishing, farming, and exchange economies, and by the thirteenth century the Greater Antilles contained complex societies led by hereditary caciques, with cassava agriculture, ceremonial plazas, and canoe networks reaching other islands and the South American coast. The labels Taíno, Lucayan, and Kalinago name broad linguistic and cultural groupings, while the Spanish categories of friendly "Arawaks" and hostile "Caribs" served colonial purposes and should not be adopted uncritically.

Iberian expansion supplied the means and models. Portuguese and Castilian experience on the Atlantic islands, with sugar, slavery, conquest, and papal authorization, together with advances in navigation and the knowledge of Atlantic wind systems, enabled Columbus's project. His geographical assumptions were mistaken, and he never recognized that he had reached unfamiliar continents, but his voyages established a route that others could follow.

The first voyage brought Columbus to Guanahaní in October 1492. The encounter was a diplomatic event in which Indigenous people offered hospitality and exchange and the Spanish enacted a legal claim of possession and seized captives. Columbus read generosity as submission and thereby misjudged the people he met. The evidence for these events is limited and mediated: his journal survives through Las Casas, other contemporary accounts are Spanish, and archaeology and Indigenous knowledge are essential correctives.

The establishment of the garrison at La Navidad, and its destruction, showed both how an armed presence changed the encounter and how Indigenous leaders could respond. The second voyage of 1493 transformed exploration into colonization, with some seventeen ships, settlers, animals, seeds, and clergy. The settlement of La Isabela revealed the dependence of the colony on Indigenous food and labor and the difficulties of establishing a European settlement in an inhabited land.

On Hispaniola, Indigenous leaders responded in different ways. Some, like Guacanagarí, allied with the Spanish; others, like Caonabo and Guarionex, resisted; and the island's political fragmentation shaped the outcome. Spanish military campaigns, backed by horses, dogs, and steel weapons and aided by Indigenous allies, defeated the principal resistance by 1498, and the final conquest of western and eastern districts came under Ovando in 1503 and 1504. Tribute, enslavement, repartimiento, and encomienda emerged through improvisation and became institutions that tied colonists' interests to control over Indigenous people.

European rulers and church authorities claimed sovereignty over the lands without Indigenous consent, through papal bulls, the Treaty of Tordesillas, and later the Requerimiento. These instruments show how law and religion were used to justify conquest, though they also provoked criticism, notably the sermon of Montesinos in 1511 and the debate that led to the Laws of Burgos. The measures did little to restrain exploitation in practice.

By about 1513 the Spanish had extended their presence from Hispaniola to Puerto Rico, Jamaica, and Cuba, depopulated the Bahamas through slave raiding, and established footholds on the mainland coast. The Caribbean network provided ports, ships, supplies, interpreters, military experience, and institutions for further expansion. Indigenous resistance took the forms of armed rebellion, flight, negotiation, and cultural adaptation, and Indigenous ancestry, language, and community identities persisted despite the catastrophe and despite the myth of extinction.

The chapter has also shown that the choice of terms matters. Encounter, contact, conquest, and invasion each emphasize different aspects of the period, and a responsible account uses them with attention to chronology, place, and perspective. It has stressed that the invasion of the mainland was not an inevitable result of 1492 but depended on contingent factors, and that the Caribbean provided methods and bases, not a predetermined outcome.

These points establish the foundation for the chapters that follow: the conquest of Mesoamerica in Chapter 39, epidemic catastrophe and demographic change in Chapter 40, the Spanish Southeast and Florida in Chapter 41, and the northern frontier of New Spain in Chapter 42. In each, students should watch for the Caribbean's legacy, the tools and ideas first developed in the Antilles, and for the specific Indigenous histories that shaped how those tools worked.

The garrison left by Columbus on Hispaniola in 1492 and destroyed by 1493; the first European fort in the Caribbean.
La Isabela
The settlement founded on Hispaniola's north coast in 1494; the first sustained Spanish town in the Caribbean, later abandoned in favor of Santo Domingo.
Tribute
A fixed obligation, in gold, cotton, food, or labor, imposed by the Spanish on Indigenous communities.
Repartimiento
In the early Caribbean, the distribution of Indigenous people to Spanish colonists for labor.
Encomienda
A grant to a Spaniard of the labor or tribute of a group of Indigenous people in return for protecting and instructing them in Christianity; in practice often coercive.
Papal bull
A formal decree of the pope; the 1493 bulls supported Castile's claims in the Atlantic and tied them to the duty to convert.
Treaty of Tordesillas
The 1494 agreement between Castile and Portugal dividing claims in the Atlantic world along a meridian west of the Cape Verde Islands; Indigenous peoples were not parties.
Requerimiento
A text drafted in 1513 to be read to Indigenous peoples demanding submission to Crown and Church before war could be waged against them.
Cimarrón
A Spanish term originally for escaped livestock, later applied to Indigenous and African people who fled bondage.
Ethnohistory
A method combining documentary, archaeological, linguistic, and Indigenous evidence to reconstruct the history of peoples poorly represented in their own written records.
Columbian exchange
The movement of plants, animals, microbes, and people between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres after 1492, introduced here and treated further in Chapter 40.

Building a Source Dossier on the First Voyage

This project asks you to assemble and analyze a small dossier of sources on one episode from the first two voyages of Columbus, comparing documentary, archaeological, and Indigenous perspectives and assessing how each shapes our understanding.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it identifies how a particular kind of history began: one in which the movement of ships, people, animals, diseases, laws, and claims across an ocean became continuous, and in which Indigenous societies on one side of the ocean found themselves confronting powers organized on another. It does not treat 1492 as a moment of discovery or a simple beginning. The Caribbean already had a long history, and the chapter's first task was to establish that. The Atlantic corridor altered the conditions under which that history continued.

The chapter also shows that processes later associated with the entire hemisphere were first worked out in specific places by specific people under specific pressures. Tribute, forced labor, the use of interpreters and allies, the building of fortified bases, and the legal vocabulary of sovereignty and conversion were improvised in the Antilles before they became standard. Recognizing this helps students see conquest as a historical process with stages, contingencies, and alternatives. It also helps them see that Indigenous leaders and communities were not simply victims: they negotiated, allied, resisted, fled, and adapted within constraints that were severe and growing.

Later chapters will show how these developments shaped the continent. Chapter 39 follows the Spanish from the Caribbean into Mesoamerica, where Indigenous states, alliances, and political divisions produced very different dynamics. Chapter 40 examines epidemic disease and demographic catastrophe with attention to regional variation. Chapters 41 and 42 follow the Spanish into Florida, the Southeast, and the northern frontier. In each, the Caribbean's legacy is visible, but so are the distinct histories of Indigenous peoples who met the invasion on their own terms.

Finally, the Caribbean story did not end in 1513, and it did not end in extinction. Indigenous ancestry, language, foods, and memory persisted, and descendants continue to reclaim and rewrite this history. The textbook's larger argument, that Indigenous peoples are continuing political and cultural communities rather than remnants of an earlier world, begins to take shape here, and it will be developed through the chapters that follow.
