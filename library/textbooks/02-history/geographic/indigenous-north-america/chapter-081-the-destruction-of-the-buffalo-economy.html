Chapter Opening

Between roughly 1820 and 1890, an animal that had anchored the economies, ceremonies, diplomacy, and daily labor of dozens of Indigenous nations was driven from nearly all of its range. The loss was ecological, economic, and political at once, and it reshaped the balance of power between Indigenous nations and the expanding settler states of the United States and Canada.

The National Archives holds a photograph, made in 1878 at Dodge City, Kansas, of a hide yard belonging to the firm of Rath and Wright. It shows hides stacked in great numbers at a commercial yard connected to the railroad network that carried them east. The image is often reproduced because it condenses a historical process into a single view: animals killed on the open Plains, their skins gathered, baled, and shipped over steel rails to tanneries, merchants, and manufacturers who never saw the country from which the hides came. What the photograph does not show is the world from which those hides were removed. Behind each stack was an animal that, for Lakota, Cheyenne, Comanche, Blackfoot, Cree, Métis, Mandan, Pawnee, and many other peoples, was a source of food, shelter, clothing, tools, trade goods, ceremony, and political independence.

This chapter studies how that world was undone. Its central question is how the destruction of the bison economy changed the balance of power between Indigenous nations and expanding settler states, and how Plains communities responded to the loss of an essential foundation of their independence. The question is deliberately broader than an account of slaughter. The near-extermination of the bison was an environmental catastrophe, but it was also an economic transformation and a political one. It removed the material base on which many Plains nations had built their mobility, their trade, their food security, and their capacity to bargain with traders, armies, and governments. It did so by means of mechanisms that this chapter traces one at a time: market incentives, industrial demand for leather, improved rifles, railroad construction, military and civil policy, settler expansion, drought and ecological pressure, and differences among regions.

Two interpretive cautions govern the chapter. The first is that the destruction of the buffalo cannot be reduced to a single cause or a single villain. Commercial hide hunters, eastern and European markets, railroad companies, army officers, Interior Department officials, Indigenous and Métis hunters who sold robes and pemmican, environmental conditions, and the growth of livestock and farming all played roles, and these roles differed from the Southern Plains to the Northern Plains to the Canadian prairies. Historians disagree about how to weigh them, and the chapter closes by presenting that disagreement. The second caution is that the loss of the herds did not end Indigenous nations, Indigenous sovereignty, or Indigenous culture. It profoundly damaged established economies and political independence, and it exposed communities to hunger and administrative control. Yet the same communities adapted, negotiated, preserved knowledge, and continued to assert their identities and rights.

The evidence for this history is unusually varied. Trade-post ledgers, railroad shipping records, and customs and freight data document commercial flows. Travelers' narratives, military reports, and naturalists' accounts describe herds and hunts, often with the biases of observers who did not share Indigenous understandings of what they saw. Government documents, including agency reports and the annual reports of the Secretary of the Interior, show how officials understood the connection between food and political control. Photographs and museum collections preserve the physical remains of the buffalo economy. Winter counts, oral histories, and recorded Indigenous testimony, much of it mediated by interpreters and non-Indigenous recorders, preserve how communities remembered the change. Each kind of source illuminates part of the story and conceals another part, and the chapter's source features ask students to read them critically.

Geographically, the chapter crosses the international boundary. The bison economy was not an American phenomenon with a Canadian footnote. The Blackfoot Confederacy, the Plains Cree, the Assiniboine, the Métis, and the Lakota who sought refuge north of the line all lived through the same collapse, under different governments with different policies, and the Canadian experience is treated in its own sections rather than appended to the American one. The chapter also looks across time: it begins before the commercial slaughter of the 1870s, because the bison's decline did not begin suddenly, and it ends with the first efforts to preserve surviving animals, while leaving the later history of Indigenous bison restoration to the chapters that treat environmental stewardship.

Within Volume VII, the chapter has a specific role. The wars of the Plains, treated in Chapters 73 through 80, are often narrated as contests of arms. This chapter argues that the conquest of the Plains also involved the destruction of a continental economic and ecological system, and that this destruction shaped the terms on which treaties, reserves, and reservations were imposed. It is therefore a bridge to Chapter 82, on Canada's numbered treaties, Chapter 83, on the Red River and North-West Resistances, and Chapter 84, on the reservation and reserve order, each of which depends on the conditions described here.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the ecology of the Great Plains bison and why historical estimates of bison populations remain uncertain.
  2. Describe the diverse political economies of Plains nations, distinguishing mobile equestrian hunting societies from village nations that combined farming, hunting, and trade.
  3. Analyze the material, social, and spiritual importance of the bison, including the central role of women's labor in processing meat and hides.
  4. Trace the growth of the robe trade and pemmican provisioning before 1870 and explain how commercial markets began to pressure both herds and communities.
  5. Explain how railroads, industrial leather demand, improved firearms, and a commercial supply chain turned a market opportunity into mass slaughter.
  6. Distinguish Indigenous and Métis commercial hunting from the later industrial hide hunt in scale, organization, and purpose.
  7. Assess the evidence for the roles of the United States Army and federal Indian policy, separating documented orders and actions from later recollection and attribution.
  8. Compare the destruction of the southern herd, the northern herd, and the Canadian prairie herds, and the consequences for the peoples of each region.
  9. Evaluate Indigenous responses, including continued hunting, negotiation, migration, diversification, and acceptance of reservation arrangements, as strategic decisions made under constraint.
  10. Analyze how hunger, rations, and the administration of food changed Plains social life and political relationships in the United States and Canada.
  11. Interpret primary sources, including a photograph, government reports, legislation, and Hornaday's 1889 study, with attention to authorship, purpose, and limits.
  12. Distinguish near-extinction from extinction and the loss of an economy from the end of Indigenous nationhood, and connect the chapter's argument to the treaty and reservation histories that follow.

Historical Context

The Great Plains stretch from the prairie-forest margins of the Mississippi and Missouri valleys to the Rocky Mountain front, and from the Texas Panhandle and the southern Plains into the grasslands of present-day Alberta, Saskatchewan, and Manitoba. Rainfall declines from east to west, tallgrass gives way to mixed-grass and shortgrass, and the climate swings between drought and wetter periods. This variable, grass-based environment supported very large populations of grazing animals, and among them the plains bison was the largest and most economically important. People had hunted bison on the Plains for many thousands of years, using cliffs, corrals, and surrounds, and Chapter 29 treated the world of Plains peoples before the horse.

The introduction of horses, traced in Chapters 49 through 51, transformed that world from the eighteenth century onward. Horses allowed hunters to cover greater distances, move heavier loads, and kill more animals more safely, and they changed warfare, wealth, and diplomacy. In the Southern Plains, Comanche power rested on horses, bison, and trade, as Chapter 50 explained. On the northern and central Plains, the Lakota, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Blackfoot, Crow, Plains Cree, Assiniboine, and others built equestrian economies centered on bison. At the same time, village nations such as the Mandan, Hidatsa, Arikara, Pawnee, and Wichita combined farming with seasonal hunts, and traded maize and other goods with mobile neighbors. The Plains were not a single culture. They were a set of interlocking economies and political relationships sharing a dependence on the bison.

By the early nineteenth century, those economies were already connected to wider markets. Furs, robes, horses, guns, and manufactured goods moved along trade networks tied to the Hudson's Bay Company, American trading firms, and New Mexican merchants, and Chapters 46 and 52 examined parts of that history. Epidemics, especially smallpox, struck repeatedly, and Indigenous nations negotiated treaties, alliances, and boundaries among themselves and with outsiders. The mid-nineteenth-century treaties of Fort Laramie and Medicine Lodge, discussed in Chapters 73 through 77, recognized hunting rights on specified lands, but made those rights depend on bison remaining in numbers sufficient to justify hunting. That conditional language proved prophetic, because the same decades saw the arrival of railroads, the growth of industrial markets, and the expansion of United States and Canadian settlement.

North of the international boundary, the Canadian Plains were undergoing their own transformation. After the transfer of Rupert's Land and the North-Western Territory to Canada in 1870, the Canadian government sought to open the prairies to settlement and to negotiate numbered treaties with Indigenous nations, a process treated in Chapter 82. The North-West Mounted Police were created in 1873 and sent west in 1874. The Métis, whose emergence Chapter 52 traced, had long organized large-scale buffalo hunts and pemmican production. These northern developments occurred in a different administrative and legal setting than those in the United States, but they depended on the same animal and, in the end, suffered the same loss.

Historians approaching this subject confront two opposite temptations. One is to treat the slaughter as an inevitable consequence of industrial expansion, as if the buffalo's fate were determined by forces beyond anyone's choices. The other is to treat it as a single deliberate plan carried out by the army or the government. This chapter takes neither course. It treats the destruction as the product of identifiable economic incentives, technologies, policies, and decisions, some of them deliberate and some of them not, operating on an ecosystem already subject to pressure, and affecting Indigenous nations who were active participants in the history rather than passive observers.

Division I — The Buffalo and the Indigenous Plains World · Section 81.1

The Great Plains and the World of the Bison

A history of the buffalo economy must begin with the animal and the grassland that sustained it. The bison is a large grazing ruminant. Adult bulls could approach a ton in weight, while cows were substantially smaller, and both sexes were adapted to cold, wind, and the seasonal scarcity of forage. The species lived on grasses and sedges, and its fortunes depended on the character of the grassland. Across the Great Plains, annual rainfall declines from east to west, which produces a rough gradient of vegetation: tallgrass prairie on the wetter eastern margins, mixed-grass prairie in the middle, and shortgrass prairie closer to the Rocky Mountains. Each zone offered different forage, and each responded differently to drought, fire, and grazing.

The relationship between grass and grazer was reciprocal. Grazing, trampling, wallowing, and the deposition of dung altered plant communities, soil structure, and nutrient cycles, and fire, whether lightning-ignited or set by people, renewed the grass and drew animals to fresh growth. Scholars continue to debate how extensively Indigenous burning shaped Plains vegetation compared with climate and natural fire, and the answer probably varied by place and period. What is clear is that the Plains were a dynamic landscape in which grass, fire, climate, bison, and human beings acted on one another, rather than a static natural background to human history.

Bison moved in response to forage, water, snow, fire, insects, and weather, and their movements were less regular than popular images suggest. Older accounts imagined a clockwork migration, north in summer and south in winter, of two great herds. Historians and ecologists now emphasize that movements were varied, local, and responsive to conditions in a given year, and that the terms northern herd and southern herd became prominent in the nineteenth century, partly after the construction of the first transcontinental railroad appeared to divide the animals into two groups. Bison gathered in large aggregations during the summer rut and dispersed into smaller groups at other times, calved in spring, and sought sheltered river valleys in severe winters. Hunters, traders, and travelers who saw very large concentrations were witnessing real phenomena, but their reports were snapshots of particular places and seasons and cannot simply be summed into a continental total.

Two forms of bison are usually distinguished. The plains bison lived across the grasslands from the Southern Plains into the Canadian prairies and also occupied some adjoining parklands and mountain valleys. The wood bison, generally described as larger and darker, lived in the boreal forests and meadows of northern Alberta, the Northwest Territories, and adjoining regions, and survived in smaller numbers. The two forms are important for different reasons in this chapter. The plains bison was the basis of the Plains economies and the object of the great commercial killing, while the wood bison, found farther north, survived in limited and remote ranges and entered later conservation history. Bison had also lived in some areas east of the Mississippi and in intermountain valleys, but their numbers in those regions fell earlier, a reminder that the Plains were the center of a larger and older pattern of range contraction.

Other animals shared this ecosystem and interacted with the bison. Pronghorn and elk used the grasslands, wolves preyed on bison and scavenged their carcasses, and ravens, magpies, and other scavengers benefited from kills. Prairie dogs and other burrowing animals altered vegetation and soil in ways that interacted with grazing. When historians speak of the buffalo economy, they therefore refer not simply to a species but to a web of ecological relationships in which people were participants, taking food and materials, using fire, moving camps, and sometimes competing among themselves for access to the herds.

The size of the bison population before the commercial slaughter is uncertain, and the uncertainty is a methodological point as much as a numerical one. Early twentieth-century naturalists proposed very high figures, with some estimates reaching 60 million or more. Later researchers, using carrying-capacity models, travelers' accounts, trade data, and ecological evidence, have argued for lower totals, often in the range of a few tens of millions or fewer, and they stress that populations fluctuated with drought, winter severity, disease, predation, and human hunting. This textbook does not adopt a single figure. The scale of the herds is better understood from their economic consequences, the regional records of their destruction, and the range of reasoned estimates than from any one number. Section 81.18 returns to the problem of measurement.

These ecological facts frame the rest of the chapter. A grassland ecosystem sustaining large migratory herds could support dense human economies but was also vulnerable to sustained, concentrated killing, particularly of breeding females, and to compounding pressures from drought, competing livestock, and habitat disruption. The system that Indigenous nations had learned to use for many centuries was resilient against the pressures of a hunting economy that moved with the herds, and fragile against an industrial commercial system that could remove animals continuously and ship their products across the continent.

Section 81.2

Indigenous Nations and the Buffalo Economy

It is tempting to describe Plains peoples as hunters who followed the herds, but that description hides the organization of the economies involved. The buffalo economy was a set of systems for producing, distributing, storing, exchanging, and governing the use of a resource, and it varied by nation, environment, and period. The Lakota, Nakota, and Dakota, the Cheyenne and Arapaho, the Kiowa and Comanche, the Blackfoot Confederacy, the Plains Cree and Assiniboine, and other peoples shared the bison but organized themselves in different ways, and the village nations of the Missouri and southern Plains combined bison hunting with agriculture in ways that distinguished them from mobile neighbors.

Among the equestrian hunting nations, the horse changed the scale and tempo of the economy rather than inventing it. Mounted hunters could locate herds across long distances, approach them swiftly, and kill efficiently, and horses carried the products of the hunt, including meat, hides, and the poles and covers of mobile households. Horse herds themselves became forms of wealth that were exchanged, gifted, raided, and used to establish status and alliances, and Chapters 49 and 50 examined that transformation. Horses were also pasture animals, and as their numbers grew they competed with bison for winter forage in river valleys, a pressure that Section 81.5 treats as part of the ecological background to the later crisis.

Hunting was organized and regulated. Many nations maintained systems to coordinate communal hunts so that individual hunters did not scatter the herd before the group was ready to act. Among the Lakota, camp police societies known as akicita enforced rules of discipline during the hunt, and comparable military or soldier societies performed similar functions among the Cheyenne, Blackfoot, and others, with details differing by nation. The seasonal round tied hunting to other activities: spring and summer gatherings, including the great summer assemblies at which ceremonies such as the Sun Dance took place, coincided with the time when bison gathered in large groups, and communal hunts, councils, trade, and diplomacy were linked in the same season. Older techniques such as jumps and pounds continued to be used, and the Head-Smashed-In Buffalo Jump in present-day Alberta, associated with the Blackfoot and used over several thousand years, indicates how long and how organized this knowledge was.

Territorial diplomacy was a central part of the system. Hunting grounds were claimed, shared, contested, and negotiated, and alliances and conflicts among Indigenous nations frequently turned on access to the herds. The Lakota expansion westward onto the Plains in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries brought them into conflict with the Crow, Pawnee, and others, and Southern Plains peace and war among the Comanche, Kiowa, Cheyenne, and Arapaho likewise shaped who could hunt where. As the herds contracted, such competition intensified, as in August 1873 when Lakota attacked a Pawnee hunting party in southwestern Nebraska in what is known as the Massacre Canyon fight. Estimates of the number of Pawnee killed vary, but the episode illustrates how scarcity could turn long-standing rivalries into mass violence, and it contributed to the Pawnee decision to leave Nebraska for Indian Territory in 1874 and 1875.

The village nations offer a necessary contrast. The Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara on the upper Missouri, the Pawnee on the central Plains, and the Wichita and related peoples on the southern Plains lived in permanent or semi-permanent villages, cultivated maize, beans, squash, and sunflowers, and conducted organized hunts, often in summer and winter. They traded agricultural produce to mobile hunting peoples in exchange for meat, hides, and horses, and their villages served as commercial centers. The Mandan and Hidatsa villages near the Knife River were long-standing nodes of exchange, and the Wichita villages were linked to the Comanche in a trading relationship across the southern Plains. Such exchange made the buffalo economy a regional system of complementary production rather than a uniform way of life. It also made the village nations vulnerable in particular ways, since epidemics, notably the smallpox of 1837 on the upper Missouri, damaged their populations severely and weakened them before the commercial pressures of later decades.

Commerce reached beyond the Plains. The Comanche and their neighbors traded with New Mexican merchants, including the Hispano traders known as comancheros and the buffalo hunters known as ciboleros, who sought robes, meat, and hides. Northern nations traded with the Hudson's Bay Company and with American firms. In every case, Indigenous producers and traders participated as partners and competitors, bargaining over prices and terms, choosing among traders, and adapting production to the goods they wanted. The buffalo economy, in short, combined subsistence, exchange, governance, ritual, and diplomacy in organized, adaptive forms. Understanding that organization is necessary to understand what was lost, because the destruction of the herds removed not merely a food supply but the foundation on which a distinctive set of political and economic institutions rested.

Section 81.3

The Buffalo in Everyday Life, Labor, and Spiritual Practice

For the nations that depended on it, the bison supplied far more than meat. Fresh meat, including the hump, ribs, tongue, and organs, was eaten after successful hunts, and surplus was preserved. Drying thin strips of meat produced a durable food, and combining pounded dried meat with rendered fat, and sometimes berries, produced pemmican, a high-energy food that stored well and traveled easily. Marrow and bone grease provided additional fat. Hides could be tanned with the hair left on to make winter robes, or with the hair removed to make softer leather for clothing, moccasins, shield covers, and the covers of dwellings. Tipi covers were sewn from many hides, and rawhide was cut and shaped into containers such as parfleches, folded cases used for storing food and goods, as well as ropes, drum heads, and saddle parts.

Other parts of the animal had similarly practical uses. Sinew served as thread and bowstring, horn was shaped into spoons, cups, and ladles, and bone became scrapers, awls, and other tools. Hair was twisted into rope and used as stuffing, stomachs and bladders became containers, and dried dung provided fuel on a largely treeless landscape. Among the Mandan and Hidatsa, hide stretched over willow frames formed bull boats for river crossings. This catalogue is sometimes presented as proof that the bison was an all-purpose resource, but the list needs qualification. Not every nation used every part in the same way, and use depended on environment, technology, trade access, and the availability of substitutes such as cloth, metal, and iron kettles obtained through commerce. The point is not that the bison was the only material base of Plains life, but that it was the central and most flexible one.

Women's labor was the foundation of this material system. Hunters killed animals, but the transformation of carcass into food, shelter, and goods was overwhelmingly women's work in most Plains nations. Butchering large animals, cutting meat into strips, drying, pounding, and rendering fat for pemmican, and preparing hides were demanding, skilled tasks performed under time pressure, because meat spoils and hides must be processed promptly. Hide preparation involved fleshing, scraping, and tanning, often with the animal's own brain, followed by smoking or other finishing, and a single robe could require substantial labor. Women made tipi covers, clothing, bags, and containers, maintained the household, and managed distribution and exchange within and beyond the camp. In many nations tipis and the goods produced in them were understood as women's property or responsibility, and women's skill and output carried social recognition. Among the Lakota, for example, quillwork was organized with its own standards of achievement, and finished work expressed status and skill.

Women's labor also connected the household to the market. When trading firms began to demand robes, the supply depended on women's capacity to process hides, and the number of robes a household could produce was limited by the labor available. Some scholars argue that rising demand for robes increased the value of women's work and may have encouraged larger households, including polygynous marriages, in some Plains societies, although the evidence is not uniform across nations and the interpretation is debated. What is clear is that expansion of commercial hide production placed particular burdens on women's labor, and the later destruction of the herds disrupted that labor system at its foundation, a point developed in Section 81.16.

Spiritual practice was inseparable from material use. Many Plains nations understood the bison as a relative, a gift, or a being with which humans maintained reciprocal obligations, and ceremonies marked hunts, renewal, and community life. The Sun Dance, held in various forms by many nations, often involved bison imagery, offerings, and the use of hide and skull. Lakota tradition recounts the coming of White Buffalo Calf Woman, who brought the sacred pipe and ceremonies, and the rare white bison has had profound meaning in Lakota and other traditions. The Blackfoot associated certain objects, including stones called iniskim, with attracting bison, and among the Mandan and Hidatsa, ceremonies sought to ensure the return of the herds. These practices differ in detail and meaning among nations and cannot be reduced to a single Plains religion. They indicate that the relationship with the buffalo was not only economic but moral, ceremonial, and relational, which is part of why the animals' destruction was experienced not simply as the loss of a resource but as the rupture of a way of living in the world.

Distinctions among nations should therefore be preserved. A Lakota camp on the northern Plains, a Comanche band on the southern Plains, a Blackfoot community in the northwestern grasslands, a Mandan village on the Missouri, and a Plains Cree camp in the parkland differed in their economies, ceremonial calendars, leadership structures, and use of the bison, and each changed over time. The generalizations in this section describe a broad pattern of dependence and integration. They are not a substitute for the nation-specific histories that appear throughout this textbook.

Historical Vocabulary
Pemmican and the Buffalo Economy

Definition. Pemmican is a preserved food made by drying meat, usually bison, pounding it, and mixing it with rendered fat, sometimes with berries added. The word comes from a Cree term associated with fat. The mixture was packed into hide bags, where it could be stored for long periods and transported conveniently.

Historical context. Pemmican was both an Indigenous food and a commodity. Plains peoples used it for winter provisions, travel, and emergency reserves, and its production depended on women's labor and on access to large numbers of animals. The Hudson's Bay Company and other fur-trade enterprises relied heavily on pemmican to provision canoe brigades and posts, and the Métis of the Red River became specialists in its large-scale production. Competition over the pemmican supply contributed to conflicts such as the Pemmican Proclamation of 1814 and the violence at Seven Oaks in 1816, which belong to the earlier history of the Red River region.

Why it matters. Pemmican shows that the buffalo economy supplied not only households but a continental commercial system. A food produced by Indigenous and Métis women connected the Plains to the fur trade and to transport networks reaching far beyond the grasslands, and the herd's decline therefore threatened trade as well as subsistence.

Potential misunderstanding. Pemmican is sometimes described as a simple traditional food unaffected by markets. In fact, its commercial production expanded in response to demand, and in the nineteenth century large-scale commercial pemmican operated differently from household production, a distinction that matters when comparing Indigenous and Métis commercial hunting with the later industrial hide hunt.

Section 81.4

The Buffalo Robe Trade and the Growth of Commercial Markets, c. 1820–1860

Indigenous bison economies were commercial before the 1870s, but their commerce was shaped by Indigenous producers' needs and constrained by their labor. The early nineteenth century saw rapid expansion of trading posts and a growing market for robes, which were valued in eastern cities and abroad as lap robes, coats, and bedding, and in sleighs and carriages. This section traces that expansion, because it established commercial relationships and pressures that later industrial hunting would exploit, and because it illustrates the difference between a commercial economy embedded in Indigenous production and one that overran it.

On the upper Missouri, the American Fur Company established Fort Union at the confluence of the Missouri and Yellowstone rivers in 1828, and the post became a major center of exchange with the Assiniboine, Crow, Blackfoot, Lakota, and other nations. Steamboat service on the river from the early 1830s brought goods upriver and robes and furs downriver. Although the trade had begun with beaver and other pelts, the decline of the beaver market and the growth of robe demand moved the trade increasingly toward bison. Reports from the 1840s indicate that the American Fur Company shipped on the order of one hundred thousand robes in many years, though figures vary by source and year. In the north, the Hudson's Bay Company, whose business had centered on furs and provisioning, also took robes, and the demand for pemmican continued to link the northern Plains to the company's supply system.

On the Southern Plains, Bent's Fort, built in the early 1830s on the Arkansas River in present-day southeastern Colorado by the partnership of Bent, St. Vrain and Company, became a principal trading center. The fort stood near the lands of Cheyenne, Arapaho, Kiowa, and Comanche peoples, and its commerce was bound up with diplomacy. William Bent married Owl Woman, a Cheyenne woman, and family ties reinforced commercial relationships. In 1840, Cheyenne and Arapaho leaders and Kiowa and Comanche leaders concluded a major peace in the vicinity of the fort, a settlement that reduced conflict and facilitated trade. The National Park Service, which now interprets the site, identifies the robe trade as a significant force reshaping commercial and social relationships on the Southern Plains. Traders offered blankets, cloth, metal tools, kettles, guns, ammunition, beads, and other goods, and Indigenous consumers exercised choice among suppliers and sought favorable terms.

The robe trade depended on women's labor, which set limits on the scale of production. A robe required skilled processing, and traders sought robes in good condition, which meant winter robes with full hair. This created a structural pressure on the animals themselves. Cow hides were generally preferred for robes because they were easier to work, and winter hunting for robes focused on cows, whose loss reduced the reproductive capacity of the herd. Historians who study the robe trade, including Dan Flores in his analysis of bison ecology and diplomacy on the Southern Plains, emphasize that the combination of robe hunting, horse herds, and drought could strain bison populations in specific regions well before the commercial hide hunt. The trade also increased demand for horses, which were needed for transport and hunting, and demand for manufactured goods, which tied households to traders.

Commercial exchange shaped relationships among Indigenous nations as well as with traders. Control over access to posts, rivalry over trade, and competition for hunting grounds interacted with older alliances and conflicts. Disease accompanied commerce: the smallpox epidemic of 1837 devastated the Mandan and affected other upper Missouri peoples, and cholera in 1849 struck the Southern Plains, and the latter contributed to the decision to abandon Bent's original fort in that year. Overland migrants crossing the Plains on the trails to Oregon, California, and Utah in the 1840s and 1850s added pressure on grass, water, and timber along their routes.

It is important to avoid overstating the effects of the early robe trade. Indigenous commercial hunting in this period was embedded in Indigenous social organization, limited by women's labor and by transport, and shaped by Indigenous decisions about what to trade and when. It was not equivalent in scale, organization, or purpose to the industrial slaughter that followed, which was driven by outside capital, killed animals for hides alone, and was not restrained by any community's labor or governance. At the same time, the robe trade should not be romanticized as ecologically benign. It concentrated hunting on cows, linked communities to markets whose demands they could not control, and created economic dependencies that made households more vulnerable when commercial conditions changed. The robe trade thus established early commercial pressures on herds and communities, and it prepared the ground, without determining the outcome, for what came after 1870.

Environmental Pressures and the Changing Plains Before 1870

The destruction of the bison did not begin in 1870. Popular memory often compresses the story into the decade of the hide hunt, but the herds were already under pressure in some regions decades earlier, and understanding that earlier pressure is necessary both for explaining why the later slaughter was so effective and for resisting the claim that the herds were inexhaustible until railroads arrived. At the same time, regional declines before 1870 differed in kind and degree from the continent-wide commercial killing that followed, and the distinction needs to be maintained.

Bison had disappeared from most of the country east of the Mississippi by the early nineteenth century, a contraction produced by hunting, settlement, and habitat change. On the Plains themselves, drought was a recurrent pressure. Dan Flores and others have argued that on the Southern Plains in the 1840s and 1850s, drought reduced forage and water, and that large horse and mule herds, owned by Indigenous nations and increasingly by traders and migrants, competed with bison for the same winter pastures in river valleys. In such circumstances bison populations could contract regionally even without large-scale commercial hunting. The evidence for the magnitude of these effects is debated, but the argument illustrates that ecological stress was already present, and that Indigenous horse herds were part of the ecological system rather than external to it.

Hunting for robes added to the strain by concentrating killing on cows, as Section 81.4 explained. Overland trails along the Platte and other rivers affected grass, wood, and water in corridors that had been important winter refuges for bison, and military posts and settlements added further demand for resources. Some historians have also pointed to diseases carried by domestic cattle, such as bovine tuberculosis, brucellosis, and anthrax, as a possible contributor to bison mortality, though the evidence is limited and the scale of any effect remains uncertain. Prudence requires labeling these possibilities as hypotheses that scholars continue to test, not as established causes of large-scale decline.

The point of this catalogue is not to shift responsibility from commercial hunters to environmental forces or to Indigenous peoples. It is to explain why the bison were vulnerable and why different regions experienced decline at different times. A population under drought stress, with reduced calf survival and a skewed sex ratio from selective cow hunting, was less able to absorb the removal of animals by commercial hunters than a population at its ecological peak. The environmental pressures before 1870 therefore did not cause the later collapse, but they conditioned it, in the sense that they reduced the margin by which the herds could withstand continuous killing.

Historians reconstruct these conditions from a range of imperfect sources. Travelers' narratives, from the accounts of the Lewis and Clark expedition in the early 1800s through the reports of army explorers and railroad surveys in the 1850s, describe the presence or absence of herds along their routes. Artists and naturalists such as George Catlin and John James Audubon recorded bison and their hunting in the 1830s and 1840s. Trade records, especially shipments of robes from posts such as Fort Union, provide quantitative indicators of commercial harvest but not of total populations, because they exclude animals killed for subsistence and animals killed but not traded. Indigenous sources, including winter counts that record notable years, preserve evidence of scarcity, abundance, and hardship, though interpretation requires knowledge of the specific count and community. Ecological research on forage, climate, tree rings, and grazing capacity provides independent constraints on what populations the grasslands could support.

Each source has limits. Travelers saw only the places they traveled, and a report of immense herds on one day tells little about numbers elsewhere. Trade records reflect market conditions as well as animal abundance. Retrospective estimates compiled decades after the events, including some of the most famous, rest on assumptions about herd density and range that are difficult to verify. Historians therefore speak in ranges and probabilities, and they treat claims of precise totals with caution. The practical conclusion for students is that the pre-1870 Plains were neither an untouched abundance nor an already collapsing ecosystem, but a dynamic and stressed environment in which regional declines were occurring, and in which a new form of commercial hunting would soon operate on a scale without precedent.

Section 81.6

Railroad Expansion and the Fragmentation of Bison Country

Transportation infrastructure was among the most important historical causes of the destruction of the bison economy, and railroads must be treated as a major subject rather than a background detail. A hide killed on the Plains had little commercial value unless it could be moved to a tannery or market at tolerable cost. Railroads reduced that cost, brought workers, hunters, and settlers into the Plains, and connected local killing grounds to industrial consumers across the United States and internationally.

Several lines mattered in particular. The Union Pacific, built westward from the Missouri River, and the Central Pacific, built eastward from California, met at Promontory, Utah, in May 1869, completing the first transcontinental railroad. The Kansas Pacific, building west from Kansas City across Kansas, reached Denver in 1870. The Atchison, Topeka and Santa Fe reached the vicinity of Dodge City in 1872 and extended into Colorado in 1873. The Northern Pacific, chartered in 1864, reached the Missouri River at Bismarck in 1873, was halted by financial collapse after the Panic of 1873, resumed construction in the late 1870s, and crossed Dakota and Montana in the early 1880s, with its transcontinental connection completed in 1883. Each of these lines was supported by federal land grants and, in many places, protected by military posts.

Railroads affected bison and Indigenous peoples through several mechanisms. Construction crews needed food, and contractors hired hunters to supply meat. William F. Cody, later known as Buffalo Bill, claimed in his own later account to have supplied thousands of buffalo to Kansas Pacific workers in the late 1860s. This is a retrospective, self-promotional claim that cannot be verified, but it points to a real practice of provisioning crews with bison meat. Railroads also advertised excursions on which passengers shot at animals from train windows, a practice that contributed little to total mortality but symbolized the casual treatment of the herds. Far more consequential was the way railroads opened the Plains to professional hunters, gave them cheap and reliable means of shipping hides, and brought outfitters, ammunition, and supplies to the killing grounds.

Railroads also disrupted the animals' movements. Tracks, construction camps, and traffic interfered with traditional routes, and the Union Pacific corridor across the central Plains came to be seen as dividing the animals into northern and southern groups. Observers at the time and Hornaday later described the two herds in these terms. The division was real in the sense that the human activity along the corridor reduced bison use of the area, but historians caution that the picture of two neatly separated herds simplifies a more complicated pattern of local movements and regional populations.

For Indigenous nations, railroads were both a threat and an object of diplomacy. The 1867 treaties at Medicine Lodge included provisions about railroad construction, and Indigenous leaders understood that the lines would bring settlers and hunters, though they could not control the outcome. Attacks on survey parties and construction crews in the late 1860s were met by military protection of the railroads, and the lines drew army posts, settlers, and towns that further transformed the country. The railroads also accelerated the transition from a Plains economy based on Indigenous production to one based on outside capital: robes and hides that had once been exchanged through trading posts could now be bought in bulk by agents who shipped them directly east.

The railroad story must also be differentiated by region. In the United States, rails reached the Southern and central Plains by the early 1870s and the northern Plains in the early 1880s, so the timing of the commercial hide hunt tracked the timing of rail access. In Canada, the Canadian Pacific Railway did not reach the western prairies until the early 1880s, and the herds had largely disappeared from the Canadian prairies by about 1879, before the railroad's arrival. The Canadian case therefore shows that railroad access, though critical in the United States, was not the only route to collapse, and that proximity to American markets, trade routes to Montana, and local hunting pressure also mattered.

Section 81.7

Industrial Leather, Improved Firearms, and the Economics of the Hide Hunt

The commercial hide hunt was an economic system, and its logic explains why killing continued even as animals became harder to find. In the late 1860s and early 1870s, tanners in the eastern United States and Europe found ways to produce serviceable commercial leather from bison hides. That leather had uses in industrial machinery, where belts transmitted power from engines to the machines of factories, and in footwear, harness, and other goods. Because industrial expansion in the United States and Europe was generating strong demand for leather, bison hides, available in large numbers from a region newly connected by rail, became a commodity of national and international commerce. Hornaday and other contemporary observers dated the beginning of the large-scale hide hunt to about 1870 and 1871, and later historians have generally accepted the broad picture while debating details of timing.

Technology reduced the cost and risk of killing. Large-caliber breech-loading rifles, notably those made by Sharps and similar manufacturers, could kill bison at long range, and hunters developed a technique of positioning themselves at a distance from a herd and shooting animals one after another. Contemporary and later accounts describe how a herd, not recognizing the danger, could sometimes be kept in place while a skilled hunter killed many animals from a single stand, though accounts vary and some of the more dramatic claims come from memoirs written long afterward. Reloading ammunition, improvements in cartridges, and the availability of supplies by rail made the work efficient. Hunting parties typically comprised a hunter, one or more skinners, teamsters, and a camp worker, equipped with wagons and horses, and operating under contract to or with credit from merchants.

The supply chain linked many participants, and profits were unevenly distributed. Hunters were paid by the hide, and prices varied with season, grade, and distance from market, in a range commonly described as from roughly a dollar to a few dollars per hide. Skinners were generally paid by the hide or by share. Merchants and freighters who bought hides at yards in towns such as Dodge City marked up prices and consolidated shipments, railroads earned freight revenue, and wholesalers, tanners, and manufacturers captured much of the final value. Historians observe that much of the profit often accrued to merchants, outfitters, freighters, and manufacturers rather than to the hunters who did the killing, and that the system's economics depended on volume. A hunter who earned little per animal could earn a living only by killing a great many.

The economics also explain practices that appear wasteful. Hunters were paid for hides, and the cost of recovering and shipping meat from remote killing grounds exceeded its value. Carcasses were therefore commonly left to rot, though tongues and some other parts were occasionally taken for sale. Hornaday and other observers noted the waste, and later historians have emphasized that this was a rational response to the price structure and not a matter of carelessness alone. This pattern differed from many Indigenous hunting and processing practices, which directed substantial labor toward meat, fat, hides, and other usable materials. Neither Indigenous hunting nor commercial hunting was uniform or free of waste; the crucial distinction was the hide market's ability to reward killing on a scale that left the nutritional value of vast numbers of animals unused.

Bones created a second market. As the hide hunt ended in a region, carcasses left on the prairie had bleached, and bones could be gathered and shipped east for use in fertilizer and in industrial processes such as refining sugar. Bone prices were low, in a range often given as a few dollars to about ten dollars a ton, but collecting required little capital, and impoverished settlers and, in some places, Indigenous people and Métis, gathered bones for cash. Bone shipments by rail demonstrate the extent of the killing indirectly, but historians use them with caution because the conversion from tonnage to numbers of animals depends on assumptions about the weight and condition of bones.

Two distinctions are essential here. First, commercial hide hunting differed from earlier Indigenous and Métis robe and pemmican production in scale, in the organization of capital and labor, and in purpose. Indigenous commerce was embedded in communities with their own rules and labor limits; the hide hunt was an extractive industry run for distant markets. Second, the hide hunt was not a single enterprise but a set of decisions by many actors responding to incentives: manufacturers seeking leather, railroads seeking freight, merchants seeking profit, hunters seeking wages, and governments choosing whether to restrict or ignore the practice. Understanding the incentives is necessary to explaining the outcome without reducing it to the moral character of any single group.

Historical Vocabulary
Commercial Hide Hunting

Definition. Commercial hide hunting was the professional killing of bison primarily for their hides, sold to merchants and shipped to tanners and manufacturers, principally during the 1870s and early 1880s.

Historical context. It developed after industrial tanning created a market for bison leather and after railroads and improved rifles made large-scale killing profitable. It involved hunters, skinners, freighters, merchants, and shipping agents, and it was distinct from the robe trade, in which Indigenous and Métis households produced tanned winter robes, and from subsistence and pemmican hunting.

Why it matters. The term identifies the mechanism that converted a market opportunity into mass mortality, and it helps students distinguish the different kinds of commercial activity involving bison.

Potential misunderstanding. The phrase is sometimes used loosely for all buffalo hunting for sale. Indigenous and Métis robe and pemmican production also involved commerce but differed in scale, organization, and purpose, and treating them as equivalent obscures both the history and the question of responsibility.

Cause and Effect

How Industrial Demand Became Mass Slaughter

Long-term causes

Growing commercial markets for robes since the early nineteenth century, regional ecological stress, expanding settlement and transportation, and an industrializing economy hungry for leather.

Short-term causes

Completion of rail lines across the Plains, development of commercial tanning for bison hides, availability of long-range rifles, and the weak restraint of federal and territorial law on hunting.

Immediate trigger or context

The opening of the central and southern Plains to rail shipment around 1870 to 1872, which made hides profitable at a scale that Indigenous robe production had never approached.

Event or transformation

Rapid commercial killing of the southern herd between about 1871 and 1878, followed by northern herd destruction in the early 1880s.

Immediate effects

Collapse of regional bison populations, loss of food and trade resources for Plains peoples, trespass on treaty hunting lands, and shifts in military and reservation policy.

Long-term consequences

Near-extinction of the species, ecological change, expansion of ranching and farming, and greater leverage for settler states over Indigenous subsistence and movement.

Historical complexity: Industrial demand did not by itself produce mass slaughter. It required railroads, rifles, capital, an open legal environment, and animals still abundant enough to hunt profitably. Removing any one condition would have changed the outcome, which is why historians resist single-cause explanations.

Section 81.8

The Destruction of the Southern Herd, c. 1871–1878

The killing of the southern herd can be traced in space and time with unusual clarity because it was organized around railheads, shipping records, and trading posts. The process began in the early 1870s on the central Plains of Kansas, where the Kansas Pacific and, from 1872, the Santa Fe provided shipping points, and it moved south and west as the animals near the railroads were killed and hunters pursued survivors. By the late 1870s it had reached the Texas Panhandle and the Llano Estacado, the last large refuge of the southern herd.

Kansas supplied the first great killing grounds. Hunters worked from forts and railroad towns such as Hays, on the Kansas Pacific, and from Dodge City, founded in 1872 beside Fort Dodge when the Santa Fe arrived. In the seasons of 1872 to 1874, large numbers of hides moved through Dodge City and other shipping points. Contemporary observers and later compilers, notably Hornaday, used railroad records to estimate how many animals were killed, and their calculations imply killing on the order of millions of animals in a few seasons. These estimates should be handled carefully. Shipping records count hides shipped, not animals killed, and hunters left many hides unrecovered or spoiled, so analysts apply multipliers whose values are assumptions. The figures nevertheless indicate scale and, perhaps more importantly, speed, because the killing of animals near the railroad lines was effectively complete within a few years.

As animals became scarce in Kansas, hunters moved south of the Arkansas River, into lands that the Treaty of Medicine Lodge in 1867 had left open to Cheyenne, Arapaho, Kiowa, and Comanche hunters. The treaty guaranteed their right to hunt south of the river as long as buffalo ranged there in sufficient numbers. Commercial hunters entered the area in 1872 and 1873 despite the treaty. In 1874, merchants established a trading post and settlement at Adobe Walls in the Texas Panhandle to supply hunters, a move that placed commercial hunting directly in the heart of Comanche and Kiowa hunting country and contributed to the war of that year, discussed in Section 81.11. After the Red River War, hunters moved even further into the Panhandle and the Llano Estacado. Fort Griffin, in north-central Texas, served as a base for hide hunters from about 1876, and by 1877 and 1878 the principal killing took place on the high plains of the Panhandle and adjoining parts of Texas and the Indian Territory.

Dodge City's growth shows how local economies became bound to the hide trade. Merchants and outfitters, among them Charles Rath and Robert Wright, whose firm operated a major hide yard, bought hides, extended credit to hunters, supplied ammunition and equipment, and arranged shipment. Saloons, freighting firms, and hotels grew in the same trade. The town's later image as a cattle town overshadows the fact that in the mid-1870s hides, not cattle, drove much of its commerce. Cattle drives from Texas reached Dodge City in the later 1870s, and the same rail line that had carried hides soon carried cattle, an economic succession that Section 81.17 examines.

By 1878 and 1879 the southern herd was effectively gone as a commercial resource. Hornaday, writing in the 1880s, reported that the hide hunt on the Southern Plains had ended by about 1878 because there were no longer enough animals to hunt profitably, and later historians generally agree on the broad outline while noting that small numbers survived in remote locations for a time. Hunters then turned north, where the Northern Pacific and the aftermath of the Great Sioux War were opening new opportunities, and Section 81.12 follows them there. The pattern of progress, exhaustion, and relocation was a recognizable feature of an extractive industry: the economic structure that made killing profitable did not provide any incentive for conservation, because a hunter who spared animals risked losing them to a competitor.

This history shows several intersections. Professional hunting, Indigenous territory, military security, railroad access, and commercial demand converged in specific places and times. The army's presence at forts such as Dodge and Hays, the use of the railroad to ship hides, the entry of hunters into treaty hunting lands, and the response of Comanche, Kiowa, Cheyenne, and Arapaho communities all belong to one story, and the next section's primary source window invites students to examine one document that captures a moment in it.

Perspectives not represented include those of Indigenous peoples, whose territory and economy were being destroyed, and of the hunters and skinners who did the labor.

Division III

Military Policy, Federal Decisions, and the Northern Herd

Division III — Military Policy, Federal Decisions, and the Northern Herd · Section 81.9

The United States Army and the Buffalo Question

No question about the destruction of the bison has been more contested than the role of the United States Army. Popular memory often asserts that the army deliberately exterminated the herds as a means of subduing Indigenous nations, and a number of statements attributed to army officers are widely repeated in support. Historians have examined the evidence more closely and have reached conclusions that are more complex. The army did not need to organize a centralized killing campaign for its actions and attitudes to matter, and the absence of such a campaign does not mean that the army was irrelevant. This section therefore separates what is documented from what is attributed or recollected.

Senior army officers understood that the bison sustained Indigenous military and political independence. Mounted Plains nations could campaign across large distances because they could find food in the field and because their households traveled with the herds. Officers on the Plains, including those at the highest levels of command, recognized that a Plains people without bison would find it harder to remain mobile and to resist, and some expressed approval, in various contexts, of the effect that commercial hunters were having. The wording and circumstances of such statements differ among sources, and students should consult the original documents rather than rely on summaries. What can be said is that army leaders were aware of the connection and that many did not regard the decline of the herds as a problem to be solved.

The army's documented actions include several kinds of facilitation and toleration. Army posts such as Fort Hays and Fort Dodge on the Kansas railroad lines were bases from which hunters operated, and the army protected railroad construction and the settlements that grew along the lines. Historians have found instances in which officers or soldiers assisted hunters, provided escorts, or supplied equipment, though the extent and regularity of such assistance varied by place and time. The army itself hunted bison to provision its own forces, a practice that contributed modestly to mortality. Where treaties barred non-Indians from Indigenous hunting lands, as in parts of the Indian Territory and, by the Medicine Lodge treaty, the lands south of the Arkansas River, the army was often the agency responsible for keeping hunters out. Enforcement was inconsistent, and the army generally did not prevent commercial hunters from operating in areas where Indigenous hunting rights were guaranteed.

Several famous statements require source criticism. A saying attributed to Colonel Richard Irving Dodge, urging soldiers and hunters to kill every buffalo possible because each dead animal meant an Indian removed, is widely repeated, but its original documentation is uncertain, and students should treat it as an attribution until a contemporary source is identified. An anecdote in which General Philip Sheridan is said to have urged the Texas legislature in 1875 to let hide hunters continue killing until the buffalo were exterminated comes from the memoir of a hide hunter, John R. Cook, published in 1907, and historians have not located a contemporary transcript confirming the exchange. Cody's claims about supplying railroad crews, as noted, come from his own later writings. These sources are not worthless, but they are retrospective, and they cannot be used as if they were contemporary orders.

The strongest contemporary evidence on army attitudes lies in official correspondence, reports, and policy statements, and historians have used these in different ways. David Smits, in a widely cited 1994 article on the frontier army and the destruction of the buffalo, argued that the army played a significant and deliberate role in encouraging the killing. Other historians, including Andrew Isenberg in his environmental history of the bison, have given greater weight to market forces, ecological conditions, and the autonomy of hunters, while acknowledging the army's tolerance and the shared interest of commanders in the outcome. Historians have not, to date, located a general order directing the systematic killing of the herds as a military operation. They have, however, documented a pattern in which army officers recognized the strategic benefit, declined to restrain commercial hunters, and in some cases helped them.

This distinction matters for causation. If the army had directed an extermination campaign, responsibility would lie in a chain of command. If the destruction resulted primarily from market forces that the army tolerated and welcomed, responsibility is shared among commercial actors, military leaders, and political authorities who chose not to act. The evidence supports the second description better than the first, but it also shows that army policy was neither irrelevant nor neutral. By failing to protect treaty rights, by supporting the railroads and settlements that created markets, and by welcoming the strategic effects, army leaders contributed to conditions in which the herds' destruction served what they understood as military ends. Section 81.20 returns to this problem as a historiographical debate.

Section 81.10

Federal Indian Policy and the Political Uses of Buffalo Destruction

The United States Army was one part of a divided federal apparatus. Indian affairs were administered primarily by the Department of the Interior through the Office of Indian Affairs, while the War Department handled military operations and, at times, controlled supply and security on the frontier. These agencies often differed over policy, and their disagreements shaped how the buffalo question was treated. In the early 1870s, under President Ulysses S. Grant's so-called Peace Policy, federal officials sought to concentrate Indigenous peoples on reservations, to encourage farming and Christianity, and to reduce conflict, while the army continued to campaign against those who resisted. For policymakers committed to concentration and to what they called civilization, the decline of the buffalo presented an ambiguity: it undermined the independence of nations that remained outside the reservation system, but it also made those nations more dependent on federal supplies.

That ambiguity appears in the annual report of Columbus Delano, Secretary of the Interior, for 1873. In that report Delano discussed the rapid disappearance of game, including the buffalo, from the Plains, and he connected the loss of the animals to the increasing dependence of Indigenous peoples on government support. He also complained about unauthorized hunting by non-Indians on Indigenous lands and reservations. The report therefore reflects two threads of Interior policy at once: a recognition that the herds' disappearance would push Indigenous peoples toward the reservation system and agriculture, and a concern, grounded in treaty obligations and in departmental authority, that outsiders were violating Indigenous lands. Historians quote or paraphrase the report as evidence that officials understood the political consequences of the herds' destruction, but its significance lies in the combination of attitudes it displays rather than in any single sentence.

Congress took up the question as well. In 1874, H.R. 921 passed the House of Representatives and the Senate. As reported in the Congressional Record, it would have prohibited non-Indigenous hunters from killing female bison in United States territories and restricted killing males beyond quantities used or preserved for food or the market; it expressly exempted Indigenous hunters and did not ban commercial sales of all male hides. President Grant did not sign it, and because Congress adjourned before the time allowed for signature or veto expired, the bill died in what is called a pocket veto. The House's historical archive identifies the measure and records the outcome. Grant's reasons have been debated, and historians have pointed to the influence of advisers, including Delano and army officers, who regarded the buffalo's disappearance as beneficial or inevitable, though the specific documentary evidence for individual motives is limited. The failure of the bill meant that the federal government declined, at a crucial moment, to impose even a limited restraint on commercial hunting. Efforts at the territorial level, such as a failed Texas bill in 1875, and a later North-West Territories ordinance on the Canadian side, were weak or unenforced.

Federal policy also operated through treaties. The 1867 Treaty of Medicine Lodge and the 1868 Treaty of Fort Laramie reserved Indigenous hunting rights on specified lands, but the treaties made those rights depend on buffalo remaining in numbers sufficient to justify the chase. Such language reflected the assumption, widely shared by treaty negotiators on both sides, that the herds would continue. When the herds declined, the legal basis for hunting outside reservations eroded, and the treaties' own terms could be cited to justify the loss of rights. The treaty history is examined in Chapters 73 through 77, and later in Chapter 82 for Canada, but the connection to this chapter is direct: the treaties, in effect, tied the legal survival of hunting rights to the ecological survival of the animal.

The political use of hunger became explicit in the late 1870s. In 1877, after the Great Sioux War, Congress attached a condition to an appropriation for Lakota subsistence that required the Lakota to cede the Black Hills and accept other terms, a measure that Chapter 77 treats in detail. The episode showed that food supplies, which Indigenous nations had once secured through hunting, could now be used as leverage, because treaty rations and annuities were the main alternative to a declining hunt. This was the policy significance of buffalo destruction. It did not need to be planned as a single scheme to function as a lever, because the combination of declining herds, military pressure, and federal control over food created conditions in which Indigenous leaders faced constrained choices.

Section 81.11

The Southern Plains Crisis: Hunger, Warfare, and Reservation Concentration

On the Southern Plains, the destruction of the herd and the military conquest of the Comanche, Kiowa, Southern Cheyenne, and Southern Arapaho occurred almost simultaneously, and they cannot be separated. The conquest of these nations in the mid-1870s was not accomplished solely by military victory. It rested on a crisis of subsistence that hunting could no longer meet, and the crisis grew from the interaction of declining herds, commercial intrusion, treaty pressures, and military campaigns.

The Treaty of Medicine Lodge in 1867 had established reservations for the Comanche, Kiowa, and Kiowa-Apache and for the Southern Cheyenne and Arapaho in the western Indian Territory, while allowing continued hunting south of the Arkansas River. Many Indigenous people, particularly those who had not accepted the treaty fully or who followed their own leaders, continued to live and hunt largely outside the reservations. The reservation system issued rations and annuities, but the supplies were often late, inadequate, or disputed. For several years the arrangement worked as a hybrid: communities drew supplies from the agencies in some seasons and hunted in others, and the hunt remained essential. As commercial hunters killed animals in Kansas and then south of the Arkansas River in 1872 and 1873, the hunting side of the arrangement weakened.

Indigenous leaders understood what was happening. Comanche, Kiowa, and Cheyenne spokesmen complained to agents and officials about the hunters' invasion, and Indigenous parties raided hunters, traders, and settlements in efforts to defend the hunting grounds and obtain horses and supplies. In June 1874, a large party of Comanche, Kiowa, Southern Cheyenne, and Arapaho attacked the hunters' trading post at Adobe Walls in the Texas Panhandle. Quanah Parker, a leader of the Quahadi Comanche, took part, as did the Comanche prophet Isa-tai, whose religious teaching had encouraged resistance and promised spiritual protection. The attack failed against the hunters' fortified buildings and heavy rifles, but it marked the beginning of the Red River War, a series of campaigns from the summer of 1874 into 1875 in which army columns converged on the region from several directions.

The Red River War appears here as context and not as a full military narrative. Its campaigns, which included the destruction of a large Indigenous encampment in Palo Duro Canyon in late September 1874 and the capture and killing of a great many horses, targeted the shelter, supplies, and mobility of Indigenous communities, rather than seeking decisive battles. In the harsh winter that followed, bands that had left the agencies found it increasingly difficult to feed their people, because the animals they would have hunted were nearly gone and their lodges, stores, and horses had been taken or destroyed. Over the winter and spring, bands surrendered in turn at the agencies, the Southern Cheyenne at Darlington and the Kiowa and Comanche at Fort Sill, and in June 1875 Quanah Parker's Quahadi Comanche, who had held out longest, came in. Army officers selected a number of men for imprisonment at Fort Marion in Florida, an episode that later connected to the founding of off-reservation boarding schools, discussed in Chapter 87.

The relationship between hunger and surrender is best understood as an interaction. Military pressure destroyed the capacity of Indigenous communities to move, store food, and maintain horse herds, and it made it dangerous for them to gather in the large camps that communal hunting required. Commercial hunters had already reduced the animals. Together, these forces left families with few alternatives to the agencies. Historians therefore resist both the claim that the army won by force of arms alone and the claim that the herds' disappearance made resistance impossible. Both conditions were needed, and the outcome reflected their combination, as well as Indigenous leaders' decisions about whether to continue resisting or to protect their people by surrendering.

After 1875, the possibility of living outside the reservation system largely disappeared. A few bands made hunting trips in later years, and the last southern hunts took place in the later 1870s, but they found little. Rations became the main source of food, and rations were often insufficient. The distress was acute enough to produce desperate responses: in 1878 a group of Northern Cheyenne, who had been moved to the Southern Plains reservation after the Great Sioux War, left the agency and attempted to return north, driven in part by hunger, illness, and homesickness, an episode that belongs to the wider history of the period. Over the following decade, Comanche, Kiowa, Cheyenne, and Arapaho communities adapted by turning to other resources, including cattle leasing, which brought income from the grazing of Texas cattle on reservation land, freighting, small-scale farming, and wage work. These adaptations show continuity of agency, but they took place within a system of federal supervision whose terms Indigenous nations had little power to set.

Section 81.12

The Destruction of the Northern Herd, c. 1876–1883

The northern herd survived the southern herd by several years, but its destruction was swifter once it began. The northern Plains of Montana, Dakota Territory, Wyoming, and the adjoining Canadian prairies remained a region of large bison populations in the mid-1870s, in part because rail access arrived later. The Great Sioux War of 1876 and 1877 and the defeat and confinement of the Lakota and Northern Cheyenne, examined in Chapter 77, removed the principal Indigenous military obstacle to commercial hunting in the Yellowstone country and adjoining regions, and the Northern Pacific Railroad's resumption of construction supplied the means of shipment.

Hide hunters who had worked on the Southern Plains moved north after the southern herd's destruction, and they were joined by new entrants. The northern commercial hunt differed from the southern in some respects. The market was more focused on robes, because the thick winter coats of northern animals made winter-killed robes valuable, and hunters worked especially in the colder months. Shipping centers developed along the Northern Pacific and the Missouri and Yellowstone rivers, including Glendive and Miles City in Montana, the latter near Fort Keogh, a military post established after the war. Steamboats on the Missouri and Yellowstone also moved robes and hides, and the combination of river and rail made the region accessible even before the railroad was complete.

Northern Pacific records, studied by the National Park Service in historical research on the Theodore Roosevelt area, document how rapidly the commercial hunt grew and collapsed. Shipments rose after the railroad reached Glendive and Miles City in 1881, peaked in 1882, fell steeply in 1883, and dwindled to a negligible amount by 1884. This sequence is striking: within about three years, the commercial resource that had justified the hunt was exhausted. Such records indicate that the herd was not only reduced but largely eliminated as a commercial resource within a very short time, though historians caution that shipping data capture only part of the killing, and that animals taken for local use, killed and wasted, or sold by other means are not counted.

Indigenous hunters took part in the northern hunt, which complicates any simple division between Indigenous and commercial hunting. After 1877, Lakota, Cheyenne, Crow, Assiniboine, Gros Ventre, and others hunted from the reservations and sold robes to traders, in part because they needed cash and goods in addition to rations, and in part because hunting remained central to their culture and economy. Reservation agents sometimes permitted hunts, sometimes restricted them. In the autumn of 1883, accounts compiled by Hornaday and later historians describe a final large hunt near the Standing Rock Agency in which Lakota hunters, with Sitting Bull among those present, and commercial hunters took a remaining northern herd. Numbers vary by account, and the episode has been remembered in different ways: as a final great hunt, as a tragedy of waste, and as evidence that even the last animals were taken partly by the communities that depended on them. These facts do not shift responsibility for the destruction away from the commercial system and policy environment that had created the conditions, but they show how scarcity could draw Indigenous hunters into the last stage of the killing.

By 1884 hide hunting in the northern United States had effectively ended for want of animals. Hornaday, who went to Montana in 1886 to collect specimens for the Smithsonian, found that locating even a small number of bison for his purposes was difficult, and his search became part of the evidence on which he based his later conclusion that the species stood on the verge of extinction. A few animals survived in remote areas of Montana, in Yellowstone National Park, in captive herds, and in Canada, as Section 81.19 shows. The collapse of the northern herd followed the southern by about five years, and it affected a different set of peoples in a different political situation, since many Lakota, Cheyenne, and Crow were already on reservations when the final killing occurred, and the Blackfeet of Montana and the peoples of the Canadian prairies faced the crisis in the early 1880s with few alternatives.

Division IV

Hunger, Dispossession, and Indigenous Responses

Division IV — Hunger, Dispossession, and Indigenous Responses · Section 81.13

The Canadian Plains and the Collapse of the Northern Buffalo Economy

The international boundary did not stop the buffalo, and it did not stop the crisis. The bison economy of the northern Plains extended across the forty-ninth parallel, and the peoples who depended on it, among them the Plains Cree, the Assiniboine or Nakoda, and the Blackfoot Confederacy, which comprised the Siksiká, Kainai, and Piikani, together with their allies the Tsuut'ina, lived on both sides of the line. The Piikani are also known as the Peigan, and the Blackfeet Nation of Montana is closely related to the Confederacy. The collapse of the Canadian herds was a continental event, but it was shaped by Canadian territorial expansion, administrative decisions, and the specific geography of the prairies.

The early 1870s were a period of transition. In 1870 the Hudson's Bay Company's claims to Rupert's Land and the North-Western Territory were transferred to Canada, opening the question of how the Canadian government would manage the prairies. Traders from Montana, based at Fort Benton on the upper Missouri, pushed north into present-day southern Alberta, where posts such as Fort Whoop-Up traded whiskey and goods to Blackfoot and other peoples for robes. On June 1, 1873, American wolf hunters and traders attacked and killed Nakoda (Assiniboine) people in the Cypress Hills, an event known as the Cypress Hills massacre. The incident intensified Canadian government concern about lawlessness on the frontier and contributed to the creation of the North-West Mounted Police in 1873. The police marched west in 1874 and established posts including Fort Macleod and, later, Fort Walsh in the Cypress Hills.

Blackfoot leaders confronted the combination of commercial hunting, the whiskey trade, epidemic disease, and the declining herds. The Canadian Museum of History identifies commercial destruction of the buffalo as a major pressure on the Blackfoot Confederacy in the years before Treaty 7 was concluded at Blackfoot Crossing in September 1877. Blackfoot leaders, among them Isapo-muxika, known as Crowfoot of the Siksiká, valued the police presence as a check on the whiskey trade and sought assurances about their land and future, and they negotiated the treaty at a time when the animals on which their economy depended were growing scarce. The treaties' terms, obligations, and Indigenous expectations are the subject of Chapter 82, and here the point is only that the negotiation took place against a backdrop of ecological decline that influenced Indigenous and Canadian assessments of the future.

Cree and Assiniboine nations faced similar conditions. Treaty 4 in 1874 and Treaty 6 in 1876 brought many Plains Cree and Assiniboine into treaty relations with the Crown. Treaty 6 contained provisions for relief in time of famine and for medical assistance, terms that reflected the concern of Cree leaders, among them Mistawasis and Ahtahkakoop, about the loss of the buffalo and the threat of starvation. Other leaders, including Mistahimaskwa, known as Big Bear, and Payipwat, known as Piapot, hesitated to accept the treaty terms or sought alternative arrangements, in part out of concern over land and autonomy. These strategic differences are developed in Chapter 82 and in Section 81.15.

By about 1879 the bison had largely disappeared from the Canadian prairies. Cree, Assiniboine, Blackfoot, Métis, and other hunters followed the remaining animals across the border into Montana, where they met American hunters, military patrols, and other Indigenous groups, among them Lakota who had left the United States after the Great Sioux War. Sitting Bull and his followers lived in Canada from 1877 to 1881, near Wood Mountain and elsewhere, and their presence added to the pressure on a dwindling resource and to Canadian officials' anxiety about the border. The Canadian government, which had agreed to treaty obligations but had made little provision for the end of the hunt, faced a population in need of food just as the Dominion's finances and administrative capacity were limited.

The Canadian herd's disappearance preceded the Canadian Pacific Railway, which reached the western prairies in the early 1880s. This chronology matters. It shows that railroad-driven commercial hide hunting, though decisive in the United States, was not the only cause of collapse in Canada. Local hunting by Indigenous, Métis, and non-Indigenous hunters, trade with American markets through Fort Benton, a climate and ecology under stress, and the movement of animals across the border all contributed, and the disappearance of animals in the United States left fewer to move north. The Canadian case demonstrates that the buffalo collapse was a continental transformation in which events on one side of the border affected the other, and in which Indigenous nations on both sides faced a common loss under different political regimes.

Section 81.14

The Métis Buffalo Hunt, Pemmican Trade, and Economic Disruption

For the Métis of the northern Plains, the buffalo was the foundation of an economy that supported both household life and a distinctive political identity. The Métis communities of the Red River, centered on the settlements near present-day Winnipeg, and later of Saint-Laurent and other settlements along the Saskatchewan River, organized large, disciplined hunts that supplied meat, hides, and especially pemmican for commercial markets. Their economic autonomy, discussed in Chapter 52, rested to a considerable degree on this activity, and the decline of the herds therefore struck at the base of Métis independence.

The organization of the hunt is well documented. Métis hunters traveled in large groups with families, using ox-drawn Red River carts, wooden vehicles whose creaking wheels were a recognizable sound on the prairie. The carts carried supplies out and meat and hides back. Alexander Ross, a Red River Settlement official and chronicler, described the hunt of 1840 as involving well over a thousand carts, and his account, though written by an outsider and subject to the usual cautions, is one of the best descriptions of the organized hunt. The hunt was governed by rules set by an elected leadership, with a captain, designated officers, and scouts, and by regulations that required discipline, forbade individuals to break from the group before the signal, and, in many accounts, prohibited hunting on Sundays. Gabriel Dumont, a prominent hunter and leader at Saint-Laurent, later played a role in formulating local hunting regulations, and the Saint-Laurent community adopted its own set of laws in 1873, a development that anticipated later political organization and is examined in Chapter 83.

The Métis also fought to protect the hunt. Their hunting range overlapped with that of Dakota and Lakota nations, and in July 1851 a Métis hunting party defended itself against a large Dakota force at the Battle of the Grand Coteau, an encounter that Métis tradition remembers as demonstrating the effectiveness of their disciplined camp and defensive tactics. Such conflicts were part of the broader competition over hunting grounds that characterized the Plains, and they remind students that the Métis participated in the intertribal politics of the region.

Pemmican connected the hunt to the commercial system. The Hudson's Bay Company and other fur-trade enterprises relied on pemmican to provision their transport brigades and posts, and Métis producers supplied a large share of it. The Dictionary of Canadian Biography and other Canadian sources document the importance of hunting and pemmican to Métis livelihoods before the decline of the 1870s. Métis involvement in the trade was also political: their insistence on the right to trade freely, against the Company's monopoly, was a major issue in Red River in the 1840s, culminating in the 1849 trial of Guillaume Sayer, whose acquittal effectively ended enforcement of the monopoly. The Métis were thus both a commercial and a political force, and their economic role strengthened their bargaining position.

As bison became scarcer, Métis hunters were forced to travel farther. Hunts from the Red River moved west and south, into present-day North Dakota and Montana, and then toward the Cypress Hills, and by the 1870s many Métis had left the Red River region for the Saskatchewan valley, where they established communities such as Saint-Laurent and Batoche. Others settled in Montana. The long journeys exposed hunters to competition with Americans and Indigenous groups, and increased costs and risks. Meanwhile, the growth of settlement in Manitoba after 1870 and the changes in transportation, including steamboat service on the Red River and, in the late 1870s, railway connections to Winnipeg, reduced the market for cart freighting, another source of Métis income.

The contraction of the herds disrupted several parts of the Métis economy at once. It reduced the supply of meat and pemmican, ended the large organized hunts, and weakened trade relationships that had depended on Métis provisioning. It also forced many households to adapt, through farming, freighting, wage labor, and seasonal work, under conditions in which land title and legal recognition remained contested. These pressures form the economic background to the political events of 1869 to 1870 and 1885, which Chapter 83 narrates. Here, the Métis case clarifies two points: that the buffalo economy supported distinct political communities as well as households, and that its collapse produced political as well as economic consequences. It also illustrates that Indigenous and Métis participation in commercial hunting was organized, regulated, and tied to community life, in contrast to the industrial hide hunt.

Section 81.15

Indigenous Strategies of Survival and Adaptation

The loss of the herds did not leave Plains nations without options, but it narrowed them sharply, and the choices people made reflect differences of circumstance, leadership, and judgment. Treating these choices as active strategies, rather than as evidence of helplessness, is necessary for a historically accurate account. At the same time, recognizing agency does not imply that the choices were free. They were made under conditions of hunger, military pressure, legal restriction, and administrative control that Indigenous people had not created and could not remove.

One strategy was to pursue the remaining herds. Hunters followed bison across the international boundary, moved seasonally between reservations or reserves and the diminishing hunting grounds, and organized large hunts when opportunity arose. The 1883 hunt at Standing Rock, the movements of Cree, Assiniboine, and Blackfoot hunters into Montana around 1879, and the final southern hunts all reflect this approach. It preserved cultural practice and provided food, but it exposed hunters to conflict, to the hostility of settlers and officials, and, increasingly, to disappointment as animals could not be found.

A second strategy was negotiation. Indigenous leaders used treaties, councils, and appeals to officials to secure food, land, and recognition. Cree leaders who agreed to Treaty 6 sought relief clauses, assistance with agriculture, and medical support, and the treaty's famine provision reflected their foresight. Blackfoot leaders negotiating Treaty 7 sought protection and assurances. Others judged that negotiation would not secure their goals and held out. Mistahimaskwa, Big Bear, sought a large contiguous reserve that would allow Cree bands to remain together and retain political weight, and he delayed adhesion to the treaty until 1882, when hunger made continued refusal impracticable. Such decisions show the range of judgment among leaders confronting the same crisis and the costs attached to each course.

A third strategy was relocation and surrender under negotiated terms. Sitting Bull's decision to bring his people back from Canada in July 1881, after the herds in the north had failed and Canadian officials declined to provide support, and the surrender of Quanah Parker's Quahadi Comanche in 1875 were decisions to protect families from starvation and military attack at the cost of independence. Quanah Parker went on to become a prominent leader on the Kiowa, Comanche, and Apache reservation, where he negotiated with cattlemen over grazing leases, worked with agents and Indigenous communities, and helped his people adapt in ways that preserved some economic and political influence. His career illustrates how leaders could use the new circumstances for community benefit, though it also reflects the constraints of reservation life.

A fourth strategy was economic diversification. Households and communities turned to farming, ranching, freighting, wage work, craft sales, scouting and police service, and the collection and sale of bones. The bone trade offers a revealing example of improvisation: the Cree term for the place that became Regina, in Saskatchewan, referred to a pile of bones, and the settlement was known as Pile of Bones in its early years, because bone gathering was a significant local activity. Bone gathering brought small sums to impoverished people, Indigenous and non-Indigenous alike. Some Plains Cree and Blackfoot communities tried farming under treaty provisions, struggling with shortages of tools, oxen, and seed and with unreliable government assistance, and Southern Plains communities leased grazing land to cattle owners. These adaptations rarely replaced the lost income and security of the hunt, but they show people working within new conditions to sustain their families, and Chapter 91 develops the economic history of Indigenous communities after conquest.

Indigenous accounts of the period must be used with care. Plenty Coups, a Crow leader whose recollections were recorded by Frank Linderman and published in 1930, said, as translated through an interpreter and set down by Linderman, that when the buffalo went away the hearts of his people fell to the ground. The statement, mediated by interpretation and by the recorder's editing, is nonetheless a memorable testimony to the emotional and spiritual dimensions of the loss, and it is typical of how many Indigenous narrators framed the end of the herds as a break in the order of their world. Other sources include winter counts, in which communities recorded notable years, and ledger drawings, testimony given to agents and officials, and later oral histories preserved by descendants. Each carries its own provenance and limitations, and students should note who recorded the account, when, and under what circumstances.

The strategies varied among nations, families, and individuals. Leaders disagreed with each other, communities were divided about whether to resist, negotiate, or adapt, and families sometimes made different decisions from those of their leaders. Women, who bore much of the burden of feeding households, often adapted most directly, and their roles are examined in the following section. Indigenous people were not simply victims of the end of the herds, and they were not free agents either. They were historical actors making decisions under duress, and the diversity of their decisions is part of the history this chapter seeks to recover.

Section 81.16

Starvation, Rations, and the Reorganization of Plains Life

The ecological collapse became a social catastrophe when it reached households. This section addresses the human consequences directly, because they were the most immediate and enduring effects of the destruction of the herds. They included hunger and starvation, increased vulnerability to disease, family hardship, disruption of established labor and exchange systems, dependence on government supplies, and political control exercised through the distribution of food.

Hunger was real and, in places, deadly. The best-known case in the United States is the Starvation Winter of 1883 to 1884 among the Blackfeet of Montana, who had lost access to the herds, found ration supplies short, and endured a severe winter. Mortality estimates vary, with a widely cited figure of several hundred deaths among a population of a few thousand, and students should treat the number as an estimate. In Canada, Cree, Assiniboine, and Blackfoot communities suffered severe hunger from about 1879 through the mid-1880s, and Canadian historical scholarship documents cases of starvation and of a rise in disease, notably tuberculosis, among malnourished populations. Hunger weakened resistance to disease, shortened lives, and affected the survival of infants and the elderly, so mortality from starvation was inseparable from mortality from illness.

The institutional framework differed between the two countries. In the United States, annuities and rations were provided under treaties and appropriations, administered by agents of the Office of Indian Affairs at reservation agencies, with the army sometimes involved in security and supply. Rations commonly included flour, bacon, sugar, coffee, and beef, often issued on the hoof, and issues occurred on specified days. At some agencies, the beef issue became an occasion for a staged hunt in which men chased and shot cattle released from corrals, a practice that preserved elements of the hunt in altered form. Appropriations were often inadequate or delayed, shipments spoiled or arrived late, and agents' decisions determined who received what. By the later 1870s federal officials increasingly expected able-bodied men to work in return for rations, tying food to labor in line with civilization policy.

In Canada, treaty annuities were small, and relief in time of need was provided by the Department of the Interior's Indian Department, with farm instructors and agents on the reserves. Canadian officials, including Edgar Dewdney, who became Indian Commissioner in 1879, and later Hayter Reed, applied policies that connected assistance to agricultural effort and, in some instances, to movement. Historians such as James Daschuk have argued that the Canadian government deliberately used the withholding or conditioning of food to compel Cree and others to move to designated reserves and to submit to administration, citing the events of 1882, when relief was tied to relocation in the Cypress Hills. The North-West Mounted Police headquarters moved to Regina in 1882; Fort Walsh itself was abandoned in 1883. Other scholars accept the documentation of manipulation but debate the degree of intent, emphasizing financial constraints, administrative incompetence, and competing priorities. The disagreement concerns the extent and motive of the policy and not whether food was used as an instrument of control, which the documents establish in specific instances. The U.S. and Canadian systems should not be assumed identical: they differed in legal structure, bureaucratic organization, and policy emphasis, and Chapter 84 compares them.

The reorganization of labor was among the deepest changes. Women whose work had centered on processing meat and hides found that work disappearing, and men whose status had depended on hunting prowess faced new roles. Households shifted to farming or gardening, to wage work and freighting, and to making goods for sale, and they took on the work of obtaining, storing, and preparing rations. Ration systems also reshaped authority and household relationships. Distribution to heads of households, ration tickets, and census counts affected how families were defined and who received supplies, and the agent's ability to reward or withhold gave him leverage over leaders and communities. Unequal access to supplies, favoritism toward some leaders or bands, and punishment by reduction of rations became tools of administration.

The consequences extended to social and cultural life. Ceremonies that had required buffalo had to adapt: accounts of the Kiowa Sun Dance, held for the last time in the later 1880s, describe the difficulty of finding buffalo for the ceremony, and across the Plains officials in this period increasingly restricted Sun Dances and other ceremonies (Chapters 87 and 89 treat the wider policy). Children, born into households living on rations, grew up in a world with few or no buffalo hunts, and later boarding and residential schools, discussed in Chapters 87 and 88, further altered transmission of knowledge. At the same time, communities maintained food traditions, taught younger generations the uses of bison materials where they could, and preserved songs and stories. The reorganization of life was thus both an imposition and a field of continued Indigenous action.

Historical Vocabulary
Ration Dependence

Definition. Ration dependence describes the condition in which a community relied substantially on government-issued food supplies for survival, typically following the loss of the economic resources, such as the buffalo hunt, that previously supported it.

Historical context. In the United States and Canada, treaties promised annuities and, in Canada, relief, and governments issued flour, meat, and other staples. As herds collapsed, the supplies became the principal food source for many communities. Officials administered rations through agents, tickets, and schedules, and used them to encourage farming, discourage movement, and secure compliance.

Why it matters. The term helps explain how the loss of the herds translated into political leverage, because control over food gave administrators power over movement, labor, and leadership. It also highlights that dependence was produced by conditions imposed on Indigenous nations rather than being a cultural trait.

Potential misunderstanding. The phrase can wrongly suggest that Indigenous people chose or preferred dependence, or that rations were a gift. Rations were often owed under treaties, were frequently inadequate, and replaced an economy that had been destroyed. Dependence was not total, and communities continued to hunt, farm, and work wherever they could.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Plains Household Facing the Disappearance of the Herds

Note: this feature is an evidence-based reconstruction of common conditions, not a narrative about a named or invented family. It draws on the kinds of sources discussed in this chapter, including agency records, treaty documents, ethnographic and oral-history testimony, and winter counts.

Food. Before the collapse, a household's food supply combined fresh meat during hunts, dried meat and pemmican stored for the lean months, and, among village peoples, maize and other crops. As hunting declined, a household increasingly relied on weekly or periodic ration issues, on garden produce where land and tools permitted, and on whatever game remained. Agency records show that rations were frequently smaller than needed and arrived irregularly, so families managed scarcity by sharing among kin and by reducing consumption.

Work. Women's labor had centered on butchering, drying, rendering, and tanning. With few animals, much of this work disappeared, and women shifted to gardening, to preparing and storing rations, to making clothing and household goods from cloth and purchased materials, and, where markets existed, to producing beadwork and other goods for sale. Men's work shifted from hunting to herding, freighting, wage labor, police or scout service, and farming, often in occupations that did not carry the same status.

Shelter and goods. Hide tipi covers, which required many hides, could not be replaced as easily, and families turned to canvas, which the government sometimes issued, and eventually to log or frame houses. Containers, clothing, and tools were increasingly replaced by purchased or issued goods. Objects in museum collections suggest the persistence of older skills even as materials changed.

Health and children. Hunger and crowding raised vulnerability to tuberculosis and other diseases, and infants and the elderly were at particular risk. Children grew up in a changing world, and many were later sent to boarding or residential schools, where transmission of language and practical knowledge was interrupted, though families and communities also worked to preserve them.

Ceremony and memory. Communities adapted ceremonies, sometimes using substitutes for buffalo and sometimes continuing in private after suppression. Winter counts and oral accounts record the disappearance of the herds as a turning point in community memory.

The Transformation of the Grasslands After the Buffalo

The removal of bison from the Plains was an ecological event as well as an economic and political one, and it changed the grassland in ways that scientists are still working to understand. The evidence must be handled carefully. Ecological research can establish that bison had specific effects on vegetation, soils, and other species, and that their removal and replacement by livestock and crops altered those relationships. It cannot support the assumption that every later environmental condition on the Plains, from soil erosion to changes in plant communities, resulted from the loss of bison alone.

Bison grazing created patchiness. Herds moved across the land, concentrating on some areas and leaving others, grazing and trampling vegetation, and depositing dung and urine that redistributed nutrients. Wallowing created shallow depressions that altered local soil and moisture conditions and supported distinct plant and animal communities. Researchers studying remnant bison herds and comparing them with cattle-grazed rangeland have found differences in the structure and diversity of vegetation, though results vary with site, stocking rate, and management, and they do not support simple generalizations about bison being always beneficial and cattle always harmful. What can be said is that the loss of free-ranging herds ended a mobile, landscape-scale grazing regime and replaced it with fixed-site grazing under fences, with different consequences for the grassland.

The death of the herds also altered relationships among scavengers and predators. Immense numbers of carcasses, left unused by hide hunters, provided food for wolves, ravens, magpies, and other scavengers for a time. Wolf hunters, called wolfers, poisoned carcasses with strychnine to kill wolves for their pelts and to protect livestock, and the poison killed many other animals as well. The decline of wolves, which accelerated as the cattle industry expanded, further changed predator-prey dynamics. Later, the bleached bones were collected and shipped as fertilizer and industrial material, removing nutrients that would otherwise have returned to the soil, though the ecological significance of this removal is not well quantified.

Economic and environmental transformation reinforced one another. As bison disappeared, cattle replaced them. Texas cattle drives to Kansas railheads, which had begun in the late 1860s, grew in the 1870s, and in the early 1880s a cattle boom extended ranching across Wyoming, Montana, and the Dakotas on the open range, the unfenced public domain on which cattle grazed with little regulation. The severe winter of 1886 to 1887 caused heavy losses and exposed the risks of the open-range system. The introduction of barbed wire, patented in 1874, allowed ranchers and farmers to enclose land, and the Homestead Act of 1862, together with railroad land grants and later legislation, encouraged agricultural settlement. Farming expanded across the eastern and central Plains, and the plow converted large areas of grassland to cropland, including areas where rainfall was marginal for dryland agriculture.

These changes interacted with Indigenous dispossession. The same grasslands that had supported bison and Indigenous economies were claimed by ranchers, farmers, and railroads, and Indigenous reservations and reserves were confined to a small fraction of the former range. The replacement of bison by cattle thus both reflected and facilitated the transfer of land, because cattle ranching and farming required exclusive control over land and water, whereas the bison economy had been based on mobility and shared use. Chapters 84 and 86 trace how reservation and allotment policy carried this transformation further.

Historians and ecologists are careful about attribution. The environmental history of the Plains after the 1880s includes drought, overgrazing, plowing, and other pressures, and later events such as the soil erosion of the 1930s were driven by agricultural practice and climate rather than directly by the bison's removal. The removal of the bison is best understood as one major change among several that transformed the grassland, and as a change that was bound up with the economic and political changes that this chapter describes. The loss of the herds marked the end of a grazing system that had shaped the Plains for thousands of years, and the beginning of a landscape reorganized around private property, fencing, livestock, and crops.

Section 81.18

Measuring the Catastrophe: Population Estimates and Historical Evidence

How many bison were there, and how many were killed? These questions seem simple but are difficult to answer, and the difficulty is instructive. Estimates of the original population vary widely, and estimates of how many animals survived at the low point depend on how and when they were counted. Historians therefore speak of ranges and of the reliability of methods, and they treat claims of precision with suspicion.

Estimates of the pre-commercial population rest on several kinds of reasoning. Some researchers estimate the carrying capacity of the Plains by calculating how much forage the grasslands produced and how much a bison consumed, adjusting for competition from other grazers and for drought. Others extrapolate from travelers' descriptions of herds, a method open to the problem of generalizing from single observations. Others use trade records, the range of the species, and the ecological effects of predators and Indigenous hunting. Each method has assumptions that cannot be tested directly. The famous figures of 30 million or 60 million, repeated in popular writing, belong to the older tradition of extrapolation, and later work has argued for lower numbers and for large fluctuations over time. This textbook does not repeat any single figure as an established count, and students should be cautious when they encounter one.

Estimates of the number killed in the commercial hunt face a parallel problem. Railroad shipping records and hide-yard accounts count hides shipped, and conversion to animals killed requires assumptions about how many animals were killed for each hide that reached market. Hunters and skinners left many hides unrecovered, damaged, or spoiled, and Indigenous and subsistence hunters killed animals that never entered commercial records. Hornaday and others used multipliers to adjust for these losses, but the multipliers are estimates. Counts near the low point of the population were also incomplete, because they depended on the knowledge of correspondents, the accessibility of remote areas, and the difficulty of verifying the existence of small and scattered herds.

The most important contemporary survey is William T. Hornaday's The Extermination of the American Bison, published in 1889 in the report of the United States National Museum, which the Smithsonian Libraries has digitized. Hornaday, born in 1854, was a taxidermist and zoologist who became chief taxidermist at the Smithsonian's National Museum. In 1886 he led an expedition to Montana to collect bison specimens, and the expedition, which struggled to find animals, convinced him that the species was in danger. He gathered information from hunters, army officers, railroad officials, naturalists, and correspondents, compiled shipping and hide-trade data, and produced a history of the bison's range and destruction and a count of survivors. His estimate for January 1, 1889 was 1,091 bison: 635 considered wild and unprotected (including an estimated 550 in the Athabasca region of Canada), 200 under protection at Yellowstone, and 256 in captivity. The Athabasca estimate concerned a remote population associated with wood bison; the components of his tally are not a field census and must not be treated as a precise count of surviving plains bison.

Hornaday must be read as both observer and participant. He documented the destruction vividly, and his work helped to arouse public concern and later conservation efforts. But he was also part of the collecting culture of the time, in which museums sought specimens, and he killed bison in Montana for the Smithsonian's display, even as he lamented their disappearance. His account concentrates on the actions of white hunters and on the zoological and market dimensions, and it gives limited attention to the Indigenous societies whose lives were being destroyed, treating them chiefly as hunters whose practices he judged against his conservation concerns. His sources, mostly men with their own interests and memories, also affect the book's reliability. Hornaday's work is therefore an essential source, but it must be corroborated with other records and read in light of its purposes.

Section 81.19

The Surviving Herds and the Beginnings of Buffalo Preservation

The bison were driven close to extinction but not over the edge, and the distinction matters both historically and ethically. A few hundred to about a thousand animals survived in scattered locations at the lowest point, and from those survivors the species recovered over the following century. How they survived, and what kind of preservation was practiced, shows both the beginnings of conservation and its limits.

Surviving wild animals persisted in a few places. A small remnant herd lived in Yellowstone National Park, created in 1872, where poaching continued despite park regulations. In 1894, the arrest of a poacher in the park, Edgar Howell, helped to draw public attention, and Congress passed legislation that year providing protection for the park's wildlife, with enforcement provisions. The park herd, perhaps numbering only a couple of dozen at its lowest, was later supplemented by animals from private herds. Wood bison survived in the remote boreal region of northern Canada, and the North-West Territories had passed an ordinance in 1877 to restrict buffalo killing, but it was poorly enforced and did little to stop the decline of plains bison.

Private and captive herds were equally important. In the 1870s and 1880s, individuals on both sides of the border captured calves and kept them. One widely repeated account credits Samuel Walking Coyote, a Pend d'Oreille (Kalispel) man, with bringing bison calves to the Flathead Reservation in the 1870s; Indigenous oral-history evidence preserved in more recent research also credits Atatitsa (Peregrine Falcon Robe) and his son Latatitsa (Little Peregrine Falcon Robe) with initiating or carrying out the rescue. The precise origin of the calves is contested, but the animals became the nucleus of the Pablo–Allard herd; later, Michel Pablo sold much of that herd to the Canadian government as reservation lands and grazing arrangements changed. Charles Goodnight on the Texas Panhandle, Charles Jones in Kansas, and others also maintained herds, and some animals were kept in zoos. The Comanche leader Quanah Parker is also associated with efforts to maintain buffalo on the reservation in Oklahoma. These individuals acted from varied motives, including curiosity, entrepreneurial interest, and concern for the species, and their efforts, taken together, preserved the genetic base from which later herds grew.

Organized conservation emerged in the early twentieth century. The American Bison Society, founded in 1905 with Hornaday and others among its leaders, promoted the restoration of bison on protected ranges, and animals from the New York Zoological Park were shipped to the Wichita Mountains in Oklahoma in 1907. The National Bison Range in Montana was established in 1908 within the Flathead Indian Reservation, on land taken for conservation without the Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes' consent; it did not cease to be part of their homeland. In 2021 the federal government transferred the range lands into trust for the Tribes, with tribal stewardship. That longer history of restoration and Indigenous land belongs principally to Chapter 135, but its origins reveal a central limitation of early conservation. In Canada, the government purchased the Pablo herd and established protected areas, including Buffalo National Park near Wainwright, Alberta, and Elk Island.

Early conservation had important limits. It sought to preserve animals as a species, as a spectacle, or as a part of national heritage, often by confining them in parks, refuges, and fenced ranges, and it was led largely by non-Indigenous sportsmen, scientists, and officials. It generally did not address the dispossession of Indigenous nations, the loss of access to hunting and ceremonial relationships, or the destruction of the economies that had depended on the herds. Preserving the animal was not the same as restoring Indigenous relationships to the animal and the land, and in some cases conservation measures restricted Indigenous access. Indigenous communities, meanwhile, continued to maintain knowledge, stories, ceremonies, and, where possible, relationships with bison, and some communities kept small herds or acquired animals in later decades. The recovery of those relationships in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries is a distinct history, and this chapter introduces it only as a contrast.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Indigenous Relationships to the Buffalo, c. 1800–1900

What Changed

  • The herds declined from very large populations to near-extinction, ending the hunt as the basis of Plains economies
  • Hunting-based economies shifted toward rations, farming, ranching, wage work, and trade in manufactured goods
  • Mobility, once the basis of political independence, was constrained by reservations, reserves, and administrative control
  • Gendered labor patterns, especially women's hide-processing work, were disrupted and reconfigured
  • Control of land passed increasingly to settlers, railroads, and cattle and farming interests

What Stayed the Same

  • Indigenous nations continued to exist as political communities and to assert treaty rights and land claims
  • Knowledge of bison, ceremonies, stories, and foodways was transmitted, often adapted to new conditions
  • Kinship networks and community institutions continued to support survival
  • Indigenous people continued to seek access to bison and to use bison-related materials where possible

When and how: Change was uneven. The southern collapse came in the 1870s, the northern in the early 1880s, and consequences unfolded over decades, with the sharpest crises in the years immediately after the herds' disappearance. Who experienced it most directly: Mobile hunting nations and women whose labor centered on the hunt experienced the most direct disruption, while village nations with agricultural bases experienced different pressures. Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity avoids the two errors identified in Chapter 1: treating the end of the herds as the end of Indigenous nations, or treating Indigenous societies as unchanged by the loss.

Section 81.20

The Destruction of the Buffalo Economy and the Conquest of the Plains

The destruction of the buffalo economy was the product of several mechanisms operating together. Market incentives drove commercial hunters, merchants, railroads, and manufacturers. Railroads supplied access, shipment, and settlement. Industrial demand for leather gave hides value. Improved rifles lowered the cost of killing. Military and government policies tolerated, and sometimes facilitated, the killing, and failed to enforce treaty rights or protect the herds. Settler expansion, ranching, and farming changed the land. Environmental pressures, including drought, forage competition, and earlier overhunting of cows, reduced the herds' capacity to withstand the pressure. No single mechanism explains the outcome, and the relative weight of each varied by region and period.

Comparing regions clarifies the pattern. On the Southern Plains, the hide hunt began early, from about 1871, driven by rail access at Dodge City and other railheads, and destroyed the herd within about seven years. It overlapped with the Red River War and with treaty-protected hunting lands, and it led to the rapid reservation concentration of the Comanche, Kiowa, Southern Cheyenne, and Southern Arapaho. On the Northern Plains of the United States, the herd persisted longer, was destroyed rapidly after the Northern Pacific arrived and after the Great Sioux War, and left the Lakota, Northern Cheyenne, Crow, Blackfeet, and others on reservations dependent on rations. On the Canadian prairies, the herd vanished by about 1879, before the Canadian Pacific Railway, as a result of local hunting, American markets, and the pressures of the whole northern system, and its disappearance occurred as Treaties 4, 6, and 7 were being implemented, leaving First Nations and Métis to confront hunger under a different administrative system.

The loss of the herds weakened the ability of many Plains communities to sustain political independence. Mobility, food security, trade, and the capacity to field and feed large gatherings had rested on the bison. Without them, nations had to rely on governments for food, could not move freely across their territories, and had fewer resources with which to bargain. Settler states gained leverage over Indigenous subsistence, movement, and political choices, and could use the distribution of food as an instrument of administration. This was the political significance of the ecological and economic collapse, and it helps explain why treaties, reserves, and reservations were imposed on terms that Indigenous nations had resisted when the herds were abundant.

That material crisis must nonetheless be distinguished from the end of Indigenous nations and sovereignty. Indigenous nations survived the loss of the herds. They continued to assert treaty rights, to organize politically, to maintain languages and ceremonies, and to adapt economically. Sovereignty, as Chapter 1 explained, is a political and legal concept, and its continued existence did not depend on the herds, although the practical capacity to exercise it was severely reduced. The catastrophe diminished the power of Indigenous nations to resist and to shape the terms of their relations with settler states. It did not extinguish the nations themselves, and it did not erase the claims they continued to make.

The chapter's findings lead directly to the next three chapters. In Canada, the numbered treaties, examined in Chapter 82, were negotiated and implemented against the background of the herds' decline, and treaty promises of food assistance and farming support took on special importance. The Métis resistances of 1869 to 1870 and 1885, treated in Chapter 83, grew in part from the economic and political uncertainty of a community whose economic base was eroding. In both countries, the reservation and reserve systems, compared in Chapter 84, became the main framework for Indigenous life after the buffalo, and administrators' control of food, land, and movement gave those systems their force. The destruction of the buffalo economy did not by itself create those systems, but it created conditions in which they could be imposed.

Historical Debate

Was the Buffalo's Destruction Primarily Commercial Exploitation, Military Strategy, or a Combination of Processes?

The question: Historians agree that the bison were destroyed in a few decades and that the destruction served the interests of settler expansion and weakened Indigenous nations. They disagree about how to apportion causation among commercial markets, military and government policy, ecological conditions, and Indigenous hunting, and about whether the destruction should be described as deliberate.

Interpretation A — Military and policy intent was significant: Some historians, among them David Smits in his study of the frontier army, argue that army officers and policymakers understood the strategic value of destroying the herds and acted to encourage or facilitate it, so that the destruction was in important respects a tool of conquest. They point to officers' statements, the army's toleration of hunters in treaty lands, assistance to hunters in some cases, and the failure to protect the herds.
Interpretation B — Commercial and ecological forces were primary: Other historians, including Andrew Isenberg in his environmental history, emphasize that market demand for hides, railroad access, and technology drove the killing, that the army and government largely tolerated rather than planned it, and that ecological pressures such as drought, competition from horses and livestock, and earlier hunting of cows had already weakened the herds. Isenberg also stresses the role of Indigenous participation in the robe trade and of ecological factors in the earlier decline.
Interpretation C — A combination of processes, with differences by region: Many historians hold that the causes cannot be separated cleanly: markets created the means and motive, officials recognized and welcomed the political effects, and ecological stress made the herds more vulnerable. On this view the question is not which cause was real but how they interacted and how weights differed on the Southern Plains, the Northern Plains, and the Canadian prairies. Canadian scholars add the question of how government policy used hunger after the herds had gone.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on railroad and trade records, army correspondence and reports, Interior Department documents, the record of H.R. 921, Hornaday's survey, hunters' memoirs, Indigenous testimony, and ecological research. Key limits are the absence of a located order directing extermination, the retrospective character of several famous statements, and the difficulty of measuring populations.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns causation and weight, the meaning of intent, and terminology: whether toleration and facilitation amount to deliberate policy, and whether language of extermination or genocide is applicable. Historians also differ on the importance of Indigenous hunting relative to commercial hunting, and these differences concern interpretation more than established facts.

Why it matters: How the question is answered affects who is held responsible, how the loss is remembered, and how it connects to the history of dispossession. A purely commercial account risks obscuring the political effects and the role of government choices, while a purely military account risks overlooking markets, ecology, and the variety of actors. The combination view is generally better supported by the evidence, but its weighting remains open.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the destruction of the buffalo economy was a transformation at once ecological, economic, and political, and that it altered the balance of power between Indigenous nations and the expanding settler states of the United States and Canada. It was not merely an episode of slaughter. It removed the material foundation on which many Plains nations had built their mobility, food security, trade, and political independence, and it created conditions in which treaties, reserves, and reservations could be imposed.

The chapter began with the animal and its environment. The plains bison lived in a dynamic grassland shaped by rainfall, fire, grazing, and drought, moving in irregular patterns that did not correspond neatly to the later image of two great herds. Estimates of its numbers vary widely, and this textbook treats the uncertainty as a feature of the evidence rather than a gap to be filled with a single figure. The wood bison of the northern forests survived in smaller numbers and followed a separate conservation history.

Indigenous nations organized diverse economies around the bison. Mobile equestrian nations such as the Lakota, Cheyenne, Comanche, Kiowa, Blackfoot, and Plains Cree combined hunting, trade, and diplomacy, regulated communal hunts through soldier societies, and negotiated access to hunting grounds. Village nations such as the Mandan, Hidatsa, Arikara, Pawnee, and Wichita combined agriculture with hunting and exchange. The buffalo economy was an organized system, not a collection of subsistence hunters, and women's labor in processing meat and hides was its foundation. Spiritual practice, kinship, and material life were bound to the animal in ways that varied among nations.

Commercial pressure developed before 1870. The robe trade, centered on posts such as Fort Union and Bent's Fort, connected Indigenous producers to eastern and international markets, concentrated hunting on cows, and increased demand for horses and manufactured goods. Pemmican provisioning tied the northern Plains to the fur trade. Drought, competition from horses and livestock, overland migration, and possibly disease added ecological stress. These pressures made herds more vulnerable but did not by themselves produce the collapse, and Indigenous and Métis commerce differed in scale, organization, and purpose from the industrial hunt that followed.

The commercial hide hunt of the 1870s arose from the convergence of several conditions: railroads that reached the Plains between 1869 and 1883, the development of industrial uses for bison leather, long-range rifles, a supply chain of hunters, skinners, freighters, merchants, and manufacturers, and a legal environment that imposed little restraint. Profits were distributed unevenly, with much accruing to merchants and manufacturers, and the economics rewarded volume and discouraged conservation. Hides were taken and carcasses often left, and bones became a secondary market. The destruction of the southern herd between about 1871 and 1878 can be traced through railheads, shipping records, and hide yards such as that of Rath and Wright at Dodge City.

Military and civil policy shaped the context. Army leaders understood the connection between the herds and Indigenous independence, tolerated commercial hunters in treaty lands, and in some cases assisted them, but historians have not found an order directing systematic extermination, and several famous statements rest on retrospective or uncertain sources. The Interior Department, as Columbus Delano's 1873 report shows, recognized that the loss of the herds would increase Indigenous dependence even as it objected to trespass. Congress's 1874 bill to protect female buffalo failed through a pocket veto. Treaty hunting rights depended on the herds' survival, and by the late 1870s food supplies had become instruments of political leverage.

The consequences varied by region. On the Southern Plains, the destruction of the herd, commercial intrusion, and military campaigns, including the Red River War, produced rapid reservation concentration. On the Northern Plains, the herd survived longer and collapsed within a few years of the Northern Pacific's arrival and the end of the Great Sioux War. On the Canadian prairies, the herds vanished by about 1879, before the Canadian Pacific Railway, as the numbered treaties were being implemented, and Cree, Assiniboine, Blackfoot, and Métis communities faced hunger under a different administrative system. The Métis, whose economy rested on organized hunts and pemmican, saw their economic base erode with political consequences examined in Chapter 83.

Indigenous peoples responded as historical actors under severe constraint. They pursued remaining herds across the border, negotiated treaties and relief provisions, held out for alternative arrangements, relocated or surrendered to protect families, diversified into farming, ranching, freighting, wage work, and the bone trade, and preserved knowledge and ceremony. Leaders and communities differed in their judgments. Testimony such as that of Plenty Coups, mediated by interpreters and recorders, conveys the depth of the loss while requiring careful source criticism.

The human consequences were severe. Hunger, disease, and the disruption of labor and exchange reshaped households. Ration systems in the United States and Canada, which differed in structure and policy, gave administrators leverage over movement, labor, and leadership, and in particular cases, such as Canada in 1882, food was used to direct relocation. Cases of severe starvation, including the Blackfeet's Starvation Winter of 1883 to 1884, show the cost. Women's labor was reorganized, ceremonies adapted, and children grew up in a changed world, though communities worked to maintain continuity.

The ecological transformation continued after the herds. Fixed-site grazing by cattle replaced mobile bison grazing, wolves were poisoned, bones were removed, and ranching, barbed wire, and farming reorganized the grassland. Hornaday's 1889 survey, a major source and the product of a scientist who was also a collector, documented near-extinction, with all the limits of its estimates. The bison survived through a few wild remnants and private herds, and early conservation preserved the animal while largely failing to address Indigenous relationships to it. The chapter's conclusion is that the loss of the herds diminished the capacity of many nations to maintain political independence and set the stage for the treaty, resistance, and reservation histories of Chapters 82 through 84, while Indigenous nations and their claims endured.

Key Terms

Plains bison
The form of bison that lived across the grasslands from the Southern Plains into the Canadian prairies, and the principal animal of the Plains economies and the commercial slaughter.
Wood bison
A larger, darker form of bison of the northern boreal forests, which survived in small numbers and followed a separate conservation history.
Buffalo economy
The set of systems for hunting, processing, exchange, ritual, and governance organized around bison, which varied by nation and region and supported mobility, trade, and political independence.
Equestrian economy
An economy transformed by the horse, in which mounted hunters pursued bison over large distances and horse herds functioned as wealth.
Akicita
Lakota camp police or soldier societies that enforced discipline during communal hunts; comparable societies existed among other nations.
Buffalo jump
A cliff or drop over which bison were driven in communal hunts, used on the Plains for thousands of years, as at Head-Smashed-In in present-day Alberta.
Parfleche
A folded, painted rawhide case used for storing and transporting food and goods, commonly made by Plains women.
Pemmican
A preserved food of dried, pounded meat mixed with rendered fat, produced for household use and for commercial provisioning, notably by Métis and Cree producers.
Robe trade
The commercial exchange of tanned bison robes, produced largely by women's labor, for manufactured goods at trading posts.
Fort Union
American Fur Company post at the confluence of the Missouri and Yellowstone rivers, founded in 1828, a major robe-trade center.
Bent's Fort
Trading post on the Arkansas River, built in the early 1830s, a center of Southern Plains commerce and diplomacy.
Carrying capacity
The number of animals a given environment can support over time, used by researchers to estimate historical bison populations, with results that depend on assumptions.
Commercial hide hunting
Professional killing of bison primarily for their hides, sold through merchants to tanners and manufacturers, chiefly in the 1870s and early 1880s.
Supply chain
The linked sequence of hunters, skinners, freighters, merchants, railroads, and manufacturers through which hides moved from killing grounds to markets.
Hide yard
A commercial facility at a railhead where hides were bought, stored, baled, and shipped, such as the Rath and Wright yard at Dodge City.
Treaty hunting rights
Rights to hunt on specified lands reserved in treaties such as Medicine Lodge (1867) and Fort Laramie (1868), conditioned on buffalo remaining in sufficient numbers.
Pocket veto
The process by which a bill fails when the President does not sign it and Congress adjourns before the period for signature or veto expires, as happened to H.R. 921 in 1874.
Red River War
Army campaigns of 1874 to 1875 against Comanche, Kiowa, Southern Cheyenne, and Arapaho communities on the Southern Plains, which ended with their concentration at agencies.
Annuities
Payments and goods promised to Indigenous nations under treaties, which became more important as hunting declined.
Rations
Government-issued food supplies, such as flour, bacon, sugar, and beef, distributed at agencies and reserves and often inadequate.
Ration dependence
The reliance of a community on government food supplies after the loss of its previous resources, produced by imposed conditions and used by administrators as a means of control.
Beef issue
The distribution of live cattle at agencies, sometimes accompanied by a staged hunt in which men chased and shot the animals.
Reservation and reserve
Lands set aside for Indigenous nations under United States and Canadian law, respectively, and administered by federal authorities, as compared in Chapter 84.
Red River cart
A wooden, ox-drawn cart used by Métis hunters and freighters on the northern Plains.
Open range
Unfenced public grazing land on which cattle ranged in the 1860s to 1880s, before the spread of fencing and farming.
Near-extinction
The reduction of a species to a very small number of survivors, distinct from extinction, since the survivors made recovery possible.
Winter count
A pictographic record of a community's history, marking one notable event per year, used by historians as evidence when interpreted with knowledge of the specific count.
Historiography
The study of how historians have interpreted a subject, here including debate over the causes of the bison's destruction.

Chapter Reflection

The destruction of the buffalo economy shows that the conquest of the Plains involved more than battles and treaties. It was also the dismantling of a continental system of food, labor, trade, and ceremony that had sustained Indigenous nations for generations, accomplished through commerce, infrastructure, policy, and neglect operating together. Understanding that system's collapse helps explain why military and diplomatic outcomes took the forms they did, and why Indigenous leaders faced the choices they faced in the late 1870s and 1880s.

The chapter also demonstrates the value of separating mechanisms. Markets, railroads, technology, army attitudes, federal decisions, ecological stress, and Indigenous strategies each played a part, and historians' disagreements often turn on weight rather than on the existence of these factors. Careful attention to evidence, including the limits of famous statements and the uncertainty of population figures, protects against both simplified villains and simplified inevitabilities.

The consequences extended well beyond the 1880s. The reservation and reserve systems, the ration economy, the reorganization of women's labor, the transformation of the grasslands into ranches and farms, and the early efforts at conservation all trace back to the events described here. So do later Indigenous efforts, treated in later chapters, to restore relationships with the buffalo and to assert treaty rights over fisheries, wildlife, and land.

Finally, the chapter reinforces the central argument of this textbook. Indigenous nations were not remnants of a vanished world. They endured a catastrophic loss of the economic basis of their independence and continued to exist as political communities with claims, knowledge, and strategies. The history of the buffalo's destruction is inseparable from that continuing history, which the chapters that follow take up through treaties, resistance, and the reservation order.
