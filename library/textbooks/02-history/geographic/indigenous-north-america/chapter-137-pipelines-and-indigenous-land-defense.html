Pipelines and Indigenous Land Defense

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

A pipeline is a narrow object: a steel tube buried a few feet beneath the ground. Yet it can cross dozens of jurisdictions, hundreds of watersheds, and the homelands of many Indigenous nations. For that reason, pipeline conflicts have become some of the clearest modern contests over a single question: who has the authority to decide what happens on and beneath Indigenous lands?

In 1977, Justice Thomas Berger of the Supreme Court of British Columbia delivered the report of the Mackenzie Valley Pipeline Inquiry to the Government of Canada. After hearing testimony in dozens of northern communities, many of them Dene, Inuit, Inuvialuit, and Métis, he recommended against a pipeline across the northern Yukon and for a ten-year postponement of any pipeline along the Mackenzie Valley, so that Indigenous land claims could be settled first. Thirty-nine years later, in 2016, thousands of people gathered on the Missouri River in North Dakota, near the Standing Rock Reservation, to oppose the Dakota Access Pipeline. They called themselves water protectors, and the camps they built became, for a time, among the largest Indigenous gatherings in modern North American history. In 2022, a partnership representing twenty-three First Nation and Métis communities in northern Alberta bought a minority, non-operating interest in seven pipelines operated by Enbridge. These three episodes do not form a simple story of defeat, victory, or reversal. Together they show that Indigenous responses to infrastructure have included inquiry, resistance, litigation, negotiation, regulation, and ownership.

This chapter studies those responses within the larger history of infrastructure in Indigenous North America. Chapter 136 examined the authority to develop or refuse mineral and energy extraction. Chapter 137 asks what follows when the oil and gas produced in one place must be moved across territories belonging to other nations. The distinction is analytically important. Owning a resource, owning a pipeline, and holding territorial jurisdiction are different kinds of authority. An Indigenous nation may hold shares in a pipeline while exercising little regulatory power over it; another may hold strong treaty or territorial claims yet receive no revenue from infrastructure crossing its homeland.

The chapter’s central argument is that pipeline conflicts are, at bottom, disputes over territorial authority: who may authorize infrastructure, determine its route, protect land and water, and set the conditions under which industrial development occurs. Pipelines are not only physical systems. They embody decisions about land, jurisdiction, economic priorities, environmental risk, and governmental power. The legal setting differs sharply across the continent. In the United States, the key concepts are treaty rights, trust lands, tribal sovereignty, and federal administrative law. In Canada, they are Aboriginal title, treaty rights, the Crown’s duty to consult, and modern land-claim agreements. In Mexico, they are community territory, constitutional recognition of Indigenous peoples, and the right to prior consultation. This chapter keeps those frameworks distinct and does not apply one nation’s doctrine to another.

The chapter is organized in five divisions. Division I establishes the historical and legal foundations: how infrastructure transformed Indigenous territories, how the pipeline economy developed, how rights-of-way and jurisdiction work, and why water is so often central. Division II examines the northern cases, including the Trans-Alaska Pipeline and the Berger Inquiry, and the earlier mobilizations that preceded Standing Rock. Division III gives extended attention to Keystone XL and to the Dakota Access Pipeline, including its legal history through 2026. Division IV follows conflicts across the continent: Enbridge Lines 3 and 5, Northern Gateway and Trans Mountain, and Coastal GasLink. Division V turns to the Yaqui in Sonora, Indigenous infrastructure ownership, and the institutional politics of consultation and monitoring.

The evidence is unusually varied and unusually contested. Historians of these conflicts draw on treaty texts, court opinions, regulatory records, environmental impact statements, inquiry transcripts, tribal government statements, corporate filings, news reporting, and oral testimony. Each carries a particular perspective. Corporate and government documents describe permits, risks, and decisions, but may say little about Indigenous governance or cultural meaning. Indigenous testimony explains relationships to land and water, yet is sometimes held to legal standards of proof that those relationships do not easily meet. Journalism provides immediacy but may confuse allegation with finding. Many of these cases remain in active litigation, and this chapter dates each contemporary status and distinguishes documented events, allegations, official explanations, and judicial findings.

Two cautions guide the narrative. First, Indigenous peoples have not responded to pipelines uniformly. Within a single nation, elected councils, hereditary leaders, families, and communities have sometimes taken different positions, and the reasons have included economic need, treaty obligations, environmental concern, and differing views of governance. Second, a movement may reshape institutions without stopping a project. Standing Rock influenced federal regulation, public understanding, and Indigenous political networks even though the pipeline entered operation. Accurate history requires taking both facts seriously.

This chapter builds on Chapters 106, 115, 117, 118, 120, 133, 135, and 136, and prepares for Chapter 138, which examines climate change in the Indigenous North. It does not retell comprehensive treaty-rights history, the full history of the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act, or general water-rights doctrine, which belong to those chapters. It concentrates on infrastructure, territorial authority, and land defense.

Historical Context

Indigenous peoples built and maintained infrastructure long before industrial pipelines: trails and portage routes, canoe networks, irrigation systems, weirs and fish traps, causeways, and the long-distance exchange routes described in Volumes I through III. These systems were embedded in kinship, law, and seasonal rhythms. What changed in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries was not the existence of infrastructure but who authorized it, who benefited from it, and how it was imposed across Indigenous jurisdictions.

By the 1880s, as examined in Volume VII, railroads, wagon roads, telegraph lines, and military posts had carried settler states into the interior. The reservation and reserve systems (Chapter 84), allotment (Chapter 86), and the Indian Act (Chapter 85) turned Indigenous homelands into smaller, administered spaces through which corridors could be cut. In the twentieth century, dams, power lines, highways, and pipelines extended that pattern. The treaty-rights struggles of Chapters 106 and 113, the land-claim settlements of Chapters 115 and 116, the constitutional developments of Chapter 117, and the sovereignty doctrines of Chapter 118 supplied the legal vocabulary that later pipeline disputes would use. Chapters 133 through 136 supplied the environmental and economic context: Indigenous ecological knowledge, treaty stewardship of fisheries and wildlife, and the ownership and refusal of extraction.

Pipelines occupy a distinctive place in this history because they separate the site of extraction from the site of benefit and risk. An oilfield or mine concentrates impacts in the producing region. A pipeline distributes risk along a line that may run for a thousand miles or more, crossing communities that neither consented to the production nor share its revenue. The geography of energy markets therefore tends to put infrastructure across many Indigenous territories at once, so that a decision made in a regulatory hearing room in Ottawa, Washington, or Mexico City may fall on people living far from the oilfield or gas basin.

The legal context also differs by country. In the United States, tribal nations hold a government-to-government relationship with the federal government, and treaties are, under the Constitution, part of the supreme law of the land; but Congress holds broad power, and federal agencies make most decisions about permits and easements on federal land and waters. In Canada, Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982, recognizes and affirms existing Aboriginal and treaty rights, and the courts have developed the Crown’s duty to consult and, where appropriate, accommodate. In Mexico, constitutional and international commitments, including the consultation obligations Mexico accepted under International Labour Organization Convention 169, frame Indigenous rights differently again. This chapter follows those differences rather than flattening them.

Finally, readers should resist two simplifications. The first is that Indigenous people simply oppose pipelines; the evidence shows opposition, negotiation, regulatory participation, and ownership, often within the same nation. The second is that pipeline conflicts are only environmental disputes. Environmental protection is central, but so are jurisdiction, treaty interpretation, governance, economic development, and historical memory.

Section 137.1

Infrastructure and the Transformation of Indigenous Territories

Indigenous North America was never without infrastructure. Portage trails linked river systems, canoe routes carried goods across the Great Lakes and the northern interior, irrigation canals watered Hohokam fields, and exchange paths moved copper, shell, and obsidian across the continent (Chapters 17 and 20). These networks were maintained under Indigenous law and diplomacy. The infrastructure of the colonial and settler states differed in purpose and in authority: it was built to integrate territories into markets and administrative systems controlled elsewhere, and it was usually built without the consent of the Indigenous nations whose lands it crossed.

Colonial roads, forts, and river landings began that process, but the railroad made its scale unmistakable. As earlier chapters describe, rail lines such as the Union Pacific, the Northern Pacific, and the Canadian Pacific tied the interior to eastern markets, brought settlers, military forces, and commercial hunters onto Indigenous lands, and in many places preceded the treaties or reserve arrangements that were later invoked to legitimize them. Telegraph lines followed, and in the twentieth century electrical transmission lines, highways, and hydroelectric projects did the same. This chapter summarizes rather than repeats that history; the point here is the pattern. A corridor, narrow in itself, gave governments and corporations a means of exercising economic control over territory far beyond any single settlement or industrial site.

Hydroelectric and flood-control projects show how infrastructure could remove land outright. On the upper Missouri River, the Pick-Sloan program of the 1940s and 1950s produced the Garrison Dam, which flooded land of the Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara at Fort Berthold, and the Oahe Dam, which flooded bottomlands on the Standing Rock and Cheyenne River reservations, displacing communities and removing timber, gardens, and homes along the river. In northern Quebec, the James Bay hydroelectric project of the 1970s led the Cree and Inuit to litigate and then to negotiate the James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement of 1975, among the first modern land-claim settlements in Canada. These cases anticipate later pipeline disputes in two ways: they involved projects planned elsewhere for distant benefit, and they produced new legal and institutional arrangements for Indigenous governments.

By the mid-twentieth century, industrial energy systems themselves became a form of territorial infrastructure. Oil and gas fields in the western interior and the Gulf Coast were linked to refineries and cities by long pipelines. These lines were, in settler-state law, largely treated as commercial infrastructure to be sited and permitted through regulatory processes. The people who lived along the route might be notified, compensated for easements, or consulted; but until the late twentieth century, Indigenous nations were rarely treated as governments with authority to approve or refuse such projects, except where treaty or reservation law gave them a particular lever.

Understanding the continuity from railroad to pipeline helps explain why Indigenous opposition to pipelines often draws on older grievances. For many communities, a new corridor recalls earlier ones: the railroad that crossed a treaty area, the dam that flooded a river valley, the highway built through a burial ground. The history of infrastructure in this chapter is thus also a history of memory, in which earlier acts of dispossession shape the way present proposals are judged.

The economics of a corridor also differ from the economics of a single mine or town. A mine occupies a defined production site; a pipeline connects many sites, governments, and owners. Its value depends on continuity. A company may have secured almost all of the land needed for a route but still be unable to complete it because a short crossing remains disputed. Conversely, once a line has been constructed, decisions concerning a few parcels may affect customers and workers hundreds of miles away. These interdependencies explain why rights-of-way have repeatedly become the focus of political pressure. They also explain why a dispute over one river or parcel may acquire national significance without ceasing to be a dispute about a particular Indigenous homeland.

Historical Vocabulary
Infrastructure and Territorial Authority

Definition. Infrastructure refers to constructed systems, such as roads, rail lines, dams, power lines, and pipelines, that move people, goods, water, or energy. Territorial authority is the power to make binding decisions about land and what occurs on it, including authorizing or refusing development.

Historical context. Settler states used infrastructure to extend economic and administrative control over Indigenous territories. Indigenous nations asserted their own authority through treaties, councils, and law, and many continue to do so.

Why it matters. The two terms are linked but distinct. Those who build or own infrastructure do not automatically hold authority over the land it crosses, and those who hold authority may lack ownership of the infrastructure.

Potential misunderstanding. Infrastructure is sometimes treated as politically neutral engineering. Every route reflects decisions about whose land will be crossed and whose consent is required.

Section 137.2

The Rise of the North American Pipeline Economy

Pipelines are classified by what they carry. Crude oil pipelines move unrefined petroleum from fields to refineries. Natural gas pipelines carry gas, which may be gathered in a producing basin, moved through large transmission lines, and delivered through local distribution networks. Refined petroleum product pipelines carry gasoline, diesel, and similar fuels from refineries toward consumers. Each category has different economics, different regulatory treatment, and different risks. Production, transportation, processing, and distribution are separate stages of an industry, and a pipeline is a transportation asset that may be owned by companies that neither produce nor refine the product.

Long-distance pipelines grew rapidly in the twentieth century. In the United States, wartime programs of the 1940s built large crude and product lines from the Gulf Coast toward the East, and postwar prosperity, suburban growth, and automobile use drove demand for petroleum and natural gas. In Canada, the discovery of major oil in Alberta in the late 1940s led to the construction of lines to Ontario and to the Pacific coast; the original Trans Mountain pipeline from Edmonton to the Vancouver area began operating in 1953, and a natural gas trunk line connecting Alberta to eastern Canada followed in the late 1950s. Enbridge’s ancestor company built the first lines from Alberta to the Great Lakes, and the Line 3 and Line 5 systems that appear later in this chapter date from the postwar decades. Cross-border energy trade between Canada and the United States grew into one of the world’s largest bilateral energy relationships.

Regulation developed alongside construction. In the United States, the Natural Gas Act of 1938 gave federal authority over interstate gas transmission, while crude oil pipelines were generally sited under state law and, where they crossed federal lands and waters, through federal permits such as those issued by the Army Corps of Engineers. In Canada, the National Energy Board, created in 1959 and replaced in 2019 by the Canada Energy Regulator, regulated interprovincial and international pipelines, while pipelines wholly within a province, such as Coastal GasLink in British Columbia, fell under provincial regulation. These divisions of authority matter because Indigenous nations had to learn which agency and which level of government held the decision they sought to influence.

The economic geography of energy explains why Indigenous lands are so often crossed. Production sites such as the Alberta oil sands, the Bakken formation in North Dakota, and the natural gas fields of northeastern British Columbia lie far from refineries, ports, and large cities. Moving the product requires continuous corridors through prairies, boreal forests, river valleys, and mountains, which are the same landscapes occupied by hundreds of Indigenous nations. Even a route chosen mainly to minimize cost or to avoid dense settlement can cross many treaty territories and watersheds. Rights-of-way, usually acquired through voluntary easements or, where law allows, eminent domain or expropriation, then secure the corridor for decades.

A pipeline company generally seeks a return on capital invested in construction and maintenance. Long-term transportation commitments can help justify construction because they provide anticipated revenue even before the first shipment. In a simplified arrangement, producers or other shippers reserve capacity and pay a transportation charge. The price a producer receives for its oil and the charge an operator collects for transportation are therefore not the same thing. Some contracts protect the operator from fluctuations in shipments more effectively than others. Changes in production, refinery demand, competing routes, and regulation nevertheless influence the long-term value of a line. This distinction becomes important when Indigenous partnerships acquire interests in transportation assets rather than in the fields that supply them.

Section 137.3

Treaty Territories, Rights-of-Way, and Infrastructure Jurisdiction

Pipeline disputes cannot be understood without separating several concepts that are often blurred in public debate. Traditional territory refers to lands that a people has historically used, occupied, and governed according to its own law; it does not necessarily match any legal boundary recognized by a settler state. Treaty territory refers to areas defined in or affected by a treaty, which may include lands reserved, lands ceded, and lands over which hunting, fishing, or travel rights were retained. A reservation (United States) or reserve (Canada) is land set aside for a particular nation or band, usually much smaller than the traditional territory. Tribal trust land is land held by the United States in trust for a tribe or individual. Allotted land is land assigned to individuals under allotment-era policy (Chapter 86); some allotments remain in trust and some have passed into other ownership. In Canada, Aboriginal title is a constitutionally protected interest in land arising from prior occupation, recognized by the Supreme Court of Canada in a series of decisions, including Delgamuukw (1997) and Tsilhqot’in Nation v. British Columbia (2014).

A right-of-way is a legal permission to use land for a particular purpose, usually through an easement. A pipeline easement grants a company the right to bury and operate a pipe across land belonging to someone else. The United States and Canada also allow, in defined circumstances, compulsory acquisition: eminent domain in the United States and expropriation in Canada. In the United States, a federal law dating from 1948 requires tribal consent for rights-of-way across tribal lands, and the consent of owners is generally required for allotted lands, which gives tribal landowners real leverage on reservations. But the same law does nothing about pipelines routed just outside reservation boundaries, and federal eminent-domain power under the Natural Gas Act applies to interstate gas lines rather than to crude oil lines.

This explains a pattern that recurs in this chapter: a pipeline located outside the formal boundaries of a reservation may still affect treaty-protected resources, sacred places, ancestral territories, and governmental interests. Reserved harvesting rights in ceded territories (Chapter 135), rivers that flow from outside into reservation lands, and cultural sites beyond reserve boundaries all lie outside the land a nation owns today. Legal ownership, political jurisdiction, cultural relationship, and reserved rights are separate things. A tribe can own none of the land at a river crossing yet hold treaty claims, environmental concerns, and burial grounds nearby.

Jurisdiction adds another layer. In the United States, tribal governments exercise authority over their lands and members, but their authority over non-member activity on non-Indian-owned land within and beyond reservations is limited by federal law and Supreme Court decisions (Chapter 118). Federal agencies decide permits on federal land and waters, and states regulate many intrastate matters. In Canada, interprovincial and international pipelines fall under federal authority, intraprovincial pipelines under provincial authority, and the Crown bears a duty to consult and, where appropriate, accommodate Indigenous peoples when its decisions may affect asserted or established Aboriginal or treaty rights. In Mexico, federal energy and environmental authorities decide, with constitutional and international obligations of consultation shaping the process.

Environmental review is the arena in which these issues commonly meet. An environmental impact assessment or environmental assessment evaluates likely effects of a project, and in the United States the National Environmental Policy Act and the National Historic Preservation Act require agencies to examine environmental and cultural effects and, under the latter, to consult tribes about historic properties of religious and cultural significance. These processes can reveal risks and require mitigation, but they typically produce a decision by the government, not Indigenous consent. The difference between consultation and consent is a central theme of the chapter. International standards such as the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (2007) refer to free, prior, and informed consent, but domestic law in the three countries gives that phrase different and limited legal force.

An easement is not ordinarily a purchase of the entire underlying estate. Its duration, width, purpose, conditions of access, and renewal provisions are matters of the grant and applicable law. If an operator has permission to maintain a line for a specified term, the fact that it previously operated lawfully does not establish a perpetual entitlement. Nor does an environmental permit automatically supply property rights that an operator lacks. The Bad River litigation provides a concrete example. In contrast, an owner who has granted a valid easement cannot necessarily withdraw permission whenever its preferences change. The documents and statutory framework determine the available remedies.

In the Canadian framework, the Supreme Court's Haida Nation decision explained why the Crown's obligation can arise before an Indigenous claim is conclusively established. If the Crown knows or ought to know about a potential Aboriginal right or title and contemplates conduct that could adversely affect it, consultation may be required; the strength of the claim and seriousness of the potential effect influence its extent. On land where title has been established, Tsilhqot’in Nation explains that consent is required unless the Crown can justify an infringement under constitutional standards. Neither proposition means that consultation is satisfied simply by mailing notices to communities. Nor does it mean that every asserted right produces the same legal remedy.

Historical Vocabulary
Right-of-Way

Definition. A right-of-way is a legal interest allowing a person or company to use a strip of land for a specified purpose, such as a road, power line, or pipeline. It is commonly created by an easement, which may be negotiated, granted by statute, or obtained through compulsory acquisition.

Historical context. Rights-of-way across Indigenous lands were authorized by treaties and statutes beginning in the nineteenth century, often without Indigenous consent. Modern law in the United States requires tribal consent for rights-of-way across tribal land; in Canada, reserve land interests are governed by the Indian Act and related federal rules; in Mexico, easements across Indigenous community land involve community decision-making bodies.

Why it matters. Rights-of-way are often time-limited, as the Line 5 case shows. When a right-of-way expires, a landowner may seek removal; whether it can compel removal depends on law and judicial remedy.

Potential misunderstanding. A right-of-way gives limited use of land, not ownership, and it does not by itself decide questions of jurisdiction or treaty rights.

Section 137.4

Infrastructure, Water, and Indigenous Environmental Security

Water has become the center of many pipeline disputes because it is both a physical resource and a legal, cultural, and political one. Pipelines cross rivers, wetlands, and aquifers; engineers may bury the pipe beneath a riverbed or drill beneath it, and a failure at a crossing can release product into a system that supplies drinking water, fisheries, wild rice beds, or ceremonial sites. For many nations, water carries obligations that cannot be reduced to supply: it is the setting of ceremony, the source of life, and the basis of treaty-protected harvesting described in Chapter 135.

Historians and students should distinguish four different kinds of statement. Environmental risk before an accident is a probability and consequence estimate, not an event. Documented contamination after an accident is an observed fact, established through monitoring and investigation. Regulatory estimates of potential harm are the conclusions of agencies, produced through formal assessments that may assume particular safeguards. Indigenous assessments of cultural and ecological consequences may weigh effects, such as desecration of a sacred landscape or loss of confidence in drinking water, that regulatory models do not measure. These categories can conflict, and they are not interchangeable. It would be inaccurate to say that every pipeline inevitably contaminates waterways, and equally inaccurate to say that engineering safeguards eliminate every risk. Spills have occurred on many pipeline systems, and the 2010 rupture of Enbridge’s Line 6B near the Kalamazoo River in Michigan, which, according to the EPA, discharged at least 843,444 gallons of oil, is cited in the Line 5 dispute as a reason for caution.

Crossings highlight these distinctions. Horizontal directional drilling can place pipe far beneath a riverbed, but engineers and critics disagree about how reliably leaks can be detected and shut off, how quickly emergency crews can respond in freezing conditions, and what a spill would mean for downstream communities. Wetlands and wild rice beds introduce different problems: construction may alter hydrology, and drilling can intercept groundwater, as happened at several Line 3 sites (Section 137.13). Long-term environmental monitoring, which tribal governments and community monitors increasingly conduct themselves, is often the only way to establish what actually occurs.

Water also connects infrastructure to health and security. Communities whose drinking water already depends on single intakes, or whose water systems have a history of failure, perceive risk differently from distant observers. Indigenous environmental security, in this sense, includes not only avoiding spills but also maintaining the capacity to monitor, respond, and govern. Environmental justice arguments, which stress that the burdens of industrial infrastructure fall disproportionately on Indigenous and other marginalized communities, build on this concern and have appeared in federal documents, including the Army Corps’ own acknowledgment in the Dakota Access environmental impact statement that tribal citizens may be disproportionately affected by negative impacts.

The significance of the Kalamazoo event is not that all oil pipelines have identical failure probabilities. It is that a major accident demonstrated the duration and complexity of recovery when petroleum reached a river. The Environmental Protection Agency recorded a discharge of at least 843,444 gallons in July 2010 and extensive cleanup under federal orders, with parts of the river closed for years. The quantity released is different from the quantity eventually recovered, and an estimate may be revised as more information becomes available. For communities dependent on a water body, even temporary closure can produce economic loss, interrupted harvesting, and changes in customary practice. An assessment concerned solely with the initial release would miss these subsequent effects.

Section 137.5

The Trans-Alaska Pipeline and Alaska Native Land Claims

The Trans-Alaska Pipeline System links the petroleum fields of Alaska’s North Slope to the ice-free port of Valdez on Prince William Sound, a distance of roughly eight hundred miles. Its history is inseparable from Alaska Native land claims, which Chapter 115 treats comprehensively; this section addresses the link between the two.

Alaska Native peoples had been asserting aboriginal claims for decades, and in the 1960s these claims intensified as statehood brought state selection of federal land. In 1966, the Secretary of the Interior imposed a freeze on land transfers pending resolution of Native claims, and Alaska Native leaders began organizing the federation in 1966, adopting the name Alaska Federation of Natives in early 1967. The discovery of a very large oil field at Prudhoe Bay in 1968 sharply raised the stakes. Oil companies and the state wanted a pipeline to the south coast; Alaska Native claims clouded title along the route, and the land freeze blocked federal rights-of-way. A settlement became, for many parties, a condition of building the pipeline.

Congress passed the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act (ANCSA) in 1971. It extinguished aboriginal claims to land in Alaska in exchange for title to roughly 44 million acres and a cash payment of $962.5 million, delivered not to tribal governments but to regional and village corporations owned by Alaska Native shareholders. The Trans-Alaska Pipeline Authorization Act followed in 1973, and construction proceeded in the mid-1970s, with the pipeline beginning operation in 1977. Construction brought jobs and contracts, including to Alaska Native businesses and workers, along with disruption and environmental concern.

Debates about the settlement continue. Supporters point to the substantial land base and the economic institutions Alaska Native corporations created; critics note that extinguishment ended aboriginal claims in a way not applied to Indigenous nations in the lower forty-eight, that the corporate form differs from tribal government, and that subsistence rights, so important to village life, were not secured by the Act in the way many leaders had hoped. The interaction between corporate and tribal institutions, and between subsistence and development, remains a distinct feature of Alaska Native politics.

The Alaska settlement emerged from political organization rather than from oil discovery alone. Alaska Native leaders had to coordinate claims across a vast region in which patterns of land use, language, and economic life varied. State land selections made the status of Native lands urgent before Prudhoe Bay became a national energy priority. The oil discovery changed the bargaining environment because resolving the land question also became important to corporations and public officials who otherwise had little incentive to accept an extensive settlement. A coincidence of interests made legislation possible without making all parties' objectives identical.

ANCSA created a corporate landholding system in which the ownership of regional and village corporations was initially based on enrollment under the statute. Regional corporations and village corporations received different land interests, and the distinction between surface and subsurface ownership mattered for later development. The institutions created under the settlement were not simply substitutes for tribal governments. They had business responsibilities to shareholders, whereas tribal governments retained governmental and community responsibilities. A person's membership in a tribal community and shareholding in a corporation were related but legally different relationships. Thus the distribution of business income did not answer every question about the exercise of Indigenous governmental authority.

A further lesson is that the pipeline and land settlement operated on different timescales. Construction employment was concentrated in a particular period, while land ownership and corporate governance continued across generations. The federal Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 subsequently established a rural subsistence priority on federal public lands, but conflicts over subsistence management continued. That later statute illustrates why an industrial agreement should not be mistaken for a final resolution of all land-use rights. Compare the Alaska experience with the Berger inquiry: both connected northern energy development with outstanding Indigenous claims, but the resulting institutions and timing differed markedly.

Section 137.6

The Mackenzie Valley Pipeline Inquiry

In the early 1970s, oil and gas discoveries in the Beaufort Sea region and the Mackenzie Delta led companies to propose a pipeline bringing natural gas from northern Canada, and eventually Alaska, to southern markets. Two consortia sought approval: one proposed a route across the northern Yukon, and another proposed to follow the Mackenzie Valley. The Mackenzie Valley route crossed the homelands of the Dene, the Inuvialuit, and Métis communities, whose territories had differing treaty histories. Many Dene leaders maintained that Treaties 8 and 11 were understood as agreements of peace and friendship rather than outright surrender of their homelands; other areas were outside the numbered treaties.

In 1974, the federal government appointed Justice Thomas Berger to examine the social, environmental, and economic effects of a Mackenzie Valley pipeline and energy corridor. Berger took an unusual approach. Rather than confine hearings to formal sessions in southern cities, the inquiry traveled to dozens of communities, held hearings in Indigenous languages with interpreters, and funded Indigenous organizations to prepare their cases. Residents testified about hunting, trapping, fishing, language, and community life, and about what a pipeline construction boom might mean for villages whose economies still depended heavily on the land. The Dene Declaration of 1975, adopted by Dene leaders, asserted the Dene as a nation with rights of self-determination, and it framed the inquiry as part of a broader claim to political recognition.

Berger’s report, Northern Frontier, Northern Homeland, was published in 1977. It recommended that no pipeline be built across the northern Yukon because of the environmental importance of that area, and that any Mackenzie Valley pipeline be postponed for ten years so that Indigenous land claims could be settled and communities could prepare. The report treated settlement of claims as a precondition, not an afterthought, and argued that northern people should have a decisive role in the future of their region. The federal government did not adopt every recommendation; a different, southern route along the Alaska Highway, in which Indigenous claims were less directly involved, was later approved. But the report had lasting influence.

Its significance has three dimensions. First, it demonstrated that public inquiry could treat Indigenous testimony as evidence relevant to national policy, shifting assumptions about who counted as an expert on northern life. Second, it linked infrastructure approval to land-claim settlement, a principle reflected in subsequent comprehensive claims in the Northwest Territories and Yukon. Third, it influenced the design of Canadian environmental assessment, which has since required greater attention to social and cultural effects and to Indigenous participation. Historians nonetheless caution against reading the inquiry as a simple triumph: it was advisory, the government retained discretion, and the economic and political pressures for northern development did not disappear.

The inquiry was itself a historical event, not merely a document issued at its conclusion. Formal technical evidence about engineering and market demand could be tested against descriptions of the economies that already existed in the North. A hunting household's production did not necessarily appear in conventional employment statistics, but the food it obtained, the knowledge required to obtain it, and the cooperation through which it was shared all had economic importance. A forecast of wage jobs that ignored these activities could exaggerate the net benefits of development. Berger's hearings allowed northern residents to explain why a temporary construction boom might carry lasting consequences for a mixed cash and subsistence economy.

The distinction between the two principal recommendations is important. Berger did not recommend the same outcome everywhere. For northern Yukon he opposed a pipeline corridor on ecological grounds; for the Mackenzie Valley he recommended a period of postponement linked to outstanding land claims. One conclusion concerned the suitability of a particular landscape, while the other concerned the sequence of decisions. To settle claims after approving construction would leave northern peoples bargaining over a project that was already committed; to settle claims first could give them a stronger institutional position. That difference in sequencing remains central to later debates about prior consultation.

The commission's report also changed the terms of public discussion beyond the North. It invited readers to consider the residents of the region as political communities with their own futures rather than as beneficiaries of an externally defined development program. Its recommendations were not a treaty, a judicial judgment, or a binding transfer of power. Their significance lay in the evidence and argument brought into national policy and in the subsequent use of that experience by Indigenous organizations negotiating with governments. The two volumes of Northern Frontier, Northern Homeland should be read in this setting, as an inquiry record and policy argument rather than as a substitute for the testimony of the people who appeared before it.

Section 137.7

Northern Land Claims, Indigenous Ownership, and the Mackenzie Gas Project

The postponement Berger recommended coincided with a period of land-claim negotiation. The Inuvialuit settled their claim in 1984, the Gwich’in in 1992, the Sahtu Dene and Métis in 1993, and the Tłı̨chǫ (Dogrib) reached a combined land-claim and self-government agreement in 2003. Not all regions settled: negotiations in the Deh Cho region proceeded for many years without a final agreement. These modern treaties established Indigenous land ownership, wildlife and environmental co-management boards, resource revenue provisions, and in some cases self-government, and so changed the institutional setting in which later pipeline proposals would be considered.

In the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, gas discoveries in the Mackenzie Delta renewed interest in a Mackenzie Valley pipeline. The proposal that emerged, the Mackenzie Gas Project, differed from the 1970s plans: it would run along the Mackenzie Valley, not cross the northern Yukon; it was proposed by a different group of companies; and it was developed after land-claim agreements and regional boards existed. A distinctive feature was the Aboriginal Pipeline Group, formed around 2000 by Indigenous organizations in the Northwest Territories to negotiate the right to own a significant share of the pipeline. The proponents agreed to offer an ownership interest, and Indigenous groups worked to arrange financing.

The project underwent a joint regulatory review, and a federal-territorial review panel issued a report in 2009 that set out conditions and recommendations; the National Energy Board issued an approval in 2010. Delays followed, and with declining natural gas prices and the rise of shale gas in other regions, the economic case weakened. By the end of 2017 the regulatory approvals had lapsed, and no pipeline had been built. The eventual outcome illustrates a recurring point: authorization and ownership arrangements do not guarantee construction, because markets and financing also decide.

The transformation from the Berger era to the Aboriginal Pipeline Group is often summarized as a shift from opposition to ownership. That summary is too simple. In the 1970s, many northern Indigenous people sought time and political power before any development; by the 2000s, land-claim agreements had given some organizations ownership of land, representation on boards, and bargaining power, and some leaders concluded that participation could secure benefits and influence. Other Indigenous voices remained skeptical about the risks, the pace of development, or the effects on wildlife and communities. Regional differences were significant: communities in the Delta, the Sahtu, and the Deh Cho had different claims positions, local economies, and attitudes. This history therefore shows not a universal reversal but a change in conditions and options.

The Aboriginal Pipeline Group introduced a further distinction between participation as an affected community and participation as a prospective investor. An equity option could allow participating communities to benefit from long-term transportation revenue if the project succeeded, rather than relying entirely on short construction contracts. But a right to acquire equity did not itself create revenue. Financing, construction, sufficient gas production, and favorable market conditions all had to occur. The later abandonment of the project demonstrates why an ownership proposal must be distinguished from ownership of an operating asset. It also shows that Indigenous governments were developing commercial institutions even when a particular project did not proceed.

Section 137.8

Indigenous Environmental Mobilization Before Standing Rock

When the Standing Rock camps appeared in 2016, many observers described them as a sudden eruption. Historians see a longer lineage. Treaty-rights activism in the Pacific Northwest and the Great Lakes (Chapters 106 and 113) had shown that fishing, hunting, and harvesting rights could be defended in court and in public. Opposition to uranium mining on Diné and Pueblo lands, to coal strip-mining on the Navajo and Hopi lands, and to the siting of waste facilities developed a language of environmental justice and sovereignty. Sacred-site struggles, such as those over mountains, rivers, and burial grounds, taught activists to argue that cultural and religious relationships to land deserve legal protection (Chapter 119). Opposition to hydroelectric development in northern Quebec and Manitoba produced Cree and other campaigns that drew international attention in the 1980s and 1990s.

Institutions took shape as well. The Indigenous Environmental Network, established in 1990, brought together grassroots Indigenous organizations concerned with toxic waste, extraction, and later climate change. Organizations such as Honor the Earth, founded in 1993 by Winona LaDuke and the Indigo Girls, linked Indigenous advocacy with national philanthropy and public campaigns. Legal advocacy organizations, including the Native American Rights Fund and Earthjustice, provided counsel in tribal cases, and relationships developed between Indigenous organizations and environmental groups. These relationships were not always easy: Indigenous leaders have criticized conservation organizations for ignoring Indigenous rights, while environmental organizations brought resources, media access, and legal expertise. The coalition-building that accompanied Keystone XL and Standing Rock relied on this earlier groundwork.

Intertribal and international networks also expanded. Indigenous delegations took part in United Nations forums in the 1980s and 1990s that eventually led to the adoption of the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples in 2007 (Chapter 143). Youth networks, social media, and a generation of Indigenous lawyers and scholars added new capacities. The vocabulary of land defense, water protection, and treaty obligation, and practical know-how in organizing, legal strategy, and communication, thus pre-dated 2016. Standing Rock was a culmination as well as a beginning.

The emergence of national networks did not erase local leadership. A legal organization might supply counsel; an environmental organization might contribute scientific research or publicity; and an intertribal network might connect delegations. Decisions about ceremony, local land use, or tribal litigation could remain with different authorities. Coalitions were often most durable when participants understood these distinctions. Conversely, disagreements over who represented a movement or what strategy to pursue could weaken cooperation. The organizational history before Standing Rock is therefore as important as the chronology of individual protests: it explains how a relatively local dispute could be communicated and supported across the continent in 2016.

Section 137.9

Keystone XL and the Politics of Cross-Border Energy Development

Keystone XL was proposed in 2008 to carry diluted bitumen and other crude oil from the Alberta oil sands and the Bakken region through Montana, South Dakota, and Nebraska toward refineries on the Gulf Coast. It was an expansion project. The existing Keystone Pipeline System, which began operating in 2010 and carries Canadian crude through the American Midwest, is a separate, operating infrastructure system, and the two should not be confused. Because Keystone XL would cross an international border, it required a presidential permit from the United States, which turned a regulatory question into a presidential decision.

Opposition drew together several constituencies. Environmental organizations objected to oil sands production and the greenhouse gas emissions associated with it. Nebraska ranchers and farmers objected to the route across the Sandhills and the Ogallala Aquifer and to the use of eminent domain by a foreign-owned company. Tribal nations argued that the line crossed treaty territory, including areas recognized for the Sioux Nation in nineteenth-century treaties, and that the project threatened water and cultural resources. Several nations, including the Rosebud Sioux Tribe, took formal positions against the pipeline. In April 2014, tribal leaders, ranchers, and farmers joined in the Cowboy Indian Alliance, which set up an encampment on the National Mall in Washington, D.C., symbolizing an unusual coalition between groups often historically opposed.

Supporters stressed jobs, energy security, and the efficiency of pipelines compared with rail transport, and some labor unions and some local officials supported the project. Presidential policy shifted over time. President Barack Obama rejected the project in November 2015; President Donald Trump issued a new permit in March 2017; and President Joseph Biden revoked the permit on January 20, 2021. TC Energy formally terminated the project on June 9, 2021. Opponents regarded the outcome as vindication, while supporters regarded it as a lost investment. Historians note that several factors interacted: market conditions, litigation, political change, and sustained opposition. No single one can be isolated as decisive.

The Cowboy Indian Alliance drew political strength from a shared concern about land and water without requiring its participants to hold identical views of history. Ranchers concerned about property rights could find common ground with Indigenous advocates asserting treaty rights. Yet the latter claims concerned political relationships established before much of the private property system in the region existed. The alliance was not proof that those legal traditions were equivalent. It was evidence that a particular proposal created opportunities for cooperation across them. This distinction is useful in understanding later coalitions in which environmentalists, Indigenous governments, unions, and local residents sometimes agreed on objectives and sometimes did not.

A second question concerns the cancellation itself. A government may withdraw one necessary authorization while leaving other segments of an existing transportation system in operation. Keystone XL's termination did not end Canadian petroleum production or the existing Keystone system. It prevented the proposed additional route from becoming operational. Nor did its cancellation automatically demonstrate that one legal claim had prevailed: the 2021 decision followed elections, executive policy changes, and years of organizing. Students should distinguish the historical influence of a coalition from a judicial finding that its particular legal claims were correct. Standing Rock provides a contrasting example in which a highly visible coalition did not prevent construction but contributed to a different kind of institutional outcome.

Section 137.10

The Dakota Access Pipeline and the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe

The Dakota Access Pipeline carries crude oil roughly 1,170 miles from the Bakken formation in North Dakota, through South Dakota and Iowa, to a terminal in Illinois. The Bakken boom of the 2000s and 2010s had made North Dakota a major oil producer, and its crude had been moving by rail and truck; a pipeline promised cheaper and, its proponents argued, safer transport. The line was owned by Dakota Access, LLC, a subsidiary of Energy Transfer.

To understand why the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe objected, one must begin well before 2016. The Lakota, Dakota, and Nakota peoples, the Oceti Sakowin or Seven Council Fires, had long occupied the northern Plains and the Missouri River valley (Chapters 29 and 49). The Fort Laramie Treaty of 1851 recognized broad territory for the Sioux and other Plains nations, and the Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868, following Red Cloud’s War (Chapters 75 and 76), established the Great Sioux Reservation, which covered what is now western South Dakota, set apart the Black Hills, and recognized additional unceded Indian territory. Gold in the Black Hills led to the Great Sioux War and to an 1877 agreement, imposed on the Sioux under threat of starvation, that took the Black Hills (Chapter 77). In 1889, Congress divided the Great Sioux Reservation into smaller reservations, including Standing Rock, which straddles the North and South Dakota border.

The twentieth century brought further losses. The Pick-Sloan plan authorized the Oahe Dam, completed in the early 1960s, which created Lake Oahe and flooded river bottomlands where Standing Rock families had lived, farmed, hunted, and gathered, forcing the relocation of communities. The Missouri River is the reservation’s main water source, and its intake lies downstream of the pipeline crossing. This history shapes how tribal members regard a new industrial structure at the same river.

The pipeline’s route was adjusted during planning, and the crossing of the Missouri was placed under Lake Oahe, upstream of the reservation’s northern boundary. The Army Corps of Engineers, which manages the lake and the land beneath it, had to grant an easement under the Mineral Leasing Act and permits under the Rivers and Harbors Act and the Clean Water Act, as well as comply with the National Environmental Policy Act and the National Historic Preservation Act. The Corps prepared an environmental assessment, not a full environmental impact statement, and concluded that the project would not significantly affect the environment. The Standing Rock Sioux Tribe argued that it had not been adequately consulted, that the assessment understated the risk of a spill upstream of its water intake, and that cultural sites along the route were not properly identified or protected.

The crossing itself lies outside the reservation: approximately half a mile upstream of the northern boundary according to the Army Corps’ own description, and less than a mile according to news reports. The dispute therefore did not turn on the pipeline crossing the reservation, which it does not. It turned on federal obligations to consult and assess, on the tribe’s interest in water supply and cultural resources, and on treaty and territorial claims. The tribe and its supporters point out that the area lies within territory the 1851 and 1868 treaties recognized as belonging to the Sioux Nation. Courts have not resolved these treaty and territorial claims in the tribe’s favor in the Dakota Access litigation, and the case should not be described as having done so. The legal wins the tribe obtained rested instead on environmental review requirements.

Lake Oahe's administrative status was one of the reasons the crossing became central to litigation. Most of a long pipeline may lie on privately owned or otherwise separately regulated land, yet a particular federal property crossing can require a distinct federal authorization. The Army Corps was not necessarily the agency with authority to approve every mile of Dakota Access. Its authority over the federal crossing and certain waters nevertheless supplied the basis for claims that it had not complied with federal review duties. The geographic scale of the environmental consequences and the administrative scale of the permitting decision were different. A central controversy concerned whether review of the crossing captured the potential consequences for the tribe downstream.

The Standing Rock Sioux Tribe's concerns also arose from the way cultural-resource surveys define the area to be studied. Archaeological inventories can identify material traces of previous settlement or use, but Indigenous historic preservation officers may have knowledge of places whose significance is not immediately apparent to a survey team. Some information about burial places or ceremonial landscapes is sensitive and cannot responsibly be made public. Consultation is therefore not simply an invitation to disclose everything a community knows. It requires procedures that allow relevant information to be considered while respecting confidentiality and the authority of those who hold it. The dispute over consultation revealed this tension between administrative documentation and Indigenous historical knowledge.

The Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868

Creator: Treaty commissioners of the United States and leaders of several Lakota, Dakota, and other bands, negotiated at Fort Laramie · Date: 1868 (ratified 1869 proclamation) · Type: Treaty text

Context: After Red Cloud’s War, the United States agreed to abandon forts along the Bozeman Trail and to recognize a large reservation. The treaty also defined unceded Indian territory and addressed hunting rights, land cession, and the presence of non-Indians.

Paraphrase of selected provisions, not direct quotation: Article II sets apart the Great Sioux Reservation for the “absolute and undisturbed use and occupation” of the Indians named, and bars unauthorized non-Indians from entering. Article XI addresses hunting rights beyond the reservation in specified areas. Article XII provides that a treaty ceding any part of the reservation held in common requires the signatures of at least three-fourths of the adult male Indians occupying or interested in it. Article XVI recognizes unceded Indian territory in which non-Indians were not to settle or pass without Indian consent.

Short quoted phrase: “absolute and undisturbed use and occupation” (Article II). Students should read the whole text.

Source access: National Archives, Fort Laramie Treaty (1868); Standing Rock Sioux Tribe, treaty text.

Analysis: The treaty is a legal and political record of what the United States promised. Parts of its text, including the three-fourths signature requirement, figure in later Sioux claims about the 1877 taking of the Black Hills.

Limitations: The treaty does not decide modern legal disputes by itself. Later acts of Congress, court decisions, and the Supreme Court’s 1980 ruling about the Black Hills affect how courts treat its provisions. Students should distinguish the treaty’s original terms from subsequent territorial changes and from contemporary legal claims. The Dakota Access litigation did not adjudicate treaty title.

Perspective: The text was written in English by treaty commissioners, with interpreters, and signed by leaders whose understanding of its terms has been debated. Lakota and Dakota oral traditions preserve their own accounts of the negotiations.

Standing Rock and the Water Protector Movement of 2016–2017

The Standing Rock movement began as local action and grew into one of the largest Indigenous mobilizations of the twenty-first century. In April 2016, LaDonna Brave Bull Allard, a Standing Rock historian and landowner, established the Sacred Stone Camp on her family land near the confluence of the Cannonball and Missouri rivers. Young people from Standing Rock and other communities organized prayer and direct-action efforts, including a relay run to Washington, D.C., that carried a petition to the Army Corps in July 2016. The tribe filed suit against the Corps in federal court that same month. As summer progressed, larger camps formed, especially the Oceti Sakowin camp, whose name referred to the Seven Council Fires. Representatives of hundreds of Indigenous nations, including delegations from across the United States and Canada, arrived, along with non-Indigenous supporters, veterans, clergy, and environmental activists. Estimates of camp populations vary, but at peak periods they included thousands of people.

Movement participants adopted the term water protector rather than protester. The word expressed a claim about responsibility: that people had an obligation to protect water as a living relation, not simply to oppose a company. The phrase Mní Wičóni, in Lakota “water is life” (spellings vary in different orthographies), became the movement’s slogan. Ceremonies, prayer, drumming, and daily fires were central, and participants described the camps as spiritual encampments as well as political ones. Women and elders played prominent roles in leadership, ceremony, and decision-making; youth organized runs, media work, and logistics; and veterans arrived to support the camps in late 2016. The camps held a kitchen, medical and legal tents, a school, and donation tents, and they depended on gifts of food, firewood, and money from around the world.

Intertribal solidarity was striking. Historic enemies camped together, and delegations processed into the camps with flags and songs. Indigenous peoples from other continents sent messages and visitors, and the United Nations Special Rapporteur on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples visited in September 2016 and expressed concern about the treatment of the tribe and its consultation. Environmental organizations contributed funds and legal help. Social media and independent livestreams, in which participants documented events as they happened, shaped public perception and challenged traditional reporting; they also created conflicts over accuracy, and critics said some content misrepresented events.

Tensions with law enforcement intensified through the autumn. On September 3, 2016, as construction crews bulldozed an area that the tribe had identified as containing cultural sites, security guards with dogs confronted protesters, and video of the incident circulated widely. On September 9, a federal judge denied the tribe’s request for a preliminary injunction, and within hours the Departments of Justice, the Army, and the Interior announced a pause on construction on Corps-managed land near Lake Oahe while reconsidering earlier decisions. Authorities from North Dakota counties and sheriff’s departments, the North Dakota National Guard, and law enforcement from multiple other states were deployed. On the night of November 20, law enforcement used water cannon in freezing temperatures against people on a bridge, and authorities and protesters gave conflicting accounts of the use of force and of the origin of fires and projectiles. Hundreds of arrests were made over the course of the movement, and cases moved through state and federal courts with varied outcomes.

Historians must handle the evidence for such events carefully. Some matters are documented by video, court records, and official statements. Others, such as allegations of excessive force, of infiltration by private security contractors, of protester violence, or of misconduct by particular officials, were disputed and were examined, where they were examined, in court cases, investigative reports, and administrative reviews that reached different conclusions. A responsible account distinguishes (1) events recorded by multiple sources, (2) allegations by participants or officials, (3) official explanations, and (4) findings made through judicial or investigative proceedings. The later civil litigation involving Greenpeace is examined separately in Section 137.12.

The relationship between the tribal government and the grassroots camps was complex. The Standing Rock Sioux Tribe, through its council and chairman, was the legal plaintiff and the formal governmental voice; it supported the camps but also distinguished its legal actions from direct-action tactics and sought to maintain safe conditions for residents. Many camp participants came from other nations or non-Indigenous backgrounds and acted under their own organization. Tribal members themselves held differing views, including on the economics of oil development, on the camps’ effects on local life, and on the pressures the movement placed on a small reservation. The movement thus encompassed formal tribal action, grassroots leadership by individuals and youth groups, and outside support, in an evolving combination.

The federal executive response also changed. On December 4, 2016, the Army announced it would not grant the easement at Lake Oahe pending an environmental impact statement and exploration of alternatives. In January 2017, a presidential memorandum directed agencies to expedite review, and on February 7, 2017, the Corps granted the easement. Authorities cleared the remaining camps in February 2017, and oil began flowing in the pipeline in June 2017. The movement did not stop the project. But it brought national and international attention to treaty claims and tribal consultation, produced a lasting body of litigation that eventually led to a full environmental impact statement, and created networks that shaped later campaigns.

The camps' emergence reflected a distinction between citizens acting as members of a political nation and citizens acting through a government. The tribal council could authorize litigation, hire counsel, and communicate officially with federal agencies. Individual citizens and families could initiate gatherings, organize travel, conduct ceremonies, and invite solidarity from other nations. These capacities reinforced one another at several points, but they were not under a single chain of command. LaDonna Brave Bull Allard's role and the initiative of young organizers help explain why an account focused only on federal officials and the pipeline company would miss the origins of the movement. Conversely, the council's status as the litigating government cannot be inferred from the prominence of individuals in press coverage.

The arrival of delegations made the encampments a site of political recognition between nations. Flags and public introductions identified participants with communities and homelands rather than dissolving them into an undifferentiated population. Visitors brought supplies, skills, and knowledge of earlier treaty struggles. For some, the gathering also renewed relationships with people separated by state or international borders. The symbolic importance of the gathering was therefore distinct from its influence on the Army's eventual decision. The movement could be consequential in the history of Indigenous relations even where its immediate policy objective was not achieved.

Daily life was a practical part of political mobilization. Gathering on a large scale required food storage, waste collection, shelter, medical support, translation, and coordination with neighboring communities. The work was often less visible than speeches and encounters with police, but it made prolonged participation possible. Winter conditions increased demands on those systems, and differences among participants about safety, resources, and political strategy became more consequential. Not all citizens could leave jobs or family responsibilities to join the camps; others supported the movement from home or through tribal institutions. A social history of Standing Rock must therefore include the people who sustained the gathering as well as those who appeared in news photographs.

Policing and protest created separate records that historians must compare. A criminal charge is a formal allegation, not a conviction; a civil complaint is not a finding of liability; and an official account of an encounter may be contested by witnesses. A video may establish that a particular event occurred while omitting the circumstances before or after it. Community oral histories can preserve experiences absent from administrative documents, but they also reflect individual positions in a diverse movement. The importance of this distinction is especially clear in later litigation involving outside organizations. The existence of a civil verdict against a particular defendant cannot be used to attribute that defendant's conduct to every participant or to the tribal government.

The December 2016 and February 2017 decisions illustrate the fragility of executive commitments when legal rights remain unsettled. The first did not permanently cancel the project; the second enabled the federal crossing to proceed but did not extinguish environmental review obligations. The period of uncertainty influenced camp participants' expectations and decisions about remaining on site. Looking back from 2017 alone might suggest the history was over. Looking at the subsequent lawsuits and final environmental statement reveals that construction and political resolution were different processes. The broader movement also supplied a repertoire of organizing and legal advocacy used in later pipeline disputes, though those movements were rooted in their own communities and should not be understood merely as extensions of Standing Rock.

Everyday Life and Social History

Community Organization, Cultural Continuity, and Collective Responsibility at Standing Rock

The camps were also a society with daily routines. Participants organized kitchens that fed thousands, firewood crews, medical and legal teams, sanitation arrangements, and winter housing as temperatures fell. Sacred fires burned continuously, and daily prayer gatherings and ceremonies anchored the camps. Elders were consulted on decisions, and responsibilities were shared according to age and skill. Children were present, and an on-site school offered instruction in Lakota and Dakota language and history alongside ordinary subjects.

For many participants, the camps expressed continuity with older Indigenous practices of gathering, council, and mutual obligation, and with the history of Plains and Missouri River communities described in earlier chapters. Others, including some tribal residents, found the camps a strain on local resources and relationships. Both experiences belong to the history. Because the camps were temporary and created their own records, historians rely on participant accounts, photographs, video, tribal and court documents, and oral histories being collected by Indigenous communities and researchers; each has limits of perspective and memory.

Section 137.12

Dakota Access After Standing Rock: Litigation, Regulation, and Unresolved Authority

The Dakota Access story did not end in 2017. After the tribe’s 2016 suit was filed, a federal district judge in Washington, D.C., ruled in 2020 that the Corps had violated the National Environmental Policy Act by issuing the easement without preparing a full environmental impact statement, given the substantial controversy over the project’s effects. On July 6, 2020, the court vacated the easement and ordered the pipeline emptied of oil within thirty days. The court of appeals stayed the shutdown order in August 2020; in January 2021 it affirmed the requirement of an environmental impact statement and the vacatur of the easement, but reversed the order to shut the pipeline down because the lower court had not made the findings required for that remedy. The Supreme Court declined to review the case in February 2022. The result was an unusual legal position: the pipeline continued to operate for years without a valid federal easement at Lake Oahe, while the Corps prepared the environmental statement.

The Corps issued a draft environmental impact statement in September 2023 and a final statement on December 19, 2025. The final statement evaluated several alternatives and acknowledged that tribal citizens could be disproportionately affected by adverse impacts. On May 21, 2026, the Corps signed a record of decision selecting an alternative that granted an easement for the crossing with added conditions, including requirements for leak detection and monitoring of groundwater, among others. The Standing Rock Sioux Tribe had withdrawn as a cooperating agency in the process in January 2022, citing transparency concerns, and it criticized the Corps for inadequate consultation and for withholding information. Officials in North Dakota and the Interior Department welcomed the decision.

The tribe’s litigation continued in parallel. In October 2024 it filed a separate suit alleging that the Corps was allowing the pipeline to operate without a valid easement in violation of federal law. A federal court dismissed that case in March 2025 as premature because the Corps had not yet made a final decision, and the tribe appealed. Following the May 2026 decision, the tribe voluntarily dismissed its earlier appeal in June 2026. A new federal complaint (No. 1:26-cv-03540) was filed October 8, 2026, and publicly announced by the tribe on October 9, seeking to set aside the new approval and halt operation. No final disposition of that new suit was established by the November 26, 2026 cutoff.

How should historians interpret a movement that achieved substantial institutional influence without halting the project? Several answers appear in the evidence. First, the litigation produced a binding finding that the original review was inadequate, and a full environmental impact statement followed. Second, the mobilization altered public awareness and strengthened Indigenous networks, affecting later campaigns and consultations. Third, the outcome also reflects limits: federal law gives agencies the final decision, courts often require procedure rather than a particular result, and the remedy of shutting down a pipeline is difficult to obtain. The result is that Indigenous governmental authority over off-reservation infrastructure that affects treaty interests remained unresolved. Authority was exercised by the Corps; the tribe’s power lay in litigation, political pressure, and public persuasion.

The litigation clarifies the distinction between a finding of illegality and the remedy for it. Under administrative law, a court may conclude that an agency has failed to comply with a required procedure and set aside its decision. An injunction compelling an operator to cease activity is a different remedy, for which the court must consider additional legal requirements. The appellate ruling did not declare the pipeline environmentally harmless. It held that the record and the district court's reasoning did not establish the necessary basis for the shutdown injunction. Conversely, requiring a full environmental impact statement did not determine in advance what alternative the agency would select after preparing one. For the tribe, the procedural victory created an opportunity to challenge the project's justification and conditions rather than a guaranteed outcome.

A parallel but legally distinct controversy concerned Energy Transfer's civil litigation against Greenpeace organizations. A North Dakota jury returned a substantial verdict in March 2025; the district court entered a judgment of approximately US$345 million in February 2026. On November 2, 2026, the court denied the defendants' request for a new trial or alteration of the judgment. Greenpeace disputed the claims and stated that it intended to appeal. The company maintained that the defendants were responsible for actionable misconduct; Greenpeace argued that the suit improperly targeted advocacy. Those positions are claims by litigants, and the verdict concerns the defendants and the issues tried, not the legitimacy of all Indigenous opposition. The case illustrates how civil litigation can shape the resources and risk calculations of organizations that participate in coalitions long after construction ends.

Enbridge Line 3 and Anishinaabe Treaty Stewardship

Line 3 is an Enbridge crude oil pipeline carrying Canadian crude from Alberta through Saskatchewan and Manitoba to Superior, Wisconsin, crossing northern Minnesota. The original line was built in the 1960s and, by the 2010s, had experienced corrosion and cracking problems that led the company to run it at reduced capacity. Enbridge proposed to replace it with a new, larger-diameter pipe, and, in Minnesota, to follow a partly new route rather than the existing corridor. The Minnesota Public Utilities Commission approved a certificate of need in 2018 and a route permit in 2020. Construction began in December 2020, and the replacement pipeline began operating in October 2021.

The new route crossed territory ceded by Anishinaabe nations in the nineteenth-century treaties, within which the signatory nations retained rights to hunt, fish, and gather (Chapter 135). Nations including the White Earth Band and the Red Lake Nation opposed the route, with other Anishinaabe governments and communities also voicing objections or intervening in regulatory proceedings; positions were not uniform among tribal members or among nations. Concerns focused on the crossing of numerous rivers and wetlands, on risks to the Mississippi headwaters and Lake Superior watershed, on the effects of construction on wild rice and other resources, and on the broader impacts of fossil fuels on climate. Supporters of the project, including some labor organizations and local communities, emphasized employment and the replacement of an aging pipe.

Manoomin, wild rice, stands at the center of the Anishinaabe case. In Anishinaabe teaching, manoomin is the food that grows on the water, found by ancestors after a long migration, and it is both a staple and a sacred relationship, harvested and processed with ceremony and care. Wild rice requires clean, stable water, and it is sensitive to changes in water level and chemistry. State and tribal governments protect it by various means, including Minnesota’s water-quality standards for sulfate in wild rice waters, and tribal governments regulate harvesting and restoration. In 2018, the White Earth Band adopted a law recognizing legal rights for manoomin, and in 2021 it brought an action in the White Earth Tribal Court asserting those rights in connection with state permits for Line 3. The case’s significance lay in an assertion of tribal legal authority; its practical and legal reach has been contested, and it did not stop construction.

Opposition took the form of testimony in regulatory hearings, appeals to Minnesota courts, tribal resolutions, and direct action by water protectors, including encampments and acts of civil disobedience. Hundreds of people were arrested during construction. State and county officials argued that protesters broke laws, while protesters argued that the law itself failed to protect treaty rights. Separately, Minnesota agencies documented that construction had caused unplanned groundwater releases at several locations, including breaches of an aquifer; in 2022, Enbridge agreed to a settlement with the state, including penalties and funds for restitution. This is an example of documented impacts after an event, as opposed to risk estimates before it. The significance of those impacts for Anishinaabe resources continues to be monitored and debated.

The importance of manoomin cannot be understood solely as the market value of a crop. Harvesting brings families to particular waters at particular seasons; its preparation and sharing transmit knowledge and establish community relationships. A change in the availability of rice may therefore affect food, social life, and the exercise of treaty rights simultaneously. The ecological conditions that sustain rice vary among lakes and streams, which makes location-specific assessment important. A claim that a project affects harvesting should be examined alongside hydrological and ecological evidence, but it need not be reduced to the sale price of lost rice.

The Minnesota DNR's enforcement records distinguish four confirmed aquifer breach locations associated with replacement construction: Clearbrook, LaSalle Creek, Mile Post 1102.5, and a site near Moose Lake in Aitkin County. The agency reached a further enforcement agreement concerning the Moose Lake site in December 2025. An artesian aquifer contains groundwater under pressure; when construction creates a pathway to the surface, water can escape without the intended controls. Such an event is not the same as a release of petroleum from an operating pipeline. It demonstrates a category of environmental effect that can arise during installation even when no oil is present in the pipe.

Section 137.14

Line 5, the Bad River Band, and the Limits of Pipeline Easements

Line 5 is an Enbridge pipeline completed in the early 1950s that carries light crude oil and natural gas liquids about 645 miles from Superior, Wisconsin, across northern Wisconsin and Michigan to Sarnia, Ontario. Within Wisconsin, a stretch of roughly twelve miles passes through the Bad River Reservation of the Bad River Band of Lake Superior Chippewa, established under the Treaty of La Pointe of 1854. The reservation includes the Kakagon and Bad River Sloughs, a wetland complex of exceptional ecological and cultural importance, with extensive wild rice beds.

The legal question here differs from that at Standing Rock. Line 5 does cross the reservation, on parcels held by the Band, by individual allottees, and in trust. The company’s easements across certain parcels expired in 2013, and the Band declined to renew them, citing concerns about environmental risk, including erosion on the Bad River, which had moved to within a short distance of the buried pipe, and the memory of Enbridge’s 2010 Kalamazoo River spill. In 2019 the Band sued in federal court, claiming trespass and nuisance. This is a different kind of case from regulatory review: the Band asserts a property right and asks a court to enforce it.

In June 2023, the district court in Wisconsin issued remedies after holding that Enbridge was trespassing on parcels in which the Band holds ownership interests, ordered the company to pay the Band several million dollars, and set a deadline in June 2026 for removing the pipeline from the reservation, along with requirements for monitoring and remediation. The court did not accept the Band’s request for an immediate shutdown, and both sides appealed. The district court later stayed the 2026 deadline while the Seventh Circuit considered the appeal. On July 30, 2026, the Seventh Circuit largely affirmed the trespass finding as to twelve allotted parcels where easements had expired, rejected Enbridge’s argument that a 1992 agreement required the Band to consent to renewal, but vacated the restitution order and the removal timeline and remanded for further consideration of remedies. Enbridge has pursued a reroute of about forty-one miles around the reservation, which the Band opposes because it would remain within the Bad River watershed; reroute construction began in February 2026, and the Band has challenged state and federal permits for the reroute. As of this writing the case remains in progress, and students should not treat the 2023 judgment as the final outcome.

The case also touches international politics, since Line 5 carries Canadian oil across American territory, and the Government of Canada has filed briefs supporting Enbridge. This dispute must be distinguished from a separate Line 5 controversy involving the Straits of Mackinac in Michigan, where the State of Michigan has sought to shut down the line’s underwater segment and where other tribal nations with treaty rights in the 1836 cession area have taken positions. The two disputes involve different legal theories, different jurisdictions, and different remedies.

The appellate decision separated two bodies of law. The Band's trespass claim concerned continued occupation of property without valid authorization. Its federal common-law nuisance claim concerned the danger associated with the pipeline near the eroding riverbank. The Seventh Circuit held that the federal Pipeline Safety Act displaced the latter claim; that legal conclusion was not a factual finding that erosion or the possibility of a rupture had ceased to exist. Safety regulation remained the responsibility of the relevant federal authorities. The court instructed the district court to reconsider monetary relief and any injunction in light of its opinion, including a reasonable opportunity for Enbridge to complete the proposed reroute. No final replacement deadline can be inferred merely from the earlier district court judgment.

Rerouting illustrates another distinction: a property solution and a watershed solution are not necessarily identical. A new route that avoids reservation parcels can address the issue of unauthorized occupation without removing all construction and operational effects from a connected watershed. Wisconsin issued relevant permits in November 2024, a state administrative law judge upheld challenged permits in February 2026, and the Army Corps issued its validated federal permit that month. Enbridge's third-quarter 2026 filing described the litigation as remanded and the reroute as a continuing project. The Band's objections to effects beyond reservation boundaries remained a separate matter from its successful trespass claim. The record available at the chapter's cutoff does not establish a final post-remand remedy.

Section 137.15

Northern Gateway and Trans Mountain: First Nations and Pacific Coast Energy Development

Two Canadian proposals aimed to carry Alberta crude to the Pacific. Northern Gateway, proposed by Enbridge, would have run about 1,177 kilometres from the Edmonton area to Kitimat, British Columbia, with a marine terminal for supertankers on the north coast. The Trans Mountain expansion would twin the existing Edmonton–Burnaby pipeline, increase capacity, and increase tanker traffic from the Vancouver area through the Salish Sea.

Northern Gateway met strong opposition from many First Nations, including coastal nations concerned about tanker traffic in waters central to their fisheries and cultures, and nations in the interior concerned about river crossings. Many nations adopted declarations barring crude oil pipelines or tankers within their territories. After a joint review panel recommended approval with conditions, the federal cabinet approved the project in 2014. Several First Nations, including the Gitxaala Nation, challenged the approval, and in June 2016 the Federal Court of Appeal quashed it, finding that the Crown had failed to provide adequate consultation in the final phase. In November 2016 the federal government announced that it would not proceed with the project, and in 2019 Parliament enacted a ban on large oil tankers on British Columbia’s north coast.

Trans Mountain followed a different path. The National Energy Board recommended approval in 2016, and the federal cabinet approved the expansion that year. Tsleil-Waututh Nation and other First Nations, along with municipalities and environmental groups, challenged the approval. In August 2018, the Federal Court of Appeal quashed the approval, holding that the National Energy Board’s review had unjustifiably excluded project-related marine shipping from its scope and that the Crown’s consultation had been inadequate. In May 2018, before the decision, the federal government had agreed to buy the pipeline from Kinder Morgan, becoming the owner. It then ordered a renewed consultation process and a revised review. The cabinet re-approved the project in June 2019. Further challenges were dismissed by the Federal Court of Appeal in 2020, and the Supreme Court declined to review them. The expanded system entered commercial service on May 1, 2024, after major cost increases.

The two projects offer a comparative lesson. In both, judicial review enforced the Crown’s duty to consult, a duty established by the Supreme Court of Canada in cases such as Haida Nation v. British Columbia (2004). In neither case did courts hold that Indigenous nations held a veto. What differed was the government’s response. After Northern Gateway’s approval was quashed, the federal government in 2016 chose to reject the project; after Trans Mountain’s approval was quashed, the federal government, which then owned the pipeline, chose to repeat and strengthen consultation and re-approve it. Political and economic factors, including the government’s ownership interest, divergent regional politics, the status of the tanker-traffic issue, and the different environmental risks, contributed. Some First Nations signed benefit agreements for Trans Mountain, and some Indigenous groups have since explored acquiring an ownership share, with a federal offer announced in September 2026, not a completed sale. Others, including Tsleil-Waututh, remained opposed, and some continue protests and legal challenges. Indigenous monitors have been involved in construction and operations through the Indigenous Advisory and Monitoring Committee created for the project (Section 137.19).

The coastal dimension of the two Canadian projects is particularly important. The construction of a pipeline terminal changes the transportation system beyond the terminal itself, because the product must continue to its customers. Coastal First Nations therefore raised questions about navigation, tanker traffic, marine ecosystems, and emergency preparedness, as well as land-based construction. The 2018 Trans Mountain ruling demonstrates why defining the scope of assessment is not a neutral technical detail: if an activity generated by a project is excluded from review, the decision-maker may not fully examine effects on the people who depend on the affected waters.

In September 2026, Canada offered 128 eligible Indigenous groups in Alberta and British Columbia a collective 15 percent ownership interest in Trans Mountain Corporation, as documented in Budget 2026. The offer represented a new economic initiative rather than a completed transfer. Its consequences depend on acceptance, financing, and final transaction terms. A group considering the offer could weigh expected revenue against debt obligations and its own policies concerning fossil-fuel development. A group opposing the pipeline could maintain its territorial position irrespective of other communities' investment decisions. The episode makes clear that a program designed to broaden asset ownership is not, by itself, a resolution of the underlying consultation or marine stewardship disputes.

Section 137.16

Coastal GasLink and Wet’suwet’en Territorial Authority

The Coastal GasLink pipeline, built by a subsidiary of TC Energy, runs about 670 kilometres from near Dawson Creek in northeastern British Columbia to Kitimat, where it supplies the LNG Canada liquefied natural gas export facility. It is a natural gas pipeline, and because it lies wholly within British Columbia, it was regulated by the province, with the provincial Environmental Assessment Office granting a certificate in 2014. This jurisdictional detail is important: the project did not require the federal review that applies to interprovincial lines.

To understand the conflict, one must understand Wet’suwet’en governance. The Wet’suwet’en people of north-central British Columbia are organized in clans and houses; they are commonly described as five clans comprising thirteen house groups. Each house holds authority over a territory called the Yintah, and house chiefs hold hereditary titles, which are passed through the feast hall (balhats) governance system. Hereditary governance existed long before the federal Indian Act, which from the nineteenth century imposed elected band councils on First Nations communities, with authority largely limited to reserve land and administered under federal law (Chapter 85). The Wet’suwet’en nation includes several Indian Act bands with elected councils, and these exist alongside the hereditary system. The two institutions have different origins, legal bases, and jurisdictions: elected councils administer reserves under the Indian Act, while hereditary chiefs claim authority over the broader territory under Wet’suwet’en law.

The legal history is central. In 1984, the Gitxsan and Wet’suwet’en hereditary chiefs filed a claim to ownership and jurisdiction over territory in northwestern British Columbia. In 1991, the trial court rejected the claim, but in Delgamuukw v. British Columbia, decided in December 1997, the Supreme Court of Canada set aside the trial result, held that oral histories must be given appropriate weight as evidence, and articulated the legal test for proving Aboriginal title. The Court ordered a new trial, which did not occur; it did not itself declare title to any particular territory. The decision has therefore been described as foundational but incomplete: it established principles without conclusively settling Wet’suwet’en title, which is why disputes over jurisdiction persisted.

The Coastal GasLink conflict arose in this setting. The company signed agreements with elected First Nations councils along the route, with sources describing about twenty such agreements, including with the five Wet’suwet’en band councils. Five Wet’suwet’en hereditary chiefs and many supporters argued that the project lacked authority because it crossed Yintah without hereditary consent. The Unist’ot’en house group, which had established a camp and healing center on the route in 2009, and the Gidimt’en clan maintained checkpoints. In 2018 and 2019 the BC Supreme Court issued injunctions against interference with construction, and in January 2019 and again in February 2020, the Royal Canadian Mounted Police enforced those injunctions, arresting people at the checkpoints. A United Nations committee on racial discrimination in 2019 called for the project to be halted pending free, prior, and informed consent; Canada and British Columbia did not accept that recommendation.

In February 2020, solidarity actions spread across Canada, including rail blockades that disrupted passenger and freight service. After talks, the hereditary chiefs, the province, and the federal government reached a draft arrangement, and in May 2020 they signed a memorandum of understanding. The memorandum recognized that Wet’suwet’en rights and title are held by Wet’suwet’en houses under their governance system and established a framework for negotiations on implementation. It was not an agreement about Coastal GasLink, and it did not resolve every jurisdictional question; the project continued. Further enforcement actions occurred in November 2021. Construction was completed in 2023, and the pipeline entered service. LNG Canada, the facility it supplies, began exporting in 2025. The 2020 rights-and-title framework remained distinct from the project’s authorization; this chapter does not establish a final implementation agreement.

Differences among Wet’suwet’en people should not be oversimplified. The elected councils that signed agreements often represented communities seeking jobs, contracts, and revenue, while hereditary leaders emphasized territorial authority and environmental concerns; but some hereditary chiefs and house members have supported the project, some elected leaders have criticized it, and many people hold nuanced positions. In 2022 Indigenous groups announced arrangements to pursue equity participation. Two investment agreements closed in October and November 2026, as explained below. These facts show that participation, objection, and negotiation coexisted.

The Wet’suwet’en case also raises the question of how a state recognizes Indigenous institutions. Under the Indian Act, a band council has defined statutory powers; that framework does not settle every claim to territory under Indigenous law. A hereditary house system, in turn, has its own responsibilities and methods of validating decisions. Outsiders who treat the two systems as simply rival elections misunderstand their different foundations. A pipeline agreement made by a council may be relevant to the citizens and institutions it represents, yet the question of authority over a wider territory can remain unresolved. Equally, it is not accurate to assume that every member of a house or band holds the same opinion as any particular leader.

Economic developments after construction further complicate the picture. In October 2026, TC Energy reported closing an investment agreement with an Indigenous-led partnership representing five First Nations. On November 26 it announced a second completed transaction with a partnership representing twelve First Nations, making seventeen participating communities across the two investment entities. The participants include communities with distinct territorial relationships to the line, not a single body speaking for all Wet’suwet’en houses. These transactions followed earlier arrangements to pursue ownership; the 2026 closings must be distinguished from the earlier options. They give the investing communities a commercial position but do not confer on them the authority to consent on behalf of nonparticipating territorial authorities. Thus the same infrastructure can be a source of anticipated revenue for one Indigenous institution and a continuing jurisdictional dispute for another.

Section 137.17

Indigenous Pipeline Conflicts in Mexico and the Southern Borderlands

A continental history cannot treat pipeline conflict as only a United States and Canadian story. In Mexico, the legal framework differs. The Mexican Constitution recognizes the pluricultural composition of the nation and the rights of Indigenous peoples and communities, and Mexico ratified International Labour Organization Convention 169 in 1990, which requires consultation with Indigenous peoples on measures that may affect them. A constitutional reform in 2024, noted in Chapter 1, recognized Indigenous peoples and communities as subjects of public law. Treaty law and Aboriginal title doctrine, as developed in the United States and Canada, do not apply; the relevant concepts are collective territory, community authority, agrarian and constitutional rights, and prior consultation. This section therefore uses the Mexican framework, not American or Canadian categories.

The Yaqui, or Yoeme, of Sonora have a long history of territorial defense. Their eight traditional pueblos, Cócorit, Bácum, Tórim, Vícam, Pótam, Rahum, Huírivis, and Belem, lie along the Yaqui River. The Yaqui resisted Spanish missionary and military incursions, fought Mexican state efforts to seize their lands in the nineteenth century, and endured campaigns of deportation under the regime of Porfirio Díaz. In 1937 and 1940, under President Lázaro Cárdenas, the Mexican government issued decrees recognizing Yaqui territory and water rights, which remain a basis for Yaqui legal claims. The Yaqui people maintain their own governing structures, in which traditional authorities and pueblo assemblies make collective decisions, and the eight pueblos do not always speak with one voice.

The Guaymas–El Oro segment of the Sonora natural gas pipeline, a roughly 330-kilometre line in the Mexican northwest, crosses Yaqui territory, including a stretch in the territory claimed by the community of Loma de Bácum. The Sonora system moves natural gas, supplied from the United States, to Mexican power plants and industry. The project was developed by a private company, with state-sector involvement through the Federal Electricity Commission’s contracts, after an international public tender. Mexico’s Secretariat of Energy conducted a consultation with the Yaqui people, and it has published documentation on that process. According to the National Human Rights Commission (CNDH), the process resulted in agreements with seven of the eight pueblos, while the authorities of Loma de Bácum expressed categorical rejection; the company signed easement contracts with the seven pueblos but not with Loma de Bácum.

Loma de Bácum’s members sought an amparo, a constitutional remedy in Mexican law, and a federal judge ordered suspension of construction on their territory in 2016 until consultation requirements were met. The Mexican Supreme Court had earlier, in a separate Yaqui case about an aqueduct, found that the Yaqui were entitled to consultation. Despite the suspension, construction proceeded in places, and on October 21, 2016, a violent confrontation occurred in Loma de Bácum between people opposed to and in favor of the pipeline, in which at least one person was killed and others were injured; the events, and the criminal proceedings that followed, were contested and remain painful. The CNDH issued Recommendation 17/2018 identifying violations of prior consultation affecting the collective property and cultural identity of the Mayo people and Yaqui tribe, and recommended remedial measures. The pipeline segment was damaged in 2017 and reported to be out of service in the Yaqui territory for years. Reports in 2021 described a proposed route revision; the operator’s 2026 filing documents a subsequent contractual rerouting arrangement and the continuing interruption of service.

The case offers a method for historians: compare official consultation records, Indigenous community accounts, and judicial and human-rights documentation. Official records emphasize that a process occurred and that most pueblos agreed; community accounts emphasize that consent was not free or collective, that factions were encouraged, and that the court-ordered suspension was ignored; judicial and human-rights documents provide independent assessment of procedure. None of the three is complete alone. The case also shows that Indigenous communities are not unitary: the same nation can contain agreement and refusal, and the question of who may speak for the whole, an issue of Yaqui internal governance, cannot be resolved by outside observers. Chapter 141 treats Indigenous Mexico and the borderlands more broadly.

Mexico's constitutional and international legal setting makes the timing and method of consultation a central concern. Consultation with a community is not identical to obtaining individual easements from landholders. Collective property, community institutions, and the right to participate in decisions that affect Indigenous identity can be implicated independently of an individual contract. The National Human Rights Commission's Recommendation 17/2018 identified deficiencies in prior consultation affecting the Mayo people as well as the Yaqui tribe. Its findings concerned the conduct of public authorities and the protection of collective rights. A human-rights recommendation should be distinguished from a binding judicial injunction or a final determination of land ownership.

According to Sempra's third-quarter 2026 filing, the affected section had remained inoperable since August 2017; the company reported that legal challenges preventing repair were resolved in March 2023. In December 2025, the company and the Federal Electricity Commission amended their agreement to provide for a reroute, with costs reimbursed through a revised tariff and a contractual requirement for service to resume no later than July 2029. This is the operator's account of the project's contractual and operating position, not an independent finding that every community dispute has been resolved. The projected return date is a contractual obligation rather than an accomplished event. At the chapter's November 2026 cutoff the rerouting remained a project, not an operating replacement.

The length of the interruption provides a striking comparison with Bad River and Keystone XL. In Sonora, construction and initial commercial operation did not secure continuous use of the contested segment. The subsequent agreement for rerouting demonstrates that an existing system can remain commercially significant even when its territorial configuration must be reconsidered. Indigenous legal advocacy and local political authority were part of the circumstances shaping that result. The Mexican case belongs at the center of a continental comparison, rather than as an exception appended to Canadian and United States history.

Section 137.18

Indigenous Infrastructure Ownership and Economic Sovereignty

Alongside resistance, Indigenous nations have pursued ownership of infrastructure. Their reasons include the persistence of poverty and limited economic opportunity in many communities, the belief that if projects proceed regardless of opposition, a share of ownership allows influence and income, and the aim of building long-term revenue streams independent of annual government transfers. Instruments include pipeline equity ownership, limited partnerships holding minority stakes, revenue-sharing and benefit agreements, employment and procurement commitments, and government loan guarantees that help nations borrow to buy shares. Chapter 136 discussed Indigenous participation in extractive enterprises, and Chapter 121 treats Indigenous economic development generally; this section concentrates on infrastructure assets.

A landmark example is Athabasca Indigenous Investments (AII). In 2022, a partnership representing 23 First Nation and Métis communities in northern Alberta acquired an interest of 11.57 percent in seven Enbridge-operated pipelines serving the oil sands region, for approximately C$1.12 billion. The interest is non-operating: Enbridge continues to operate the lines. The purchase was financed by borrowing, supported by a loan guarantee from the Alberta Indigenous Opportunities Corporation, a provincial agency, and the arrangement was structured so that income from the pipelines would service the debt and later flow to the participating communities. Federal Indigenous loan-guarantee programs have since been created to support similar transactions.

The terms of this economic strategy deserve careful analysis. An ownership interest gives a right to a share of income and some governance rights as an investor. Operational control is the power to run the pipeline, which remains with the operator. Regulatory authority is the power of governments to approve, condition, or order the shutdown of a project, which remains with the Crown and its regulators. Territorial consent is the authorization of a nation with jurisdiction over a territory, which may or may not accompany investment. A nation may own shares in a pipeline across territory it does not govern, or may govern territory crossed by a pipeline in which it owns no share. For these reasons, equity ownership should not be confused with sovereignty over the pipeline.

The risks and tradeoffs are real. Pipelines carry environmental liability, though liability usually remains with the operator; investment returns depend on volumes, market prices, and long-term demand, which energy transition could reduce; debt service creates financial obligations; and community accountability raises questions about how decisions are made and how benefits are distributed. Critics, including some Indigenous critics, argue that equity ownership can bind Indigenous communities to fossil-fuel interests and divide communities. Supporters argue that participation is a pragmatic exercise of self-determination and builds capital for community priorities. Both positions are held by Indigenous people, and this textbook treats them as legitimate disagreements within Indigenous politics, not as evidence of a single Indigenous view.

A limited partnership allows several communities to invest collectively through a common vehicle, subject to the agreement governing the partnership. Pooling capital can reduce the difficulty of negotiating and administering many separate interests. The partnership may receive distributions from its investment and use them to pay expenses and service borrowing before funds become available to participating communities. This arrangement differs from a benefit agreement promising specific payments or employment in exchange for cooperation with construction. Equity may provide an enduring claim on distributions, but it also exposes the investor to the performance of the asset and the obligations of the financing arrangement.

A hypothetical example makes the distinction concrete. Suppose an Indigenous partnership invests C$100 million, of which C$80 million is borrowed. If its annual distribution is C$9 million and annual debt service is C$6 million, only C$3 million remains before other expenses and reserves. If distributions fall to C$5 million, they no longer cover that illustrative debt service. These invented figures are not the financial terms of any actual transaction in this chapter; they demonstrate why the headline purchase price and ownership percentage cannot tell a community how much cash will become available for its programs. A government loan guarantee can improve access to financing and reduce lenders' exposure, but the guarantee does not create risk-free operating income.

The 2025 Westcoast transaction demonstrates how participation may evolve between announcement and closing. Enbridge announced a C$715 million investment involving 36 First Nations and a 12.5 percent interest; a later company account described 38 participating nations and approximately C$736 million at completion. The distinction between proposed and final terms is important when comparing transactions. The Coastal GasLink closings in 2026 and the proposed Trans Mountain sale provide further examples of different stages in the development of Indigenous infrastructure investments. None of these transactions implies that the same ownership arrangement would be suitable for every community.

Section 137.19

Land Defense, Consultation, and the Institutional Politics of Infrastructure

Across the cases, Indigenous nations used a range of strategies, which can be grouped as resistance, negotiation, regulation, and ownership. Resistance includes public mobilization, civil disobedience, and blockades. Negotiation includes benefit agreements, memoranda, and settlement talks. Regulation includes participation in environmental review, monitoring, and standard-setting. Ownership includes equity stakes. Most nations and movements used several, and the choices reflected legal position, resources, political context, and internal politics.

Comparison clarifies why strategies differed. At Standing Rock, the tribe held treaty claims and standing under federal environmental statutes but did not own the land at the crossing, so it relied on litigation under administrative law and on mass mobilization. At Bad River, the Band held ownership interests in parcels and could refuse an easement renewal, so it brought a property-based trespass suit. In the Trans Mountain and Northern Gateway cases, nations used judicial review to enforce the Crown’s duty to consult, which could set aside approvals but did not confer a veto. At Coastal GasLink, the Wet’suwet’en hereditary chiefs asserted authority based on title and governance, while elected councils signed agreements; a framework for negotiating rights and title emerged, not a comprehensive resolution or a judicial decision establishing consent to the pipeline. In Mexico, Loma de Bácum used the amparo, a constitutional protection, and human-rights complaints, while the state relied on a consultation record. The differences reflect the differing legal systems.

The concept of free, prior, and informed consent, set out in the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, has become a common demand. Domestic law differs in how it treats it. The United States endorsed the Declaration in 2010 but treats it as aspirational, and federal agencies engage in government-to-government consultation without a general requirement of consent. Canada adopted legislation in 2021 committing to align federal laws with the Declaration, and British Columbia enacted similar legislation in 2019, but courts have continued to distinguish consultation from consent, outside circumstances such as established title. Mexico has international consultation obligations under ILO Convention 169, enforced through the courts. Governments and Indigenous leaders disagree about whether consent means a right to refuse.

Institutions of monitoring have developed. In Canada, the Canada Energy Regulator has supported Indigenous Advisory and Monitoring Committees associated with the Trans Mountain expansion and the Line 3 replacement program (the Canadian portion), which bring Indigenous representatives into oversight of construction and operations, and Indigenous monitors have worked in the field. Indigenous guardian programs, in which communities employ people to watch over territories, are another model, and tribal environmental departments in the United States conduct water monitoring. A critical distinction remains between participation in monitoring and authority to authorize or refuse infrastructure. Committees may advise, inspect, and report, and may influence conditions; the formal power to approve, suspend, or cancel generally remains with governments and regulators.

Protest and policing have also been historical variables. Peaceful protest and civil disobedience brought attention and sometimes costs: arrests, injunctions, and prosecutions. Governments argued that they were enforcing the law and protecting safety and property; protesters argued that the law failed to protect treaty and territorial rights, and Indigenous and human-rights organizations criticized policing practices in several cases. Media representation shaped public perception, and Indigenous media, social media, and international solidarity amplified Indigenous voices. These dynamics will recur in Chapter 143, which treats Indigenous internationalism.

The quality of consultation is partly a question of timing. If a route has already been selected, contracts signed, and capital committed, a later opportunity to comment may leave little practical room for changing the proposal. Consultation that begins earlier may identify an alternative route, a sensitive site, or an affected harvesting practice before decisions become costly to reverse. This does not mean that every early consultation will lead to agreement. It means that the scope of possible accommodation is often affected by the sequence of administrative and commercial decisions. Berger's emphasis on settling claims before construction is an earlier expression of this problem.

Historical Debate

Does Indigenous Participation in Infrastructure Regulation Strengthen Sovereignty or Preserve External Control?

The question: When Indigenous nations join monitoring committees, consultation processes, or equity partnerships, do they gain real authority, or do they legitimize decisions made by others?

Interpretation A — Participation strengthens sovereignty: Participation builds expertise, creates institutional channels for Indigenous knowledge, secures conditions on projects, and provides revenue. Self-determination, on this view, includes choosing to engage with the economy and regulatory systems rather than only resisting.
Interpretation B — Participation preserves external control: Advisory roles leave final authority with governments, and participation can be used to claim that consultation occurred without any power to refuse. Equity ownership may tie communities to the projects’ success and divide communities.
Interpretation C — Outcomes depend on design and context: Some scholars and leaders argue that the answer depends on whether participation carries binding authority, adequate funding, access to information, and a genuine possibility of refusal.

Evidence: Committee mandates and reports, regulatory conditions, benefit agreements, financial statements, tribal government statements, and judicial decisions on consultation.

Points of disagreement: Mainly interpretation and definition: what counts as authority and as consent. There is also empirical uncertainty about long-term effects.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians judge strategies, and whether present arrangements are stepping stones or limits on sovereignty.

Section 137.20

Pipelines, Territorial Authority, and the Future of Indigenous North America

The chapter began with the observation that a pipeline is a narrow object that raises broad questions. The history traced here connects territorial dispossession, treaty rights, Indigenous environmental knowledge, infrastructure development, water protection, community mobilization, Indigenous governments, legal advocacy, economic participation, and the energy transition. Indigenous land defense cannot be reduced to opposition to industrial development. It encompasses the broader capacity of Indigenous nations to protect territories, exercise political authority, set economic priorities, and shape relationships with governments and corporations.

The four responses examined in this chapter, resistance, negotiation, regulation, and ownership, are not stages in a progression and do not sort nations into types. A single nation may resist one project and invest in another. The Mackenzie Valley inquiry, Standing Rock, Line 5, Trans Mountain, Coastal GasLink, and the Yaqui case show that the available tools depended on law, geography, history, internal governance, and political opportunity. They also show that no tool, by itself, guaranteed a result.

The chapter also points forward. Fossil-fuel infrastructure is linked to greenhouse gas emissions, and the global energy transition changes the economic assumptions behind both pipelines and Indigenous investments. Northern Indigenous communities, as Chapter 138 examines, experience climate change through coastal erosion, permafrost thaw, and changing wildlife patterns, in some cases far more directly than the communities that consume the oil and gas. Indigenous nations are also involved in the new energy systems, including transmission lines, solar and wind projects, and critical-mineral development, which raise many of the same questions about consent, jurisdiction, and benefit. Chapter 136 and Chapter 138 therefore bracket this chapter: extraction, transport, and the climate consequences that follow.

A comparison of the cases reveals three broad historical patterns. The first concerns the relationship between the legal starting point and the available remedy. In Alaska and the Mackenzie Valley, outstanding claims affected the timing and terms of proposed northern development. At Bad River the Band's ownership interests supported a trespass judgment, whereas Standing Rock's litigation centered on federal administrative obligations at a crossing outside the reservation. In Mexico, community authority and constitutional consultation remedies supplied a different framework again. These are not interchangeable legal models. They show why Indigenous nations have developed different institutional strategies even when defending similar relationships to land and water.

The second pattern concerns the difference between a decision and its implementation. The Berger inquiry recommended postponement; it could not legislate a settlement itself. Keystone XL was ultimately terminated after the loss of an international authorization. Northern Gateway was abandoned after an approval was quashed, while Trans Mountain was approved again following renewed review and consultation. Dakota Access remained in operation during years of litigation, and its 2026 approval became the subject of a new challenge. The pipeline across Yaqui territory became inoperable and a reroute was later contracted. Each outcome resulted from the interaction of political choices, legal remedies, commercial commitments, and material circumstances, rather than from a single universal rule about Indigenous influence.

The third pattern concerns the growing diversity of economic institutions. Alaska Native corporations, northern pipeline equity proposals, and contemporary Canadian limited partnerships belong to different moments in a history of Indigenous economic organization. A revenue stream can strengthen a community's ability to administer programs, but an equity interest may coexist with uncertainty about environmental liability, governance, and long-term demand. Conversely, successful land defense may protect a resource on which a community's economy already depends without generating a new monetary asset. Treating only investment as economic development overlooks subsistence and harvesting; treating only resistance as sovereignty overlooks the choices made by communities seeking financial autonomy.

This approach also guards against treating the most publicized movement as representative of the whole continent. Standing Rock was a major turning point in public awareness, but the Dene hearings, Anishinaabe harvesting institutions, Wet’suwet’en houses, and Yaqui community assemblies have their own histories. Their forms of authority were not created by the pipeline controversies. They shaped the responses to those controversies and will continue to matter as energy systems change. Infrastructure sovereignty is thus less a description of a completed legal status than a historical question about the capacity of Indigenous peoples to make and implement collective choices concerning the networks that cross their homelands.

Chapter Summary

This chapter argued that pipeline conflicts are struggles over territorial authority: who may authorize infrastructure, determine routes, protect land and water, and set the terms of industrial development. It treated infrastructure as a political institution as well as a physical system, and it distinguished ownership of resources, ownership of infrastructure, and territorial jurisdiction.

Division I placed pipelines within a longer history of corridors, from railroads and telegraph lines to dams and power lines, that integrated Indigenous territories into settler-state economies. It explained how the pipeline economy developed, why energy geography routes pipelines across many Indigenous territories, and how treaty territories, reservations, trust and allotted lands, Aboriginal title, easements, and eminent domain differ. It showed why a pipeline outside a reservation can still affect treaty-protected resources, and why water is so often central.

Division II examined the Trans-Alaska Pipeline, whose construction was bound up with the settlement of Alaska Native land claims and the creation of Native corporations. It then examined the Berger Inquiry, which, through community hearings and Indigenous testimony, recommended against a northern Yukon route and for postponement of a Mackenzie Valley pipeline until claims were settled. The later Mackenzie Gas Project, with its Aboriginal Pipeline Group, showed changed conditions and options, not a universal reversal of Indigenous attitudes, and the project was not built.

The chapter also showed that Indigenous mobilization did not begin in 2016. Treaty-rights litigation, anti-mining and environmental-justice campaigns, sacred-site struggles, opposition to hydroelectric development, the founding of the Indigenous Environmental Network, and international advocacy created the vocabulary, institutions, and relationships on which later campaigns drew.

Division III treated Keystone XL as a cross-border conflict in which tribal nations joined ranchers and farmers, and which ended in termination in 2021. It then gave extended attention to Dakota Access and Standing Rock, grounding the dispute in the Fort Laramie treaties, the reduction of the Great Sioux Reservation, and Missouri River dam construction. The crossing lies outside the reservation, and the disputes rested on federal environmental review and consultation obligations as well as treaty and territorial claims, which courts did not resolve in that litigation.

The Standing Rock movement of 2016–2017 was a social and political movement with ceremony, intertribal solidarity, youth and women’s leadership, and international support, and also with contested confrontations with law enforcement. The chapter distinguished documented events, allegations, official explanations, and judicial findings. The pipeline entered service in 2017, but litigation established that the original review was inadequate; the Army Corps completed a final environmental impact statement in December 2025 and granted a conditioned easement in May 2026, and the tribe filed a new suit in October 2026.

Division IV compared conflicts across jurisdictions. Line 3 involved Anishinaabe treaty harvesting territory and wild rice, and the replacement began operating in 2021 despite opposition, with documented aquifer breaches. Line 5 involved a property-based trespass case in which the Seventh Circuit in July 2026 affirmed trespass on expired easements, held the federal common-law nuisance claim displaced, and remanded trespass remedies. Northern Gateway and Trans Mountain showed that judicial review of consultation could set aside approvals without conferring a veto, and that governments chose different responses.

The Coastal GasLink case showed the importance of distinguishing hereditary governance, Indian Act band councils, Aboriginal title, and project consent. Delgamuukw set out principles but did not declare title; the 2020 memorandum recognized that rights and title are held by Wet’suwet’en houses without resolving the pipeline dispute. The pipeline was built, two Indigenous investment agreements closed in 2026, and territorial questions remain distinct from these transactions.

Division V turned to the Yaqui, where a pipeline segment across Loma de Bácum raised questions of consultation, collective consent, and community division in Mexico’s distinct legal framework. It then examined Indigenous infrastructure ownership, including Athabasca Indigenous Investments, and distinguished ownership interest, operational control, regulatory authority, and territorial consent.

The chapter analyzed the strategies of resistance, negotiation, regulation, and ownership, and the debate about whether participation in monitoring strengthens sovereignty or preserves external control. It stressed that Indigenous monitoring committees and guardian programs expand participation but generally do not carry authority to refuse projects.

Finally, it concluded that Indigenous land defense cannot be reduced to opposition to development. It is a continuing historical struggle over the authority of Indigenous nations to determine what occurs in their homelands, and it connects to the energy transition and to the climate consequences examined in Chapter 138.

The 2026 record adds three important distinctions: a new conditional authorization can be challenged in court; a proposed equity offer is not a completed transaction; and an agreed rerouting deadline is not proof of restored service. The Bad River ruling, Trans Mountain offer, Coastal GasLink investments, and Sonora contract amendment illustrate these distinctions.

Area defined by or affected by a treaty, which may include reserved lands, ceded lands, and retained rights.
Ceded territory
Lands transferred by treaty to a settler state, within which signatory nations may have retained harvesting rights (United States and Canada).
Aboriginal title
In Canada, a constitutionally protected interest in land arising from prior occupation, defined in cases such as Delgamuukw and Tsilhqot’in.
Tribal trust land
In the United States, land held by the federal government in trust for a tribe or individual Indian.
Eminent domain
In the United States, the power to take property for public use with compensation; available to some pipelines under particular statutes.
Expropriation
In Canada, compulsory acquisition of land interests under statute with compensation.
Environmental impact assessment
Formal evaluation of a project’s likely environmental, social, and cultural effects; in the United States an environmental impact statement is the most detailed form under NEPA.
Environmental assessment
A less extensive review; in the United States it can result in a finding of no significant impact.
Cumulative effects
The combined impacts of multiple projects and activities over time on a region or resource.
Watershed
An area of land draining to a common river, lake, or sea.
Water security
Reliable access to safe water for drinking, subsistence, ceremony, and ecological health.
Spill response
Planning and capacity for detecting, containing, and cleaning up releases.
Treaty-protected harvesting
Rights to hunt, fish, or gather reserved by treaty, which may extend beyond reservation or reserve boundaries.
Sacred landscape
Land or water holding religious and cultural significance; protection in law varies widely.
Water protector
Term used by participants at Standing Rock and later movements for people acting out of responsibility to protect water.
Land defense
Organized action to protect territory, including legal, political, and direct-action strategies.
Environmental justice
Principle and movement concerning the unequal distribution of environmental burdens across communities.
Civil disobedience
Deliberate, usually nonviolent, breach of law to protest or change it.
Judicial injunction
A court order requiring or prohibiting specific actions; used both to halt and to protect construction.
Administrative review
Court review of an agency’s decision for compliance with procedure and law.
Duty to consult
In Canada, the Crown’s obligation to consult and, where appropriate, accommodate Indigenous peoples when its decisions may affect asserted or established rights.
Free, prior, and informed consent
Standard in the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples; its legal force differs among the United States, Canada, and Mexico.
Hereditary governance
Indigenous governing systems in which authority is held through lineage and titles, such as Wet’suwet’en houses.
Band council
In Canada, an elected governing body of an Indian Act band, with authority mainly over reserve matters.
Intertribal solidarity
Cooperation among different Indigenous nations in common causes.
Infrastructure equity ownership
Ownership of a share in infrastructure assets, giving income and investor rights but not necessarily control.
Indigenous environmental monitoring
Oversight of projects by Indigenous monitors or committees, which may be advisory or have defined powers.
Energy transition
The shift of energy systems away from fossil fuels toward lower-emission sources.

Chapter Reflection

Why does a chapter about steel pipes belong in a history of Indigenous North America? Because infrastructure is where abstract questions of sovereignty become concrete. A line on a map decides whose land is crossed, whose water is at risk, and whose consent is sought. The histories in this chapter show that Indigenous nations have repeatedly insisted that such decisions are not purely technical.

The chapter reveals continuities across a century and a half. The railroad that crossed a treaty territory, the dam that flooded a river valley, and the pipeline that passes beneath a reservoir are different technologies, but communities experience them as part of one pattern. Equally, Indigenous responses have drawn on older traditions of council, treaty-making, ceremony, and law, and on newer tools such as environmental litigation, international advocacy, and corporate partnership.

The chapter also shows how limited formal outcomes can be. Courts often enforce procedure, governments often retain the final decision, and projects sometimes proceed despite sustained opposition. Yet outcomes cannot be measured only by whether a pipeline was stopped. Inquiries, litigation, and mobilization changed regulatory practice, public understanding, and Indigenous political networks, and in some cases led to negotiation of rights and title or to new forms of economic participation.

These developments shaped later chapters in two directions. Chapter 138 turns to climate transformation, where fossil-fuel infrastructure, emissions, and the burdens borne by northern Indigenous communities meet. Chapter 139 turns to Indigenous governments themselves, which must decide how to respond to proposals, manage revenue, and defend territory.

The enduring lesson is that Indigenous land defense is not simply a movement against pipelines. It is a continuing historical struggle over the authority of Indigenous nations to determine what happens within their homelands, and over the institutions through which that authority is recognized, contested, and exercised.
