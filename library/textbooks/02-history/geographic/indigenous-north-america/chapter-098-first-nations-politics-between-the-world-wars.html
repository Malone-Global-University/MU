First Nations Politics Between the World Wars

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

Between 1918 and 1939, First Nations in Canada built conventions, councils, petitions, delegations, and associations to defend treaties, land, and self-government, while the federal department that administered their affairs held legal powers to obstruct that very organizing.

In December 1918, within weeks of the Armistice, Frederick Ogilvie Loft, a Mohawk veteran of the Canadian Expeditionary Force from Six Nations of the Grand River, began organizing what became the League of Indians of Canada. Nine months later, in September 1919, delegates met at Sault Ste. Marie for the League's first major convention. These are two events, not one, and distinguishing them matters: the League began as correspondence and planning before it became a visible national gathering. That sequence illustrates how interwar First Nations politics typically worked, through letters carried between reserves, meetings that depended on borrowed money and the goodwill of host communities, and constant negotiation with an administration that could refuse permission, withhold funds, or declare a gathering irregular.

The chapter's subject is the organized political action of First Nations in Canada across two decades. Its principal case studies are the League of Indians of Canada and its western offshoots; the Allied Tribes of British Columbia and their campaign for recognition of Aboriginal title; the Cayuga chief Deskaheh's attempt to carry the case of the Six Nations to the League of Nations at Geneva; the federal replacement of the Six Nations hereditary council with an elected council in 1924; and the Prairie treaty organizations that culminated in the founding of the Indian Association of Alberta in 1939. Around these cases the chapter places treaty-making in Ontario and the Mackenzie Valley, the transfer of natural resources to the Prairie provinces in 1930, the prosecution of the potlatch, and a comparative study of Métis organizing in Alberta.

The chapter does not simply repeat the reform campaigns introduced in Chapter 95. That chapter examined the broad crisis of assimilation policy and the reform movements that criticized it. This chapter asks narrower and more institutional questions: who led these organizations, who joined them, how they raised money, what arguments they made, where their members disagreed, and what the Canadian state did in response. It also treats the military service examined in Chapter 94 only for its postwar political consequences.

Its central argument is that First Nations leaders and communities developed increasingly coordinated strategies to defend treaties, territorial rights, traditional governments, and community autonomy. They confronted a federal administration that claimed protective authority while frequently restricting Indigenous organization, access to legal remedies, and control over internal affairs. Despite legal suppression, financial obstacles, and regional divisions, Indigenous political institutions persisted. They did not win most of what they sought between the wars. What they preserved was the capacity to keep seeking it, and that capacity formed an organizational foundation for later national movements.

The evidence is uneven. Government records, especially the records of the Department of Indian Affairs held at Library and Archives Canada, are voluminous and shaped by officials who often regarded Indigenous organizers with suspicion. Published parliamentary proceedings preserve testimony, but the Indigenous speakers were examined by committees whose assumptions framed the questions. Indigenous voices survive in petitions, convention resolutions, letters, pamphlets, speeches, later memoirs, and oral histories, and historians increasingly read official records against these. Much of the evidence about internal disagreement, such as that within Six Nations, reaches us through the partisan documents of each faction.

Terminology also requires care. The statutory words "Indian," "band," "status," and "enfranchisement" appear in this chapter because they were the legal categories that governed daily life, not because they describe Indigenous peoples' own political identities. Where possible the chapter uses specific national names: Haudenosaunee, Cree, Haida, Squamish, Nisga'a, Kwakwaka'wakw, Dene. It distinguishes First Nations organizations from Métis organizations, whose histories, claims, and relations with governments differed.

The chapter belongs to Part XVII, "Rebuilding Indigenous Political Institutions." Chapter 97 turns to the United States and the Indian Reorganization Act, a federal initiative that reshaped tribal governments from above. The Canadian interwar story differs in important respects: no equivalent federal reform arrived, and the most prominent Indigenous initiatives came from Indigenous leaders themselves, often against official resistance. Comparing the two helps students see how settler states with similar goals developed different instruments of control and how Indigenous nations answered each.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish among treaty relationships, hereditary and other traditional governments, statutory band councils, and the Department of Indian Affairs' administrative system as of 1918.
  2. Explain how First Nations veterans' postwar advocacy turned military service into a political argument about fair treatment and treaty obligations.
  3. Trace the institutional history of the League of Indians of Canada from December 1918 through the mid-1920s, distinguishing its organization from its first major convention in September 1919.
  4. Analyze the methods, finances, and geographic obstacles that shaped national and regional Indigenous organizations.
  5. Explain why Aboriginal title in British Columbia was a distinct legal and political question that could not be reduced to reserve administration.
  6. Assess the Allied Tribes of British Columbia, the Special Joint Committee of 1927, and the relationship between Indigenous leaders and non-Indigenous advisers.
  7. Analyze the March 31, 1927 Indian Act amendment, distinguishing its text and legal scope from the broader political consequences attributed to it.
  8. Compare Deskaheh's international appeal with domestic reform strategies, and evaluate the roles of the British, Canadian, and Haudenosaunee actors.
  9. Evaluate the Six Nations government dispute of 1924, accounting for both internal disagreement and federal imposition.
  10. Interpret the Williams Treaties, Treaty 11, and the Natural Resources Transfer Agreements as contested agreements whose meanings differed among signatories and governments.
  11. Compare First Nations treaty organizations with Métis political associations in Alberta while preserving their distinct identities and legal strategies.
  12. Assess competing interpretations of interwar First Nations movements and the capacity for political action preserved by 1939.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did First Nations communities exercise political authority within a system designed to subordinate many of their decisions to federal officials?
  2. Why did wartime service become a political argument, and what limits did that argument encounter?
  3. What made national organization so difficult for First Nations between the wars, and what did regional organization make possible?
  4. Why could the claim to Aboriginal title in British Columbia not be satisfied by better reserve administration?
  5. What does the 1927 amendment reveal about the use of law to restrict political opposition rather than resolve its grievances?
  6. How did Deskaheh's international appeal differ in its premises from petitions addressed to Parliament?
  7. How did divisions within Six Nations shape, and how were they used by, federal administrators?
  8. Why did the same treaty or agreement mean different things to Indigenous signatories and to governments?
  9. Why did First Nations and Métis organizations in Alberta develop different legal strategies despite shared experiences of dispossession?
  10. Is the interwar record better described as a struggle for administrative reform, for treaty sovereignty, or for strategic adaptation, and what evidence would settle the question?

Historical Context

Two sets of earlier developments frame this chapter. The first is the treaty and statutory system that organized relations between First Nations and the Crown in Canada. The British Crown and Indigenous nations negotiated treaties from the colonial period onward, most extensively after Confederation in 1867. Volume VII (Chapter 82) examines the numbered treaties of the Prairies and the North-West; others, such as the Robinson treaties and Treaty 9, covered much of Ontario. These were political agreements between nations, and many First Nations understood them as continuing relationships involving obligations on both sides. The Canadian state, by contrast, usually treated them as land cessions with defined terms, administered through the Indian Act.

The Indian Act, first consolidated in 1876 and examined in Chapter 85, defined who was a legal "Indian," established the reserve system, created statutory band councils in place of other forms of government where the Department chose, and gave the Superintendent General, a cabinet minister, extensive powers over land, money, schooling, and conduct. The Department's senior civil servant for most of this chapter's early period was Duncan Campbell Scott, Deputy Superintendent General from 1913 to 1932, whose views on assimilation shaped policy and who regarded independent Indigenous organizations with hostility. Local Indian agents, who supervised reserves, controlled permits, rations, and travel, and whose reports determined much of what Ottawa knew.

The second context is the wartime and postwar period examined in Chapters 94 and 95. Roughly four thousand status Indians are generally estimated to have enlisted in the Canadian forces, though the true number is difficult to establish. Their service, combined with the broader wartime rhetoric of democracy and self-determination, encouraged new organizing. At the same time, the postwar federal government pursued policies of enfranchisement, consolidation of land, and increased control. This chapter studies the collision between these tendencies.

Regional contexts differed sharply. In Ontario, long-established bodies such as the Six Nations council and the Grand General Indian Council (whose roots go back to the nineteenth century) interacted with a mix of treaty and non-treaty history. On the Prairies, treaty annuities, farming instruction, and the memory of the 1885 North-West Resistance shaped relationships, and Indian agents enforced pass restrictions and ceremonial limits. In British Columbia, with the exception of the Douglas treaties on Vancouver Island and Treaty 8 in the northeast, most First Nations had never signed treaties, so the central issue was land that had not been ceded. In the Mackenzie Valley, treaty-making was still under way in 1921. No account of "Canadian First Nations" can treat these regions as interchangeable.

Section 98.1

First Nations Politics in Canada, 1918: Treaty Nations and Federal Control

When the First World War ended, First Nations in Canada lived under overlapping systems of authority that did not fit together neatly. The first was the treaty relationship. Where treaties had been made, from the Robinson treaties of 1850 to the numbered treaties of the 1870s and after, communities held that they had entered a continuing relationship with the Crown, often sealed with ceremony, with oral commitments remembered by elders alongside the written text. Nations without treaties, as in most of British Columbia, held that their relationship with the Crown remained undefined and that their title to the land had not been surrendered.

The second system was Indigenous governance itself. Many communities retained hereditary chiefs, clan mothers, councils of elders, societies, and other institutions that predated the Indian Act. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy Council at Grand River is the best-known example in this chapter, but similar structures existed among Coast Salish, Nisga'a, Kwakwaka'wakw, Blackfoot, and Cree communities, often operating alongside, beneath, or in resistance to statutory arrangements. Chapter 26 describes the Confederacy's political philosophy; this chapter follows its confrontation with the Canadian state.

The third system was statutory. The Indian Act authorized the Governor in Council to replace traditional leadership with elected band councils on the Department's initiative and defined their powers narrowly. Councillors could pass certain by-laws, but major decisions on land surrenders, finances, and membership required departmental approval. The Act also gave the Superintendent General broad powers over reserve land, trust funds, enfranchisement, schooling, and the regulation of ceremonies, and it was repeatedly amended. A fourth, informal layer was the Indian agent, a departmental employee posted to a district. Agents issued permits for selling produce or cattle, managed annuity payments, reported on community politics, and could hinder travel by council delegates. In many communities the agent was the daily face of federal authority.

Historians therefore need to avoid two simplifications. One treats First Nations as passive wards with no political life outside the Act. The other treats the Act as irrelevant to communities that continued to govern themselves by older law. Communities actually moved among these systems, using statutory forums when useful, relying on hereditary or customary authority when legitimate, and combining both when they could. Councils elected under the Act sometimes became vehicles for resistance, and hereditary councils sometimes sought recognition under federal law. The central historical question is how authority was maintained, shared, or contested within a framework designed to subordinate it.

Regional contexts shaped the possibilities. In southern Ontario, communities such as Six Nations, the Mississaugas, and the Chippewas lived in close proximity to expanding settlement and had long petitioned Ottawa. On the Prairies, Cree, Saulteaux, Assiniboine, Blackfoot, and others had signed treaties in the 1870s, and treaty days, annuity payments, and farming policy structured relations with agents. In British Columbia, the absence of treaties made land the dominant issue. In the Mackenzie Valley and other northern districts, treaty-making was incomplete and federal administration was thin. First Nations organizers had to speak across these differences, and their failures to unify nationally often reflect real differences in legal position rather than lack of effort.

The difference between a political claim and a request for administrative relief is particularly important. A request that an agent distribute rations fairly accepted, at least provisionally, the official's authority to distribute them; a claim that a treaty nation should decide how its resources were used challenged the source of that authority. In practice the same council might pursue both courses at once. Treaties were recalled as spoken agreements witnessed by several communities, while Ottawa ordinarily preserved a written text and departmental interpretation. This difference did not make Indigenous understandings merely local custom. It meant that governments were attempting to govern a relationship for which the parties retained different accounts of their respective obligations.

The federal department's files therefore cannot be read as a neutral inventory of Indigenous political life. Agents reported the names of people who approached an office, the requests they regarded as reasonable, and meetings they considered troublesome. Councils also operated through family discussion, ceremonies, visits to neighbouring communities, and the transmission of obligations between generations. A historical account that begins only with official correspondence will tend to exaggerate Ottawa's initiative and underestimate the organization that made a petition or delegation possible. The political movements examined below connected those less visible local institutions to provincial and national forums.

Historical Vocabulary
Treaty Sovereignty and Crown Obligations

Definition. "Treaty sovereignty" is a modern shorthand for the position that treaties were agreements between political communities that retained their own governing authority, and that the Crown's obligations under them were continuing. "Crown obligations" refers to the specific commitments, such as annuities, reserves, schools, farming assistance, and harvesting rights, that treaty-making nations understood the Crown to have undertaken.

Historical context. Interwar leaders did not necessarily use the phrase "treaty sovereignty." They argued from the treaty councils themselves, from promises remembered by elders, and from earlier relationships such as the Covenant Chain (Chapter 56) and the Haldimand grant to the Six Nations. Federal officials generally read treaties as land cessions governed by Parliament's authority over "Indians."

Why it matters. Whether a petition sought better administration of a treaty or recognition of enduring political authority could be a decisive question, and §98.19 returns to it.

Potential misunderstanding. The two positions were not always opposed. A leader might ask for better schools under a treaty while holding that the treaty bound the Crown as one nation to another.

Section 98.2

First Nations Veterans, Unequal Benefits, and the Demand for Political Recognition

Chapter 94 examines Indigenous participation in the war. Here the focus is on what veterans did with that service after 1918. Returning soldiers reentered communities that had also contributed in other ways, through donations to patriotic funds, labor, and the sale of produce, and they returned to an administration that, with few exceptions, continued to treat them as wards of the Crown. The disjunction between wartime recognition and peacetime status was a recurring theme in their letters and meetings.

The clearest grievances concerned benefits. Non-Indigenous veterans could draw on settlement programs administered by the Soldier Settlement Board, established in 1917, which offered land and loans. Status Indian veterans were generally directed through the Department of Indian Affairs, which administered reserve land under the Indian Act. Historians have documented that Indigenous veterans' access to land, loans, and other forms of assistance was substantially less favorable than that of other veterans, and that the Department's management of reserve land sometimes ran against veterans' interests. Reserve land itself was also affected by policies of the period: amendments to the Indian Act in 1918 enlarged the Department's power to lease uncultivated reserve lands to non-Indigenous farmers, and communities objected that land was being lost while their own veterans struggled to establish farms.

Veterans' political arguments combined several elements. First, they invoked service as evidence of loyalty and entitlement to equal treatment. Second, some linked that loyalty to the treaty relationship, arguing that Indigenous nations had fought in the Crown's cause as allies rather than as subjects, an argument with deep roots in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century alliance traditions. Third, they pointed to the contradiction of fighting overseas for freedoms they could not exercise at home, including the federal franchise, which status Indians could obtain only by giving up legal status. Wartime legislation had briefly extended a limited vote to some Indigenous soldiers, but the postwar franchise settled back into the pre-war pattern; Chapter 93 examines the broader politics of citizenship.

The evidence for these arguments comes from veterans' correspondence with the Department and with members of Parliament, from reports of community meetings recorded by agents, and from convention resolutions. These sources have limits. Letters passed through agents, who sometimes forwarded, summarized, or withheld them. Agents' reports often framed veterans' complaints as disloyalty or agitation. Veterans' voices appear most reliably when they wrote directly or spoke at gatherings recorded by sympathetic observers. Even so, the pattern is consistent enough to show that military service widened political demands: veterans such as Loft provided leadership, organizational experience, and credibility with some officials and public audiences.

It would be a mistake, however, to reduce interwar organizing to veterans alone. Many leaders, including hereditary chiefs and elders whose political authority predated the war, saw no need to ground their claims in military service. Veterans' arguments became one strand of a wider effort, and the next section follows the most ambitious attempt to unite those strands in a national organization.

The structure of the settlement programs helps explain why apparently similar benefits could have different results. A returned non-Indigenous soldier might acquire a new holding through a loan and purchase arrangement. A status First Nations veteran could face a land base already held under collective restrictions, the necessity of departmental approval, and disputes over whether reserve lands should be used at all for settlement purposes. The comparison must distinguish the rules authorizing benefits from decisions by local officers and the actual number of applicants who received them. It is not enough to say that an Indigenous veteran was entitled to assistance; the historian must ask whether the assistance could be accessed on workable terms.

Military service also furnished a language through which organizers appealed to a public otherwise accustomed to describing First Nations as dependent wards. Loft's uniform and wartime experience strengthened his claim to be heard by people who might otherwise ignore a First Nations petition. Yet the emphasis on service created its own limitation: treaty promises were not contingent on having worn a uniform, and women, elders, and people excluded from military service had no lesser claim to land or political authority. Veterans' activism mattered most when it strengthened rather than displaced these wider collective arguments.

Section 98.3

Frederick Ogilvie Loft and the League of Indians of Canada, 1918–1924

Frederick Ogilvie Loft, known by the Mohawk name Onondeyoh, was born at Six Nations of the Grand River in 1861 and, in the First World War, served as an officer in the Canadian Expeditionary Force; his career is documented in the Dictionary of Canadian Biography. He returned with a veteran's standing and connections across Six Nations and beyond, and he began in December 1918 to organize a league of Indians that would speak nationally.

The League's first formal public step was a convention held at Sault Ste. Marie in September 1919. That date should be kept distinct from December 1918: between the two lay months of correspondence, local meetings, and fundraising appeals. The convention brought delegates from communities in Ontario and beyond to discuss shared concerns and to adopt an organizational structure. The League's stated aims, as reflected in its resolutions and Loft's correspondence, included protection of treaty rights, improvement of schooling, opposition to discriminatory or arbitrary administration, redress of veterans' grievances, and the creation of a means of cooperation across First Nations. Further conventions followed in Manitoba, Saskatchewan, and Alberta between 1919 and 1922, according to the Dictionary of Canadian Biography, each hosted by a local community that took on the cost and labor of reception.

The League's methods were simple and demanding. Loft wrote circulars to chiefs and councils, explaining the League's purposes, inviting delegates, and asking for contributions. Membership dues and donations paid for travel, printing, and postage. Conventions provided a public forum and a place to pass resolutions that were then forwarded to the Department and to Parliament. Delegates also used gatherings to compare conditions, discover that a grievance on one reserve was shared on another, and sometimes to assert that the Department's treatment violated treaties. Resolutions asked for changes in schooling, in the administration of reserve funds, and in the powers of agents.

The League faced severe obstacles. The first was geography and cost. Canada's reserves were scattered across thousands of kilometres; many communities had little cash, and travel to conventions required both money and permission from agents, who might discourage or delay it. The second was official hostility. Senior Department officials, notably Scott, viewed the League as unrepresentative and its leaders as agitators, and the Department sometimes declined to recognize its resolutions or discouraged agents from cooperating. The third was legal and political. The 1920 amendments to the Indian Act, which strengthened compulsory school attendance and introduced compulsory enfranchisement provisions that were repealed in 1922 and, in modified form, restored in 1933, were among the measures Loft and the League opposed, and the Department's responses showed how little formal weight organized objections carried.

Internally the League also faced the question of representation. Treaty nations in the West, communities in Ontario, and others did not share a single legal position, and local leaders were wary of joining a body led from Ontario. As the next section shows, western organizers soon developed their own structures. Loft's national organization weakened in the early and mid-1920s as funds dwindled and officials refused cooperation, although Loft himself remained active in advocacy until his death in 1934. The League's significance lies less in lasting institutional strength than in demonstrating that a national forum was possible and in training a generation of organizers.

Loft's circumstances illuminate both the League's possibilities and its vulnerabilities. He had experience dealing with English-language institutions and understood the practical value of formal correspondence, public argument, and newspaper attention. Yet access to metropolitan networks did not guarantee that Ottawa would treat him as the representative of many separate nations. Departmental objections to his authority could become self-reinforcing: officials denied an organization recognition because they doubted its membership, while withholding the cooperation and information that might have enabled the organization to demonstrate a broad mandate. That tension made the League's organizational paperwork politically consequential rather than merely clerical.

The surviving November 1919 circular provides one entry into this process but should not stand in for a membership roll. To evaluate reach, students must compare the circular with minutes of local councils, correspondence replying to the appeal, evidence of contributions, and convention attendance. The distinction between intending a national body and maintaining one is especially important in an era of expensive railway travel and inconsistent communications between remote communities. Loft's achievement lay in creating a framework of shared political questions that later western associations could adapt, even without a continuous national secretariat.

Cause and Effect

Why Interwar Indigenous Political Organizations Expanded

Long-term causes

Treaty promises perceived as unfulfilled; accumulated grievances over land, schooling, and agents; older traditions of councils, petitions, and delegations to the Crown, including delegations to London.

Short-term causes

Wartime service and veterans' return; wartime and postwar leasing of reserve land; the 1920 amendments; growth of literacy, rail travel, and mail that made correspondence networks possible.

The end of the war in November 1918 and Loft's circular organizing in December 1918, followed by the 1919 convention.

Conventions across several provinces; new regional organizations; resolutions to Ottawa; official countermeasures and suspicion.

Section 98.4

Political Organization on the Prairies: Treaty Advocacy and Regional Networks

On the Prairies, the League's conventions encountered a political tradition already shaped by treaty-making. Cree, Saulteaux, Assiniboine, Blackfoot, and other nations had negotiated the numbered treaties in the 1870s, and their leaders remembered the councils and promises. Since then they had lived under the farming policy, the pass system, and agents' supervision examined in earlier chapters. After 1885, many communities had also seen their leaders imprisoned or disciplined, a memory that made open organizing risky.

Western organizers did not simply join Loft's League. They adapted the model to their circumstances, forming western branches and then regional organizations, often under local leaders. Among those associated with western activity was Edward Ahenakew, a Plains Cree Anglican clergyman and writer from Saskatchewan whose later writings preserve Cree perspectives on the period. Other prominent western figures included Joseph Dreaver and, later, John Tootoosis, who had helped organize the League of Indians of Western Canada at Thunderchild in 1921 and whose later work connected interwar networks to Saskatchewan organizations. The western network is best understood as an independent development rather than an extension of Loft's career: it drew on treaty memory, local leadership, church connections, and kinship ties among reserves.

Their concerns were practical and treaty-grounded. They asked for fulfillment of treaty promises regarding schooling and agricultural assistance; protection of reserve boundaries and resources; fair conduct by agents; and the maintenance of hunting and fishing rights. The loss of reserve land through surrenders and leases, the effects of restrictive permits on Indigenous farmers' ability to sell produce, and the condition of schools were common agenda items. Western leaders also raised the treatment of veterans and the effect of the Department's decisions on local economies.

Gatherings were the political infrastructure. Treaty-day meetings, Sun Dance gatherings (where they continued despite restrictions), church assemblies, and conventions allowed representatives from different reserves to meet. They compared agents' conduct, shared information about the Department's decisions, and drafted common petitions. A resolution carried by delegates from several treaty areas carried more weight than a single band's request, and it also protected individual leaders, who could point to collective support. Because agents controlled permits and travel, attendance at such gatherings sometimes required evasion, persuasion, or the assistance of sympathetic clergy or local settlers.

The costs of keeping these networks alive were considerable, and they fell on communities that often lacked cash. Fundraising was perpetual. The Department's suspicion remained, and the western organizations' fortunes rose and fell with local leadership and economic conditions, especially during the Depression. Yet the network persisted. As §98.15 shows, it was the foundation on which the Indian Association of Alberta emerged in 1939 through organizing by John Callihoo and Malcolm Norris, among others.

John Tootoosis is an especially useful corrective to a narrative that begins Prairie organization only in the 1930s. University of Saskatchewan historical material places him among the organizers of the League of Indians of Western Canada at Thunderchild in 1921. His subsequent prominence grew out of an existing interwar network, not a wholly new political generation. Edward Ahenakew likewise combined clerical and educational work with close knowledge of Cree community affairs, while the careers of other leaders followed distinct local paths. The chapter should resist describing every leader as a branch official of Loft's League, because western organizing developed its own constituencies and priorities.

An organizational convention was both an event and an exercise in Indigenous diplomacy. Hosts arranged accommodation, food, and places for discussion; visitors carried concerns that had been debated at home; speakers sought a formulation that representatives from several treaty areas could endorse. A unanimous resolution was not necessarily evidence that every participant agreed on all questions, and a small attendance list did not mean the underlying issues lacked support. Those limitations make meeting records valuable but incomplete. Later associations inherited ways of circulating information and securing legitimacy from these gatherings, demonstrating continuity even when institutional names changed.

Section 98.5

British Columbia's Land Question: Unceded Territory and Indigenous Political Claims

British Columbia occupies a distinctive place in this history. Across most of the province, no treaties were made. The principal exceptions were the fourteen agreements negotiated by James Douglas on southern Vancouver Island in the early 1850s and Treaty 8, which reached the northeastern corner in 1899. Elsewhere, including the Lower Mainland, the Interior, the northern coast, and Haida Gwaii, Indigenous nations such as the Nisga'a, Coast Salish peoples, Nlaka'pamux, Secwepemc, Haida, Tsimshian, and many others continued to occupy and govern territory that no agreement had surrendered. When British Columbia entered Confederation in 1871, responsibility for "Indians and lands reserved for the Indians" passed to Ottawa, but the provincial government controlled the Crown land base and followed policies, associated with Chief Commissioner Joseph Trutch, that denied or minimized Indigenous title and kept reserves small.

Indigenous nations contested these policies from the beginning, and the interwar campaign was the continuation of decades of organizing. The Nisga'a Land Committee, active from about 1907, sent petitions and delegations to Ottawa and London, and in 1913 the Nisga'a presented a petition asserting title to their territory in the Nass Valley. Interior and Coast Salish leaders also petitioned, and organizations such as the Indian Rights Association, formed in the 1910s with the assistance of lawyers and clergy, worked to coordinate claims across the province. These movements often appealed to the Royal Proclamation of 1763, which many First Nations read as recognizing Indigenous interests in land not yet ceded, and to British legal principles that treaties, not unilateral assertion, were the proper means of acquiring land.

The Royal Commission on Indian Affairs for the Province of British Columbia, known as the McKenna–McBride Commission, sat from 1912 to 1916. Its mandate was to adjust the size and location of reserves, not to examine title. Commissioners visited communities and heard testimony, and many Indigenous witnesses used the opportunity to assert that the land had never been surrendered. The commission's work, and subsequent federal and provincial legislation implementing its recommendations, reduced some reserves and added others without Indigenous consent on title. This outcome sharpened the sense that a coordinated effort was needed.

It is important to understand why the claim for title could not be reduced to better reserve administration. A reserve is a parcel of land set aside under statute and administered by the Department; title, in Indigenous understanding, concerned the entire territory in which a nation hunted, fished, gathered, and governed. Recognition of title would imply negotiation, compensation, and shared jurisdiction, whereas reserve reform would merely modify the existing relationship. Federal and provincial governments disagreed with one another, and with Indigenous nations, over whether title had ever existed or had been extinguished by the assertion of Crown sovereignty. That legal argument was not settled in the interwar period and, as Chapter 117 shows, remained live for decades.

The distinction between reserves and Aboriginal title is crucial to interpreting British Columbia politics. Reserve commissions selected comparatively limited parcels for village or livelihood use, whereas an assertion of title concerned the legal and political relationship to a much larger traditional territory. A government could increase a reserve allotment without conceding that an Indigenous nation had retained an underlying interest in the land outside it. For communities whose economies relied upon dispersed fishing stations, seasonal gathering sites, hunting grounds, and travel routes, a reserve-focused settlement could leave the central grievance untouched. This is why the Indian land question remained politically distinct from the administration of particular reserve boundaries.

Political organizing grew through earlier delegations as well as through the legal language of the twentieth century. Petitioners assembled local histories of occupation, references to Crown assurances, and records of provincial decisions concerning land. These materials could be translated into arguments for a court reference, but they also expressed a claim to be treated as peoples entitled to negotiate. Neither the absence of a written nineteenth-century treaty nor an adverse provincial view should be treated as proof that Indigenous political relationships to territory ceased. The Allied Tribes sought a forum capable of hearing precisely that disputed question.

Historical Vocabulary
Aboriginal Title and Unceded Territory

Definition. "Aboriginal title" refers to Indigenous peoples' interest in land arising from their prior and continuing occupation and use. "Unceded" describes territory that has not been surrendered by treaty or other agreement.

Historical context. The interwar generation usually spoke of "Indian title" or "the land question." The concept had no settled definition in Canadian law in the 1920s, and governments disputed its existence. Indigenous leaders grounded their claims in occupation, in their own laws, and in the Royal Proclamation of 1763 and later colonial statements.

Why it matters. The Allied Tribes' campaign and the 1927 restrictions cannot be understood unless one sees title as a claim about territory and governance, not a request for a better administered reserve.

Potential misunderstanding. Modern court decisions on Aboriginal title came later (Chapters 117 and 119). The interwar petitioners did not have those precedents, and no court heard their claim.

Section 98.6

The Allied Tribes of British Columbia: Peter Kelly, Andrew Paull, and James Teit

The Allied Tribes of British Columbia were formed in 1916 as a coalition of Indigenous organizations and communities, bringing together interior and coastal nations that had previously pursued separate campaigns. Their purpose was to present a unified claim to Aboriginal title and to seek a fair settlement through negotiation or adjudication. The Union of BC Indian Chiefs preserves a detailed chronology of the land-title campaign that situates the Allied Tribes within a longer sequence of petitions from the 1860s onward.

Three figures are especially associated with its work. Peter Kelly, a Haida from Haida Gwaii and a Methodist minister, served as chair and was a widely respected speaker. Andrew Paull, a Squamish leader from North Vancouver, served as secretary and spokesman in the 1920s and became one of the most visible Indigenous political organizers of his generation. James Teit, born in Scotland, lived at Spences Bridge among the Nlaka'pamux, married Lucy Antko, a Nlaka'pamux woman, and worked as an ethnologist with long-standing connections to interior communities. Teit served as an organizer and secretary for the interior nations' campaign, and his language skills and knowledge of communities made him an important intermediary. His role was one of service to Indigenous-led organizations: he assisted with correspondence, arranged meetings, and helped prepare documents. He died in October 1922, before the climactic events of 1926 and 1927, and the Allied Tribes' later leadership rested primarily on Kelly and Paull.

The Allied Tribes depended on meetings, delegations, and fundraising. Assemblies drew representatives from communities across the province, and delegations traveled to Ottawa, sometimes after months of collecting contributions from households that had little cash. Written statements set out the claim: that the nations had owned and governed their territories, that British principles required agreement before land could be taken, and that a fair settlement was owed. The organization also employed legal advisers; Arthur E. O'Meara, a former Anglican missionary who became a lawyer, assisted in presenting the claim to Ottawa and to British authorities. Indigenous leaders and their lawyers did not always agree on tactics, and the Allied Tribes' strategy, which prioritized obtaining a judicial determination of title through a reference to the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council, reflected both Indigenous priorities and legal advice about forums.

Unity was both a strength and a challenge. A single, province-wide claim was strategically powerful because it showed that title was a shared concern, not a local grievance, and it made divide-and-conquer responses harder. But the nations differed in territory, history, and political institutions: the Nisga'a, who had long pressed their own petition, did not always see their interests as identical to those of southern interior nations; coastal nations with potlatch traditions faced different pressures than interior nations. Some communities withdrew or were sceptical about the terms of the Allied Tribes' petition, and the dependence on outside advisers raised questions about how decisions were made. Maintaining coordination across such distances, with no guaranteed income, was an ongoing struggle.

The relationship between Indigenous leaders and non-Indigenous advocates should be analyzed carefully. Lawyers and missionaries gave access to legal forums and political connections that were otherwise closed, but they also mediated what governments heard and could shape strategy. Historians have documented both aspects. The Allied Tribes' political leadership remained Indigenous, and the claim itself expressed long-standing Indigenous positions, but the structure of the campaign made it dependent on resources and channels that the government could, and ultimately did, restrict.

The coalition's breadth was its strength and a continuing organizational problem. Coast and interior nations did not necessarily share one land-tenure system or one history of contact with colonial authorities. Coordination therefore demanded agreement on a sufficiently general claim: that the Crown had not secured title by treaty over much of the province and that unilateral reserve administration did not discharge that obligation. Local leaders could endorse this position while disagreeing about which legal route was most promising or which evidence should be emphasized. The public petition concealed a considerable amount of political negotiation among the communities that supported it.

Peter Kelly, Andrew Paull, and James Teit occupied different positions within this work. Kelly and Paull spoke as Indigenous leaders engaged in a struggle over their peoples' rights; Teit, an ethnographer and ally who served in organizational and secretarial roles, supplied particular writing and liaison capacities. A biography-centered account can inadvertently place outside advisers at the centre because their correspondence survives in accessible repositories. The test of historical leadership is who defined the claim, authorized the delegation, and bore the political consequences of its success or failure. Attention to these questions restores the Allied Tribes as a coalition of Indigenous governments rather than an advocacy campaign conducted for them.

Section 98.7

The Allied Tribes' Petition and the Parliamentary Inquiry of 1927

In the mid-1920s, after years of lobbying, the Allied Tribes obtained a hearing before a Special Joint Committee of the Senate and the House of Commons appointed to examine their claims. They presented a petition setting out the history of the land question, asserting that Aboriginal title had never been extinguished, and asking that the claim be referred to the courts. The Allied Tribes prepared statements and appeared before the committee, with Kelly and Paull among the principal Indigenous speakers and O'Meara assisting on legal arguments. The committee's proceedings were published and are a major source for this episode.

The issues were clear. The Indigenous delegates argued that Indigenous nations had occupied and governed their territories before colonization; that the Royal Proclamation and British practice recognized the need for agreement; that provincial and federal land policy had disregarded this; and that reserves allotted by the McKenna–McBride process did not address the underlying claim. Government witnesses and committee members took a different view. They contended, in various forms, that the Indigenous claim to title had not been established in law, that British Columbia's colonial policies had not recognized it, and that reserve allotments and other measures were the proper form of provision. Both sides drew on history and legal argument, but they began from incompatible premises about whether Crown sovereignty had displaced Indigenous interests.

The committee concluded that the Indigenous claim to Aboriginal title, as presented, had not been established, and it recommended arrangements for continuing assistance to British Columbia's Indigenous communities in place of recognizing the claim. The committee's reasoning reflected its interpretation of the law and its reading of the historical evidence, and it did not hear the claim before a court. Students should therefore distinguish three things: the Indigenous assertion of title, the committee's interpretation of the legal position, and the historical question of whether title existed. The committee's conclusion settled the matter as a political decision in 1927, but not as a matter of historical legitimacy. Later courts and governments, as Chapters 117 and 119 explain, reexamined the question and reached very different conclusions.

The consequences were significant. The report ended the Allied Tribes' campaign for a judicial reference, and the organization's activity diminished after 1927. The handling of the claim also shaped how the federal government treated organized land advocacy: officials had defeated the petition and, as the next section shows, had in the same year enacted a law restricting the means by which such campaigns were funded. The sequence matters: the amendment was enacted on March 31, 1927, while the committee was still sitting, and the committee's report followed later that year. The two events were closely connected in effect, but they should not be placed in reverse order.

The committee's published proceedings permit a closer inquiry into the unequal character of the hearing. Members could decide which witnesses were called, how long statements continued, and which kinds of documentary history were treated as legally persuasive. Allied Tribes representatives entered the proceedings as claimants before a parliamentary body rather than as parties to a negotiation with an agreed framework. Their case concerned an alleged continuing territorial interest; the committee approached the question within an interpretation of Canadian constitutional and colonial law that favoured existing governmental authority. Students should assess not only the stated conclusion but also the institutional premises that made that conclusion possible.

The chronology of the hearings deserves particular care. The Allied Tribes petition was submitted in 1926; the special joint committee assembled and heard the matter in 1927; and its final conclusions followed the March 31 Indian Act amendment. Parliamentary records include the committee's published report and evidence, and contemporary House proceedings distinguish early procedural reports from the final report considered in April. The restriction on claims-related fundraising was enacted while the inquiry remained underway. A textbook that describes the amendment as a response enacted after the final committee verdict obscures this overlap and the pressure it placed on the means of pursuing future claims.

The defeat cannot simply be reduced to failure to convince a judge. The Allied Tribes wanted access to a judicial forum for a question that Parliament instead considered politically. The distinction matters because a parliamentary recommendation, even if adopted by governments, did not adjudicate the historical facts in the manner of a trial. Nor did a committee's dismissal extinguish the Indigenous nations' own accounts of their territorial relationships. The later emergence of Aboriginal-title litigation belongs to a different period, but it illustrates why historians must separate a decision not to recognize a claim from evidence that the claim had no foundation.

Section 98.8

The 1927 Indian Act Amendment: Legal Restrictions and Political Suppression

On March 31, 1927, Parliament enacted an amendment to the Indian Act, initially designated section 149A and subsequently consolidated as section 141. It made it an offence, without the written consent of the Superintendent General, to receive, obtain, solicit, or request from an Indian any payment or contribution, or promise of one, for the purpose of raising a fund to prosecute a claim that the tribe or band had or was represented to have. A summary conviction carried a fine, and in some circumstances imprisonment. (The wording summarized here should be checked against the statute itself; see the Primary Source Window below.) The provision remained in force until 1951.

The text was narrow, but its practical reach was wide. It restricted raising or receiving money for the advancement of claims. It did not, on its face, prohibit Indigenous people from meeting, petitioning, or speaking, and it did not forbid every form of legal representation. But claims advocacy of the kind the Allied Tribes had practiced, which depended on collecting contributions from households, paying lawyers, financing delegations, and printing materials, required money. If a campaign needed written consent from the minister whose department opposed the claim, the consent requirement effectively gave that department a veto over the funding. Lawyers could not be paid from community contributions to prosecute claims without authorization, and community fundraising risked prosecution of the solicitor and exposure of contributors.

The effect was especially acute in British Columbia. The Allied Tribes had no endowment; their campaign had been financed by community contributions and by the sacrifices of leaders. The 1927 amendment arrived at the moment the campaign was being tested before Parliament, and its enactment, together with the committee's rejection of the claim, left little means of continuing. Organizing in other regions was also constrained: any association seeking funds for claims, whether over treaty annuities, reserve land, or other matters, had to consider the risk. Historians debate how often prosecutions occurred; the more consistent evidence concerns deterrence, with organizations and legal advisers avoiding activity that might invite charges.

This section demonstrates how legislation could function as a tool for restricting political opposition rather than resolving its underlying grievances. The government did not address title; it narrowed the channels through which title could be pressed. Yet the provision was not a total prohibition on Indigenous political activity, and it did not end it. Communities continued to meet, to petition, to develop regional associations, and in some cases to seek authorization for specific activity. The provision's endurance until 1951 meant that a generation of organizers worked under its shadow, and its removal in the early 1950s opened space for the post-Second World War organizing examined in Chapters 106 and 107.

The original designation of the provision also matters. Section 149A was added in March 1927 and appeared as section 141 in a subsequent consolidation; references to section 141 are therefore not evidence of a separate 1927 enactment. In close reading, the law addressed receiving, obtaining, soliciting, or requesting payments or promises for a defined claims-related purpose without written consent. It did not state that every community meeting was criminal. By placing the permission decision with the Superintendent General, however, it joined the financing of a claim to the discretion of the official responsible for administering the system being challenged. That institutional conflict is the key to its political significance.

Enforcement records and deterrence should be distinguished. A small number of criminal prosecutions would not, by itself, prove that the law had little influence. Leaders who knew that collecting subscriptions might create legal exposure could refrain from soliciting them, postpone a trip, or seek means of advocacy that required fewer funds. This is an effect that is difficult to measure from prosecution totals. Conversely, it would be inaccurate to infer that no legal work or petitioning occurred for the next twenty-four years. Indigenous communities continued to make arguments in other forums, sometimes under the limits established by this provision and sometimes by organizing outside its narrow coverage.

The amendment thus changed the political economy of Indigenous claims. Expenses for travel, translation, copying land records, retaining counsel, and circulating resolutions did not disappear because Parliament restricted certain collections. They became harder to meet, especially for geographically dispersed coalitions without access to institutional donors. The resulting asymmetry favoured the federal government, which financed its legal and administrative work from public resources, over nations required to gather comparatively small contributions from individual households. The legal provision is best understood together with the Allied Tribes' financial organization and the committee's treatment of their case.

Section 98.9

Deskaheh and the Six Nations Appeal to the League of Nations, 1923–1924

Deskaheh, whose English name was Levi General, was a Cayuga chief of Six Nations of the Grand River who became speaker for the Haudenosaunee Confederacy Chiefs Council in the early 1920s. The Council, composed of hereditary chiefs from the Confederacy's nations, regarded itself as the legitimate government of the community. Its claims rested on the Confederacy's long history of treaty relations with the British Crown, examined in Chapters 26 and 56, and on the Haldimand grant of 1784, which settled Six Nations people on the Grand River after the American Revolution (Chapter 59). In the Council's understanding, the Six Nations were allies of the Crown and had never become subjects of the Canadian government.

The Canadian government held a different view. It treated the Six Nations as "Indians" within the meaning of the Indian Act, subject to Parliament's authority, and the Department pressed for administrative changes at Grand River including involvement in land leases, the question of the Council's authority, and the replacement of hereditary government by an elected council. The Council resisted. By 1921 and 1922 it was petitioning the British Crown directly, rejecting Canadian jurisdiction, and seeking an arrangement under which the Six Nations' relationship with Britain, rather than with Canada, would be recognized.

In 1923 Deskaheh traveled to Europe. He went first to London to present the Six Nations' case to the Crown; the British Colonial Office declined to deal with the matter directly and referred it to Canada, consistent with the constitutional position that Canadian Indian affairs fell within the responsibility of the Dominion. Deskaheh then pursued a different forum, the newly established League of Nations at Geneva. He was assisted by supporters including George P. Decker, an American lawyer from Rochester, New York, and René Claparède, a Swiss writer and international advocate, who helped him make contacts and prepare documents. Representatives of several smaller states expressed interest in the case, and in 1924 the issue was raised by supporters in the League setting, but Britain objected to consideration of what it described as a matter internal to the British Empire and Canada.

The League did not formally hear and resolve the Six Nations claim. British objections to international consideration were central to the failure of the initiative, and Canada maintained that the matter was domestic. Deskaheh's campaign nonetheless drew public attention in Europe and North America. He spoke and published to explain the Six Nations' position, and his pamphlet addressed to a general audience circulated widely. The campaign's significance lies in its premise. Other contemporary organizers petitioned the Canadian state for redress within its legal framework. Deskaheh denied that the framework applied. His appeal asserted that the Six Nations were a political community entitled to be treated as such by the international community, a position later generations of Indigenous leaders revived in international forums (Chapter 143).

While Deskaheh was abroad and then in the United States, the dispute at home escalated. In these years federal police were deployed at Grand River and the Department moved toward replacing the Council, as the next section explains. Deskaheh, who did not return to Canada, spoke to audiences in the United States and died in June 1925 at Tuscarora, in New York State. His death ended the campaign's principal advocate, but his argument remained part of Haudenosaunee political memory. The distinction between asserting sovereignty internationally and seeking domestic administrative reform was thus embodied in his career, and it frames the historical debate in §98.19.

Deskaheh's appeal illustrates a strategy different in kind from asking Ottawa for improvements to the Indian Act. If the Six Nations were an allied polity with treaty commitments to the Crown, then the treatment of their government could be presented as an international dispute. The appeal to the League of Nations was therefore an attempt to obtain a hearing before an institution outside Canada's departmental hierarchy. It was also a calculated use of the postwar vocabulary of nations and collective security. Deskaheh's diplomatic difficulty was that the League's member states and their political relationships, not simply the force of his argument, determined whether the matter would receive formal consideration.

A stronger documentary basis is available than an unattributed recollection of his speeches. The George P. Decker Papers at St. John Fisher University identify an item titled The Redman's Appeal for Justice, dated August 6, 1923, in Series IIID, Box 7, Folder 12. Its catalog entry establishes the existence and archival location of the publication, although the wording of a passage still needs to be checked in the actual item before being quoted. The document should be read as an advocacy text addressed to an international audience, not as a transcript of every political view held at Grand River. The contrast with the elected-council campaign demonstrates why Indigenous politics cannot be described through a single voice.

Section 98.10

The Six Nations Government Crisis and the Imposition of an Elected Council, 1924

The Six Nations of the Grand River were not politically uniform. Alongside the hereditary Confederacy Council, many residents held different views about governance, land, and relations with Ottawa. Some supported electoral government and the greater individual say it offered; some were Christians in communities with their own church organizations; some were veterans and younger people who felt the Council did not represent them; others strongly defended the Confederacy Council and its authority under Haudenosaunee law. These divisions are documented in petitions to the Department from both sides and in later community memory, and historians should treat them as real, not as manufactured by outsiders.

The Department took advantage of them. In March 1923 the government appointed Andrew Thorburn Thompson to conduct an inquiry into conditions at Six Nations. His report, delivered in 1923, concluded that the hereditary council was not representative and recommended its replacement with an elected council under the Indian Act. The government followed by order in council in September 1924, withdrawing recognition from the Confederacy Council and providing for an elected council. Mounted Police intervention accompanied enforcement of the change, and the Confederacy chiefs were excluded from their council house. A new council was elected on October 21, 1924, under the Act's provisions.

The legal positions need careful statement. Canada's position was that the Indian Act authorized the Governor in Council to establish an elected band council and that the Confederacy Council had no legal standing in Canadian law. The Confederacy Council's position was that its authority derived from Haudenosaunee law and from treaty relations that predated the Act, that Canada had no right to dissolve it, and that an imposed council lacked legitimacy. Many who supported the Confederacy boycotted the election, and the elected council's legitimacy was contested within the community for decades. The hereditary chiefs continued to meet and to assert their authority, and the two systems coexisted uneasily. Chapter 118 shows how later disputes over tribal and band jurisdiction echoed this conflict.

The episode became a major example of how disagreement within a First Nation could be used by federal administrators to justify intervention. The Department could and did cite the existence of a pro-election faction as evidence that the Council did not speak for the community, and it presented the replacement as a response to local demand. This framing obscured a crucial fact: even if some residents sought change, the form and timing were imposed by the federal government against the formal objections of the recognized governing body. Both aspects must be held together. The community was divided, and the federal government acted to settle that division in favor of its preferred institutions.

Students should also resist reading the outcome as a simple victory of "modern" over "traditional" government. Elected councils became, over time, important institutions at Six Nations, and the community's later politics reflect an ongoing relationship between the elected council and the Confederacy chiefs. The 1924 events matter because they show a government replacing the institutions through which a people had governed itself, in a context where Indigenous nations had no means of appeal beyond the administration that made the decision.

The Thompson inquiry provides a particularly clear sequence of documentary events. Andrew Thorburn Thompson conducted his investigation under a commission issued in March 1923; his report was completed late that year but publicly released in 1924. The federal order of September 17, 1924 imposed the elective system, and the October 21 election followed. Government historical accounts also describe Mounted Police intervention to remove or exclude Confederacy chiefs from the council house. These dates clarify what was at issue: the inquiry and administrative decision preceded the vote, rather than the election freely initiating the entire constitutional change.

That chronology does not eliminate divisions within Six Nations. Those supporting elected administration could have serious grievances concerning accountability and the conduct of existing leaders; those defending Confederacy government could insist that the Canadian state lacked authority to replace it. An adequate account asks how each side defined legitimate political change. It also asks what alternative procedures might have allowed Six Nations to debate and alter institutions without surrendering the final decision to Ottawa. The episode shows both the internal politics of Indigenous government and the asymmetry created when a settler-state department claimed to decide which Indigenous representatives it would recognize.

Section 98.11

The Williams Treaties of 1923: Land Claims and Harvesting Rights in Ontario

While Six Nations pressed its claim for self-government, other Ontario nations were drawn into a different treaty process. In October and November 1923, the Crown concluded the Williams Treaties with seven First Nations in central and southern Ontario: the Chippewas of Beausoleil, Georgina Island, and Rama (Mnjikaning), and the Mississaugas of Alderville, Curve Lake, Hiawatha, and Scugog Island. The agreements, named for the federal commissioner who led the negotiations, addressed claims that these communities had never been compensated for lands in a large region of central Ontario, and they were negotiated alongside provincial involvement.

The historical background was long. Earlier treaties, including the Robinson treaties of 1850 and the Upper Canada land surrenders, had covered much of the surrounding territory, but a large area between Lake Simcoe and the Ottawa River had not been surrendered by agreement. By the early twentieth century the claim had been raised repeatedly by the communities, and the federal and Ontario governments agreed to resolve it. The 1923 treaties provided for cash payments, reserve land, and the surrender of the Indigenous nations' claims to the territory in question, a large area of south-central Ontario.

Harvesting rights were central. Communities such as the Chippewas of Rama and the Mississaugas of Curve Lake depended on hunting, fishing, and gathering across their territories, and their representatives understood that treaties should continue to protect these livelihoods. The Williams Treaties differed from earlier arrangements in that the written texts did not expressly preserve ongoing rights to hunt and fish on ceded lands, and the governments' later position was that the agreements had surrendered those rights, leaving communities subject to provincial game and fishery laws. The communities' understanding, preserved in later testimony and in historical research, was that they had not given up the ability to harvest. The treaties are therefore an unusually clear example of a divergence between the documents the Crown relied on and the understandings that signatories brought to the table.

The later history of litigation and settlement can be noted briefly as evidence that interpretations remained contested. Communities pressed their claims for decades, and in 2018 the Crown and the seven First Nations reached settlements addressing the treaties' history and harvesting rights; Chapter 135 returns to treaty stewardship. For the interwar period the lesson is that treaty-making did not end disputes, and that the form of a written agreement could be used to define away rights the other party believed it retained. Indigenous organizers' insistence on treaty memory, discussed throughout this chapter, should be read against this background.

The Williams Treaties demonstrate why the existence of a signed document does not end questions about its meaning. Indigenous negotiators encountered claims framed in the language of past land grants and outstanding Crown title, while communities retained understandings of continued harvesting and responsibility for territory. Interpretation turned not only on written clauses but on the circumstances of the discussions and what each party believed it was acknowledging or surrendering. Later evidence of disagreement cannot simply be inserted into a 1923 participant's mind, but it warns historians against treating a governmental summary of the agreement as the complete record of consent.

For interwar First Nations political history, the significance of these agreements also lies in organizing capacity. A nation faced with a legal challenge to hunting or fishing rights had to identify what had been promised, obtain documents, communicate among affected communities, and decide whether to seek administrative accommodation or assert a treaty right. The process often required the same political skills used by the League of Indians and western treaty organizations. The Williams Treaties therefore belong in a chapter about institutions and claims, not only in a narrative of formal treaty dates.

Section 98.12

First Nations Women, Indian Status, and the Politics of Community Membership

Political authority in First Nations communities depended on who counted as a member, and the Indian Act gave the federal government control over that question. Under its provisions, an Indian woman who married a man who did not have Indian status lost her own status and, with it, her band membership, and her children did not acquire it; by contrast, a non-status woman who married a status man gained status. The rule, which had roots in nineteenth-century legislation, treated a woman's legal identity as determined by her husband's. Scholarly legal histories examine its operation and its long-term consequences, including the later reforms that began with Bill C-31 in 1985.

The consequences for communities and individuals were substantial. A woman who lost status could lose the right to live on the reserve, to inherit or hold property, to share in band funds, to be buried in the community cemetery, and, in practice, to participate in community life. Families could be divided. At the same time, non-Indigenous women who married status men might be added to band lists, altering membership in ways communities had not chosen. For communities such as Six Nations, where descent through the mother had long organized clan membership and political office (Chapter 26), the statutory rule conflicted with Indigenous law as well as with fairness.

Women nevertheless took part in political life in many ways. In some nations, clan mothers retained roles in selecting and advising leaders and in preserving law and ceremonial knowledge. Women signed petitions, wrote to the Department, supported organizers, hosted gatherings, and shaped community opinion on issues such as schools and the treatment of children. Documented episodes exist, in departmental correspondence and community records, of women protesting administrative decisions affecting their families. Historians must use such documents carefully, and this chapter does not attribute later women's-rights campaigns to unidentified interwar activists. What the evidence supports is that women were politically active participants whose roles were often unrecorded or minimized in official sources, and that the status rules were a continuing source of grievance.

It is important to distinguish women's participation within Indigenous institutions from the voting rights recognized by the statutory band-council system. Under the Indian Act's electoral provisions, women were not given a vote in band council elections until the 1951 revision. At Six Nations, therefore, the hereditary system recognized roles for women, such as clan mothers, that the imposed elected system did not. This contrast complicates any simple story in which elected councils are the more inclusive form, and it shows why the form of governance mattered to women as well as men.

Finally, the status system was also tied to enfranchisement. A status Indian who sought the federal vote or the ordinary rights of citizenship could do so only by being enfranchised, which ended legal status and band membership. Some individuals did so, but many refused as a matter of principle, and the 1920 amendments that allowed compulsory enfranchisement were among the measures that organizers opposed. Questions of membership, status, and citizenship thus connected personal family decisions with national political demands.

Status provisions altered political community by changing the legal consequences of family decisions. A woman who lost recognition through marriage might lose statutory rights connected with residence, annuities, or property even though her kin recognized her continuing membership. Children could also be affected by the federal classification. This produced a divergence between the administrative roll and relationships understood through family, community law, or matrilineal systems. Because councils and organizations relied on members' knowledge and contributions, the narrowing of legal recognition could carry political consequences long before a formal debate about voting rights arose.

Women also sustained interwar organizing through work that often appears only indirectly in surviving records. Accommodation for visitors, the transmission of news, the maintenance of correspondence, and the gathering of resources for meetings made political action possible. Some women held authority within hereditary or kinship institutions that the Indian Act did not recognize, including Haudenosaunee clan mothers. Historians should neither erase those roles because a convention's published speakers were men nor assign identical offices to women in all First Nations. Community-specific evidence is essential, as is the recognition that gender discrimination in federal law did not define Indigenous women's actual participation in political life.

Historical Vocabulary
Enfranchisement, Indian Status, and Political Membership

Definition. "Indian status" was the legal classification under the Indian Act that determined whether a person was recognized as an "Indian" and a member of a band. "Enfranchisement" in this context meant the legal process by which a person or family ceased to be an Indian under the Act, usually in exchange for the right to vote federally and hold property in fee simple. "Political membership" refers to belonging within a First Nation's own system of citizenship or kinship.

Historical context. The statutory definitions did not coincide with Indigenous definitions of belonging. Enfranchisement was named for the vote but removed legal membership, and compulsory enfranchisement provisions were introduced in 1920, repealed in 1922, and restored in modified form in 1933.

Why it matters. The status system affected who could vote in band elections, live on reserve, and participate in community institutions.

Potential misunderstanding. "Enfranchisement" sounds like an expansion of rights; in practice it meant the loss of legal status, which is why many Indigenous people resisted it.

Section 98.13

Treaty 11 and Northern First Nations Political Relationships, 1921–1930

Treaty 11, the last of the numbered treaties, covered the Mackenzie Valley and surrounding districts of the Northwest Territories, the homeland of Dene peoples including the Slavey, Dogrib, Hare, Gwich'in, and others (Chapter 34). Earlier numbered treaties, notably Treaty 8 (1899) and Treaty 10, had been negotiated as settlement and resource development pushed north, but the Mackenzie Valley, with few settlers and a fur-trade economy, had been left outside them. Dene communities had long-standing relationships with traders, missionaries, and the Hudson's Bay Company, and some leaders had asked, over the years, whether and how a treaty might protect their livelihood.

The federal government's decision to negotiate changed after 1920. Imperial Oil's discovery of oil at Norman Wells in that year gave Ottawa a strong interest in clearing any uncertainty about title to the region and securing access for development. A treaty party led by Commissioner Henry Anthony Conroy traveled down the Mackenzie in the summer of 1921, meeting Dene at Fort Providence, Fort Simpson, Fort Norman, Fort Good Hope, and Arctic Red River, among other places. The treaty was ratified by the federal government in October 1921, and a further adhesion was made at Fort Liard in 1922, bringing additional communities within its terms.

The written text followed the model of earlier numbered treaties. It recorded a cession of Indian title in exchange for reserves, an annual payment, supplies, and assurances that the signatories could continue to hunt, trap, and fish, subject to government regulation. The Dene signatories, however, understood the negotiations differently. Oral accounts preserved by Dene elders and recorded in later research emphasize that the treaty was understood as an agreement of peace and friendship, that commissioners gave assurances about continued access to the land and livelihood, and that Dene had not agreed to give up their land or governance. These accounts, collected mainly in later decades, have their own limitations, since they are recollections transmitted across generations, but they have been taken seriously by courts and historians because they show consistent patterns and because the written record of negotiations is thin and filtered through officials. Chapter 82 and Chapter 117 discuss how later litigation and claims processes returned to these differing understandings.

Implementation was uneven. Annuities were paid at periodic gatherings, but reserves were largely not surveyed in the early years, since the Dene lived by trapping and hunting across wide territories and showed little interest in fixed parcels, and the promised assistance was modest. The Department's presence was light; the Royal Canadian Mounted Police and treaty-party physicians were the most regular federal contacts. Meanwhile, resource developments, game regulations, and disease affected communities. The treaty thus did not end the political relationship but opened it, and the question of what had actually been agreed remained alive among Dene and in later northern politics.

For this chapter, Treaty 11 illustrates that treaty-making was an ongoing political issue, not a process completed when signatures were obtained. It also shows that the interwar federal government was still extending the numbered-treaty system while simultaneously restricting organized Indigenous efforts to influence it. Because Dene communities were isolated from the southern organizing networks described earlier in this division, their political responses took different forms, including participation in treaty payments, local assemblies, and, in later generations, regional organizations.

The official treaty documents permit a reconstruction of the itinerary that is more exact than a generalized description of northern travel. Henry Anthony Conroy conducted the principal negotiations during the summer of 1921 at locations including Fort Providence, Fort Simpson, Fort Wrigley, Fort Norman, and Rae. After Conroy died in April 1922, Thomas William Harris completed the Fort Liard adhesion on July 17 of that year. The records also reveal the commission's logistical constraints, including seasonal river travel and the difficulty of reaching every community. A chronological table should distinguish the initial signatures from the adhesion rather than describing the entire territory as having signed at one place or date.

The Department's treaty research report notes that the officials sent to secure signatures had little room to bargain over the text. That limitation gives particular significance to oral assurances and to community understanding. Northern people relied on hunting, trapping, travel, and fisheries across a landscape that could not be represented adequately as a set of settled farm lots. Treaty negotiations were therefore also negotiations over future livelihood and authority, even if federal paperwork emphasized land cession and annuities. Later discussions of northern resource development must be read against these different understandings of what was promised and what, if anything, was relinquished.

Section 98.14

The Natural Resources Transfer Agreements of 1930 and Prairie Treaty Rights

When Manitoba, Saskatchewan, and Alberta joined Confederation (Manitoba in 1870, Saskatchewan and Alberta in 1905), Ottawa retained control of their Crown lands and natural resources, unlike the older provinces, which controlled their own. The prairie provinces long campaigned for equal treatment. In 1930, after negotiations between Ottawa and each province, agreements transferred administration of Crown lands, minerals, forests, and wildlife to the provinces. These agreements, known as the Natural Resources Transfer Agreements, were confirmed by federal and provincial legislation and by an imperial statute.

Indigenous nations were not parties to these negotiations, yet the resources concerned were central to their livelihoods and treaty entitlements. Control of Crown land determined where reserves could be established, whether hunting and trapping could continue, and who regulated fisheries and game. Treaty annuity and land entitlement obligations of the Crown to First Nations were affected by decisions about which government held the land. The principal historical lesson is that constitutional transfers between Canadian governments could materially affect Indigenous peoples even when Indigenous nations were not parties to the bargain.

The agreements contained provisions addressed to Indigenous interests. They provided that the provinces would set aside unoccupied Crown land as needed to enable Canada to fulfil treaty obligations to provide reserves, and that existing reserve lands continued to be administered by Canada. They also contained a paragraph on hunting, fishing, and trapping. In the Alberta agreement this was paragraph 12; the corresponding provisions of the Saskatchewan and Manitoba agreements were numbered differently. The paragraph in substance applied provincial game laws to Indians while assuring them the right to hunt, trap, and fish game and fish for food at all seasons on unoccupied Crown lands and on other lands to which they had a right of access.

The agreements did not constitute a new numbered treaty, and they did not by themselves extinguish existing treaty relationships. They did, however, change the legal landscape in ways whose meaning was disputed. Provincial governments gained authority to regulate wildlife and resources, and the paragraph's phrase "for food" and its reference to "unoccupied Crown lands" and lands of "right of access" raised questions about commercial harvesting, the scope of regulation, and what counted as occupied land. First Nations argued that treaties had guaranteed harvesting as a continuing way of life and that the transfer agreements narrowed rather than confirmed their rights; provincial officials treated the paragraph as the full measure of the rights in question. Courts and governments debated these questions for the rest of the twentieth century, as Chapter 135 explains.

In the interwar years, the practical effect was that First Nations hunters and trappers on the Prairies faced provincial enforcement of game and fishing regulations, and the Department of Indian Affairs, which should have been their advocate, was sometimes reluctant to challenge provincial policy. The petitions and conventions discussed in §98.15 frequently raised these concerns. The absence of Indigenous participation in the original bargain also reinforced the argument made by treaty organizers that their relationship was with the Crown as a whole and that governments should not rearrange it without consulting them.

The Alberta agreement's paragraph 12 provides a useful exercise in legal interpretation because it combines a general rule with a specific assurance. Provincial game laws were to apply, but the text also protected hunting, trapping, and fishing for food at all seasons on unoccupied Crown lands and other lands of lawful access. The qualifying phrases matter. They identify an activity (food harvesting), a set of places (unoccupied Crown land or other accessible land), and a continuing relationship between provincial regulation and Indigenous rights. The agreement did not grant the province authority to define away every treaty obligation, nor did it describe a general unconditional right to hunt anywhere for any purpose.

The 1930 transfers were negotiated between governments of Canada and the Prairie provinces. First Nations were not parties, even though the arrangements affected land management and the administration of wildlife rules that could alter everyday treaty practices. The constitutional redistribution of authority was thus a political event in First Nations history, not merely a technical adjustment in federalism. For treaty advocates it furnished another reason to insist that commitments made to the Crown were not subject to unilateral rearrangement by the governments that administered them. Scholars should distinguish the 1930 agreement's original wording from later judicial interpretation, which developed in circumstances outside this chapter's core period.

Section 98.15

Prairie Treaty Organizations during the Depression and the Indian Association of Alberta, 1939

The Great Depression struck prairie First Nations communities on top of drought, falling grain and fur prices, and a farming policy that had never provided adequate capital. Rations and relief were administered by agents, and complaints about their adequacy and fairness were common. Employment off reserve declined, and the Department was reluctant to expand spending. In these conditions, the cost of organizing was high and the need for it clear. Political activity continued, but it was increasingly regional and local, as communities addressed immediate questions of welfare, schooling, reserve land, treaty payments, and harvesting rights.

The western branches of the League of Indians and the associations that grew from them kept the network alive. Delegations met agents and, when possible, officials in Ottawa; letters and resolutions were sent; individual leaders, often with experience from the 1920s conventions, carried information across districts. Churches, especially Anglican and Catholic missions, sometimes provided meeting places and communication links, though they also had their own interests in schooling. These networks were informal and depended on kinship and personal reputation, and they were sustained despite the financial restrictions on claims-related fundraising that §98.8 described, since many of their activities, such as meetings to discuss welfare or schools, were not covered by the 1927 provision.

Alberta saw the most significant institutional development. In 1939, First Nations leaders founded the Indian Association of Alberta, an organization of treaty Indians drawing on the networks of the previous two decades. John Callihoo, an Alberta leader from the Michel community who had been active in earlier organizing, is documented among its early leaders. The founders sought an institution more closely tied to Alberta's reserve communities and their circumstances than the earlier, more diffuse western structures, one that could speak for treaty First Nations in provincial matters and press Ottawa for action on education, welfare, and land. The association's founding date and its later importance are documented in scholarly histories of Alberta First Nations political organization.

The chapter introduces the association through its interwar origins rather than its later accomplishments. In 1939 it was a new and modest organization, working within the legal constraints of the Indian Act, with limited funds and no guarantee of recognition. Its significance lay in its continuity with earlier organizing: members and leaders who had taken part in the conventions of the 1920s and in Depression-era activity carried their experience forward. Its later prominence in the 1960s, including its role in responding to federal policy proposals, belongs to later chapters (Chapters 107 and 114), and should not be read back into the 1930s.

An adequate account of the 1939 Indian Association of Alberta must connect it to earlier organizing without treating the institutional transition as automatic. John Callihoo brought experience of Alberta First Nations politics and treaty communities, while Malcolm Norris, a Métis political organizer, contributed relationships and organizational skill. Their cooperation did not dissolve the difference between First Nations treaty claims and Métis demands for land and public services. Rather, it shows that strategic collaboration across distinct Indigenous constituencies was possible when the participants identified common administrative obstacles. The association that emerged would later develop its own debates over federal responsibility, citizenship, and the legal force of treaties.

The Depression made organizing more expensive at precisely the moment its subject matter became more urgent. Delegates needed fares, accommodation, stationery, and opportunities to meet; many families were coping with inadequate food, market collapse, and constrained employment. Reports of organizational dormancy must therefore be read in their economic setting. A council might continue to debate common issues even when a formal association could not pay for a meeting or circulate a printed bulletin. The significance of 1939 lies in the new institutional structure established from these long-running conversations, not in an imagined sudden awakening of political consciousness.

Everyday Life and Social History

Organizing in Practice: What a Delegation or Convention Required

The surviving record does not allow reconstruction of an individual participant's thoughts or conversations, and this feature does not invent them. It describes, instead, the practical conditions that the documented conventions of the 1919–1922 period, the Allied Tribes' delegations to Ottawa, and the Depression-era meetings of western organizers had in common. Each depended on the same resources.

First came money. Rail fares, lodging, printing, and postage had to be raised in communities with little cash, often from household contributions. Second came permission and time: agents controlled permits and could discourage travel, and delegates had to leave farms and traplines during working seasons. Third came hospitality: host communities, often small, fed and housed delegates, and gatherings were usually combined with ceremony or church services that gave them wider social meaning. Fourth came correspondence: circular letters, handwritten notes, and, where available, typed documents moved between communities, and literate members served as secretaries. Women hosted and fed delegates, passed along information, and in some communities participated in the discussion, though official records seldom name them. Finally came risk. Organizers and their communities could be refused rations or permits, labelled agitators, or, after 1927, exposed to legal peril if funds were raised for claims.

These practical demands help explain both why organizations were short-lived and why they were so often rebuilt. Each gathering was an act of collective labour, and the networks they created, rather than any single institution, were the most durable political asset.

Section 98.16

Métis Political Organization in Alberta, 1928–1938: A Comparative Case

Métis in Alberta formed a distinct people with their own history, language traditions, and claims, separate from First Nations treaty nations (Chapters 52 and 83). Historically, Métis communities had emerged through the fur trade and the Red River settlement, and many Métis families had received scrip, a certificate redeemable for land or money, in place of the treaties that First Nations signed. By the 1920s, many Alberta Métis lived in poverty, often on road allowances or at the edges of settlements, without reserves, treaty benefits, or a recognized land base, and with limited access to schooling and health care. The Department of Indian Affairs did not administer them, and provincial governments gave them little attention.

Organizing began in the late 1920s. In 1928, Métis leaders came together to discuss their conditions, and in 1932 they established the Métis Association of Alberta, known in its early years by a longer title in French and English. Its leading figures included Joseph Dion, a teacher and writer; Malcolm Norris, an organizer and later political figure; Felix Callihoo; Peter Tomkins; and James Brady. The Métis government's own historical timeline provides an account of the organizational development and these leaders. They worked through meetings, delegations, and petitions to the provincial government, since Métis issues fell largely within provincial jurisdiction in matters such as health, education, and relief.

Their demands concerned land, housing, health, education, and economic security. They sought secure land on which Métis families could live and farm, relief from the poverty that followed the Depression, and schooling for their children. They argued that the Métis had historical claims arising from their role in the Prairie economy and from earlier promises of land. The association was an organization of Métis people, advancing their own case, and not a branch of First Nations bodies.

In 1934 the Alberta government appointed the Royal Commission on the Condition of the Half-Breed Population of Alberta, known as the Ewing Commission after its chair. It heard evidence from Métis communities and others and reported with recommendations on land, schooling, and health. The commission's findings contributed to the Métis Population Betterment Act of 1938, which laid the legislative foundation for provincial Métis settlements, areas of land set aside for Métis use and governance under provincial law. The act was a real achievement in recognition of Métis land needs, but it was also limited: the settlements were governed within a provincial statutory framework, and the Métis remained under provincial, not federal, responsibility.

The comparison with First Nations organizations is instructive. First Nations treaty organizations faced a federal Department, based their arguments on treaties and the Indian Act framework, and were hampered by the federal legal restrictions described in this chapter. Métis associations faced a province, argued from Métis identity and historical claims, and succeeded in persuading a provincial government to act. The strategies differed because the legal relationships differed: Métis had no treaty with the Crown for which to demand performance, and no federal statute controlling their internal affairs, while First Nations had both. At the same time, both groups experienced exclusion and dispossession and sometimes cooperated, and they sometimes shared concerns and occasionally cooperated. The comparison should not blur the two peoples' distinct histories, but it shows that the interwar prairie political landscape included several overlapping movements pursuing recognition by different routes.

Comparing the Métis Association of Alberta with First Nations organizations requires attention to jurisdiction. Treaty nations could frame particular demands as the fulfillment of commitments to the Crown and the protection of reserve resources. Métis organizers faced a different history of land alienation and government classification and often had to persuade a provincial government to assume responsibility for pressing material needs. Legal categories were not merely terms of description; they influenced which office could be petitioned, which remedies were plausible, and which public funds might be available. Nevertheless, administrative separation did not prevent organizers from learning from one another or collaborating on particular initiatives.

The Ewing Commission is best treated as a stage in this political process, not simply as an inevitable path to settlement legislation. Testimony about poverty and the need for a land base could support the argument for provincial action, but the later settlement framework also contained supervisory features and limits. A policy designed to provide land might leave decision-making authority in officials' hands. This tension parallels, without equating, the First Nations disputes over treaty and reserve administration discussed elsewhere in the chapter. Both movements asked who had the right to identify a community's needs and determine the institutions through which those needs would be addressed.

Section 98.17

Political Authority and Cultural Suppression: The Potlatch Prosecutions and Compulsory Schooling

Federal control of ceremonial life was part of the wider effort to subordinate Indigenous authority. The potlatch, a ceremonial institution of Northwest Coast peoples including the Kwakwaka'wakw, Haida, Tsimshian, Nuu-chah-nulth, and others (Chapter 33), had been prohibited by an amendment to the Indian Act in 1884. Officials and missionaries regarded it as wasteful and as an obstacle to assimilation. For the communities that practiced it, the potlatch was far more than a performance. It was a legal and political institution through which hereditary names and privileges were validated before witnesses, marriages and successions were recognized, debts and obligations were settled, wealth was redistributed, and relationships among families and nations were maintained. To prohibit it was to attack the means by which a society recorded and enforced its own law.

Enforcement was uneven for decades but intensified in the early 1920s. In December 1921, Dan Cranmer, a high-ranking Kwakwaka'wakw man, hosted a large potlatch at Village Island (Mimkwamlis), in the context of marriages and the transfer of privileges. The Indian agent at Alert Bay, William Halliday, took action, and prosecutions followed in 1922. Dozens of participants were charged, and the authorities offered suspended sentences to those who agreed to surrender ceremonial regalia and give up the potlatch, while others were imprisoned. Historians and Indigenous cultural institutions document that a large quantity of masks, coppers, and other ceremonial objects were confiscated; many were sent to museums in Ottawa and elsewhere and to private collectors. Their long campaign for return led, in the 1970s and later, to repatriation and to the establishment of cultural centres at Alert Bay and Cape Mudge; Chapter 125 treats this history.

The prosecutions fit the chapter's political argument. They targeted an institution that held a nation's law, property, and social order together, and they showed how the Indian Act's criminal provisions could be used against political and legal practices that the state found threatening. The Kwakwaka'wakw responded in several ways: some surrendered regalia under pressure; others went underground, holding ceremonies in secret or in modified form; some, at later stages, supported the prohibition's repeal. The prohibition on the potlatch remained in the Act until the 1951 revision. The case also shows regional variation, since the potlatch was not central to Prairie or Ontario communities, where other ceremonial restrictions, such as limits on Sun Dances and costume, were applied.

Compulsory schooling was a second instrument for changing community authority and intergenerational relationships, examined in detail in Chapters 85 to 88. For this chapter the key point is legal. The 1920 amendments to the Indian Act strengthened compulsory school attendance for Indian children, generally of ages seven to fifteen, and authorized the government to require attendance at a school of the Department's choosing. It would be inaccurate to say that all First Nations children were legally required to attend residential schools specifically; the amendments concerned attendance at school, with residential, day, and industrial schools among the options, and many children attended day schools or none. But in practice, for many communities, the state's power to compel attendance, and to remove children to distant residential institutions, interfered with parents' authority and with the transmission of language and law. Organizers such as the League of Indians protested the schools' quality and the compulsion they involved, and school conditions were a repeated agenda item in conventions and petitions.

The institutional significance of the potlatch becomes clearer when the legal consequences of the 1921 gathering are followed. The U'mista Cultural Centre identifies forty-five people charged after Dan Cranmer's Village Island potlatch. Twenty-two received suspended sentences under conditions connected with surrendering ceremonial regalia, three cases were held for appeal, and twenty people were imprisoned. The prosecutions were not simply the policing of a performance. The confiscated objects embodied family histories, privileges, artistic work, and ceremonial authority. Their removal disrupted the means by which relationships and obligations were publicly affirmed. Later repatriation of those belongings, though outside the interwar period, demonstrates the continuing importance of what was taken.

A parallel issue arose in schooling. The 1920 Indian Act amendments strengthened compulsory attendance powers, but the law should not be paraphrased as if it required every child to attend a residential school and no other form. The operation of day, industrial, and residential institutions differed, as did the actual options available to families in particular communities. Government intrusion nevertheless affected the ability to transmit language and political knowledge between generations. Bringing the potlatch prosecutions and school policies into one section highlights how the state could interfere with institutions that exercised authority outside parliamentary or council structures, while community efforts to maintain those practices remained political acts.

Section 98.18

Strategies of Resistance: Petitions, Diplomacy, Councils, and Continuing Political Organization

The preceding sections show a range of strategies, and it is useful to compare them. Petitions to Parliament and the Crown were the oldest and most widely used method, and they were adapted, as in the Allied Tribes' submission, to the parliamentary committee format. Delegations to Ottawa and London carried arguments directly to officials. Legal advocacy, whether through lawyers presenting claims or through requests for judicial reference, sought a forum outside the Department's discretion. Conventions, as in the League, provided public organization and mutual support. International appeals, as in Deskaheh's campaign, denied the premise of Canadian authority. Traditional councils continued to function as governments. And everyday resistance, in the form of refusing to cooperate with administrative decisions, boycotting imposed elections, or continuing prohibited ceremonies, took forms that left little documentary trace.

Financial obstacles and legal restrictions shaped which strategies were viable. The 1927 provision limited claim-related fundraising, and the broader dependence of Indigenous organizations on community contributions meant that sustained legal campaigns were difficult. Organizations responded by emphasizing activities that did not depend on contested fundraising: local meetings, correspondence, representation on welfare and school issues, and appeals to sympathetic members of Parliament, churches, and the press. They also sought, at times, formal authorization from the Department for specified activities, a path that kept organizations alive but placed them under official oversight.

Leaders disagreed about strategy. Some pursued cooperative negotiation with officials, reasoning that a working relationship might win practical improvements and that open confrontation invited repression. Others rejected federal jurisdiction over their nations altogether, as Deskaheh and the Confederacy Council did, arguing that negotiation within the Indian Act's framework conceded the principle at stake. Between these positions lay many others: Peter Kelly and Andrew Paull, for example, pressed claims through Parliament while insisting on recognition of title, and Prairie leaders used treaty days to demand performance of treaties while criticizing particular agents. These choices reflected differences in legal position, in available forums, and in political judgment, and individuals sometimes shifted their approach over time.

Non-political networks sustained political organization. Correspondence among literate leaders kept communities informed. Kinship connections across reserves provided channels of trust. Ceremonies and gatherings, where they continued, offered occasions for discussion. Church networks, particularly Anglican, Methodist, and Catholic missions, gave some organizers meeting places, translation, and access to the press, though churches could also be allies of the Department on schooling and ceremony. Community gatherings associated with treaty payments, summer assemblies, and funerals allowed leaders to coordinate.

The central point of this section is that political organization did not necessarily end when a national association dissolved or a government rejected a petition. The League's national structure weakened, the Allied Tribes' campaign ended after 1927, and Deskaheh died in 1925, yet the networks, experience, and arguments persisted and reappeared in new forms. Historians who measure interwar organizing only by legislative outcomes will therefore find mostly failure; those who measure by institutional and political continuity will find that the capacity for collective action survived.

Strategy was shaped by the audience a movement hoped to persuade. A petition to the Superintendent General might invoke an existing departmental rule, whereas an appeal to the Crown could invoke treaty promises and a relationship older than the Indian Act. A judicial reference would ask a court to decide a claim according to law; Deskaheh's international campaign asserted that the very question of who had authority was being wrongly confined within Canadian jurisdiction. These routes could be combined, but they were not equivalents. Organizers weighed what each forum could deliver and what concessions might be implied by submitting to it.

Political work also required a chain of translation. Grievances expressed at a community meeting had to be formulated as resolutions, carried to delegates, written for officials, and sometimes restated by a lawyer or translator. At each stage a demand could become narrower or be understood differently. Historical research is stronger when it compares the local account with the formal petition and the departmental response, rather than treating the last surviving document as the original Indigenous position. This method is particularly important where the government preserved voluminous files and Indigenous organizational records survived only in fragments.

Nor should success be measured only in legislation enacted by 1939. Networks enabled leaders to compare experiences, transmit treaty knowledge, coordinate responses to the conduct of agents, and preserve political options for later years. They also generated disagreement, since a collective program had to represent nations with different territories and priorities. The capacity to sustain these debates is itself an institutional result, even when a parliamentary committee dismissed a petition or a national league lost the money to hold another convention.

Section 98.19

Historical Debate: Treaty Sovereignty or Administrative Reform?

Three interpretations of interwar First Nations organizing emerge from the historical record, and each explains a different part of it. An administrative-reform interpretation emphasizes demands for better schools, medical services, accountable officials, and fair reserve management. It explains why resolutions often addressed specific departmental practices. Yet it is incomplete if it assumes that a petition for an improved school conceded that Ottawa held final authority over First Nations education. The same delegates could insist that the education promised under treaty carried obligations the Crown had not fulfilled.

A treaty-sovereignty interpretation focuses instead on land title, political jurisdiction, and the continuation of nation-to-nation relationships. Deskaheh's Geneva diplomacy and the Allied Tribes' claim that title had not been surrendered are particularly strong evidence for this interpretation. Its limitation is not that such claims were marginal, but that it can understate the variety of aims pursued by leaders who needed immediate administrative relief for families while maintaining broader political positions. It may also flatten differences between treaty-making Prairie nations and much of British Columbia, where the fundamental contention was that no treaty of cession existed.

The most convincing synthesis treats interwar organizing as strategic politics under conditions of unequal power. A community might negotiate with an agent, petition Parliament, defend a hereditary council, and seek external legal review without regarding those acts as contradictory. Decisions about tactics depended on funding, geography, official resistance, community consensus, and the prospects of each forum. This interpretation does not make the sovereign claim disappear into pragmatism. It asks historians to distinguish a movement's immediate request from its account of the political authority that made the request legitimate, and to test both against specific documents rather than a single generalized label.

The League of Indians demonstrates the overlap in practice. Loft asked for remedies in education and administration while insisting that First Nations should combine their voices as political communities. The Allied Tribes pursued a court hearing about title, not merely additional acreage within reserves. Deskaheh sought a forum beyond the Canadian state because the basic point at issue was the status of the Haudenosaunee government itself. The contrast is instructive: each movement used institutions available in its time, but they did not all seek the same final constitutional relationship. Any interpretation that assigns one ideology to every organizer will miss these differences in the actual demands recorded in petitions and correspondence.

For the historian, the strongest method is to examine purpose, audience, and jurisdiction together. An appeal to a federal official can be cautious because the addressee controls immediate resources; a public address to the League of Nations can be more explicit about sovereignty because it seeks an alternative authority. Different language in two documents does not necessarily prove that a leader changed convictions, but neither can those differences be ignored. Students should test claims about objectives against convention resolutions, archival letters, committee transcripts, statutory provisions, and community records, identifying whose interpretation is preserved in each source. This analytical approach recognizes unequal power without denying the choices, compromises, and disagreements that made interwar First Nations politics a complex field of action.

Historical Debate

Were Interwar First Nations Movements Seeking Fairer Administration or Defending Sovereign Authority?

The question: Were interwar First Nations movements principally seeking fairer treatment within Canadian administration, or defending political relationships and sovereign authority that the Indian Act had attempted to subordinate? Historians approach the question in different ways, and the sources are open to more than one reading.

Interpretation A — Administrative reform: This view emphasizes the content of many resolutions and petitions: better schools, health services, honest management of reserve funds, fair treatment of veterans, and more responsible agents. It notes that many leaders worked through existing institutions, asked the Department for changes, and did not demand the abolition of the Act. On this reading the movements were reformist, seeking to improve the system's operation.
Interpretation B — Treaty-based nationhood: This view emphasizes the claims of continuing political authority, territorial rights, and relationships with the Crown that Indigenous nations considered binding. It points to the Haudenosaunee Council's rejection of Canadian jurisdiction, the Allied Tribes' insistence on unextinguished title, and the treaty memory invoked on the Prairies. On this reading, requests for better administration were secondary to defense of a political relationship the Act had tried to subordinate.
Interpretation C — Strategic adaptation: This view holds that communities used the instruments available to them, administrative petitions, statutory councils, court arguments, parliamentary hearings, and international diplomacy, as opportunities and obstacles dictated. Because the same leaders might ask for better schools and assert treaty authority, the categories of "reform" and "sovereignty" may reflect scholars' classifications more than participants' own distinctions.

Testing the interpretations: The League of Indians' resolutions on schooling and administration fit Interpretation A, but its claims about treaty protection fit B. The Allied Tribes' title claim fits B most clearly, yet their appeal to Parliament and a judicial reference used the state's own forums, as C suggests. Deskaheh's campaign fits B most directly, while the elected-council supporters at Six Nations may be read as reformers within A. Prairie organizers combined treaty demands with practical grievances, illustrating C.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and emphasis rather than established facts, and partly concerns evidence: official records emphasize petitions for administrative remedy, while oral history and later memory emphasize treaty and sovereignty claims.

Why it matters: If interwar movements were chiefly reformist, later sovereignty movements appear as a new departure; if they were defending sovereignty, later movements appear as continuations. Neither reading should be imposed on the evidence, and the approaches could overlap even though their underlying political assumptions were not identical.

Section 98.20

First Nations Politics in 1939: Institutional Continuity and the Unfinished Struggle for Recognition

By 1939, the political landscape differed from that of 1918. The League of Indians' national structure had faded, and Loft had died in 1934. The Allied Tribes' campaign had ended after the 1927 rejection and restrictions, although British Columbia land-title advocacy did not disappear: leaders such as Andrew Paull continued to work, and new organizations, notably the Native Brotherhood of British Columbia, founded in 1931 among coastal peoples and active in fisheries and community affairs, offered a different institutional base. Prairie associations continued, and in Alberta the new Indian Association of Alberta gave treaty communities a provincial voice. The Haudenosaunee dispute at Six Nations remained unresolved, with the elected council and hereditary chiefs coexisting in tension. Métis in Alberta had won a legislative foundation for settlements in 1938.

The legal framework had not changed in the direction organizers sought. The Indian Act's powers remained intact; the 1927 provision restricted claims-related fundraising; the potlatch ban stayed in the statute; reserve administration and schooling remained under the Department. Statutory restrictions and administrative control limited political success. They did not extinguish political life, because organizers had learned to operate within and around them, and because the sources of political authority, treaty memory, kinship, ceremony, and community institutions, lay partly outside the Department's reach.

The relationship between interwar and postwar organizing should be stated with care. Chapters 101 to 108 show that Indigenous military service in the Second World War, the postwar political climate, a Parliamentary inquiry in the late 1940s, and the repeal of the 1927 provision in 1951 opened new opportunities. The interwar organizations were not the sole cause of those developments, and many postwar leaders built new institutions. But the interwar generation supplied networks, experience, arguments, and examples: the regional associations of the 1930s, the Prairie treaty network, the practice of conventions, the precedent of international appeal, and the knowledge of how the Act's restrictions operated.

Continuing disagreements also deserve emphasis. There was no uniform national representation; the League had never spoken for all First Nations, and different regions held different constitutional positions: treaty nations of the Prairies and North, mostly non-treaty nations of British Columbia, Ontario nations with older treaties, and Haudenosaunee communities asserting a distinct status. Leaders differed on whether to cooperate with the Department, and communities were divided over elected versus traditional government. Métis and Inuit followed separate paths, as Chapter 100 will show for the Inuit.

The conclusion of this chapter is therefore not one of resolution. Canadian Indigenous political claims on treaties, title, and self-government were not resolved by 1939; many remained unaddressed for decades. First Nations entered 1939 with functioning political traditions and accumulated organizational experience that later generations could build upon. The central outcome of the interwar period was not the resolution of Canadian Indigenous political claims, but the preservation and development of the capacity to pursue them.

A continental comparison clarifies what was distinctive about this Canadian interwar experience. In the United States, the 1934 Indian Reorganization Act opened a controversial federal pathway to constitutional and corporate organization, though it retained substantial Interior Department supervision. Canadian First Nations received no comparable nationwide reorganization measure during the 1930s. Their principal gains in organizing capacity arose through their own conventions, treaty assertions, councils, and regional associations in the face of persistent administrative constraints. The comparison does not imply that American reorganization delivered full sovereignty or that Canadian political communities remained institutionally static; it reveals two different configurations of federal power and Indigenous initiative.

The enduring questions also concerned representation. Who could speak for a nation when governments recognized one council and community members recognized another? Could a coalition make a title claim without subordinating the distinct territorial interests of its constituent nations? Did collaboration across First Nations and Métis lines create useful alliances without blurring separate legal histories? These were not weaknesses that discredited Indigenous organizing. They were substantive constitutional and political questions that active nations had to answer as their movements expanded. The interwar associations made such disagreements visible and provided ways to contest them collectively.

By the outbreak of the Second World War, the practical repertoire of First Nations politics included petitions, convention resolutions, direct negotiation, international appeal, legal argument, and public advocacy. Some campaigns had been defeated and important restrictions remained in force. Nevertheless, experienced organizers and community networks survived into the next era. The later growth of provincial and national Indigenous political bodies should not be treated as the first appearance of Indigenous political consciousness; it drew on an established institutional inheritance whose significance is already evident by 1939.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the emergence and transformation of organized First Nations political action in interwar Canada. It has argued that leaders and communities developed coordinated strategies to defend treaties, land, and self-government against a federal administration that claimed protective authority while restricting Indigenous organization.

The institutional conditions of 1918 were complex. Treaty relationships, hereditary and traditional governments, statutory band councils, and the Department of Indian Affairs coexisted uneasily, and regional differences among Ontario, the Prairies, British Columbia, and the North meant that no single political position could represent all First Nations.

Veterans' advocacy gave postwar organizing a basis in wartime service, and the unequal administration of benefits made grievances concrete. Frederick Ogilvie Loft's League of Indians of Canada, begun in December 1918 and holding its first major convention in September 1919, was the first sustained attempt at national organization, but it faced geographic, financial, and official obstacles and weakened by the mid-1920s. Western organizers built regional networks grounded in treaty memory that developed independently of Loft's career.

In British Columbia, the absence of treaties made Aboriginal title the central question. The Allied Tribes, led by Peter Kelly and Andrew Paull with the assistance of James Teit until his death in 1922 and of non-Indigenous advisers, pressed a province-wide claim. The Special Joint Committee rejected the claim as presented, a political and legal judgment that did not settle the historical question, and the March 31, 1927 amendment to the Indian Act restricted fundraising for claims, constraining the kind of organizing the Allied Tribes had practiced.

Among the Haudenosaunee, Deskaheh's appeal to the League of Nations asserted sovereignty on an international stage and failed in the face of British objection. At home, the federal government replaced the Confederacy Council with an elected council in 1924, exploiting real divisions within Six Nations while imposing its own preferred arrangement over the Council's objections.

Treaty-making and treaty interpretation remained contested. The Williams Treaties of 1923 and Treaty 11 of 1921 each produced written texts that differed from signatories' understandings, and the Natural Resources Transfer Agreements of 1930 shifted resource jurisdiction among governments without Indigenous participation, with consequences for treaty harvesting rights.

The status system, with its discrimination against women who married non-status men, shaped membership and community life, and women took part in political life in ways official sources often obscured. The prosecution of the potlatch after the Cranmer potlatch of 1921 and the strengthening of compulsory school attendance in 1920 showed state efforts to reshape the institutions through which communities exercised authority.

The Métis comparison in Alberta showed how people with different legal relationships to the Crown pursued different strategies: Métis leaders, addressing a province, obtained the Ewing Commission and the 1938 Métis Population Betterment Act, while First Nations treaty organizations negotiated within federal constraints and established the Indian Association of Alberta in 1939.

Strategies of resistance ranged from petitions and conventions to international appeals and the defense of traditional councils. Historians interpret these as administrative reform, treaty-based nationhood, or strategic adaptation, and the categories overlap even though their assumptions differ.

By 1939 the Indian Act's powers and the 1927 restriction remained in force, and claims remained unresolved. Yet First Nations had preserved and developed networks, institutions, and arguments that later generations built upon. The chapter's central finding is that the interwar period preserved the capacity to pursue Indigenous political claims, a capacity that Chapters 99 through 108 and Volume X show being exercised in new circumstances.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows First Nations politics as a story of institution-building under constraint. The interwar years are sometimes remembered only as a period of legal repression and failed petitions. The evidence supports a fuller picture: leaders and communities built conventions, coalitions, councils, and networks, argued their cases in Parliament and in Europe, and kept seeking recognition when doing so was costly and legally risky.

It also reveals how a settler state could govern through law and administration. The 1927 amendment, the replacement of the Six Nations council, the prosecution of the potlatch, and the inter-governmental resource transfers each operated through ordinary legal instruments, not only force. Understanding these mechanisms helps explain why Indigenous claims remained unresolved and why later reform required changes to statutes, courts, and constitutional understandings, as Chapters 114 to 118 show.

The chapter connects directly to its neighbors. Chapter 97 shows the United States attempting reorganization from above; here, Canadian organizers built institutions without such a program, and met official resistance. Chapter 101 shows how wartime service again became a political resource, and Chapters 106 to 108 show the repeal of the 1927 restriction and the new organizations that followed.

Continuities survive beyond 1939: treaty memory as political argument, the tension between negotiation and refusal of jurisdiction, the debate over elected and hereditary governance, and the distinction between First Nations and Métis paths. Understanding the interwar period on its own terms, without importing later achievements, lets students see why the capacity to keep pursuing claims was itself a historical achievement.
