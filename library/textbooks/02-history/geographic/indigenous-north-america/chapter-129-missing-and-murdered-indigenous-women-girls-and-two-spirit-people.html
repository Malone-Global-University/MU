Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women, Girls, and Two-Spirit People

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History
Content note. This chapter discusses violence, disappearance, and death, including violence against children and youth. It names victims only where families or public records have made their names part of the historical record, and it does not reconstruct the circumstances of any death. Readers who need support may wish to contact a trusted person, a local Indigenous health or women's service, or a crisis line in their region.

Chapter Opening

The crisis examined in this chapter did not begin when governments started to count it. Families searched for daughters, sisters, mothers, aunts, and Two-Spirit relatives long before inquiries, task forces, or statutes existed, and they often searched against institutions that were slow to listen. The chapter's central question is the distance between recognizing the scale of violence and building systems that actually prevent it, investigate it, and deliver justice.

In the spring of 2006, families, Indigenous leaders, police officers, provincial officials, and service providers gathered in Prince George, British Columbia, to discuss a stretch of road. Highway 16, running roughly 720 kilometres between Prince George and Prince Rupert through the territories of many First Nations, had become associated in public discussion with the disappearances and deaths of women and girls, most of them Indigenous. The gathering, the Highway of Tears Symposium, produced a document of 33 recommendations. That the meeting was needed at all says something about how the crisis was first understood: not as a headline, but as a long accumulation of unanswered questions held mostly by families.

Four years later, in 2010, the Native Women's Association of Canada reported that its Sisters in Spirit research initiative had documented 582 cases of missing and murdered Indigenous women and girls. In 2014 the Royal Canadian Mounted Police published its own overview recording 1,181 cases within defined periods and categories. In 2018 the Urban Indian Health Institute in Seattle reported 506 cases in 71 selected American cities, and it found that the records meant to track such cases were incomplete, inconsistent, and in many instances silent about who the victims were. These numbers are not interchangeable and cannot be added together. Each was produced by a different organization, with different definitions, time frames, sources, and purposes. Part of the work of this chapter is learning to read them properly.

The chapter therefore treats the history of this crisis as a history of conditions, documentation, and response. The conditions include settler colonialism, the disruption of Indigenous governance, gender discrimination built into colonial law, economic marginalization, and the fragmentation of criminal jurisdiction. The documentation includes family testimony, Indigenous-led databases, police files, and government audits. The response includes search parties, vigils, public inquiries, statutes, task forces, and, increasingly, demands that Indigenous nations and communities hold meaningful authority over the safety of their own people.

Three distinctions govern the narrative. First, violence and disappearance are not identical: some cases involve homicide, some disappearance, some suspicious death, some unresolved circumstances, and some situations in which a person was first reported missing and later found. Second, patterns of institutional failure do not mean that every case has the same cause or the same perpetrator; the historical argument concerns structural conditions and institutional responses, not a single explanation of who commits violence. Third, the experiences of First Nations, Inuit, Métis, American Indian, Alaska Native, and other Indigenous peoples, and of Two-Spirit and other gender-diverse people within them, are not interchangeable. They are shaped by different laws, geographies, governments, and community institutions.

The evidence is unusual in its variety and in its fragility. Historians here rely on public inquiry transcripts and reports, parliamentary and congressional records, statutes, police operational reports, government audits, community-based research, advocacy publications, and the public statements of families. Many of these sources were produced by institutions whose own conduct is under examination. Others were produced by Indigenous organizations with limited funding. Most of the cases behind the numbers are not documented in any source accessible to a general reader, and that absence is itself part of the history.

This chapter extends the reckoning begun in the two preceding chapters. Chapters 127 and 128 examined investigations into historical residential and boarding-school systems. This chapter concerns a continuing crisis: contemporary violence, disappearance, homicide investigation, and the protection of living people. Residential and boarding schools appear here only as part of the background of gendered violence and institutional inequality. The wider history of policing, incarceration, and tribal courts belongs to Chapter 130, child welfare to Chapter 131, and the general history of monuments and public memory to Chapter 132.

The chapter is organized in five divisions: historical origins and Indigenous-led advocacy; the Canadian movement and national inquiry; American jurisdiction, data, and federal reform; regional experiences across Alaska, Inuit Nunangat, Two-Spirit communities, and Mexico; and the politics of memory, implementation, and unfinished justice. It tries to keep victims' humanity visible without exposing their deaths to unnecessary detail, and to treat official recognition of the crisis as a historical development that is distinct from proof that Indigenous lives are now safer.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how settler colonialism, dispossession, discriminatory law, and disrupted Indigenous governance shaped conditions of vulnerability to violence for Indigenous women, girls, and Two-Spirit people.
  2. Distinguish among missing-person cases, homicides, suspicious deaths, unresolved cases, and trafficking, and explain why these categories are not interchangeable.
  3. Trace the emergence of family-led and Indigenous-led advocacy before governments created inquiries or special investigative units.
  4. Analyze how racial misclassification, incompatible databases, and inconsistent records affect investigation, public policy, and families' access to information.
  5. Evaluate the Sisters in Spirit database, the 2014 RCMP overview, and the 2018 Urban Indian Health Institute study as different kinds of evidence, and explain why their counts cannot be combined.
  6. Describe the Highway 16 corridor as a historical case study in transportation vulnerability and institutional response.
  7. Assess the work of Canada's National Inquiry, its 231 Calls for Justice, and its genocide finding, attributing that finding accurately.
  8. Explain how overlapping tribal, federal, state, and local jurisdiction complicates investigations in the United States.
  9. Compare Savanna's Act, the Not Invisible Act, Operation Lady Justice, and the Not Invisible Act Commission, and distinguish enacted law from demonstrated implementation.
  10. Compare the regional circumstances of Alaska Native communities, Inuit Nunangat, Two-Spirit people, and Indigenous women in Mexico without treating them as interchangeable.
  11. Interpret Red Dress remembrance and the Canadian National Action Plan, distinguishing remembrance and announced commitments from measurable protection.
  12. Evaluate competing positions on who should hold principal authority over investigation and prevention, including Indigenous jurisdiction and joint governance.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why is the crisis better understood as a historical pattern of conditions and institutional responses than as a collection of unrelated criminal cases?
  2. How did colonial law, policy, and economic dispossession create unequal exposure to violence, and what limits should historians observe when moving from structure to individual cases?
  3. What is lost when the number of cases is treated as a single, precise total?
  4. Why did Indigenous-led documentation precede, and in some cases shape, official action?
  5. How did jurisdictional arrangements in Canada and the United States affect who was responsible for searching, investigating, and informing families?
  6. What was the National Inquiry's reasoning in reaching a finding of genocide, and what is the difference between an inquiry's finding and a judicial judgment?
  7. Why do implementation reviews matter as much as the passage of laws and plans?
  8. How do the experiences of Inuit, Alaska Native, urban, and Mexican Indigenous women differ, and what do those differences imply for policy?
  9. How should communities and governments divide authority among police, independent oversight bodies, Indigenous governments, and jointly governed systems?
  10. How can historians distinguish public recognition of a crisis from demonstrable protection of lives?

Historical Context

Indigenous societies across North America organized gender, kinship, and authority in many ways. Some were matrilineal, with women holding formal roles in selecting leaders and controlling land use, as among the Haudenosaunee, treated in Chapter 26. Others distinguished multiple genders or social roles, and in some communities people who combined or crossed gender roles had recognized places in ceremonial and family life. Women's authority was not uniform, and this chapter does not suggest an idealized past. But colonial states that arrived with different assumptions about gender, property, and political authority repeatedly legislated against Indigenous arrangements. Canada's Indian Act, examined in Chapter 85, for generations stripped status from First Nations women who married men without status, while the reverse did not occur. Allotment (Chapter 86), boarding and residential schooling (Chapters 87 and 88), relocation (Chapter 105), and extensive child removal (Chapter 131) each severed families and displaced Indigenous governance. Those processes did not cause any particular act of violence, but they shaped the economic, legal, and social conditions in which violence occurred and in which responses were weakened.

A second strand of context is institutional. In both Canada and the United States, criminal jurisdiction over Indigenous territories was divided among governments. In the United States, the Major Crimes Act of 1885, Public Law 280 of 1953, the 1978 Supreme Court decision in Oliphant v. Suquamish Indian Tribe limiting tribal prosecution of non-Indians, and later statutes produced a patchwork described in Chapter 118 and in Section 129.9. In Canada, First Nations policing arrangements, provincial responsibility for the administration of justice, and the federal role in reserves created different but also layered authority. Police agencies were in many places distant, understaffed, or mistrusted by the communities they served, a subject taken up more broadly in Chapter 130.

A third strand is Indigenous organizing. Indigenous women's organizations, including the Native Women's Association of Canada (founded in 1974), Pauktuutit Inuit Women of Canada, and in the United States the National Indigenous Women's Resource Center and tribal coalitions against domestic violence, built a record of advocacy and service long before the 2010s. This movement drew on the earlier political traditions covered in Chapters 107 through 110 and on the legal changes examined in Chapters 114 and 117.

A fourth strand is the problem of records. Police databases, coroners' files, and national statistics were designed for purposes other than counting Indigenous victims. Indigenous identity was often not recorded, was recorded inconsistently, or was recorded by an officer's perception. Jurisdictions did not share systems. As a result, when governments and researchers later asked how many Indigenous women and girls were missing or murdered, no single reliable source could answer. The effort to build one, and the question of who should control it, runs through the entire chapter.

Historical Origins, Gendered Violence, and Indigenous Demands for Justice

The conditions that exposed Indigenous women, girls, and Two-Spirit people to violence, and why families struggled to obtain effective institutional responses.

Section 129.1

Colonial Dispossession, Gendered Violence, and Indigenous Vulnerability

Historians who study the present crisis begin with a cautious claim: colonial structures created conditions in which violence was more likely to occur and less likely to be answered. They do not claim that colonization explains any single act. Explanations of individual violence belong to criminal investigation and the courts, where responsibility rests with identified persons. The historical question is why Indigenous women, girls, and Two-Spirit people have, according to a large body of inquiry and research, been exposed to violence at disproportionate rates, and why institutions have so often failed to respond.

Several mechanisms are documented. The first is the legal subordination of Indigenous women within colonial systems. Under Canada's Indian Act, for much of the twentieth century a First Nations woman who married a man without Indian status lost her own status and her community membership, while a man who married a non-Indigenous woman retained status and conferred it on his wife. The 1985 amendments (Bill C-31) restored status to many, but later litigation and further amendments showed that discrimination in status rules continued. Loss of status often meant loss of housing, band membership, and ties to home communities, with later consequences for safety and for access to services. The wider history of the Indian Act is told in Chapter 85.

The second mechanism is the disruption of Indigenous governance. Colonial governments replaced or overrode councils, clan structures, and women's political roles with elected band councils, tribal constitutions modeled on federal templates, and agents appointed by outside authorities. Where women had held formal authority over land, lineage, or leadership selection, the loss of that authority narrowed the community mechanisms available to address violence. This did not occur everywhere in the same way, and it should not be read as evidence that all pre-colonial societies were free of gendered violence. The point is that colonial law actively interfered with Indigenous systems of accountability.

The third mechanism is displacement and economic marginalization. Loss of land, restriction to reserves and reservations, policies of relocation to cities, and dependence on wage labor in low-income settings placed many Indigenous women in precarious housing, transportation, and employment situations. Residential and boarding schools, covered in Chapters 87, 88, 127, and 128, separated generations of children from families and, in documented cases, exposed them to abuse; scholars and the National Inquiry have connected these institutions to later patterns of trauma and instability. That connection is a statement about community-level and intergenerational harms, not an attribution of cause to any individual's experience.

The fourth mechanism is stereotype. Colonial and settler cultures long portrayed Indigenous women through images that treated them as either sexually available or dispossessed and "disposable." Historians trace such images through colonial writing, popular media, and the practices of institutions, including police. Where such images shaped how officials weighed a report, a woman's disappearance could be treated as less urgent. The next section examines how this operated in investigations.

Finally, the forms of vulnerability differ. Indigenous women, girls, and Two-Spirit people do not face one shared set of risks. Girls may be vulnerable in child-welfare and school contexts; women on reserves may have limited access to shelter; women in cities may face housing insecurity or exploitation; Two-Spirit people may be excluded from services that assume heterosexual or cisgender families (Section 129.15); and those in remote communities may be far from any police station (Sections 129.13 and 129.14). These forms overlap, and the term intersectionality is used for the way discriminations combine. But they must be described specifically.

Section 129.2

Missing Persons, Homicide, and the Historical Problem of Institutional Neglect

The phrase "missing and murdered" joins several categories that are distinct for legal and statistical purposes. A missing-person case begins with a report that someone cannot be located; most such cases end with the person's return or discovery. A homicide is a death caused by another person, and whether it is classified as murder, manslaughter, or another offence depends on legal findings. A suspicious death is one that investigators or families regard as unexplained but that has not been determined to be homicide. An unresolved case may be a disappearance with no known outcome or a homicide with no charges. Human trafficking may be present in some cases, but it is a distinct offence, and the connection between trafficking and any particular disappearance has to be established case by case. Treating these categories as one obscures what was investigated and what was not.

Families and Indigenous organizations nonetheless describe a common problem: reports of disappearance were sometimes delayed, not taken seriously, or closed prematurely. The public record includes testimony before the National Inquiry from families who said police did not accept missing-person reports promptly, did not search, did not keep them informed, or suggested the person had left voluntarily. Several official reviews have said that police practices in some places fell short. Human Rights Watch's 2013 report on policing in northern British Columbia, discussed in Section 129.6, and the 2012 report of British Columbia's Missing Women Commission of Inquiry, which examined the police response to disappearances of women from Vancouver's Downtown Eastside, are examples. The latter report is also the subject of ongoing debate because Indigenous organizations criticized the inquiry's process.

Historians should be careful in how they frame this evidence. It supports a conclusion that there were failures in particular places and periods and that stereotypes and procedural gaps contributed. It does not support the claim that every report was mishandled or that police in every jurisdiction acted the same way. Investigations in some cases led to arrest and prosecution, and some police services have changed practice. Canadian police data from the RCMP overview, discussed in Section 129.7, recorded similar rates of resolution for Indigenous and non-Indigenous female homicide victims within its dataset, a finding that complicates a simple account of neglect and shows why careful distinction of categories matters. Disappearances, in contrast, were harder to resolve and were less consistently recorded.

Another element of institutional neglect is the way systems depended on proximity and trust. A family unfamiliar with police procedure, with limited English or French, or distrustful of police because of historical experience of the removal of children, may have been less likely to press a report or follow up. Police agencies in turn might lack Indigenous-language capacity, relationships with communities, or protocols for searching rural terrain. In the United States, tribal police departments often lacked access to national databases or the resources to enter missing-person data, so that a case could exist on a reservation without appearing in a national system. These matters of data and jurisdiction recur in Sections 129.4, 129.9, and 129.10.

The result was a gap between how families experienced the response and how institutions recorded it. The institution might record a closed file; the family might experience an unanswered question. The sections that follow use the term "institutional neglect" to describe documented patterns of delayed, incomplete, or unaccountable response, while noting that causes differed from case to case.

Section 129.3

Families, Survivors, and the Emergence of Indigenous-Led Advocacy

The movement began with families. Mothers, grandmothers, aunts, siblings, and friends organized searches on foot, by car, and by telephone, distributed posters, appealed to local media, and pressed police for updates. Some organized memorial walks and vigils along roads and rivers where women had last been seen. Their efforts frequently began as private responses to a particular loss and became public because they discovered others in similar situations. A family in one community learned of another family's experience in a distant province, and organizations formed to share information and support.

Indigenous women's organizations provided infrastructure. In Canada, the Native Women's Association of Canada gave national attention to violence in the 1990s and 2000s; Amnesty International's 2004 report Stolen Sisters, prepared in consultation with Indigenous women's groups, documented discrimination and violence and helped draw international attention. Local groups, shelters, friendship centres, and grassroots coalitions such as Walk 4 Justice, which organized long-distance walks to Ottawa in the late 2000s, brought families together. In the United States, tribal coalitions against domestic violence, the National Indigenous Women's Resource Center, and community advocates connected violence against women to questions of tribal sovereignty, arguing that safety depended on restoring tribal authority over those who committed offences within their territories. Advocacy led to the tribal provisions of the Violence Against Women Act, discussed in Section 129.9.

Survivors played a distinct role. Many testified about their own experiences of violence and about the barriers they faced in accessing police protection, shelter, health care, or justice. Their testimony linked disappearances and homicides to a wider continuum of violence. Their evidence also corrected the impression that the movement concerned only the dead: advocates have consistently insisted that preventing violence and supporting survivors are part of the same demand.

Legal advocacy shaped the movement as well. Indigenous lawyers, human-rights organizations, and families brought complaints before international bodies such as the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights, which in 2014 published a report on missing and murdered Indigenous women in British Columbia, and the United Nations Committee on the Elimination of Discrimination against Women, which examined the issue in Canada in an inquiry procedure completed in 2015. These international processes added pressure but did not replace domestic action.

The key historical point is sequencing. Family search efforts, Indigenous-led documentation, and public protest preceded the governmental inquiries, task forces, and statutes treated in later sections. Governments responded to a movement that had already established the issue, gathered evidence, and framed the demands. That does not mean governments had no role; some officials and police personnel acted in support, and public inquiries added resources and legal authority that families lacked. But a history that begins with official action misplaces the starting point.

Everyday Life and Social History

The Practical Burdens of Searching for a Missing Relative

Public discussion often focuses on police files and numbers. The testimony of families before inquiries and in community reports emphasizes a different layer: the practical labor of searching. Families described travelling long distances, sometimes at their own expense, to reach police stations, hospitals, and search areas; taking unpaid leave or losing jobs; arranging child care for other children while an adult searched; printing and distributing posters; maintaining social media pages; and repeatedly contacting agencies for updates. Some had to request records, file access-to-information requests, or find legal or advocacy assistance to learn what had been done.

Remote communities faced additional costs. Where road access is limited, search parties depend on boats, snowmobiles, small aircraft, or borrowed vehicles. In places without local police, families might rely on volunteers or on community-organized patrols. Many families also became de facto communicators, carrying information between police, coroners, health services, and relatives who lived elsewhere, often without formal victim-services support. The emotional burden is part of this history: the experience of ambiguous loss, in which a family does not know whether a person is alive, is described in testimony as a form of continuing suffering.

Evidence note: These patterns come mainly from family testimony and community-based research. They describe common experiences, not every family's experience, and they are used here with respect for the families who chose to make them public.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might the practical costs of searching fall more heavily on some families than others?
  2. How do these burdens help explain why families became organizers and researchers?
  3. What kinds of institutional practice, such as a designated family liaison, could reduce these burdens?
Section 129.4

Counting the Missing: Race Classification, Police Records, and Data Sovereignty

Counting seems straightforward and is not. A researcher who asks how many Indigenous women and girls have been murdered or have gone missing must decide who counts as Indigenous, what time period applies, which kinds of death or disappearance are included, which sources are acceptable, and whether a case must be confirmed by police. Different decisions yield different numbers, each valid for its own purpose and none a complete count.

Police and government records add practical problems. In Canada, the identification of a victim as Aboriginal depended on what police knew or recorded, and national systems did not consistently capture Indigenous identity. In the United States, race and ethnicity in law-enforcement records have often been recorded by an officer's judgment, and Indigenous people have been miscategorized as white, Hispanic, or Asian, or listed as unknown. Because Indigenous identity can be expressed through tribal citizenship, family, community, or self-identification rather than appearance, the practice of recording race by observation produces systematic undercounting. National systems, state databases, tribal police files, and medical examiner records also operate separately. A case might be entered in one and not another, and tribal police often lacked access to some national databases.

The distinction between a documented case count and a population-wide estimate is critical. A documented count is the number of cases that a given researcher or agency has identified and verified. It is a minimum with respect to what it examined; it is not an estimate of the total number of cases that occurred, and it is not a rate. A population-wide estimate requires a defined population, a complete or representative sample, and a consistent definition. Most MMIWG counts are documented counts, and the organizations that produced them say so. Treating such counts as national totals, or comparing them across studies, creates errors.

Historical Vocabulary
Missing Persons, Homicide, Unresolved Cases, and Data Misclassification

Definition. A missing-person report records that someone cannot be located; a homicide is a death caused by another person; an unresolved case is one without a final determination or resolution; and misclassification is the recording of a person's race, ethnicity, or Indigenous identity incorrectly, or not at all.

Historical context. Systems for recording these categories were created by police services, coroners, health agencies, and statistical offices for administrative purposes. They did not share definitions and rarely treated Indigenous identity as a field requiring verification with communities.

Why it matters. What a database counts determines what a researcher can know. A number of "Indigenous victims" in police data means "victims recorded as Indigenous by that police service under its rules," which may differ from the number of victims who were Indigenous.

Potential misunderstanding. A larger number in one report than another does not show that one is wrong. The reports may cover different periods, regions, and categories. Counts from different systems should not be added.

Indigenous data sovereignty is the principle that Indigenous nations and communities have the right to govern the collection, ownership, and use of data about their peoples. In Canada, First Nations have articulated the principles of OCAP (ownership, control, access, and possession), developed through the First Nations Information Governance Centre. Internationally, the CARE principles (collective benefit, authority to control, responsibility, ethics) were proposed in 2019 by Indigenous data scholars as a complement to open-data frameworks. These ideas matter for this history because Indigenous-led databases such as Sisters in Spirit were created partly as alternatives to external record-keeping, and because families and communities may wish to control how a loved one's information is used.

Data deficiencies are not merely technical. They affect how investigations proceed, because patterns, such as repeated disappearances along a corridor, are invisible if records are fragmented. They affect public policy, because governments allocate resources according to what they can measure. And they affect families, who may be unable to obtain information about their relative's case because agencies cannot find, share, or release it. The history in the following divisions repeatedly returns to this problem: each major initiative, from Sisters in Spirit to the 2018 urban study to the statutes of 2020, was in part an attempt to improve the record.

Section 129.5

Sisters in Spirit and the Documentation of Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women

Sisters in Spirit was a research, education, and policy initiative of the Native Women's Association of Canada (NWAC), funded beginning in 2005 through a federal program for women's equality. Its purpose was to document the circumstances and the numbers of missing and murdered Indigenous women and girls, to raise public awareness, and to press for policy change. The project's method distinguished it from police record-keeping. It worked with families and communities, collected information from public sources such as media reports and police and coroner information where available, and drew on family accounts, creating a database in which cases could be recorded even when police data did not identify the victim as Indigenous.

In 2010, NWAC published What Their Stories Tell Us: Research Findings from the Sisters in Spirit Initiative. The report identified 582 cases of missing or murdered Indigenous women and girls across Canada. NWAC reported that a majority of the cases involved homicide, and that a smaller number involved disappearance or suspicious death, with cases drawn from a span of several decades and concentrated in the western provinces, with the period since 2000 heavily represented. NWAC also stated that the number was a minimum rather than an estimate of the total, because the project relied on verifiable information, and information was incomplete.

The report's classification was more specific than a simple missing-versus-murdered division. Of the 582 documented cases, 393 involved deaths attributed to homicide or negligence, 115 involved missing women or girls, 21 involved deaths considered suspicious, and 53 had an unknown case nature. These were the project's evidence categories, rather than verdicts in individual prosecutions. Recording the unknown category openly was itself a methodological decision: it preserved cases requiring further inquiry instead of forcing uncertain material into a definitive label. Those distinctions matter when comparing Indigenous research to police homicide statistics, and when a family continues seeking answers after an official file is closed.

The scope and limits of the 582 count are important. It is a documented-case figure, not an incidence rate. It covers a specific time span and sources. It was assembled by a small research team with limited funding and depended on what could be confirmed. Some cases may have been known to the project but could not be verified to its standard; many others were not known to it at all. It should not be directly compared with the later RCMP figure, which used a police dataset with different periods and categories (Section 129.7).

The initiative also had a political history. Its federal funding was not renewed after 2010, and NWAC criticized the decision, arguing that the loss of funding interrupted work that families had come to rely on. The government at the time stated that it was supporting other measures related to violence against Aboriginal women. Historians generally treat the end of Sisters in Spirit funding as one of the factors that increased demands for an independent inquiry, since it left families without a funded Indigenous-led data effort. NWAC continued to work on the issue with other resources, including the database of cases, and later contributed extensively to the National Inquiry.

The historical importance of Sisters in Spirit lies partly in what it showed about knowledge. An Indigenous women's organization, with comparatively few resources, assembled a record that governments had not produced, and it did so through methods grounded in families' trust. The work demonstrated that the existing official record was incomplete, and it established a framework of community-centred documentation that later projects, in Canada and the United States, would follow.

Section 129.6

The Highway of Tears: Families, Transportation, and Institutional Failure

Highway 16 crosses northern British Columbia from Prince George west to Prince Rupert, a distance of about 720 kilometres, passing through the territories of many First Nations and through small communities spaced widely apart. Many residents of these communities, particularly those without private vehicles, depended on hitchhiking to reach medical care, schools, shopping, and family. Public transit between communities was limited. The corridor became known as the Highway of Tears because of disappearances and deaths of women and girls, most of them Indigenous, whose cases families and advocates associated with the route.

The number of cases connected to the highway is itself a matter of definition. The RCMP's investigative project known as E-PANA reviewed 18 cases of women and girls, dating from 1969 to 2006, that it identified as meeting its criteria, including some where the person was not Indigenous. Families and Indigenous organizations have maintained that more cases should be included, based on geography, time, and circumstances, and some lists cited in public discussion contain more than forty names. The difference reflects different criteria and is not a simple dispute over fact. Specific cases involved individual families, among them those of Ramona Wilson and Aielah Saric-Auger, whose relatives took part in public advocacy.

Families and advocates organized. The Highway of Tears Symposium, held in Prince George in March 2006 and hosted by the Carrier Sekani Family Services, brought together families, First Nations leaders, police, and government representatives. The symposium produced a report with 33 recommendations organized around four themes: prevention and education, emergency response, victim and family support, and community development. The recommendations included a safe transportation system along the highway, public awareness, improved communication with families, and investment in Indigenous communities. The report's methods and its collaborative form made it a model for later community-driven responses.

Scrutiny continued. In 2013 Human Rights Watch published a report on policing in northern British Columbia, based on interviews with First Nations women and girls. It described allegations of inadequate police protection and of abuse by some officers, and it called for an independent investigation. The RCMP responded to some of the findings and stated that it was addressing the concerns. The Inter-American Commission on Human Rights' 2014 report on missing and murdered Indigenous women in British Columbia and the later National Inquiry also addressed the highway's history. Transportation became a visible policy issue. The provincial government announced a Highway 16 transportation action plan in 2015, and, after the withdrawal of Greyhound Canada from most western routes in 2018, community shuttle services and local bus programs became important. Evaluations of the transportation measures varied; advocates argued that coverage was incomplete and that long-term funding was uncertain.

The Highway of Tears is a case study in how geography and institutional design interact. Distance, limited transit, and poverty created risks that were not specific to Indigenous communities but were concentrated among them because of where people lived and what services were available. At the same time, the response of police and governments demonstrated a pattern of delay and fragmentation. The historical point is the combination, not either factor alone.

Section 129.7

The 2014 RCMP Report and the Demand for a National Inquiry

In May 2014, the Royal Canadian Mounted Police released Missing and Murdered Aboriginal Women: A National Operational Overview. It was the first national police compilation of its kind. The RCMP drew on data from police services across Canada and reported 1,181 cases within its defined periods and categories: 1,017 homicide victims, covering 1980 to 2012, and 164 women and girls classified as missing, covering unresolved cases as of the end of 2013 with no determination of outcome. The report found that Aboriginal women were substantially overrepresented among female homicide victims compared with their share of the female population, and it reported that homicide solve rates were comparable for Aboriginal and non-Aboriginal female victims in the dataset.

The overview's significance was twofold. First, it was an official acknowledgment, by the national police force, of overrepresentation, and its figure was higher than the Sisters in Spirit count, which led some to argue that the earlier numbers had underestimated the problem. Second, it showed the limits of police data. The report applied its own definitions and dates. It relied on police determination of Aboriginal identity, which, as the RCMP acknowledged, was not consistently recorded across services. It did not include all categories examined by Sisters in Spirit, such as some suspicious deaths. The two numbers answer different questions and should not be compared as if one corrected the other. Similarly, the overview's finding on solve rates concerned homicides within its dataset; it does not address missing-person cases or the quality of the investigations, which families have continued to dispute.

The overview was assembled by reconciling homicide records with material supplied by police forces and by seeking to identify Indigenous victims, a process limited by the quality of data entered by the agencies. Homicide and unresolved-missing-person records were not sampled in the same way: homicides were tracked over 1980–2012, while the missing-person portion described cases outstanding at the end of 2013. A disappearance later resolved would not necessarily remain in that stock of open cases. Investigators also faced uncertainty about Indigenous identity in records created without a consistent classification practice. These facts explain why the total should be read as a police-defined reconstruction, not a full census of violence.

The RCMP's 2015 follow-up report addressed developments after the original overview and became part of the continuing disagreement over what official data could establish. Even when a homicide is recorded as cleared, clearance is an administrative measure of case disposition; it does not tell historians whether the victim's family received timely information, whether policing was culturally appropriate, or whether more effective prevention might have been possible. Families seeking an inquiry were therefore asking for more than another numerical series. They wanted an examination of institutions, practices, and responsibility that police incident data alone could not supply. The demand helped transform a dispute about statistics into a dispute about public accountability.

The overview did not end calls for an inquiry. Indigenous leaders, families, NWAC, the Assembly of First Nations, and opposition parties argued that a police report could not examine police conduct or the structural conditions behind the numbers. The federal government at the time maintained that the matter was best addressed through policing, criminal justice, and prevention measures rather than a national inquiry, a position that was criticized by Indigenous organizations and others. Political change followed. In the 2015 federal election campaign, the Liberal Party committed to a national inquiry, and in December 2015, following the election, the new government announced the process that became the National Inquiry.

Cause and Effect

Why Demands for a Canadian National Inquiry Intensified

Long-term causes

Decades of family advocacy; documented overrepresentation; colonial laws and institutions that created unequal vulnerability; mistrust of police and perceived failures in earlier investigations.

Short-term causes

The end of Sisters in Spirit federal funding in 2010; the findings of Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch, the Inter-American Commission, and the UN CEDAW inquiry; the limits of earlier inquiries, including criticism of the Missing Women Commission of Inquiry process.

A change in political position, and the announcement of a national inquiry in December 2015.

Public attention to individual deaths became significant because families, organizations, and international bodies had already built an argument that these were part of a pattern. The inquiry also resulted from electoral politics, and historians note that commitments made in campaigns do not determine how a process will operate. Individual criminal proceedings, such as the 2018 trial connected to Tina Fontaine's death, which ended in an acquittal, are separate matters from the policy question.

Section 129.8

The National Inquiry, 2016–2019, and Its 231 Calls for Justice

The National Inquiry into Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls began on September 1, 2016. Its mandate was to examine and report on systemic causes of violence against Indigenous women and girls, including institutional policies and practices, and to make recommendations. Marion Buller served as Chief Commissioner, joined by Michèle Audette, Qajaq Robinson, and Brian Eyolfson; Marilyn Poitras resigned in 2017. The inquiry adopted what it called a trauma-informed approach, holding hearings in communities, including ceremony, support staff, and Elders, and allowing families and survivors to tell their stories in their own way, with options for private statements. It also held hearings with experts, Knowledge Keepers, and institutional witnesses. By the inquiry's own account, 2,386 people participated in the truth-gathering process, a broader figure than the 1,484 family members and survivors who provided testimony. It released an interim report in 2017, and its public hearings concluded in 2018.

The inquiry faced difficulties. Families and organizations criticized delays in communication, staffing turnover, the pace of hearings, and perceived lack of support for some families, and some voiced concern about the extent to which they were consulted. Some commissioners and staff departures drew public attention. Others defended the process as groundbreaking in giving families a public voice. The inquiry requested and received an extension. Historians should treat such disagreements as part of the record, since the inquiry's legitimacy for many Indigenous participants depended on the quality of the process as well as the product.

On June 3, 2019, the inquiry delivered its final report, Reclaiming Power and Place, with 231 Calls for Justice. The Calls are addressed to governments, police, institutions, service providers, industries, the media, and all Canadians, and are organized around themes including human and Indigenous rights, culture, health and wellness, human security, and justice. The report includes separate Calls for Inuit, Métis, and for 2SLGBTQQIA people (Section 129.15). It framed the violence as rooted in colonization and in the denial of Indigenous rights, and it emphasized Indigenous leadership of solutions.

The report concluded that the violence amounted to genocide. The inquiry reached this finding by examining the experience of Indigenous women and girls in light of the definition of genocide in the 1948 Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide, which includes acts such as causing serious bodily or mental harm and imposing conditions calculated to bring about destruction of a group, and it relied on legal analysis accompanying the report. It argued that colonial policies, including the Indian Act, residential schools, child removal, and the failure to address violence, together amounted to a pattern of deliberate and persistent actions, which it described as race-based, colonial, and ongoing. The Canadian government subsequently acknowledged the finding in public statements.

The finding must be attributed accurately. It is the conclusion of a public inquiry, supported by the commissioners' reasoning. It is not a criminal conviction; no court has tried any government or institution for genocide on this basis, and an inquiry's findings do not have the legal force of a judicial judgment. Scholars, legal experts, and public figures have differed about the application of the term, some endorsing the inquiry's reasoning, some arguing that the Convention's requirement of intent to destroy a group is not met, and others preferring terms such as colonial violence. This chapter reports the finding, explains its reasoning, and notes that debate continues.

The United States: Jurisdiction, Data Failures, and Federal Reform

How Indigenous-led research exposed deficiencies in the American response and helped produce new legislation and investigative initiatives.

Section 129.9

Jurisdictional Fragmentation and Violence Against Indigenous Women

In the United States, the question "who investigates?" does not have one answer. Responsibility for a missing-person or homicide case in or near Indian country depends on where the event occurred, whether the victim and the suspect are Indian or non-Indian, the nature of the offence, and whether the state has been granted jurisdiction. Tribal police and courts, the Bureau of Indian Affairs, the Federal Bureau of Investigation, United States Attorneys' Offices, state and county sheriffs, and municipal police may each have a role. The wider history of tribal courts and federal Indian law is treated in Chapters 118 and 130; this section concerns only how these arrangements affected missing and murdered Indigenous women and girls.

Several legal developments produced the present system. The Major Crimes Act of 1885 gave federal courts jurisdiction over certain serious crimes by Indians in Indian country. Public Law 280, enacted in 1953, transferred criminal jurisdiction over Indian country in several states to state governments, often without added funding for tribal or state law enforcement, and without tribal consent. In Oliphant v. Suquamish Indian Tribe (1978), the Supreme Court held that tribes lacked inherent criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians. Because many violent crimes against Indigenous women are committed by non-Indians, Oliphant created a gap that tribal governments could not prosecute. In 2022 the Supreme Court held in Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta that states share concurrent jurisdiction with the federal government over crimes by non-Indians against Indians in Indian country, which tribal leaders argued complicated rather than clarified responsibility.

Congress partially addressed the gap. The Tribal Law and Order Act of 2010 sought to improve federal coordination and tribal sentencing authority. The 2013 reauthorization of the Violence Against Women Act (VAWA) recognized "special domestic violence criminal jurisdiction" allowing participating tribes to prosecute certain non-Indian offenders for domestic violence, dating violence, and violations of protection orders. The 2022 reauthorization expanded this into "special Tribal criminal jurisdiction," covering additional offences such as sexual violence, stalking, sex trafficking, and some crimes against children and law-enforcement officers, and authorized a pilot program for certain Alaska tribes. These provisions are significant but not complete. They apply to specific offences and require tribes to meet procedural conditions, and not all tribes have the resources to implement them. They also do not cover every category of homicide.

The practical consequence of this layered authority is uncertainty. When a person goes missing near a reservation boundary, it may not be clear which agency should take the report; tribal police may lack access to national databases; a federal agency may decline a case that does not meet its thresholds; a state agency may treat the location as outside its normal responsibility. Reporting obligations also vary: a tribal police department may enter data in a tribal system but not in a national one, while a county sheriff may not record a victim as Indigenous. Families describe being referred from one agency to another. Oversight bodies, including the 2026 audit examined in Section 129.12, have identified the need for clearer guidelines that define each agency's responsibility. The existence of overlapping authority does not by itself cause failure, and cooperative arrangements such as cross-deputization and memoranda of understanding have worked in many places. The history shows that the quality of coordination determines outcomes.

Historical Vocabulary
Tribal Jurisdiction, Concurrent Jurisdiction, and Investigative Responsibility

Definition. Jurisdiction is a government's legal authority to investigate, prosecute, and adjudicate. Tribal jurisdiction is a tribe's authority over its territory and, within limits, over persons. Concurrent jurisdiction exists when two or more governments may each exercise authority over the same matter. Investigative responsibility is the practical question of which agency must take reports, search, collect evidence, and inform families.

Historical context. The categories arose through treaties, statutes, and court decisions across two centuries and differ by state and tribe.

Why it matters. Legal authority to prosecute does not guarantee that someone is responsible for the first hours of a missing-person response.

Potential misunderstanding. Overlapping authority is not the same as shared responsibility. Without agreed guidelines, each agency may assume another is acting.

Section 129.10

The Urban Indian Health Institute and the Discovery of Missing Data

Most Native Americans in the United States live outside reservations, many in cities. Yet public discussion of violence against Native women has often focused on reservations, and data about urban Indigenous women were scarce. The Urban Indian Health Institute (UIHI), the research division of the Seattle Indian Health Board, set out in the late 2010s to examine whether law enforcement agencies in American cities had recorded cases of missing and murdered Native women. Its lead researcher, Annita Lucchesi, and its director, Abigail Echo-Hawk, designed the study around Indigenous research methods and the recognition that police records alone would not suffice.

The 2018 report, Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls, identified 506 cases across 71 selected cities. The researchers drew on records requests to police departments, media reports, social-media information, and information provided by families and community members. The report counted 128 cases of missing women and girls, 280 cases of homicide, and 98 cases in which the status was unknown, based on the information available. Cities with the most cases documented included Albuquerque, Anchorage, Tucson, and Seattle, but the numbers also reflect each city's population, record-keeping, and responsiveness to requests.

The study's most important finding concerned records. Many police departments did not respond to public-records requests or reported that they could not identify Indigenous victims. Researchers found that race was often recorded inconsistently, that Native women were sometimes recorded as another race, and that databases did not communicate. The report cited a 2016 figure of 5,712 reports of missing American Indian and Alaska Native women and girls in the FBI's National Crime Information Center, and only 116 cases entered in the National Missing and Unidentified Persons System (NamUs), a public database. These figures come from different systems and count different things: reports to law enforcement, which include people who were later found, versus entries in a publicly accessible database for unresolved cases. The numerical difference should not be presented as a count of uninvestigated or missing cases. It does show that the systems were not aligned.

Interpretation requires care. The 506 figure is not a nationwide incidence rate, and the 71 cities were selected rather than randomly sampled; the study chose cities with significant Native populations or those near tribal communities, and not all selected cities provided data. The findings are therefore a documented minimum for the cities studied, not an estimate for all urban areas. They are also not comparable to the Canadian counts, which concern different populations, periods, and definitions. The report is best read as demonstrating the existence of a documentation gap and a pattern of non-response by police agencies, rather than as measuring the size of the crisis.

The study's sources also illustrate why comparing a city with another city can mislead. Some police departments answered information requests in detail; others declined, offered incomplete totals, or used categories that could not be reconciled with those of neighboring jurisdictions. Researchers used public reporting and voluntarily supplied community information to locate additional cases, but media coverage itself was uneven and sometimes relied on harmful assumptions about the victim. The selected 71 cities were chosen for their relevance to urban Indigenous populations and existing networks, not by a random national sampling design. A high count in a city might reflect both actual cases and comparatively successful documentation. A low count might reflect underreporting, poor classification, or limited access to records. This is why the study identified a national knowledge problem without claiming to have calculated a national prevalence rate.

Section 129.11

Savanna's Act, the Not Invisible Act, and Federal Legislative Reform

Federal legislation grew from family and community campaigns. Savanna LaFontaine-Greywind, a young woman from the Spirit Lake Dakota Nation, was killed in 2017 in Fargo, North Dakota, and her infant daughter survived. Her family's advocacy and the response of North Dakota's then-senator Heidi Heitkamp led to the introduction of a bill bearing her name. The bill was introduced in successive Congresses and was supported by Indigenous advocates and later by Senator Lisa Murkowski of Alaska. A companion measure, the Not Invisible Act, was developed by Representative Deb Haaland of Laguna Pueblo, later Secretary of the Interior, with Murkowski and others.

Both bills were signed in October 2020. Savanna's Act (Public Law 116-165) directs the Department of Justice to improve the federal response to missing and murdered Indigenous persons. Among its purposes, as described by the Inspector General's 2026 audit, are clarifying the responsibilities of law-enforcement agencies, increasing coordination and communication among agencies, empowering tribal governments with resources and information, and increasing data collection and sharing. It requires United States Attorneys' offices with Indian country in their districts to develop "regionally appropriate guidelines" for responding to cases, and provides for training, technical assistance, and reporting to Congress. The Not Invisible Act (Public Law 116-166) created a Commission, jointly overseen by the Interior and Justice Departments, made up of law-enforcement representatives, tribal leaders, family members of victims, survivors, and service providers, to make recommendations on combating violent crime against Native people and human trafficking, and it directed coordination within the Bureau of Indian Affairs.

Related reforms accompanied the laws. The Department of the Interior established a Missing and Murdered Unit in the Bureau of Indian Affairs in 2021. In November 2021 President Joseph Biden issued an executive order on improving public safety and criminal justice for Native Americans and addressing the epidemic of missing or murdered Indigenous people, directing federal agencies to develop strategies. States also acted: Washington created a statewide alert system for missing Indigenous persons, and Minnesota established an office dedicated to missing and murdered Indigenous relatives, among other state measures. These show that reform was not only federal, but they are outside this section's main concern.

The statutes should be analyzed for what they do and what they do not. Legislation creates duties, processes, and bodies; it does not by itself change what police do in a particular case. A statute might require guidelines, but whether those guidelines are written, tailored, known to tribes, and used depends on implementation. The next section examines what later reviews found. A further distinction is that American law and policy often address missing or murdered Indigenous persons (MMIP) generally, including men and boys, rather than only women, girls, and Two-Spirit people. This chapter's focus is on the latter, but the broader legislation bears on them and is examined where relevant. The wider framework reflects the fact that violence affects people of all genders, and the experiences of men and boys are not diminished by a focus on women and girls.

Section 129.12

Operation Lady Justice, the Not Invisible Act Commission, and Federal Accountability

Operation Lady Justice, a federal interagency task force on missing and murdered American Indians and Alaska Natives, was created by executive order in November 2019. It was charged with improving coordination among federal agencies, developing protocols for investigations, and consulting with tribes. The Department of the Interior and the Department of Justice developed further initiatives. The Justice Department's initiatives included coordinators in United States Attorneys' offices, and in 2023 the department established an MMIP Regional Outreach Program, placing attorneys and coordinators in selected districts. The FBI also described an initiative called Operation Not Forgotten, active from 2023 to 2026, to resolve violent-crime cases including MMIP cases.

The Not Invisible Act Commission, after listening sessions and consultations, submitted its report, Not One More: Findings and Recommendations of the Not Invisible Act Commission on Reducing Violent Crime Against American Indians and Alaska Natives and Combating the Epidemic of Missing and Murdered Indigenous People and Human Trafficking, in November 2023. The report presented findings on coordination among agencies, the role of survivors and families, services and resources, trafficking, and accountability, and issued recommendations. In March 2024 the Departments of Justice and the Interior published their response, committing to a range of measures. One example, documented by the 2026 audit, was a commitment to the "permanent placement" of five attorneys and five coordinators in five regions through the MMIP Regional Outreach Program.

On July 29, 2026, the Department of Justice Office of the Inspector General published Report 26-076, an audit of the United States Attorneys' Offices' compliance with Savanna's Act and their efforts in response to missing or murdered Indigenous persons. The audit's scope ran from October 2020 to February 2026. It examined compliance with Section 5 of the Act, which required regionally appropriate guidelines with six required subsections, and the effectiveness of the MMIP Regional Outreach Program. The Inspector General reviewed guidelines from 49 districts with Indian country and found that 39 complied, while 10 did not include all six required subsections or were not tailored to the district's circumstances; seven did not include all subsections and eight were not tailored. Feedback from tribes was mixed: some were unaware of the guidelines or did not find them effective, while others said they provided a foundation for improved communication. The audit also found that the outreach program had never been fully staffed, with an average vacancy rate of about 33 percent between January 2024 and February 2026, and a 40 percent vacancy rate as of February 2026. It found no standardized definition of MMIP work and no performance metrics, so that evidence of success remained anecdotal.

The audit made three recommendations: to coordinate with the ten districts to revise their guidelines; to address staffing vacancies; and to develop a statement of expectations with a standardized definition and performance metrics. The Executive Office for U.S. Attorneys concurred, and the recommendations were classed as resolved but not yet closed. The audit also noted practices that improved coordination in some places, such as regular status updates on pending cases and an assigned prosecutor and victim-witness specialist for each tribe. It is a review of one federal component's compliance; it does not measure rates of violence or the outcomes of individual cases, and it does not evaluate tribal, state, or local agencies. It shows that implementation, a decade into the modern federal response, remained uneven.

A second federal oversight report sharpened this distinction later in 2026. On September 1, 2026, the Department of the Interior Inspector General issued audit 2024-CR-019 on the Bureau of Indian Affairs' Missing and Murdered Unit. It found that the unit had not handled case referrals efficiently and had not consistently ensured that victims received required services; the report made eight recommendations. Unlike the Justice Department's July review of U.S. Attorneys' guidelines and outreach staffing, this audit examined the Interior unit's own case-intake process and victim assistance. Taken together, the reports show that problems can arise at several points: a referral can be received without a sufficiently effective intake system, investigations can encounter resource constraints, and families can still lack the services they are entitled to receive. The audits record administrative deficiencies, not an adjudication of every individual case; their findings nonetheless supply concrete measures against which federal promises can be assessed.

Section 129.13

Alaska Native Women, Rural Communities, and the Problem of Access

Alaska's circumstances differ from those of reservations in the contiguous states. Most Alaska Native villages are not connected to the road system. Travel depends on aircraft, boats, snowmobiles, and river or sea ice, and weather can close access for days. Many villages have no resident police officer, and some have no local law-enforcement presence at all. The state's Village Public Safety Officer program, intended to provide first-response capacity in small communities, has faced chronic turnover and funding limits. Troopers and federal agents may have to travel long distances, and evidence collection, medical examination, and the transport of victims or witnesses can be delayed.

Legal structure adds complexity. The Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of 1971 (Chapter 115) extinguished most aboriginal land claims and created regional and village corporations, not reservations, and the extent of "Indian country" in Alaska has been a matter of litigation and policy. Alaska is a Public Law 280 state, so the state holds primary criminal jurisdiction in most places, while the status of tribal authority in villages has been contested. Villages have tribal governments recognized by the federal government, but their ability to exercise criminal jurisdiction has been limited. The 2022 VAWA reauthorization authorized a pilot program permitting certain Alaska tribes to exercise special tribal criminal jurisdiction, an early test of whether expanded tribal authority improves response.

Alaska Native women's organizations and tribal governments have built local responses. These include shelters and safe houses, advocacy programs run by Alaska Native women's and regional organizations, tribal court initiatives, and community patrols. Researchers and advocates have documented high reported rates of sexual and domestic violence against Alaska Native women, and the Urban Indian Health Institute's study identified Anchorage among the cities with many documented cases. These figures come from varied sources and should not be read as a single rate. Anchorage also illustrates a pattern in which rural residents travel to a city for health care, education, or work and encounter new risks in an urban environment where services may be unfamiliar.

The Alaskan case illustrates a general principle of this chapter: problems of access, not only of law, shape outcomes. A statute that requires coordinated response means little where there is no responder to coordinate. At the same time, Alaska Native communities have not been passive. Tribal governments have negotiated with the state and federal governments, and village-level programs have emphasized culturally grounded safety measures. The chapter treats Alaska as distinct from reservation and urban settings in the contiguous states, and from Inuit Nunangat in Canada, with which it shares Arctic geography but not a legal system.

Section 129.14

Inuit Women, Northern Canada, and the Arctic Context

Inuit Nunangat, the Inuit homeland in Canada, comprises four regions: the Inuvialuit Settlement Region, Nunavut, Nunavik in northern Quebec, and Nunatsiavut in northern Labrador. Inuit are a distinct Indigenous people in Canadian constitutional law, with land-claim agreements and regional governments quite different from the First Nations system treated under the Indian Act. The National Inquiry included Inuit-specific Calls for Justice, and Inuit organizations, including Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami (ITK) and Pauktuutit Inuit Women of Canada, have insisted that Inuit experiences not be treated as an extension of southern cases. The Inuit case of Loretta Saunders, killed in Halifax in 2014, is one example of the geographic range of Inuit lives, but the central Inuit context is northern.

The circumstances described in Inuit-led reports and public testimony include severe housing shortages, which can force women and children to remain in overcrowded or unsafe homes; a limited number of shelters, some communities having none, so that a woman seeking safety may have to leave her community by air; the high cost and limited availability of travel and emergency transportation; policing arrangements in which a small detachment may serve a large area and officers may rotate in from elsewhere; and cultural and linguistic barriers in a setting where Inuktut is a first language for many people and services are often provided in English or French. Inuit leaders have also pointed to histories of forced relocation, the sled-dog slaughter in some regions, and residential schooling and the "day school" system as part of the context (Chapter 100). These are statements about conditions, not about the cause of any individual case.

Inuit organizations produced their own policy documents. The National Inuit Action Plan on Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women, Girls, and 2SLGBTQQIA+ People, developed by ITK with Inuit partners and released in 2021, is a principal document for this history. It sets out Inuit-specific priorities, such as housing, shelter and healing infrastructure, culturally grounded services, and Inuit leadership in design and governance. It was issued as Inuit's contribution to the national plan discussed in Section 129.18 and is intended to be implemented through Inuit-led structures. Pauktuutit's work on violence prevention, shelters, and trafficking has been influential.

A caution applies to data. Police-reported statistics in northern Canada show high rates of violent crime, but reporting practices, population sizes, and policing differ among regions, and small populations produce large year-to-year variation. Inuit organizations have emphasized that numbers alone do not capture the lack of services or the causes of silence. The Arctic case shows how a regional context transforms the meaning of a national framework: "access to police" means something different where the nearest detachment is in another community and where a flight may be the only route.

Section 129.15

Two-Spirit People, Gender Diversity, and Institutional Exclusion

Two-Spirit is a term adopted in 1990 at an intertribal gathering of Indigenous gay and lesbian people in Winnipeg, drawing on an Anishinaabemowin concept, as an Indigenous-defined alternative to outside categories. It has since been used by many Indigenous people in the United States and Canada to describe gender, sexuality, and spiritual or social roles, and by organizations as part of acronyms such as 2SLGBTQQIA+ or MMIWG2S+. It is not a universal category. Many Indigenous nations have their own terms and understandings, and some people do not use the term. The chapter uses it as a culturally situated identity term, and uses the specific names communities use when known.

Historical evidence for varied gender roles in Indigenous societies comes from oral traditions, ethnographic accounts, and missionary and colonial writing, which often misunderstood or condemned them. Colonial institutions, including missionary schools, laws against ceremonies, and later state policies, suppressed or stigmatized such roles. In contemporary conditions, Two-Spirit and other gender-diverse Indigenous people may face discrimination in more than one respect, as Indigenous people and as gender-diverse people, which scholars describe as intersectional. The National Inquiry's testimony and Calls for Justice included dedicated attention to 2SLGBTQQIA people, and Indigenous organizations have documented experiences of discriminatory policing, exclusion from shelters and services designed for women or families, housing instability, family rejection in some cases, and violence.

Data are especially weak. Police and coroner records often do not record gender identity or sexual orientation, or record it according to the victim's sex at birth, so Two-Spirit and transgender victims may be uncounted or misclassified. The Sisters in Spirit work, the RCMP overview, and the 2018 UIHI study each focused on women and girls, and so did not systematically count Two-Spirit people. Indigenous-led research projects, such as the Sovereign Bodies Institute's database, have attempted to include them. The absence of counts should not be read as absence of violence.

Responses have been Indigenous-led. Two-Spirit organizations in Canada and the United States have organized gatherings, produced policy recommendations, and pressed governments for services and for inclusion in national plans. The 2021 National Action Plan includes a 2SLGBTQQIA+ priority, discussed in Section 129.18, and the Canadian government released a separate action plan for 2SLGBTQI+ communities in 2022. Advocates continue to argue that inclusion requires Two-Spirit leadership in design and funding rather than addition to existing programs.

The terminology used by the movement is itself historically changing. In Canada, the National Inquiry used the broad designation 2SLGBTQQIA for some of the people whose testimony and experiences it documented, and its dedicated Calls for Justice addressed the safety and inclusion of those communities. The term Two-Spirit, popularized through Indigenous organizing from 1990, cannot be retroactively applied as though it named the same status in every nation before colonization. Older Indigenous concepts of gender, family, and ceremonial responsibility were locally specific; their histories must be learned from the relevant communities. Equally, a contemporary person may identify as Two-Spirit, transgender, lesbian, gay, or in another way, and no institution should substitute an externally imposed label for that person's expressed identity.

In practical terms, reform requires more than changing a form's list of identity categories. A person seeking safety may encounter shelters with restrictive eligibility rules, policing systems that disregard a chosen name or pronouns, and service providers without training in the community's languages or protocols. A missing-person database that records only a sex designation assigned by an official can also obscure relevant details when families search across jurisdictions. Indigenous advocates have therefore called for confidential, consent-based data systems, appropriate staff training, safe emergency housing, and funded participation by Two-Spirit organizations in program design. These remedies need to be judged by whether the people using them can obtain protection and information, rather than by whether a policy document mentions inclusive terminology.

Section 129.16

Indigenous Women, Disappearances, and Gendered Violence in Mexico

This section offers a bounded comparison. Mexico is home to many Indigenous peoples, including Nahua, Maya, Zapotec, Mixtec, Otomí, Rarámuri, and many others (see Chapters 39 and 141), and Indigenous women are active in organizations at the community, regional, and national levels. But the institutional framework that produced "MMIWG" as a documented category in Canada and the United States does not map directly onto Mexico, and numbers from those countries cannot be transferred to Mexican contexts.

Mexican law and public discussion use different categories. Feminicidio, the killing of women for gender-related reasons, was shaped by the activism that followed killings of women in Ciudad Juárez in the 1990s and was made a federal criminal offence in 2012, with state-level variations. Mexico has a national registry of disappeared and missing persons, which lists well over a hundred thousand people of all backgrounds in the mid-2020s, but it does not routinely disaggregate cases by Indigenous identity, so the number of Indigenous women and girls is not established by it. National surveys on violence against women provide data on prevalence but have limits in capturing Indigenous-language speakers and remote communities.

Documented concerns include language access, because many Indigenous women speak an Indigenous language as a first language and may lack interpreters when reporting crimes or giving testimony; distance from prosecutors' offices; discrimination by officials; and the involvement of military or other armed actors in some regions. A leading legal example is the pair of cases of Inés Fernández Ortega and Valentina Rosendo Cantú, Me'phaa women from Guerrero who reported rape by soldiers; in 2010 the Inter-American Court of Human Rights found that Mexico had violated their rights, including through failures in investigation, lack of interpretation, and the use of military jurisdiction. These cases are specific and are not representative of all Indigenous women's cases, but they show how access to justice has been litigated. Indigenous women's organizations, including regional collectives and national networks, have pressed for effective state responses, culturally appropriate services, and recognition of Indigenous justice systems where communities exercise them. Mexico's 2024 constitutional reform on Indigenous peoples as subjects of public law (noted in Chapter 1) raises further questions about the relation between state and community systems.

The comparison thus yields cautions rather than totals. The North American MMIWG framework arose from particular alliances of Indigenous women's organizations, public inquiries, and legislatures; Mexico's debate is organized around feminicide, disappearance, and Indigenous rights in separate legal channels. Students should compare institutional problems, such as language access, data gaps, and distance, without importing counts or conclusions from Canada and the United States.

Section 129.17

Red Dress Day, Memorialization, and the Politics of Public Attention

Red dresses became one of the most recognizable symbols of the movement. The REDress Project, created by the Métis artist Jaime Black beginning around 2010–2011, installed empty red dresses in public places, such as trees, gallery spaces, and university campuses, to represent absent women and girls. Black has described the color red as connected to the spirits of the missing and as visible against the landscape. Others adopted the practice, and red dresses now appear in vigils, marches, school events, and community displays across Canada and the United States.

May 5 is observed as a day of awareness, called Red Dress Day in Canada and Missing and Murdered Indigenous Persons Awareness Day in the United States. Its observance developed through community initiative before official recognition; legislative bodies and executive offices in both countries later issued formal recognitions. Annual events include marches, ceremonies, feasts, and public readings of names. Memorialization has also taken the form of murals, benches, roadside memorials, quilts, songs, films, and artworks created by families and Indigenous artists. Walks and vigils, such as those along the Highway of Tears, have served both as remembrance and as protest.

Families have a central role in how loved ones are remembered. Many families welcome public attention as a way of keeping a person's name alive and maintaining pressure on investigators. Others prefer privacy, object to the use of a person's image or story by others, or feel that symbols can erase the individuality of the person. Some Indigenous commentators have also noted that the red dress is a symbol associated with particular artistic projects and that not every community uses it. The ethical principle used in this chapter is that names and stories are included when families or public records have made them part of the public record, and that the chapter avoids reconstructing details of deaths.

Remembrance and reform are different things. Public attention can create political conditions for action, and in this history the visibility of red dresses and annual observances helped sustain the demand for an inquiry and for legislation. But a day of awareness is not evidence that investigations have improved, that services are adequate, or that fewer people are being harmed. The measure of reform has to be found in other sources, such as audits, funding, and outcomes, which later sections consider. The broader history of monuments, museums, and public memory is the subject of Chapter 132; this section treats Red Dress remembrance as part of the movement itself.

Section 129.18

Canada's 2021 National Action Plan and the Implementation of the Calls for Justice

On June 3, 2021, two years after the inquiry's report, the Government of Canada and partners released the National Action Plan on Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women, Girls, and 2SLGBTQQIA+ People, and the federal government released its own contribution, the Federal Pathway. The Action Plan was developed with Indigenous organizations, families and survivors, and provincial and territorial governments, and included contributions from First Nations, Inuit (the National Inuit Action Plan), Métis, urban Indigenous, and 2SLGBTQQIA+ partners, organized around priorities that include families and survivors. The Federal Pathway set out commitments in culture, health and wellness, human safety and security, and justice, and the federal government announced funding of about $2.2 billion over five years in its 2021 budget.

Reception was mixed. Supporters stated that the plan was a significant formal commitment and a framework for continuing work. Critics, including some families and Indigenous women's organizations, said that it lacked timelines, targets, and independent oversight, and some said they felt insufficiently included in its development. These criticisms concern process and design, and they remain part of the historical record.

Subsequent reporting must be read with attention to what is measured. Canada committed to annual progress reports, and the fifth, covering April 2025 to March 2026, was released near the plan's fifth anniversary in June 2026. Its published figures, corrected on July 30, 2026, report 174 initiatives across 29 federal departments and agencies actively advancing 163 Calls for Justice. This is a government account of activity and progress, and the phrase "progress on" is different from full implementation of each Call. Many Calls are addressed to provinces, territories, police services, institutions, and the public, not to the federal government alone. The distinction among announced commitments, funded programs, measurable outcomes, and independent assessments is therefore essential.

Independent analysis has been more cautious. In June 2026, the Canadian Centre for Policy Alternatives and the National Family and Survivors Circle published a report titled Fragile Progress, which warned that expiring federal funding put gains at risk, and argued that sustained, accountable action was a legal and human-rights obligation rather than optional policy work. The report is an advocacy-affiliated analysis and should be weighed as such, alongside the government's own reports. As of November 2026, the evidence available to this textbook does not establish that the crisis has been resolved; it shows an extensive set of programs and reports, continuing Indigenous-led demands for permanent funding and measurable outcomes, and a lack of agreed national indicators demonstrating reductions in violence. Readers should consult current official and independent reporting, because this is a rapidly changing area.

There is a further distinction between administrative activity and outcomes for people facing danger. An initiative may be planned, funded, launched, continuing, or evaluated; these stages should not be reported as if they all demonstrate the same result. A department's claim that its program advances a Call for Justice is different from independent evidence that the Call has been fulfilled across all responsible jurisdictions. To assess safety, historians and policy researchers would also need consistent measures: the timeliness of missing-person reporting, the availability of emergency housing and transportation, reliable follow-up with families, access to culturally safe support, and outcomes examined over several years. Changes in reporting practice can raise documented case numbers even when underlying rates do not rise. Conversely, unchanged or falling counts cannot alone demonstrate greater safety if people remain misclassified or unreported. These problems make independent evaluation and Indigenous control over relevant data central to accountability.

From Early Community Organizing to National Policy

What Changed

  • The issue moved from private family searches to national inquiries, statutes, and annual government reports.
  • Governments acknowledged overrepresentation, and Canada's inquiry named the violence as genocide in its findings.
  • Data practices diversified, with Indigenous-led research complementing police records.
  • Two-Spirit and Inuit-specific priorities entered national frameworks.

  • Families continue to search, organize, and press for information.

Section 129.19

Accountability, Family Participation, and the Reform of Investigations

Reform proposals cluster around several practices. One is standardized missing-person reporting: clear rules that reports are accepted promptly regardless of the person's circumstances, entered in databases, and assigned to a responsible agency, with attention to cross-boundary cases. A second is interagency information-sharing, including tribal access to national databases and the exchange of information among tribal, federal, state, provincial, and local police. A third is case review: periodic reexamination of unresolved cases, sometimes by dedicated units or with outside reviewers. A fourth is victim and family services, including a designated contact, timely notification, culturally appropriate support, and help with practical costs. A fifth is oversight, through independent review bodies, audits, and public reporting. A sixth is Indigenous leadership in design, staffing, and governance.

Canada and the United States have taken different routes. Canada emphasized a public inquiry followed by a national action plan with annual reporting, and its Calls for Justice include measures on police practice, oversight, and Indigenous policing. One especially concrete illustration of family authority came after the killings of Indigenous women in Winnipeg and the controversy over searching the Prairie Green Landfill for the remains of Morgan Harris and Marcedes Myran, both of Long Plain First Nation. Families persisted in demanding a search despite early governmental hesitation and arguments about its difficulty. Manitoba subsequently supported a humanitarian recovery operation. The women's remains were recovered, the search concluded on July 9, 2025, and private ceremonies took place on July 14 and 15. That result should not be compressed into a generic claim that searches are always feasible or successful; it demonstrates that family advocacy altered a major public decision and led to a documented outcome. In the United States, reform proceeded through statutes, task forces, federal and tribal programs, and oversight reviews, with states also creating their own alert systems and offices. The July and September 2026 inspector-general audits then supplied different tests of whether those systems functioned as intended.

The Prairie Green case also reveals how institutional accountability extends beyond the criminal trial. A conviction addresses criminal responsibility, but it cannot by itself locate a loved one's remains, make official information accessible, or resolve disputes over who has authority to conduct a search. Family members pressed governments to recognize the obligations that continued after prosecution, and search staff, technical specialists, Indigenous leaders, and provincial officials had to establish a workable process. The public record distinguishes that completed 2025 recovery from other continuing searches, including subsequent work at Winnipeg's Brady Road landfill. Historians should likewise distinguish decisions not to investigate, decisions about what resources to allocate, and the eventual results of a particular search, rather than projecting the outcome backward as if it had been inevitable.

Meaningful influence for families and Indigenous governments requires more than consultation. Advocates and some reports distinguish symbolic consultation, in which views are collected but do not alter decisions, from structural roles: seats on oversight bodies with real authority, funding controlled by Indigenous organizations, agreements that give tribal or First Nations police access to data and jurisdiction, and mechanisms for families to receive case information and challenge decisions. Federal reviewers have also noted that tribes were sometimes unaware of guidelines meant to benefit them, which suggests that consultation had been incomplete.

Multiple Perspectives

How Different Groups View the Reform of Investigations

Note: these are analytical syntheses of positions documented in testimony, reports, and public statements. They are not quotations from any individual, and groups are internally diverse.

Families and survivors: Many emphasize prompt response, honest communication, access to case information, and respect for the person who is missing. Some prioritize better policing; others distrust police and prefer community-led or independent processes; many want both accountability for past failures and services now.
Tribal, First Nations, Inuit, and Métis governments: Commonly stress that safety is part of self-government and call for jurisdiction, funding, data access, and a decision-making role. Positions differ by nation and by capacity, and some governments seek expanded authority while others seek stronger partnerships with existing agencies.
Police and prosecutors: Often point to the legal limits of their authority, resource constraints, the need for evidence, and the value of training, specialized units, and cooperation with communities. Some acknowledge past failures and have changed practices; others contest broad characterizations.
Service providers and advocacy organizations: Emphasize shelters, housing, health care, and prevention, and note that funding is short-term and demand outpaces capacity. They also stress the need for culturally grounded services and for including Two-Spirit people.
Federal, provincial, and state governments: Describe new programs, reporting, and coordination, and tend to emphasize progress. Oversight bodies within government, such as inspectors general, may present findings that identify gaps. Governments differ in resources and in constitutional responsibilities.
Historical Debate

Who Should Hold Principal Authority Over Investigation and Prevention?

The question: Should principal authority over investigating and preventing violence against Indigenous women, girls, and Two-Spirit people rest with existing state institutions, independent oversight bodies, Indigenous governments, or jointly governed systems?

Strengthen existing institutions: Police agencies and prosecutors hold investigative expertise, forensic capacity, and legal powers across jurisdictions. Reformers in this view seek training, oversight, specialized units, data standards, and accountability within existing structures, noting that violence frequently crosses boundaries and that smaller governments may lack resources.
Independent oversight: Because some failures involved institutions' own conduct, others argue for external review bodies, civilian oversight, and independent audits with authority to examine files and require responses, so that agencies are not judging themselves.
Indigenous governance and jurisdiction: Many Indigenous leaders argue that safety is inseparable from self-determination, that communities understand local conditions, and that expanded Indigenous jurisdiction, policing, and data control are necessary. Evidence includes the effects of jurisdictional gaps and the early use of tribal jurisdiction under VAWA.
Jointly governed systems: Others propose shared models, such as cross-deputization, joint task forces, agreements on data, and boards with Indigenous and governmental members, combining resources with community authority.

Evidence: Inquiry testimony, the National Inquiry's Calls for Justice, the Not Invisible Act Commission's report, the 2026 audit, tribal justice programs, and comparative experience in Indigenous policing.

Points of disagreement: The disagreements concern capacity (can small governments sustain investigations?), jurisdiction (who has legal authority over non-members and across boundaries?), accountability (who reviews failures?), and consent (how do families and survivors shape processes?). They are not a simple choice between safety and sovereignty; many participants argue that effective safety requires Indigenous authority and adequate resources together.

Why it matters: The answer determines who is funded, who has access to data, who answers to families, and how failures are corrected.

Student task: For each model, identify what evidence supports it, what conditions would be needed for it to work, and what risks it carries. Then explain whether a hybrid design could address the limits you identified.

Section 129.20

Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women, Girls, and Two-Spirit People: Unfinished Justice

The preceding sections allow a synthesis. The crisis has a history that includes colonial law, dispossession, stereotype, and the fragmentation of authority; a record produced largely by families and Indigenous organizations; official recognition in the form of inquiries, statutes, task forces, and plans; and regional differences that make a single national solution inadequate. No single factor accounts for it, and no single reform addresses it.

Prevention depends on conditions that are outside police work: safe and affordable housing, transportation, shelters, health and mental-health care, income support, and community-based programs, many of which the Calls for Justice and the Not Invisible Act Commission identified. Effective investigation depends on prompt reporting, clear responsibility, tribal and Indigenous access to data and databases, trained personnel, and relationships of trust. Trustworthy data depend on consistent identification of Indigenous victims and on Indigenous governance of information. Indigenous jurisdiction and self-government may expand the tools available and align responses with community values, but require funding and, in some places, legal change. Lasting accountability depends on oversight that continues after attention fades.

Official inquiries and reforms have strengths and limits. The National Inquiry gave families a public forum and produced a detailed program; the American statutes created duties and bodies; Canadian annual reports and the American audit make implementation visible. But inquiries cannot compel action, laws can be uneven in their effect, and reports on activity are not measures of safety. The 2026 audit shows that even straightforward statutory duties may be partly met, and Canadian civil-society warnings about expiring funds show that programs may not be sustained.

The governing distinction of the chapter is between recognizing the scale of violence and establishing effective systems capable of preventing it, investigating it, and providing justice. Recognition has occurred; the evidence of protection is still incomplete. The reasons to avoid claiming otherwise are historical as well as ethical: previous eras also announced reforms, and families' experience repeatedly showed a gap between announcement and practice.

The movement continues to be led by families, survivors, Indigenous women's and Two-Spirit organizations, and Indigenous governments. Their demands include permanent funding, Indigenous control of data and services, accessible investigation, honest communication, and recognition of the humanity of each person. The next chapters take up related issues: policing and criminal justice (Chapter 130), child welfare (Chapter 131), and historical memory (Chapter 132).

The Canadian and American records point to different institutional weaknesses. Canada's national inquiry produced an extensive program of Calls for Justice and a federal implementation framework, but determining whether promises have changed everyday safety requires sustained, independently reviewable evidence across provincial, territorial, municipal, and Indigenous jurisdictions. The United States enacted coordination laws and convened a national advisory commission, but its 2026 oversight reports found problems in both prosecutorial guidance and the Interior Department's intake and victim-support operations. In neither country does a government-issued list of activities by itself establish reduced violence. A durable historical assessment must examine timelines, independent oversight, stable resources, case outcomes where disclosed appropriately, and the experience of families who seek assistance.

These measures must also respect differences among Indigenous nations and regions. An urban family seeking a cross-state missing-person alert, an Inuit woman seeking safe transportation from a remote community, and a Two-Spirit person excluded from a local shelter do not necessarily need the same intervention. Yet each needs institutions that respond promptly, share information lawfully, and remain answerable to those affected. The historical task for this chapter is not to prescribe one national program, but to show how Indigenous advocates shifted the conversation from whether a crisis existed to who must act, who controls relevant information, and what results count as justice.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the crisis of missing and murdered Indigenous women, girls, and Two-Spirit people cannot be understood as a collection of unrelated criminal cases. Its history involves colonial dispossession, gendered violence, discriminatory institutions, jurisdictional barriers, deficiencies in law-enforcement response, and persistent problems with official records. Individual cases have individual circumstances and responsibility; the historical argument concerns structure and institutional response.

Division I traced the conditions of vulnerability: colonial laws such as the Indian Act's status rules, the disruption of Indigenous governance, displacement and economic marginalization, and stereotypes that shaped institutional responses. It distinguished missing persons, homicides, suspicious deaths, unresolved cases, and trafficking, and showed that families searched and organized long before governments acted. It also showed that data problems, including racial misclassification and incompatible systems, are not merely technical but affect investigation, policy, and families' access to information.

Division II examined Canada. Sisters in Spirit, funded from 2005, documented 582 cases by 2010 through community-centred research. The 2006 Highway of Tears Symposium produced 33 recommendations, linking prevention, emergency response, family support, and community development along Highway 16. The RCMP's 2014 overview recorded 1,181 cases within its own definitions, and its figure, like the others, cannot be combined with different studies. Demands for an independent inquiry intensified for multiple reasons, and the National Inquiry (2016–2019) produced Reclaiming Power and Place and 231 Calls for Justice on June 3, 2019.

The inquiry's conclusion that the violence amounted to genocide was a finding supported by commissioners' reasoning and legal analysis. It was not a criminal conviction or a judicial judgment, and debate over the term continues. The inquiry's process also drew criticism from some participants, which belongs to its historical record.

Division III turned to the United States, where overlapping tribal, federal, state, and local authority, shaped by the Major Crimes Act, Public Law 280, Oliphant, VAWA reforms, and Castro-Huerta, produced uncertainty about who must act. The Urban Indian Health Institute's 2018 study identified 506 cases in 71 selected cities and revealed gaps in police records; it was neither a nationwide rate nor a random sample, and the contrast between 5,712 reports and 116 NamUs entries reflects different systems rather than a precise count of uninvestigated cases.

Savanna's Act and the Not Invisible Act, enacted in October 2020, created duties, guidelines, and an advisory commission, followed by Operation Lady Justice, the Commission's 2023 report Not One More, and federal responses in 2024. The July 2026 Inspector General audit found that 39 of 49 reviewed districts' guidelines complied with Savanna's Act Section 5, that 10 did not, that the MMIP Regional Outreach Program had substantial vacancies, and that there was no standard definition or performance metrics. It demonstrates why legislation and implementation must be analyzed separately.

Division IV compared regions. Alaska Native communities face distance, limited local policing, and Public Law 280 and ANCSA contexts. Inuit women in Inuit Nunangat face housing and shelter shortages, transportation and language barriers, and have produced their own National Inuit Action Plan. Two-Spirit and gender-diverse people face intersectional exclusion and statistical invisibility, and the term Two-Spirit is culturally situated rather than universal. Mexico has a distinct legal and statistical framework centred on feminicide, disappearance, and Indigenous rights, and North American counts cannot be transferred to it.

Division V considered memory and accountability. Red dress remembrance, led by artists, families, and communities, sustained public attention, but remembrance is not evidence of reform. Canada's 2021 National Action Plan and Federal Pathway produced extensive reporting, including a 2025–2026 report claiming progress on 163 Calls through 174 initiatives across 29 federal departments and agencies (figures corrected July 30, 2026), while independent analysts warned of expiring funding; reports of activity differ from measures of safety. Proposals for reform of investigations, and the debate over who should hold principal authority, show that families, Indigenous governments, police, service providers, and governments hold different, and sometimes overlapping, positions.

The chapter's governing distinction is between recognizing the scale of violence and establishing effective systems capable of preventing it, investigating it, and providing justice. Recognition has been achieved through Indigenous-led effort. Demonstrable protection of lives, reliable data, Indigenous authority, and lasting accountability remain unfinished.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a history can be both contemporary and long. The conditions behind present violence were shaped by laws and institutions examined across many earlier chapters, yet the story told here is mainly about the last three decades, when families and Indigenous organizations made a hidden pattern public and demanded that governments take responsibility.

It also reveals how knowledge is produced. Counts, reports, and inquiries are not neutral mirrors; they depend on definitions, records, funding, and who controls information. The data sovereignty questions raised here recur in Chapter 145 on knowledge sovereignty.

The chapter connects to the justice questions of the surrounding chapters: policing and tribal justice in Chapter 130, family separation and child welfare in Chapter 131, and memory and decolonization in Chapter 132. It links regions as well, showing that Highway 16, a Seattle police file, an Alaska village, an Inuit community, and a Mexican court each reveal a different face of institutional distance.

Finally, the chapter leaves a question rather than a conclusion. Whether recognition will become protection depends on implementation that is measured, funded, and shaped by Indigenous leadership. For historians, the task is to keep that distinction clear, to preserve the names and humanity of those lost, and to continue to follow the evidence as it develops.
