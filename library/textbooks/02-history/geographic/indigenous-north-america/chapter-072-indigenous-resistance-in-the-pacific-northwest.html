Indigenous Resistance in the Pacific Northwest

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

In June 1855, thousands of people gathered in the Walla Walla Valley to negotiate with a U.S. territorial governor. The council shows the chapter's argument in miniature: Northwest Indigenous nations did not simply await American administration. They bargained, objected, fought, and sought to protect what mattered most.

This chapter asks how Indigenous nations of the Pacific Northwest responded to American expansion during the 1840s and 1850s, and how diplomacy, warfare, and forced removal transformed their political relationships with the United States. Its geography runs from Puget Sound and the Olympic Peninsula through the Willamette Valley and southwestern Oregon to the Columbia and Snake river systems and the inland Plateau.

The Northwest was not one political region. Coast Salish villages, Chinookan river towns, Kalapuya and Molala communities, Rogue River peoples, and Plateau nations such as the Yakama, Cayuse, Nez Perce, Spokane, and Coeur d'Alene differed in language, economy, leadership, and diplomatic priorities. Their responses differed accordingly.

The governing argument is that resistance was not exclusively military. Negotiating treaties, defending fishing places, refusing inadequate reservations, maintaining alliances, and preserving community institutions were all ways of shaping the future. Evidence includes treaty texts, military and Indian Office reports, trial records, Gustavus Sohon's sketches, and tribal oral histories, each reflecting a different perspective.

The chapter closes the principal narrative with the settlements of the late 1850s. The Dakota War of 1862 belongs to Chapter 73, and the Nez Perce War of 1877 to Chapter 78; only the treaty background for the latter appears here.

The chapter's wars differed from one another. Cayuse leaders faced a mission crisis and an early territorial prosecution; southwestern Oregon communities encountered gold prospectors, vigilantes, and campaigns that ended in removal; southern Puget Sound nations challenged reservations that endangered their livelihoods; and Plateau peoples defended interlinked river and horse economies. These histories belong in one chapter because they arose under the expanding American territorial order, not because all participants sought an identical political settlement.

The chronology must be read with unusual care. Some agreements were signed and ratified within months, others remained unratified for years, and the Coast Treaty never entered into force. In several places removal or warfare occurred before final treaty ratification. The resulting political order cannot be interpreted by dating only signature ceremonies or only military battles: the organization of settlement, the operation of courts, and the location of fisheries and reservations were also sites of contest.

The evidentiary record is unequal. Government files document proceedings and troop movements in considerable detail while often reducing Indigenous people to categories such as friendly or hostile. Treaty texts preserve precise federal language but only fragmentary representations of Indigenous negotiations. Visual sources, tribal histories, and descendant accounts recover different dimensions of authority and experience. Throughout the chapter, readers should distinguish documented acts from officials' justifications for them and recognize where a source study is an attributed editorial paraphrase rather than a transcription.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish the major Indigenous nations and political systems of the Northwest.
  2. Explain the salmon-based economies underlying Indigenous sovereignty.
  3. Contrast the fur-trade era with settler-colonial demand for land.
  4. Analyze the Whitman Mission killings and Cayuse War with attention to Cayuse perspectives.
  5. Explain the Donation Land Act and its conflict with unextinguished Indigenous title.
  6. Distinguish treaty signing from ratification.
  7. Compare the Rogue River, Puget Sound, Yakama, and 1858 Plateau conflicts.
  8. Describe removal to Grand Ronde and the Coast Reservation.
  9. Assess reserved fishing rights as rights retained, not granted.
  10. Evaluate Leschi's prosecution as a contested case.
  11. Interpret treaties as both dispossession and preservation.
  12. Explain the continuity of Indigenous institutions after the wars.

Historical Context

By the middle 1840s the Pacific Northwest was a contested region rather than an empty frontier newly available for American settlement. Indigenous nations maintained established political communities and regional economies; Hudson’s Bay Company traders, American and British missionaries, and emigrants negotiated with those nations for passage, labor, and access. Epidemics had devastated some communities, but the survivors still possessed customary lands and obligations to particular fisheries, prairies, and villages. The 1846 agreement between Britain and the United States allocated imperial claims without securing Indigenous consent.

Congress created Oregon Territory in 1848, shortly after the killings at Whitman Mission and the onset of the Cayuse War, and established Washington Territory in 1853. The Donation Land Act of 1850 accelerated settlement before all Indigenous rights were addressed by treaties. Federal officials then pursued cessions and reservations, while local volunteers and regular troops attempted to secure places where settler and Indigenous claims collided. The pattern varied sharply between Coast Salish fisheries, western Oregon valleys, and the horse and salmon economies of the Plateau.

The vocabulary of treaty documents—cession, reservation, annuity, jurisdiction, and exclusive use—was written principally in English for American institutions. Indigenous participants brought different systems of authority and historical experience to the councils. Many sought to protect fishing and gathering sites, family security, and future generations; others rejected boundaries that made their economies unworkable. When conflict followed, settler sources often depicted resistance as a breach of a completed settlement even when the agreement had not yet been ratified.

The chapter therefore distinguishes four overlapping processes: immigrant settlement, diplomatic treaty-making, military action, and forced reservation residence. These processes did not always proceed in that order, and the same nation might use negotiation and armed defense at different times. Its central interpretation is that the wars and treaties of the 1850s transformed the American territorial order but did not extinguish Indigenous political life or the rights reserved in binding agreements.

Division I — Indigenous Homelands and the Coming of American Expansion

Section 72.1

Indigenous Peoples and Political Geography of the Pacific Northwest

Before American expansion, the Northwest contained many independent peoples. On Puget Sound, Coast Salish communities were organized around villages, kin networks, and shared watersheds rather than centralized states. Chinookan peoples held towns along the lower and middle Columbia; the Kalapuya and Molala occupied the Willamette Valley and nearby uplands; and Takelma, Shasta, and Athabaskan-speaking peoples lived in the Rogue River country. East of the Cascades, Yakama, Cayuse, Walla Walla, Umatilla, Nez Perce, Spokane, Coeur d'Alene, Palouse, and others maintained inland political networks tied by marriage, trade, and shared use of resources.

Leadership differed: coastal headmen drew authority from lineage, wealth, and skill; Plateau bands often combined village leaders, war leaders, and councils. These were not a confederation awaiting American administration, and later treaty commissioners' consolidation of many communities into "tribes" often imposed political unities that had not existed.

The coast, valleys, and plateau of the Northwest were not merely environmental zones; they were places whose peoples organized authority through relationships with specific waters, fisheries, burial grounds, gathering areas, and neighbors. Communities around the Salish Sea spoke several Coast Salish languages and maintained extensive networks of kinship without becoming a single Coast Salish government. Nisqually, Puyallup, Squaxin Island, Suquamish, Duwamish, Lummi, and other peoples occupied distinct and sometimes overlapping areas. A chief influential in one village might have relatives and obligations in another, but a signature obtained from that person could not automatically alienate the rights of every related community. This distinction became fundamental when American treaty agents demanded a few representatives for large groups.

Along the lower Columbia, Chinookan-speaking towns connected the coast and interior through trading stations, fisheries, and canoe routes. Their authority rested on particular village sites and commercial relationships as well as the ability to negotiate access and passage. In the Willamette Valley, Kalapuya peoples organized around local communities that managed valley bottoms, prairies, and adjoining uplands. The term Kalapuya includes several related peoples; it must not be mistaken for a single centralized polity. Molala communities occupied different ecological and political positions in the Cascade foothills. Southwest Oregon included Takelma-speaking peoples of the upper Rogue region, Shasta-related communities, and Athabaskan-speaking groups along rivers and coastal valleys, none properly reduced to one generic 'Rogue River tribe.'

East of the Cascades, the Columbia Plateau was integrated by major waterways and seasonal resource rounds but politically diverse. Yakama communities and associated bands used several river basins and upland gathering places. Cayuse, Walla Walla, Umatilla, and Nimiipuu communities held their own territories and histories; the Nez Perce name comes from outside usage, while Nimiipuu is an Indigenous identification. Spokane, Coeur d'Alene (Schitsu'umsh), Palouse, and neighboring peoples participated in trade, diplomacy, and sometimes common defense while remaining distinct political actors. Horses, adopted in different places and periods, changed inland mobility and warfare but did not erase long-standing fishing and gathering economies.

The word 'tribe' occurs repeatedly in nineteenth-century treaty texts, often as a governmental classification that differs from how people identified themselves. American officials sought a head chief, a tract of land, and a reservation boundary, partly because these categories made negotiations legible to Washington. A treaty council might bring together several speakers whose legitimacy rested on different local constituencies. One leader's willingness to preserve peace might coexist with another community's objection to a proposed cession. The chapter accordingly uses the specific national names and historical communities whenever evidence permits, recognizing that spellings have changed and contemporary tribal governments may use names different from those in 1850s records.

The catastrophe of epidemics before large-scale settlement makes this political geography harder to reconstruct. Villages whose populations had declined were still communities with laws, sacred places, and inherited entitlements. Small surviving populations did not create vacant land. The fundamental analytical question is therefore not whether a future American surveyor could draw a continuous line around Oregon or Washington Territory, but whose consent, use, and governance were visible when newcomers treated those territories as open for settlement. This distinction between a map's claim and actual Indigenous political relationships will recur in the treaty councils and the reservation removals.

Section 72.2

Salmon, Rivers, Trade, Land, and the Northwest Indigenous World

Indigenous sovereignty rested on ecological knowledge. Salmon runs, shellfish beds, camas fields, cedar for canoes and houses, and seasonal movement structured economic life. On the Plateau, horses expanded travel and trade. Celilo Falls on the Columbia was a major fishing and exchange center, linking coast and interior, and Puget Sound waterways and interior valleys served similar roles.

For this reason, control of fishing stations, trails, and gathering grounds mattered as much as village sites. Families held rights to particular places, and mobility between them sustained the economy. This explains why treaty language protecting fishing "at all usual and accustomed places" later carried such weight (§72.16).

Salmon was central to many Northwest societies, but an account based only on fish would obscure their environmental knowledge. Families scheduled seasonal rounds around different salmon runs, shellfish beaches, berries, roots, game, and the materials needed for housing, canoes, baskets, and clothing. Along the Columbia, fishing platforms and rapids were places of work, exchange, kinship, and spiritual obligation. At Celilo Falls and The Dalles, people from many communities gathered for commerce, food processing, marriage arrangements, and diplomacy. These places linked inland horse routes to coastal canoe routes long before Americans founded territorial towns.

Rights in these resources were often held or exercised through families, villages, customary relationships, and negotiated access. The precise form varied greatly: a river fishing station might have a recognized steward or family right, while broad hunting or root-gathering grounds could be used by overlapping groups under understood obligations. A seasonal camp abandoned for part of the year was not vacant in the sense assumed by a settler's land claim. Fish-drying locations, seasonal houses, and a network of gathering sites formed an economic geography that a small fixed reservation could not readily reproduce. For coastal peoples, access to tidal shores mattered alongside access to river mouths and forests.

Camas harvesting provides another example of governance through ecology. In wet prairies, women often held detailed knowledge of growth cycles, productive patches, digging methods, processing, and the controlled use of fire that could maintain open landscapes. Prairie burning and selective harvesting were interventions in the environment, not signs that land was untouched. Cedar trees supplied wood for houses and canoes and fibers for weaving. Labor, ceremony, and intergenerational instruction were embedded in these practices. When fences, roads, and settler livestock changed access to camas grounds, the harm extended beyond a lost commodity to the interruption of social knowledge and responsibilities.

Inland, horses increased travel distances and supported commercial movement among the Nez Perce, Cayuse, and other Plateau peoples. Yet a leader's ability to move horses along a road, to reach traditional fisheries, or to cross a mountain pass relied upon specific agreements and relationships rather than unlimited freedom of transit. Waterways that Americans treated as navigational avenues were also Indigenous roads and food systems. The Columbia, Snake, Yakima, and Nisqually rivers were corridors with established social geography, not obstacles to be conquered by arriving armies. This is why later attempts to regulate crossings and restrict off-reservation fishing produced disputes that were as political as they were economic.

The Northwest treaties reserved rights to fish at usual and accustomed sites because negotiators understood that confinement to small reservations could destroy existing economies. That phrase carried contested meanings in 1855 and continued to do so long afterward. In Indigenous understanding, reserved access recognized longstanding practices and obligations. American agents sometimes imagined it as a narrow exception to a larger transfer of land. A careful historian must not read the later doctrine backward as though all parties shared a fully defined modern legal concept. What the treaty text indisputably establishes is that the economic geography of salmon, roots, and seasonal travel remained essential to the agreements.

Section 72.3

Fur Trade, Epidemics, Missions, and the Transformation of the Northwest

British and American traders reached the region from the late eighteenth century. The Hudson's Bay Company, centered at Fort Vancouver, depended on Indigenous hunters, laborers, and trade partners, and intermarriage created kinship ties. This commercial system sought furs, not permanent farmland, and required Indigenous cooperation.

Epidemic diseases, including waves in the 1830s, devastated many communities well before large-scale immigration. Missionaries added cultural exchange and conflict. Indigenous nations therefore entered the 1840s with established international relationships but also grievous demographic losses. American settlement, which demanded exclusive agricultural land, represented a different order of pressure.

The fur-trade Northwest was neither a period of harmony nor a simple advance guard of American settlement. Beginning in the late eighteenth century, Indigenous communities negotiated with maritime traders, fur companies, and visiting expeditions while preserving control over many commercial routes and resources. After the Hudson's Bay Company consolidated its Columbia Department, Fort Vancouver became an administrative and exchange center. Traders needed hunters, interpreters, guides, craftspeople, canoemakers, and agricultural workers. Indigenous families used company connections for goods and opportunities while also weighing the risks of debt, dependence, and shifting alliances.

Fort Vancouver connected European and North American markets to a landscape already structured by Indigenous trade. Its workforce included Indigenous people, French Canadian workers, and families of mixed ancestry. Marriage and kinship ties often made commercial obligations durable, but the company's disciplinary powers and economic priorities were not always consistent with Indigenous goals. Some communities benefited from the presence of a trading post as an alternative to violence and as a place of negotiation. Others faced pressure on fur-bearing animals, local labor, or access to markets. These experiences were varied and should not be collapsed into a romantic picture of a benign commercial empire.

Disease altered the social and political environment before the donation-land era. Smallpox had struck earlier, while a severe epidemic usually described as malaria or intermittent fever devastated many lower Columbia and Willamette communities in the 1830s; the precise pathogen and demographic scale remain subjects of historical investigation. Declining populations weakened some villages' capacity to maintain fisheries and fields, while survivors reorganized kin networks and settlements. These losses also contributed to later settler assertions that land was unused. Such assertions confused the aftermath of disease with a lack of prior territorial ownership. Indigenous survivors retained obligations to villages and graves even when houses stood empty.

Missionaries brought new religious teaching and literacy along with misunderstandings and opportunities for conflict. Catholic and Protestant missions differed in doctrine and practice, while individual Indigenous people could be students, critics, laborers, or strategic participants in mission networks. The Whitman Mission in Cayuse country became especially contentious as the Oregon Trail brought more newcomers. Some settlers relied on missions and company posts for protection and provisioning. Native communities saw the increasing number of emigrants as a threat to fields, horses, food, and the ability to control who entered their lands.

The shift to American agriculture was therefore consequential. The Hudson's Bay Company generally sought continuing trade relations, even while exerting commercial power. Settlers increasingly wanted exclusive title, fences, farms, towns, and officials who would enforce new land claims. A seasonal fishing place could coexist with several trading relationships; a donation claim purported to turn the place into one person's permanent property. Territorial governments converted this settlement demand into courts, surveys, militias, and treaty programs. The tensions of the 1850s cannot be understood without this transition from negotiated access to territorial dispossession.

Section 72.4

The Oregon Trail, Whitman Mission, and the Cayuse War, 1847–1850

Overland immigration surged in the 1840s, bringing emigrants through Cayuse and Walla Walla country. At the Whitman Mission, near present-day Walla Walla, disease intensified tensions in 1847; a measles epidemic killed many Cayuse, and some Cayuse blamed the missionaries. On November 29, 1847, Cayuse men killed Marcus and Narcissa Whitman and others, and took captives.

Oregon's provisional government raised volunteers, whose operations disrupted Cayuse families and subsistence. Five Cayuse men surrendered or were delivered, were tried in Oregon City, and were executed in June 1850. Historians emphasize that the evidence of individual guilt, the trial's jurisdiction, and Cayuse understandings of the events remain contested. The killings cannot be attributed to all Cayuse people, and the trial did not conclusively establish who was responsible.

The Oregon Trail passed through landscapes whose inhabitants had already developed relationships with travelers. Cayuse, Walla Walla, and neighboring peoples traded horses and food, provided information, and sometimes helped migrants cross difficult terrain. The scale of wagon immigration changed those relations. Travelers damaged grazing land, consumed scarce resources, and brought disease. At Waiilatpu, the Whitmans' mission increasingly served emigrant needs, and Cayuse leaders questioned whether missionaries were fulfilling the commitments they understood had been made. These were disputes about jurisdiction, land, health, religion, and mutual obligation, not merely about differences of belief.

A severe measles epidemic in 1847 killed many Cayuse people, especially children. American settler accounts frequently present the subsequent violence as a single irrational reaction to disease. Cayuse explanations were shaped by the responsibility attributed to a healer whose patients died and by suspicion of the mission's role in settlement. The deaths of Marcus and Narcissa Whitman and other people on November 29 were real killings that require direct acknowledgment; the circumstances also demand that Cayuse fears and experiences be treated as historical evidence. Neither the presence of an epidemic nor Indigenous concerns about land determines individual responsibility for every death.

The Cayuse War emerged when Oregon's provisional government raised volunteer forces and framed the killings as an attack demanding territorial punishment. The campaign did not involve a series of large conventional battles. Volunteers seized horses and livestock, obstructed planting and harvest, and pushed Cayuse families toward hunger and dispersal. Negotiations continued through the upheaval, and different Cayuse leaders made different choices about how to protect their communities. The Hudson's Bay Company and other intermediaries participated in communication. The formal creation of Oregon Territory in 1848 did not turn every earlier action into an exercise of uncontested federal jurisdiction.

To end the threat of continued campaigns, Cayuse authorities eventually delivered five men for trial. The proceeding in Oregon City in May 1850 used territorial legal forms, but its jurisdiction over the 1847 events and the evidence against individual defendants were contested. Contemporary legal participants disputed whether the men had personally committed the acts charged. The jury convicted all five; they were executed June 3, 1850. The National Park Service now identifies them individually and emphasizes that the trial was shaped by prejudicial evidence and contested rules. Presenting this outcome merely as the lawful punishment of proven killers would convert a disputed historical judgment into settled fact.

The consequence for Cayuse communities extended beyond the executions. War disrupted seasonal gathering, treaty prospects, and relationships with neighboring nations, while American memorialization of the Whitmans often made the Cayuse into figures of collective guilt. Cayuse descendants and the Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation have challenged that framing and preserve a different memory of family loss and sovereignty. The chapter uses the episode to introduce a recurring problem: territorial authorities defined the right to punish Indigenous resistance even while the lawfulness of their own occupation and jurisdiction remained disputed. That argument does not require excusing violence; it requires applying evidentiary scrutiny to all parties.

The National Park Service now presents the Cayuse Five through the names preserved by Cayuse descendants rather than only through their trial aliases. This is a methodological change with substantive consequences: the people tried and executed appear not merely as defendants in an American criminal proceeding but as members of families whose descendants continue to seek their remains. The testimony used in 1850 is also not an exhaustive record of Indigenous jurisdiction or of decisions made in Cayuse councils. A first-edition chapter should preserve these differences without inventing new first-person statements from the accused.

Division II — American Territorial Government and the Treaty System

Section 72.5

The Oregon Boundary, Territorial Government, and the Donation Land Act

The Oregon Treaty of 1846 settled the U.S.–British boundary; neither government consulted Indigenous nations. Oregon Territory was created in 1848 and Washington Territory in 1853. The Donation Land Act of September 1850 offered land to qualifying settlers within Indigenous territories before Indigenous title had been extinguished through valid agreements.

The contradiction was fundamental: federal policy acknowledged Indigenous land rights while distributing the same land. A claim created under American law was not an uncontested right to Indigenous territory, and settler claims became a driving pressure for treaties.

The 1846 Oregon Treaty divided competing British and American claims along the forty-ninth parallel for most of the region, while leaving several geographical complexities concerning islands and waterways. Neither signatory represented the Indigenous nations whose territories were being described. The arrangement settled an imperial dispute, not Indigenous title. Creation of Oregon Territory in 1848 and Washington Territory in 1853 then supplied American governors, legislatures, courts, surveyors, and Indian officials with the capacity to act upon the claimed boundary. The distinction between international recognition and local authority remained profound: towns could be governed more readily than remote fisheries and uplands.

Congress's Donation Land Act of September 27, 1850 rewarded qualifying settlers for occupying and improving land in Oregon. The statute is often presented as a mechanism of development or family farming, but land was already inhabited and used under Indigenous systems. A survey line dividing a prairie into private parcels might cross hunting routes, camas beds, and burial places, even where it did not run through an occupied house. Settlers often treated the offer of a federal land claim as evidence that their title was legitimate, while federal Indian officials still needed treaties to address Indigenous claims. The contradiction was not a technicality: it created pressure on treaty agents to make the land distribution appear lawful after settlement had already advanced.

Land policy also reflected racial and gender hierarchies. Federal donation qualifications changed over time and extended particular benefits to some married claimants, while the emerging territorial order excluded Indigenous people from comparable power over their own homelands. In the Willamette Valley, property boundaries transformed fields and prairies on which Native communities relied; in Washington, speculative anticipation of treaty cessions pushed settlers toward river mouths and prairies before questions of consent had been settled. Claims to mills, ferry crossings, and townsites could control access to routes and fisheries beyond the acreage written on a claim certificate.

Territorial institutions were not a single coordinated machine. Governors pursued settlement and sometimes negotiated concessions; federal military officers could disagree with territorial volunteers over attacks and the treatment of noncombatants. Indian agents needed appropriations that often arrived late. Congressional committees and the Senate controlled treaty ratification, while settlers could act before legal procedures were complete. These divisions explain how a treaty might be signed but await ratification for four years, or a reservation might be created by executive action where a treaty remained unratified. The outcome was sometimes legal confusion and often rapid Indigenous dispossession.

Students should distinguish the legal categories at every stage. The United States asserted external sovereignty after 1846; the territories established administrative jurisdictions; the Donation Land Act offered claims within those jurisdictions; and treaties purported to obtain Indigenous land cessions in exchange for reservations, payments, and retained rights. These were overlapping instruments, not identical acts. The American right to distribute parcels under domestic law did not demonstrate that an Indigenous family had surrendered its traditional fishing station or that every band had consented through one negotiator. The coercive interaction among these policies drove the next decade.

Section 72.6

Western Oregon Treaty-Making, 1851–1855, and the Loss of Indigenous Lands

Treaties negotiated in 1851 with Kalapuya and other western Oregon communities were never ratified. Superintendent Joel Palmer later negotiated a series of agreements, many of which were ratified, aiming to consolidate communities onto reservations and open land to settlers. The 1855 Coast Treaty, by contrast, was never ratified. Each people's history, including Kalapuya, Molala, Chinookan, Takelma, and Umpqua communities, remains distinct, and scholarship such as that of David G. Lewis provides an Indigenous-centered foundation.

Treaty-making in western Oregon was not one conference yielding one agreement. Anson Dart and other federal representatives negotiated a series of agreements with local peoples in 1851, but those agreements were not ratified. Their failure mattered because officials had encouraged expectations of payment and recognition while settlers continued occupying Indigenous territory. A community's willingness to negotiate a cession could be used politically against it even when the promised terms never became binding. The early treaty effort also revealed the administrative difficulty of forcing dozens of distinct Indigenous communities into an arrangement imagined from Washington as one or two large reservations.

Joel Palmer, who later became superintendent of Indian affairs in Oregon, pursued new treaties during 1853–1855. The Rogue River Treaty of September 10, 1853, the Cow Creek Umpqua agreement later that month, the November 1854 agreements, and the January 22, 1855 Willamette Valley Treaty illustrate the variety of peoples, conditions, and dates involved. The Confederated Tribes of Grand Ronde maintain a treaty inventory that distinguishes the dates of signature and ratification and lists the peoples implicated. The Willamette Valley Treaty, for instance, linked a wide range of Kalapuya, Molala, and Chinookan communities in one document; that legal grouping must not be mistaken for a single political society existing before the treaty.

The terms offered money or goods, assistance with farming, and assigned reservation lands, but the principal American objective remained the opening of land to settlers. For some Indigenous leaders the negotiations were a way to obtain protection from increasingly violent newcomers or to retain some part of their territory. Others questioned whether removal to unfamiliar land would be survivable. Treaty commissioners often treated reservation agriculture as a universal solution, neglecting differences between valley-root economies, coastal fisheries, and the mobility of families whose homelands extended through several watersheds. When payments or adequate supplies failed, a signed promise did not produce an actual safeguard.

Particularly revealing is the Coast Treaty negotiated in August 1855 with coastal peoples. It envisioned cession and reservation arrangements but was never ratified. The federal government nevertheless created a large Coast Reservation by executive order on November 9, 1855. The distinction is confirmed in later federal litigation concerning the Tillamook and other coastal peoples: an unratified treaty did not extinguish their title simply because administrators moved communities onto a reservation. Nor did the executive creation of that reservation make earlier Indigenous land rights disappear without compensation. The subsequent legal cases illuminate the gap between federal action and the consent necessary for a valid treaty cession.

Western Oregon treaty history thus resists an easy contrast between a good negotiation and a bad war. Negotiations occurred amid settler pressure, periodic violence, and epidemic loss. Agreements represented both efforts to preserve a people and instruments of enormous territorial transfer. An Indigenous signatory could sincerely seek protection while rejecting the idea that a distant federal government possessed an unrestricted authority to redefine village life. This section treats consent as a historical question requiring inquiry into who negotiated, how the language was explained, what alternatives remained, and whether treaty promises were fulfilled.

The Coast Treaty example also demonstrates why treaty law cannot be taught simply as a sequence of signing ceremonies. In 1946 the United States Supreme Court considered original Indigenous title in a dispute involving Tillamook and other coastal peoples, reviewing the August 1855 treaty that never gained Senate approval and the November executive reservation. The later litigation does not reverse the human effects of nineteenth-century confinement, but it confirms that legal title questions survived federal occupation and that a government could be required to reckon with rights it had assumed were extinguished.

Vocab

Historical Vocabulary — Cession, Reservation, and Ratification

Cession: A purported transfer of specified land claims or interests under a treaty. Reservation: Land designated for Indigenous use under a treaty or federal order, often after a larger cession. Ratification: The formal approval stage under U.S. constitutional treaty procedures, not identical to signature at a council.

Why it matters: A treaty could be signed yet never ratified, as in the 1855 Coast agreement. A reservation might nevertheless be established separately by executive order. Misconception: A signed but unratified agreement automatically accomplished a legally valid land transfer, or every reservation originated in a ratified treaty.

Section 72.7

Isaac Stevens and the Puget Sound Treaties, 1854–1855

Isaac Stevens, as governor and superintendent, pursued rapid treaty-making. The Medicine Creek Treaty (December 26, 1854) was followed in January 1855 by Point Elliott, Point No Point, and Neah Bay. These treaties ceded land, created reservations, promised annuities, and reserved fishing and hunting rights. Negotiation relied on Chinook Jargon, which limited precision, and questions arose about who could represent whom.

Nisqually and Puyallup leaders objected that reservations were too small and lacked access to water and fishing. Leaders adopted different strategies, from acceptance to protest. Medicine Creek was ratified March 3, 1855; Point Elliott not until April 11, 1859.

In Washington Territory, Isaac Stevens combined the powers of governor and superintendent of Indian affairs, allowing him to coordinate political administration with a campaign of rapid treaty-making. His project was ambitious: secure cessions in much of western Washington, concentrate populations on designated reservations, and preserve sufficient off-reservation resources to make the agreements acceptable. The first major treaty, Medicine Creek, was signed on December 26, 1854, with representatives identified in the document as several southern Puget Sound peoples. It was ratified by the Senate on March 3, 1855 and proclaimed on April 10. These are separate legal milestones, each relevant to the implementation of its provisions.

The treaty's first article delineated a large cession through rivers, watersheds, and other landmarks. Its reservation provisions, however, allocated small sites that many Nisqually and Puyallup people regarded as insufficient or badly situated. An agreement proposing land unsuitable for fishing communities did not become acceptable merely because a high-ranking interpreter had read it aloud. The text preserved rights of taking fish at usual and accustomed grounds and stations and of gathering roots and berries under stated conditions. Those provisions were substantive, but later conflicts emerged when settlers and officials asserted ownership or attempted to interfere with access to traditional fisheries.

The Point Elliott treaty of January 22, 1855 involved multiple peoples around central and northern Puget Sound, including Duwamish and Suquamish signatories, and the participation of prominent leader Seattle. Point No Point followed later in January, as did Neah Bay with the Makah on January 31. Their particular reservations and retained activities were not interchangeable. The Makah treaty, for example, addressed whaling and sealing in addition to fishing. Point Elliott's Senate ratification on March 8, 1859 and presidential proclamation on April 11 show that signing in January 1855 was not immediately identical to a fully ratified international agreement. A treaty could meanwhile shape settler expectations, agency programs, and Indigenous fears.

Interpretation was a persistent challenge. Chinook Jargon enabled contact among speakers of several languages, yet its vocabulary was not necessarily sufficient for precise American legal concepts such as permanent cession, fee title, and exclusive reservation residence. The English text was the document later presented to Congress, but it did not record in full the conversations among Indigenous participants, interpreters, and federal officials. Historians also note disputed marks and differing recollections of who consented. These problems do not establish that every signer rejected the treaties. They establish that signatures alone provide an incomplete record of understanding and authority.

Disputes over reservation suitability contributed to the Puget Sound War, but treaty-making also continued during and after the fighting. At the Fox Island council in August 1856, Stevens and Indigenous participants agreed on revisions enlarging or relocating Nisqually and Puyallup reservations and adding the Muckleshoot reservation; presidential approval followed in January 1857. That revision demonstrates that Indigenous objection could alter terms after the first signing. It does not erase the coercion that surrounded the original council, nor the fate of Leschi. To teach this history accurately is to present both the severe land loss and the concrete protections and revisions that communities sought to obtain.

Section 72.8

The Walla Walla Treaty Council of 1855 and Plateau Indigenous Diplomacy

In May and June 1855, Stevens and Palmer met Yakama, Nez Perce, Walla Walla, Cayuse, and Umatilla delegations. Kamiakin, Peo Peo Mox Mox, Young Chief, and other leaders held differing positions on boundaries, roads, livestock, and fishing. The Yakama and Walla Walla treaties were signed June 9, and the Nez Perce treaty June 11, 1855. The Yakama treaty was ratified March 8, 1859.

Americans expected rapid cession; Indigenous leaders insisted on preserving autonomy and economic access. Ceremonial participation, documented in Gustavus Sohon's sketches, cannot by itself prove consent.

The Walla Walla council of 1855 brought Indigenous leaders from several political and ecological worlds into one crowded diplomatic setting. Governor Stevens and Superintendent Palmer hoped to conclude treaties that would clear land for American settlement while setting aside reservations and permitting roads. The meeting involved Yakama-associated bands, Nez Perce communities, and Walla Walla, Cayuse, and Umatilla peoples among others. Gustavus Sohon's drawings supply rare visual evidence of the assembled delegations and military presence, but they are sketches produced from the perspective of a member of the American expedition, not neutral illustrations of every participant's understanding.

Chief Kamiakin represented important Yakama political interests, while Peo Peo Mox Mox had a different authority among the Walla Walla. Young Chief spoke for Cayuse interests. Their concerns did not converge simply because Americans wanted a few names on three agreements. They discussed the protection of fisheries, horse pastures, gathering grounds, roads, and communities' capacity to move across their territories. For Plateau peoples, a riverbank, seasonal mountain meadow, or fishing station could matter more than the rectangular acreage of a small administrative reservation. Leaders had to weigh the risk of dispossession against the possibility that refusal would lead to settler occupation without compensation.

The treaty with the Yakama and associated bands was signed June 9, 1855; the Walla Walla, Cayuse, and Umatilla treaty bears the same date; and the Nez Perce treaty was signed June 11. The texts specified extensive cessions but also delineated reservations and reserved rights to fish and other forms of subsistence. The Yakama text explicitly defined the treaty signatories as a confederated people for the purpose of the agreement. That legal choice helped create an enduring framework of government-to-government relations; it did not mean a single political authority had always exercised command over all the constituent communities. Different signatories continued to disagree about how particular places should be governed.

The temporal sequence is important. The Yakama and other 1855 treaties were ratified by the Senate on March 8, 1859, after years of conflict. The Walla Walla agreement was proclaimed on April 11 and Yakama on April 18. The Nez Perce treaty was proclaimed on April 29. The act of signing occurred before the Yakama War; final federal ratification followed its most destructive campaigns. Those gaps reveal that the federal state expected the treaties to facilitate settlement even while their legal status remained unsettled and Indigenous consent to implementation was contested.

The council's history also contains evidence of diplomacy not reducible to assent or refusal. Some people sought alliances with settlers or with other Indigenous nations, and others favored neutrality. Speeches preserved in federal records were translated and edited, and official reports often emphasize the commissioners' achievements over disagreement. A responsible reconstruction should therefore identify the documentary limits and compare treaty text with tribal histories and the subsequent behavior of the parties. In the years after the council, multiple Indigenous nations contested the encroachment of miners, troops, and roads on the lands whose disposition they believed had not been resolved on acceptable terms.

Division III — The Northwest Wars of the 1850s

Section 72.9

The Rogue River Wars and Indigenous Resistance in Southwestern Oregon, 1853–1856

Gold mining, settler immigration, land seizure, and vigilante violence escalated conflict among Takelma, Shasta, Rogue River Athabaskan-speaking peoples, and settlers. Treaties in 1853 failed to end violence. The final war began October 8, 1855, with attacks on Indigenous settlements, and ended in June 1856. Volunteers, regular troops, Indigenous leaders, and civilians played distinct roles; communities chose resistance, negotiation, protection, or neutrality. Most survivors were then removed.

The Rogue River country was home to peoples with differing languages and relationships to the valley, its tributaries, and the southwestern Oregon coast. Mining discoveries brought settlers into an already politically complex region, with new settlements near important Indigenous routes and food sources. The wars usually grouped as the Rogue River conflicts did not begin in October 1855; there had been violence and campaigns earlier, including 1853 treaty negotiations at Table Rock. Those agreements promised protection and compensation but failed to halt attacks by outsiders and competition for land. In this setting some Indigenous communities accepted temporary reservation residence as a defense against violence, while others rejected confinement or distrusted the promises of federal and territorial officials.

The immediate beginning of the final war on October 8, 1855 was especially consequential. White volunteers associated with James Lupton launched attacks against Native people, including women and children, near Table Rock. The Oregon Encyclopedia identifies the offensive as an initiating episode rather than simply a military response to a previously organized Indigenous insurrection. Survivors fled or retaliated, and violence spread along the Rogue River. Volunteers and regular soldiers did not always agree about tactics; some military officers regarded the attacks on peaceful camps as reckless or criminal. The resulting conflict quickly exceeded the control of any one local commander and drew noncombatants into its course.

Indigenous leadership was varied. Some Takelma communities remained on reservations and refused to join the fighting. Others moved downriver and fought to protect families from advancing volunteers and regular troops. The battle at Hungry Hill, also called Grave Creek Hills, involved armed resistance but also the presence of women and children seeking safety. Later fighting around Big Meadows and other locations reflected different decisions by different communities under the pressure of an increasingly militarized region. A single casualty number for the entire war cannot capture the range of circumstances; scholars distinguish civilians, regular soldiers, volunteers, and Indigenous people, and surviving reports differ in reliability.

The roles of gold and public finance were central. Mining supplied both settlers eager to take land and local pressure for armed patrols. The attraction of volunteer pay, rations, and compensation sometimes increased enthusiasm for military operations. Territorial politics encouraged the view that military victory would make future land acquisition secure. Even where an Indigenous community sought peace, it could be attacked as hostile because militia leaders assumed that neutrality was temporary or because they wanted access to its land. State power was thereby extended through a mix of private violence, local militia, and federal military action, not a single centrally planned campaign.

When the final war ended in June 1856, most surviving people from southwestern Oregon faced removal to the Coast Reservation system or Grand Ronde rather than a restoration of their earlier political geography. Some descendants remained or later returned to ancestral areas, while new confederated communities formed under federal administration. Historians must therefore treat the war's outcome as forced transformation, not as the disappearance of those peoples. Its history also exposes the limits of treaty protection: families who had accepted arrangements intended to keep them safe could nonetheless be targeted or moved, and federal institutions could neither prevent every attack nor fulfill every promise.

A further distinction is necessary between communities gathered at Table Rock under the expectation of federal protection and those who fled westward after the October attacks. The decision of one leader to remain on a reservation was not consent for vigilantes to attack another community, and the violence of a particular group of fighters cannot be assigned to all peoples in the region. This differentiated approach is essential when using military reports that categorize whole villages as hostile. It also helps explain why federal regulars sometimes viewed uncontrolled territorial volunteers as an obstacle to peace.

Section 72.10

The Puget Sound War, 1855–1856, and the Leadership of Leschi

The Puget Sound War involved Nisqually, Puyallup, Muckleshoot, and other participants, as well as volunteers and the U.S. Army. Leschi and Quiemuth led resistance rooted in the Medicine Creek reservation dispute; Leschi sought viable reservations and protection of homelands, not rejection of every treaty. Events included raids, civilian deaths, and the Battle of Seattle on January 26, 1856. Some Indigenous people allied with the Americans, reflecting political divisions.

The Puget Sound War emerged from the collision of treaty proposals, settler demands, and Indigenous decisions about survival. After Medicine Creek, Nisqually and Puyallup leaders objected to reservation sites too small or poorly located to support their households and fisheries. Leschi and Quiemuth became central figures in the opposition, but they were neither the only leaders nor representatives of all Coast Salish communities. Some families argued for accommodation; others took up arms; still others attempted neutrality. British company representatives and local settlers were divided in their relationships with Indigenous people, and the conflict changed as it moved through several watersheds.

The conflict spread in the fall of 1855. American volunteers and U.S. regular troops fought Indigenous defenders while other Native people acted as guides, auxiliaries, or mediators. Raids and reprisals sometimes killed civilians. The January 26, 1856 fighting near Seattle demonstrated how exposed American settlements remained even where territorial authorities claimed jurisdiction. It also demonstrated that Indigenous forces were not a single army: different leaders had different strategic goals and means. Writers who treat the war as one unified effort to drive all non-Indigenous residents into the sea impose a retrospective simplicity on decisions that were much more local and contingent.

Military geography mattered. River valleys, forests, portages, and the Cascades created routes for hiding, moving families, and crossing to inland communities. The Army could move forces by water around Puget Sound, but inland routes were difficult and required knowledge of trails and seasonal conditions. Leschi's movements east of the Cascades complicate any narrative that locates the conflict entirely within the reservation area designated by Medicine Creek. Indigenous tactical flexibility repeatedly frustrated territorial militia expectations of a decisive engagement. At the same time, the continuing presence of more soldiers and settlement networks made sustained Indigenous military resistance increasingly difficult.

Negotiation accompanied warfare. The Fox Island Council of August 1856 addressed complaints about the reservation arrangements and led to enlargement or relocation of important reservations. The revised arrangements were a response to Indigenous demands, but arrived amid pressure, arrests, and loss. They did not secure immunity for the leaders of resistance. Quiemuth, after surrendering, was killed while in custody in Olympia in November 1856. Leschi faced trial and execution despite the argument that armed hostilities should be treated under laws of war rather than as an ordinary murder. The law and memory of those events are examined more fully in Section 72.17.

An adequate account of the Puget Sound War must give the Nisqually and other affected peoples their own political histories. The causes included land and fishing access, but the conduct of the war also involved soldiers' choices, coercive removals of communities considered friendly, the vulnerability of civilians, and the construction of racial narratives about who could be trusted. A treaty could simultaneously be a severe cession and a source of rights Indigenous signatories defended afterward. The conflict illustrates resistance as a sustained political strategy, not simply battlefield action, and it shows why some of the most important outcomes occurred around negotiating tables rather than in combat.

The postwar revision of the Nisqually and Puyallup reservation arrangements is a particularly useful counterexample to the assumption that Indigenous resistance could never affect policy. New boundaries were an official response to the recognition that the original locations were untenable for established economies. Yet the revised arrangement was negotiated after deaths, flight, and dislocation, and Leschi was not present at the Fox Island negotiations; his documented transfer into territorial custody occurred later, in November 1856. Students should therefore distinguish meaningful concessions obtained under pressure from an ideal of equal bargaining that the surrounding facts cannot support.

Perspective

Multiple Perspectives — Why Did the Puget Sound War Occur?

Nisqually and Puyallup families: Reservation locations were inadequate for fishing, travel and household security; some leaders argued that the treaty arrangements violated the political and economic reality of their homelands.

Territorial officials and settlers: Stevens and some settlers treated the signed treaty and settler land claims as authority for relocation, while considering armed opposition a threat to public safety. Their accounts often foregrounded settler fear and minimized Native displacement.

U.S. military officers: Regular army officers sometimes disagreed with volunteers and territorial prosecutors about the character of the war, how to treat noncombatants, and whether Leschi should be executed as a murderer.

Section 72.11

The Yakama War, Kamiakin, and Resistance East of the Cascades

Miners' incursions into Yakama country and the killing of subagent Andrew J. Bolon in 1855 preceded U.S. military deployment. Kamiakin and allied leaders made independent decisions; the fighting was not a contest between two unified armies. Columbia River crossings and Cascade passes shaped campaigns. American claims that defeat extinguished Yakama political authority were contested then and later.

The Yakama War followed closely upon the 1855 Walla Walla treaty council, but its immediate causes require attention to the local situation. Miners and prospectors moved into land claimed and used by Yakama communities, in some cases before agreements had been ratified. They sought gold, routes, and access across regions that Indigenous leaders understood to remain their country. The killing of federal subagent Andrew J. Bolon in September 1855 intensified official demands for military punishment. Territory Governor Stevens and army officers interpreted the crisis through different administrative responsibilities, and campaigns soon reached beyond the community or people directly implicated in the subagent's death.

Kamiakin, often identified as a central figure in resistance, was an influential Yakama leader rather than the military commander of every group that took part. The treaty had linked many named bands in a federal category, but each local community assessed threats and alliances through its own circumstances. Yakama leaders weighed the protection of homelands and fishing access against the risks of battle; neighboring Palouse, Klickitat, and other peoples made decisions for themselves. Their commercial ties and relationships across the Cascade passes increased the conflict's geographical range. American accounts sometimes folded them into a single 'hostile' population, erasing the differences that shaped participation.

An early American column under Major Granville Haller encountered determined resistance in the Yakima Valley in October 1855, undermining the assumption that the territorial government could quickly enforce settlement. Subsequent U.S. Army movements and territorial volunteer campaigns traversed valleys, river crossings, and mountain passages. The war's western and eastern theaters were linked through movement but not identical. Some groups in the Puget Sound conflict communicated with eastern peoples, while other alliances were local. A map that shows only the battles will fail to reveal why controlling a ferry landing, the Naches Pass, or a Columbia River crossing could be more important than holding an isolated ridge.

Warfare affected more than combatants. Households lost livestock, fields, and freedom of movement; merchants and farmers endured insecurity; Indigenous families hid or traveled to relatives outside the immediate combat zone. The use of volunteer companies created disputes about command, payment, and the treatment of communities identified as friendly. At the same time federal officers sought treaties and peace arrangements, partly because a broad war strained the small forces available on the frontier. Even in places where Indigenous warriors were defeated, victory did not immediately establish secure American presence throughout the Plateau. Settlements remained sparse, and political obligations survived military encounters.

The broader meaning of the Yakama War is thus not an uncomplicated transfer from one sovereignty to another. It was a conflict over who could determine movement, resource use, and settlement while the legal terms of cession were still awaiting Senate action. The later 1858 campaigns would further constrain military opposition, and the 1859 ratification would impose a federal legal settlement. Yet the Yakama Nation's treaty and reserved rights show that a military campaign did not dissolve the Indigenous political community. Its ongoing authority was partly reshaped within the treaty framework and partly sustained through customs, kinship, and place-based rights that predated the agreement.

Section 72.12

The Inland Northwest Campaigns of 1858: Spokane, Coeur d'Alene, and Palouse Resistance

On May 17, 1858, a force under Lt. Col. Edward Steptoe was defeated near Pine Creek by Spokane, Coeur d'Alene, Palouse, and other fighters. Colonel George Wright then led a campaign, winning the battles of Four Lakes (September 1) and Spokane Plains (September 5). Wright destroyed horses and food stores, demanded submission, and ordered executions of prisoners. The campaign targeted subsistence and mobility as well as armed resistance.

Events in 1858 involved a new and particularly severe sequence in the inland Northwest. Lieutenant Colonel Edward Steptoe entered the Plateau with a small force, expecting to move through territory inhabited by Spokane, Coeur d'Alene, Palouse, and other communities. The expedition's purpose and the limits of its authority were disputed locally. On May 17, 1858, a coalition of Indigenous fighters defeated his column near Pine Creek, in an encounter also remembered by the Indigenous name Tohotonimme. U.S. accounts often described the battle as an ambush or an affront to federal power; Indigenous perspectives emphasized territorial defense and opposition to unauthorized military movement.

Colonel George Wright organized a heavily equipped punitive expedition after Steptoe's defeat. He brought substantial numbers of troops and comparatively effective arms. At Four Lakes on September 1 and Spokane Plains on September 5, the American column won engagements against assembled Indigenous forces. The term 'coalition' is preferable to the idea of a single tribal army. Spokane, Coeur d'Alene, Palouse, and allied combatants had relationships and overlapping interests, but their leaders and territories remained distinct. A battlefield account limited to tactics would miss the role of federal officials in demanding submission and punitive destruction after armed opponents were already in retreat.

Wright's campaign included deliberate destruction of economic assets. On September 8 troops slaughtered hundreds of horses, commonly described in the sources as roughly eight hundred, and burned property and food stores. Horses were necessary for mobility, trading, hunting, and political independence on the Plateau. Their destruction was designed to limit the ability to escape, fight, or live beyond the army's reach. Wright also ordered executions of selected Indigenous prisoners, including later hangings near the Latah or Ned-Whauld River. These events underscore how commanders used collective punishment and exemplary violence as instruments of policy; they were not merely incidental collateral damage of battles.

The surviving evidence is heavily weighted toward Army reports, soldiers' memoirs, and visual records created by men traveling with the military. Artist Gustavus Sohon sketched events in the campaign, and his work can help establish how soldiers represented the battlefield and assembled Indigenous fighters. It does not supply access to every Native combatant's intention or the conditions of families away from the front. Tribal historical accounts and later scholarship are essential for analyzing decisions to confront Steptoe and the impact of losing horse herds and stored food. The exact number of people killed or animals destroyed varies across accounts and should be presented as reported claims rather than artificially precise totals.

The campaign damaged the material foundations of further open military resistance but did not end Indigenous political life. Some leaders signed agreements or sought negotiation under the threat of further attacks, while other communities continued attempting to preserve their territorial claims. Federal authority expanded across strategic routes more quickly than it could be exercised in every valley. For the chapter's larger argument, 1858 demonstrates an important limit of treaty-centered explanations: a state that lacked agreement from every relevant people could still impose conditions through military force. The resulting settlement therefore combined written obligations, intimidation, and the continued existence of Indigenous nations whose autonomy was diminished but not erased.

The source record also reveals a chronology of choices after the fighting. A military commander could have accepted submission following the September battles, but Wright proceeded to destroy horses and stores and to order executions. That sequence makes it possible to separate actions required to repel an immediate armed attack from actions intended to reshape the political future of communities. Whether the latter were justified was disputed, and the people whose animals were killed faced consequences for their households and trading relationships extending far beyond the days of battle.

Cause and Effect — From Treaty Pressure to the 1858 Punitive Campaign

Structural pressures: American immigration, land grants, gold prospecting, and territorial institutions pushed into existing Indigenous homelands while epidemics and economic disruption limited some communities' capacity to absorb shocks.

Section 72.13

The Removal of Willamette Valley Peoples to Grand Ronde, 1855–1856

Kalapuya, Molala, Chinookan, and other peoples were removed from the Willamette Valley to Grand Ronde under pressure from settler claims, violence, and federal policy. Grand Ronde was established by executive order in 1857. Indigenous accounts remember an Oregon "Trail of Tears," an analogy that should be distinguished from the Cherokee Trail of Tears and the Diné Long Walk. Distinct languages and histories were brought together on one reservation.

The removals of western Oregon peoples began under severe pressure from settlement, warfare, and the destruction of subsistence places. Kalapuya, Molala, Chinookan, Umpqua, and neighboring communities entered negotiations while their fields and routes were increasingly enclosed. A reservation within the Coast Range could not substitute geographically for every river, prairie, or seasonal camp from which a family had drawn its living. The policy nevertheless assumed that several distinct peoples could be aggregated under one federal agent, receive supplies, take up supervised farming, and make room for settlers to occupy their former homelands.

The chronology deserves careful treatment. Forced removals to northern reservation destinations began in 1856, before the formal Grand Ronde Reservation executive order of June 30, 1857. The Confederated Tribes of Grand Ronde document a removal march begun from the Table Rock area in February 1856 under Indian Agent George H. Ambrose. The published sources give conflicting departure counts: the Tribe’s educational account uses 325, while an online transcription of Ambrose’s closing report gives 395. Both describe a thirty-three-day journey of roughly 263 miles, with eight deaths and eight births recorded in the diary. The uncollated numerical difference should be disclosed rather than hidden. The precise institutional names of destination sites shifted as temporary encampments became formal reservation agencies. Historians should not suggest that the government's 1857 formal designation was the first moment Indigenous people were being coerced onto that land.

The physical experience of removal cannot be reduced to a line on a map. People were compelled to travel during winter conditions, sometimes with insufficient clothing, food, and transport. Children, elders, pregnant women, and the sick had different capacities to walk. Families left graves, fisheries, root grounds, houses, and local political relationships behind. The new settlements often lacked adequate infrastructure or provisions. The promise that an agent would supply food did not erase the practical damage of being removed from a familiar agricultural and gathering cycle. Nor did promises of compensation replace the loss of control over sacred places and ancestral lands.

The phrase 'Oregon Trail of Tears' is used in the Grand Ronde community to remember particular removals and the larger history of forced displacement. It should not imply a single walk followed by every Indigenous person in western Oregon or identical experiences to the removal of the Cherokee and other southeastern nations. Some people traveled in separate groups, some were moved from temporary reservations, and others remained near their homelands or later returned. The community that formed at Grand Ronde included peoples who had been distinct political and linguistic communities before confinement. Their creation of new relationships under coercive circumstances belongs to the history of Indigenous agency as well as state violence.

Grand Ronde also reveals the inadequacy of using a single word, 'reservation,' as an outcome. A federal reservation might represent land set aside under an agreement, a refuge from local settler violence, and a site of federal surveillance all at once. The families arriving there did not surrender their identities when an agency combined their administrative records. Descendant governments today preserve memories of their constituent nations and continue interpreting the treaties that their ancestors signed. These continuities are central to an account of removal that recognizes both the profound involuntary loss of home and the endurance of political community.

Demography is important but easily misused in telling removal history. A count of people departing a temporary camp might not include everyone previously living in a homeland, and a count of those arriving does not by itself distinguish illness, death, birth, flight, or later arrival. Ambrose's record of deaths and births on one journey is valuable precisely because its limits are identifiable. The chapter should resist multiplying it into an all-Oregon death estimate or treating its administrative categories as a complete description of the separate nations on the march. The Tribe’s educational account records 325 travelers, while a published transcription of the diary reports 395; the numerical discrepancy remains unresolved without direct collation.

Section 72.14

The Coast Reservation, Siletz, and the Removal of Southwestern Oregon Nations

The Coast Reservation was created in 1855 by executive action; the 1855 Coast Treaty itself was never ratified. After the Rogue River wars, survivors were moved through temporary sites, including Table Rock, and by coastal routes under military escort, and the Siletz Agency developed. Takelma, Rogue River, Coos, Coquille, Umpqua, and other peoples came under new federal institutions.

The Coast Reservation was conceived as a vast coastal and inland area into which federal officials hoped to concentrate many western Oregon peoples. President Franklin Pierce established it by executive order on November 9, 1855 after an unratified Coast Treaty had been negotiated. The treaty itself did not become binding simply because a reservation appeared on an executive map. In later legal proceedings, coastal peoples' claims to compensation reflected precisely that distinction between an unratified cession and government occupation. Treaties, orders, and the actual arrival of prisoners or displaced families must therefore be tracked as separate historical events.

Removal routes differed. Families from the Rogue River war region could be held at Table Rock or other temporary sites and then moved north under military and agency supervision; others traveled coastal routes or by water. At the destination, people entered administrative units that later developed into Grand Ronde, Siletz, and Alsea arrangements. Siletz became particularly associated with southwestern Oregon and coastal peoples who were forced into shared institutional conditions. A person identified by an agent as 'Coast Indian' might be Takelma, Coos, Coquille, Chetco, Tolowa, Umpqua, or a member of another people with an independent language and homeland.

The removal policy also created sharp problems for coastal communities whose food systems depended upon beaches, estuaries, fish runs, and seasonal harvesting. Some proposed locations lacked the resources or access they needed, while administrative restrictions prevented travel to traditional gathering areas. For families who had lived on the Rogue River or upper valleys, unfamiliar climate, food, and rules made the move especially difficult. Agents promised new farms, rations, and schooling, but hunger, disease, and failures of supply repeatedly undermined those claims. Military confinement intended to provide peace could reproduce insecurity on a different landscape.

Scholarly histories sometimes describe the Coast Reservation as an early institutional whole and the later Siletz Reservation as its successor. These terms are useful when dates are clear but misleading if treated as interchangeable across the entire 1850s. The large Coast Reservation underwent subdivisions and reductions, with later administrative reorganizations beyond the central chronology of this chapter. Students should identify when a historical document refers to the original 1855 executive reservation, a particular agency within it, or a subsequent Siletz institution. Boundaries and bureaucratic names shifted even though the memory of forced removal remained with the affected families.

The lasting consequence was not complete social dissolution. Removed communities continued sharing languages, ceremonies, and histories, sometimes forming new intercommunity households while preserving knowledge of earlier places. They also contested the legitimacy of federal treatment and, over time, pursued the legal implications of treaties that had been promised but not ratified. The Coast case thus helps explain why some Indigenous rights survived American settlement without a recognized, ratified treaty: customary title and political existence were not created by a federal signature. The unratified treaty and executive reservation demonstrate both the coercive reach of the state and the legal limits of its claims.

Indigenous sovereignty: The continuing political authority of Indigenous peoples to govern themselves and sustain relationships with their lands and communities.

Section 72.15

Reservation Life, Federal Administration, and Indigenous Community Survival

Agents, rations, farming expectations, schooling, missions, and travel restrictions governed reservation life, while inadequate provisions and disease created hardship. Conditions differed between coastal fishing communities and inland farming and pastoral peoples. Confinement did not eliminate community organization: people maintained kinship, decision-making, trade, ceremonies, languages, and ties to ancestral places.

The early reservation system translated American goals into the daily life of families. Agents distributed food or tools, monitored travel, encouraged farming, assigned work, and reported to distant officials on whether Indigenous people were becoming 'civilized' according to federal expectations. Those reports described people primarily as administrative subjects and often omitted their objections. An agent might celebrate acreage planted while families were concerned with access to traditional fishing or gathering grounds. Appropriations arrived irregularly, and an officially promised ration did not mean the food was present on a given day or sufficient for a household's needs.

Conditions differed considerably among agencies. Coastal fishing peoples attempted to maintain access to tidal waters and river systems; Willamette Valley communities faced the disruption of camas prairies and riverine subsistence; Plateau people fought to preserve access to fisheries and seasonal movement. In some locations unfamiliar soils or new epidemics undermined farming plans. Crowding people from distinct homelands together strained relations but could also produce networks of mutual assistance. New forms of leadership emerged as people tried to mediate between federal personnel and the obligations of clan, village, or band.

Federal supervision affected children, marriage, household authority, and religious practice. Missionaries and school programs sought linguistic and religious transformation, though large-scale later assimilation programs should not be projected backward wholesale onto the 1850s. Some Indigenous people learned English or engaged with Christian institutions for their own reasons; others rejected religious pressure or combined new practices with existing responsibilities. Women continued maintaining foods, kinship ties, and ceremonial knowledge while bearing burdens of care amid food insecurity. These histories require oral testimony and tribal records because federal accounts usually privileged the perspectives of male administrators.

The reservation's boundary did not define the full geography of a people's interests. A relative might live beyond a line; a traditional fishing ground could remain outside; the bones of ancestors rested in another valley. Federal restrictions on movement could interrupt ceremonies, marriages, trade, and harvesting even where treaty provisions purported to protect those rights. Disputes about permission to leave or return arose because the administrative ideal of a fixed agrarian population conflicted with established seasonal economies. Indigenous people negotiated exceptions, traveled despite restrictions, and maintained information networks among communities separated by federal policy.

To describe reservation life as survival is not to minimize coercion. It means recognizing the daily decisions that allowed people to raise children, exchange knowledge, retain languages, and recover political voice under conditions they had not chosen. Many reservations became bases for later collective advocacy, including claims to treaty rights and land. The contrast between an agent's report that a population was being pacified and a family history recording the preservation of names and fishing places is a productive source study. Both records describe aspects of the same setting, but only the second can recover much of the community's own understanding of what continued.

The archival evidence might record the party's departure date, miles traveled, rations issued, and births or deaths along the route. It may not record a child's loss of a familiar language environment, a grandmother's knowledge of a gathering ground, or the practical challenge of replacing a salmon diet with unfamiliar provisions. Those omissions change the scale on which historians measure harm.

Daily decisions included how to feed children, care for ill relatives, rebuild shelter, maintain ceremonies, and preserve communication with family members outside the reservation. Women's labor and elders' authority were central to these efforts. Oral histories can show dimensions that agent reports neglect, while official records may help locate dates and routes. Neither kind of evidence should be used alone to generalize an identical experience to every Indigenous family.

Section 72.16

Fishing Rights, Salmon Economies, and the Meaning of Treaty Reservations

Treaties reserved the right to fish at "usual and accustomed" places, including outside reservation boundaries. These rights were reserved, not newly granted. Puget Sound, Yakama, and Nez Perce provisions differ; the Makah treaty uniquely addresses whaling and sealing; and Columbia River fisheries including Celilo Falls remained central. A reservation boundary did not necessarily extinguish rights expressly reserved elsewhere. The 1974 Boldt decision illustrates the continuing legal importance of these promises.

Fishing rights were among the most consequential provisions in Northwest treaties because the economic life of many Indigenous nations could not be contained within a reservation. The Medicine Creek agreement's Article 3 reserved taking fish at usual and accustomed grounds and stations, including the use of temporary houses for curing fish. The Yakama treaty preserved fisheries along with particular hunting, gathering, and travel rights, subject to conditions in the text. These provisions reflected both Indigenous insistence and American recognition that the proposed reservations would not provide everything needed for subsistence. They did not invent the fishing practices or the political relationships that had governed access for generations.

A reserved right differs fundamentally from a privilege newly granted by a conqueror. In treaty interpretation, the Indigenous parties retained rights not explicitly surrendered, although the exact scope of that principle and its application would be litigated later. An American agent could see the clauses as an exception allowing limited movement onto land otherwise ceded to the United States. An Indigenous leader could reasonably understand them as confirmation that the people would keep fishing where their ancestors had fished and where living families held obligations. The difference in understanding foreshadowed later legal conflicts over whether private property and state regulation could interfere with treaty fisheries.

Not every treaty used identical language. The Makah Treaty of Neah Bay specifically included whaling and sealing, reflecting the maritime economy of the Makah communities of Cape Flattery and adjacent waters. The 1855 Nez Perce, Walla Walla, and Yakama agreements each addressed subsistence activities within their own geographies. The Columbia River, especially Celilo Falls, remained an intertribal fishing and trading center not fully captured by any single reservation boundary. Close reading must therefore examine the article number, wording, parties, and geography of each treaty instead of quoting the phrase 'usual and accustomed' as if every community received one standard legal package.

These rights were disputed almost as soon as settlement increased. Fish wheels, commercial enterprises, dams, land claims, and regulation eventually created additional conflicts, some much later than the period narrated here. The chapter can briefly identify the twentieth-century Boldt litigation as evidence of the treaties' enduring force without narrating that litigation in detail; Chapter 113 is reserved for the modern Fish Wars and associated sovereignty cases. The relevant point for the 1850s is that treaty negotiators believed they were addressing not merely the location of villages but the continued possibility of making a living in the region after cession.

A comparative study could place the Medicine Creek fishing clause beside the Makah provisions and the Yakama article on fisheries. Students should ask whether American officials offered access as compensation for land or acknowledged pre-existing rights that Indigenous leaders demanded be preserved. They should also examine what a treaty did not say: it rarely documented the detailed customary rules governing a fishing station, the seasonal labor of families, or the spiritual obligations associated with salmon. Those omissions are not proof that such relationships lacked political significance. Instead they reveal how a narrow written instrument attempted to manage a much richer geography of resource rights and sovereignty.

Historical use: Nineteenth-century Northwest agreements recognized fishing at longstanding sites and, depending on the text, hunting, gathering, and maritime practices beyond reservation boundaries.

Section 72.17

Leschi's Trial, Execution, and the Criminalization of Indigenous Resistance

Leschi was arrested and tried for murder; his defenders argued that a wartime combatant could not be prosecuted for killing in war. After trials, he was executed on February 19, 1858. Quiemuth was killed in custody in November 1856. A special historical court in 2004 exonerated Leschi, but this did not overturn the nineteenth-century conviction through ordinary appellate proceedings. The case shows a territorial government recognizing a war while criminalizing selected opponents.

Leschi's case is a particularly revealing instance of the difficulty American territorial authorities had in deciding whether Indigenous resistance constituted war or ordinary crime. Leschi was a Nisqually leader associated with the opposition to Medicine Creek reservations and the Puget Sound fighting. He was accused of participating in the killing of militia member Abram Benton Moses, who died during hostilities in October 1855. The prosecution depended both on disputed identification and on the assumption that a combatant could be charged with murder for acts occurring in an armed conflict. The historical problem is not solved by reciting that a court found him guilty.

Leschi initially faced proceedings that produced a hung jury. The contested legal issue was whether the hostilities amounted to a war in which the killing of an opposing fighter could not be prosecuted as an ordinary murder. The second trial removed or altered an instruction that had allowed jurors to consider that principle, and a conviction followed in 1857. The distinction matters because judicial procedure was itself shaped by a territorial political environment eager to punish leaders of resistance. The evidence of Leschi's presence at the incident remained contested; later public claims of innocence were not invented solely by twentieth-century commemorators.

Federal military officers and territorial authorities disagreed about the propriety of the sentence. Army officials were unwilling to allow the execution to take place on federal military land, in part because they regarded Leschi as a combatant rather than a common murderer. He was executed on February 19, 1858 outside Fort Steilacoom. Quiemuth, another prominent Nisqually leader and Leschi's brother, had been killed in custody in Olympia in November 1856; no comparable public reckoning secured a criminal conviction for that killing. Juxtaposing these outcomes exposes the unequal distribution of the power to define violence as legitimate or criminal.

The question of jurisdiction extended beyond an individual's guilt. American territorial officials claimed authority to try a Nisqually leader in a legal system that had only recently asserted dominion over his homeland. The treaty and the war both arose from contested reservations. Leschi's defenders argued that war existed; prosecutors treated the alleged act as murder. A historical analysis can acknowledge that Indigenous resistance included attacks endangering settlers without assuming that territorial courts necessarily applied rules of evidence or war consistently. The fact of a trial is evidence of state power, not a complete explanation of justice.

In December 2004, a specially convened Historical Court of Inquiry and Justice unanimously exonerated Leschi on the ground that the killing of a combatant in war should not have been prosecuted as murder. This historical body did not function as a conventional appellate court vacating the original criminal judgment; its importance was public recognition of an injustice long contested by the Nisqually people. The chapter should distinguish the precise institutional status of that proceeding from its moral and historical significance. The case invites students to ask whose legal order defined the conflict, how courts treated Indigenous sovereignty, and why descendants continued seeking recognition long after the territorial wars ended.

The legal controversy also offers a clear lesson in the limits of retrospective justice. Later historians and the 2004 court could reassess whether the charged killing occurred in war, but they could not restore Leschi's life, recover every lost record, or reproduce the full political deliberations of the 1850s. The court's lack of direct appellate power is a legal limitation, not a reason to dismiss the historical significance of its declaration. Nisqually remembrance remains a form of political history because it has sustained attention to sovereignty, military status, and legal inequality across generations.

Section 72.18

The Treaty Settlements of 1859 and the Limits of American Authority

Ratification of several 1855 treaties in 1859 (including Yakama, Nez Perce, and Point Elliott) placed them at a different legal stage from their signing. Signatories, non-signatories, communities rejecting boundaries, and peoples with unratified agreements remained distinct. Ratification coincided with expanded military presence, territorial administration, and settler access. Unresolved Nez Perce treaty questions foreshadow the 1863 dispute, which Chapter 78 treats.

The ratification of important Northwest treaties in 1859 marked a legal turning point but did not retroactively remove the coercion of the intervening years. Medicine Creek had been approved earlier, whereas Point Elliott, Yakama, Nez Perce, Walla Walla, and other agreements awaited Senate action until March 1859. The Indian Office and territorial officials acted amid that uncertainty: settlers moved onto claimed cessions, military forces fought wars, and reservation policies developed. A signed treaty could exert practical pressure while remaining formally unratified; later ratification did not transform every preceding occupation into an event freely consented to by Indigenous communities.

The precision of dates matters. The Yakama, Walla Walla, and Nez Perce treaties were approved by the Senate March 8, 1859; proclamations followed on different April dates. Point Elliott was signed January 22, 1855, approved by the Senate March 8, 1859, and proclaimed April 11. The Makah Treaty of Neah Bay was signed January 31, 1855 and proclaimed in April 1859. Each document also had provisions determining when reservations or assistance would become operative. A classroom timeline should display at least three columns: council and signature, Senate advice and consent, and presidential proclamation. That prevents the narrative from treating 'the treaty of 1855' as a single instantaneous legal event.

Other communities occupied a different legal position. Some western Oregon treaties were ratified, but the 1855 Coast Treaty never became binding, leaving Indigenous rights to be contested through other federal actions and, eventually, litigation. A community whose leaders did not sign a particular treaty could also be affected by an agreement negotiated with neighbors. Indigenous confederations identified in treaty language might subsequently become important centers of collective identity, but federal classification did not exhaust the histories of their constituent peoples. Ratification imposed a formal relationship; it did not by itself prove that the understanding of the relationship was identical on all sides.

The outcome of the decade can be measured in several dimensions. Settler farms, towns, and transportation routes expanded. Army garrisons and Indian agencies developed capacities to move people and administer reservations, although their resources remained limited. Indigenous nations lost access to large portions of their homelands and experienced forced movements and deaths. Yet treaty clauses preserved fishing and other rights, and Indigenous people continued to negotiate, resist restrictions, and sustain political institutions. A map colored uniformly as 'United States' by 1859 would therefore communicate one part of the story while concealing the continuing significance of Indigenous governance and resource use.

The later Nez Perce treaty controversy, including the 1863 attempt to reduce the land base and the subsequent War of 1877, shows that the 1855 settlement left difficult questions unresolved. Chapter 78 treats that later history in full; Chapter 72 need only identify the original treaty's terms, the delay to ratification, and the conflicting expectations that survived into the next decade. This focus allows the chapter to conclude at the consolidation of the late-1850s territorial order without implying that either Indigenous political life or federal treaty obligations ended there.

Section 72.19

Indigenous Families, Women, Diplomacy, and Cultural Continuity after the Wars

Women were political and economic actors: gatherers, fishers, traders, and keepers of knowledge who devised family strategies during war and removal. Intertribal marriage, ceremony, and community rebuilding continued, as did leadership by hereditary and elected leaders and elders. Accommodation to new circumstances was not abandonment of sovereignty or identity. Tribal histories and oral accounts balance the military and administrative record.

The official records of the Northwest wars foreground governors, treaty commissioners, military officers, and male leaders named as signatories. This is a structural limit of the archive, not evidence that women and children stood outside politics. Women organized fishing and food processing, maintained camas fields, prepared preserved salmon, and supported the movement of households between seasonal sites. These activities were essential to political autonomy because communities could not bargain, migrate, or withstand military disruption without food, labor, and knowledge. A battle report that merely counted horses or captured stores may conceal what those losses meant for women's work and children's welfare.

War and removal affected kin networks differently by region. In the Puget Sound conflict, families could seek shelter with coastal relatives or on islands and inlets; in southwestern Oregon some groups moved toward remote valleys or coastal corridors; on the Plateau families could travel over large distances but were increasingly vulnerable when troops destroyed horse herds. These were acts of agency under coercion, not evidence that the pressures were minor. Households that chose peace often made that choice because they had children, old people, and scarce supplies to protect. The surviving military documents frequently recorded such decisions as submission, a term that obscures the calculations made by families.

Treaty councils also depended on labor not visible in the list of signatories. Women and elders could influence leaders through household and community discussions; interpreters and relatives transmitted news; collective judgments shaped whether a reservation offer was acceptable. The surviving evidence is too thin to attribute a specific treaty position to unnamed women without documentation. A responsible textbook should therefore describe the political importance of those relationships while distinguishing securely recorded individual actions from reasonable general interpretations. Tribal oral histories may preserve particular names, places, and experiences that the official record omitted, and should be presented with their community provenance.

People removed to Grand Ronde or Siletz encountered other peoples whose languages and histories differed from their own. Shared residence could generate conflict, but it also produced marriages, joint ceremonies, translation, and new forms of collective advocacy. Elders taught stories of river bends, camas grounds, and fishing places even when children could not easily visit them. Some Indigenous people continued returning to familiar territories or trading with relatives outside agency boundaries. Changing residence did not necessarily end the obligations and identities through which those people understood themselves.

The history of survival should not be offered as a reassuring final chapter after an otherwise unqualified story of loss. It is part of the explanation of how Indigenous political rights endured. Treaties could continue to matter because communities remembered, interpreted, and defended them; fishing places remained central because families maintained techniques and relationships; distinct peoples persisted within confederated reservation governments because generations deliberately preserved knowledge of ancestors and homelands. For students, this section asks how a history written from domestic and community experience changes a narrative otherwise constructed around land acreage, battles, and official signatures.

Continuity: Indigenous kinship networks, fishing and gathering practices, ceremonial obligations, village histories, and political claims remained meaningful after territorial conquest. Leadership continued to be exercised within local communities and through the defense of treaty promises.

Change: American governments increasingly controlled land allotment, roads, reservation policy, armed campaigns, and court proceedings. In western Oregon, forced consolidation created new confederated communities; on the Plateau, military destruction and treaty ratification reshaped access to pastures and rivers.

Section 72.20

The Meaning of Northwest Resistance: Conquest, Treaty Rights, and Indigenous Sovereignty

Across Puget Sound, western Oregon, the Columbia, and the Plateau, diplomacy, armed resistance, removal, and administration reshaped Indigenous life without erasing Indigenous peoples. The United States obtained land and military control; Indigenous nations preserved collective identities, institutions, and reserved rights. Treaties were not only instruments of land loss; they were documents through which leaders tried to protect resources, communities, and future generations under extraordinary pressure. The struggle for sovereignty continued beyond 1859.

The Northwest wars and treaties transformed the balance of power across the region, but no one event ended Indigenous politics. By 1859 the United States exercised far greater authority over towns, roads, military corridors, and reservation agencies than it had a decade earlier. Settlers occupied land through donation claims and other instruments, while military campaigns forced some communities from their homelands. The state also obtained written treaties purporting to cede enormous territories. Those facts must be acknowledged without presenting them as a single complete conquest in which all nations lost the ability to make decisions.

Three relationships defined the outcome. The first was between governments and land: federal officials claimed the authority to allocate farms and create reservations, while Indigenous peoples maintained older claims grounded in occupation, use, and political tradition. The second was between war and law: military pressure shaped treaty councils, and courts later treated selected Indigenous war leaders as criminals. The third was between reservation boundaries and continuing rights: communities lost much of the land they had occupied but preserved, in many texts, rights to fish, hunt, gather, and maintain relations with particular places outside the reservation lines. Each relationship involved unequal power, yet none erased Indigenous agency.

The regional contrasts are as important as the common pattern. Puget Sound nations negotiated in a landscape of villages, fisheries, islands, and coastal transport. Western Oregon communities faced removal from prairies, forests, and valleys into new reservation systems, and some never obtained ratified treaties. Plateau peoples fought to protect large networks of fisheries, horse pastures, and trails, with the 1858 campaigns striking directly at their material capacity to resist. These differences caution against a single narrative of 'Northwest Indians' surrendering land. Such language would erase peoples whose communities have different historical grounds for their political claims today.

The treaty system itself carried a contradiction. American officials principally sought to open land to settlers and establish administrative control. Indigenous leaders often negotiated because they sought protection, payments, territorial security, or the preservation of essential practices. The same text could therefore embody a coerced loss and a lasting right. Later disputes over salmon, reservation lands, and governmental authority would demonstrate that agreements signed in the 1850s remained living political documents. A reader should not understand the retained rights as favors extended by a benevolent conqueror, but as terms demanded by pre-existing Indigenous nations within unequal negotiations.

Indigenous survival closes the chapter because it challenges the implied finality of nineteenth-century state maps. The surviving political communities maintained kinship, language, ceremony, and remembered relationships with land, sometimes under new confederated governments formed through forced relocation. The 1850s did not close their history, and later campaigns were not simply echoes of identical old wars. The Nez Perce crisis of 1877 and the modern Northwest treaty-rights movement each demand their own chapters. The analytical achievement of Chapter 72 is to show how war, diplomacy, removal, and the continuity of political obligation were intertwined at the moment the American territorial order was being imposed.

Historical Debate

Were the Treaties Instruments of Dispossession, Preservation, or Both?

Some emphasize that treaties were drafted under pressure to open land to settlers. Others emphasize that Indigenous leaders secured reserved rights, reservations, and recognition of nationhood that endure. Many historians hold that both are true. Evidence for dispossession: The Donation Land Act, immense cessions, coercive removals, and violent campaigns show how treaty diplomacy accompanied territorial acquisition. Evidence for preservation: Reservation promises, continuing political recognition, and reserved fishing rights were negotiated safeguards through which Indigenous nations asserted continued presence.

Chapter Summary

The Pacific Northwest in the 1840s and 1850s comprised distinct Indigenous nations whose political orders were rooted in villages, rivers, salmon, camas fields, maritime economies and inland travel. The international Oregon boundary of 1846 transferred rival imperial claims but did not secure Indigenous consent. Epidemics, fur-trade relationships, missionary activity and expanding wagon immigration formed the backdrop for subsequent territorial legislation, treaty councils and war.

The Cayuse War followed the Whitman Mission killings amid disease and contest over migration; the 1850 trial and execution of five Cayuse men remains a contested example of territorial jurisdiction. The Donation Land Act accelerated settler claims before Indigenous titles were settled. In western Oregon, some treaties were ratified while others, including the 1855 Coast Treaty, were never ratified, even as federal officials created reservations and forced people onto them.

Isaac Stevens's Puget Sound treaties and the 1855 Walla Walla council transferred enormous land claims but also recorded reserved fishing and other rights. Signing, Senate ratification and presidential proclamation happened at different moments, sometimes separated by years of warfare. The Rogue River, Puget Sound, Yakama and 1858 Plateau campaigns arose from different local pressures. Soldiers and volunteers used tactics that affected noncombatants and subsistence; the 1858 destruction of hundreds of horses made that strategy especially clear.

Forced movement to Grand Ronde and the Coast Reservation interrupted ancestral geography and family life. The formal creation of a reservation did not necessarily follow a ratified treaty, nor did it make every act of removal voluntary. At the same time Indigenous people sustained languages, kinship, practices and governance across newly imposed administrative boundaries. Leschi's prosecution and his historical exoneration demonstrate the continuing dispute over whose law defined the conflicts.

The central historical conclusion is that the treaties were instruments of territorial dispossession and, in significant clauses, instruments preserving rights that Indigenous nations had always exercised. By 1859 American territorial authority had expanded greatly, but Indigenous political existence did not end. Later treaty-rights struggles and distinct later wars should be studied in their own periods, not treated as evidence that the 1850s had reached an uncomplicated conclusion.

The federal approval process after field signature, distinct from presidential proclamation.

1855 gathering at which distinct Plateau peoples negotiated several treaties.

Legal designation joining specified bands for a treaty relationship while not erasing their separate histories.

Treaty expression referring to longstanding fishing grounds and stations.

Chapter Reflection

The wars of the 1850s did not end an era in which all Northwest nations bargained with empires as equals; even earlier commercial and diplomatic relationships were marked by unequal leverage and profound losses. What changed most was the reach of American settlement, military administration, and law. Yet reserved fishing rights, remembered homelands, and the political communities that endured Grand Ronde, Siletz, and the Plateau campaigns carried Indigenous sovereignty forward. The later Nez Perce conflict belongs to Chapter 78, and the modern treaty-rights movement to Chapter 113. Chapter 73 turns to the Dakota War of 1862. The essential lesson of Chapter 72 is the coexistence of coercive land loss and Indigenous political persistence, a combination visible in treaty clauses as well as the histories of surviving families.
