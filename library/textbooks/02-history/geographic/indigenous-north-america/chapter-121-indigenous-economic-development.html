Chapter Opening

Indigenous economic development is not the arrival of enterprise in communities that had none. It is the reconstruction, under twentieth- and twenty-first-century legal and financial conditions, of economic institutions that colonization disrupted, constrained, or tried to dismantle.

Consider five settings that appear in this chapter, each documented in institutional or public records. In northeastern Wisconsin, the Menominee Nation manages a forest and a sawmill under a harvest system designed to keep the forest productive across generations. In the Sierra Juárez of Oaxaca, a Zapotec community assembly decides how timber revenues will be divided between a forest enterprise, public services, and communal projects. In Navajo country, a tribal utility extends electric and water service to households that private utilities long judged unprofitable to serve. In western Canada, First Nations governments pledge a share of their own property-tax revenue to secure borrowing through a collective financing authority. In Nebraska, a small nation with one of the poorest reservation economies in the Midwest charters a corporation that bids on federal contracts and builds housing. These are different institutions in different legal systems. What they share is an effort to convert political authority into productive capacity.

That effort stands on the ground prepared by the six chapters of Part XX. Chapters 115 through 120 traced how Indigenous nations won, defended, or redefined rights to land, water, self-government, and legal standing. Part XXI asks what nations have done with those rights. Chapter 121 considers economic capacity: enterprises, capital, land use, natural resources, public revenue, and the institutions that connect them. Chapter 122 treats gaming, which this chapter mentions only where the comparison requires it. Chapters 123 through 126 turn to language, education, repatriation, and cultural production, which complete the picture of resurgence.

The chapter's central argument is that Indigenous economic development has been an effort to turn political sovereignty into material and institutional capacity. Nations have pursued greater control over employment, capital, production, and public revenue through nationally owned enterprises, private entrepreneurship, financial institutions, resource management, and community investment. The results are substantial and uneven. Differences in territory, location, population, legal framework, and access to capital mean that the economic circumstances of Indigenous nations are as varied as their political histories. No single success story, and no single statistic, represents the continent.

The chapter also resists a common compression. Popular discussion often treats Indigenous economies as having begun in the late twentieth century, or as having a single source. Chapter 1 argued against treating Indigenous history as a prologue to someone else's story, and the same principle applies here. Indigenous nations had agricultural systems, fisheries, manufacturing traditions, markets, and long-distance trade for many centuries before colonization. Dispossession, administrative control, and restrictions on commerce then reshaped them during the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Modern development is best understood as reconstruction under constraint, not as creation from nothing.

Three analytical distinctions run through the chapter. The first is between ownership and authority: a tribal government, a tribally owned corporation, an Alaska Native shareholder corporation, a private Indigenous business, and a cooperative are different institutions with different accountability and legal status. The second is between revenue and prosperity: gross revenue, net income, government distributions, wages, household income, and public investment measure different things. The third is between growth and sovereignty: an enterprise can be profitable without increasing a nation's control over its own future, and a modest enterprise can strengthen that control considerably.

The evidence is uneven and must be handled critically. Statutes, regulations, and court decisions supply firm institutional evidence. Government surveys and censuses supply comparative data, with categories that may not match how nations define themselves. Corporate annual reports and institutional histories are valuable but written by the organizations they describe. Oral histories, community reports, and the work of Indigenous economists and practitioners help fill gaps. Financial figures are used here sparingly, with their reporting periods identified, because published figures change annually and are often self-reported. Students should consult the most recent reports from each institution discussed.

Geographically, the chapter follows the continental framework of the textbook. It begins in the precolonial economies of the whole continent, then concentrates on the United States from the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 to the present, where the institutional record is densest. It then gives sustained attention to Canada, where Indigenous financial institutions and fiscal governance developed along distinct lines, and to Mexico, where communal landholding and community assemblies shaped Indigenous enterprise. Alaska enters through the discussion of Alaska Native corporations, whose institutional origins are treated in Chapter 115; Canadian Inuit economic institutions build on the modern treaties discussed in Chapter 116. Greenland is outside the principal modern case studies in this chapter.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the diversity of Indigenous economic systems before colonization and explain how economic activity was embedded in political, kinship, and territorial relationships.
  2. Trace how dispossession, allotment, and administrative control undermined Indigenous economic independence, linking poverty to specific institutions rather than to inherent conditions.
  3. Explain the economic provisions of the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934, including Section 17 corporate charters, and assess their limits.
  4. Analyze the shift from termination-era policy to self-determination and its consequences for Indigenous economic decision-making.
  5. Distinguish among tribal government departments, tribally chartered corporations, federally chartered corporations, limited liability companies, joint ventures, Alaska Native corporations, and private Indigenous businesses.
  6. Explain how land tenure, collateral rules, and regulatory approval requirements have shaped investment, and what the HEARTH Act of 2012 changed.
  7. Evaluate the role of Native financial institutions and federal credit programs in widening access to capital.
  8. Compare agriculture, forestry, fisheries, energy, and tourism as sectors of Indigenous enterprise, identifying distinct opportunities and risks.
  9. Compare Cherokee Nation Businesses and Ho-Chunk, Inc. as models of diversified tribal enterprise, using published institutional evidence critically.
  10. Assess how commercial revenue is converted into public goods, and why corporate revenue alone cannot measure community well-being.
  11. Compare Indigenous economic institutions in the United States, Canada, and Mexico, including First Nations fiscal institutions and Oaxacan community forestry.
  12. Evaluate competing interpretations of whether enterprise development strengthens sovereignty, and distinguish economic growth from economic sovereignty.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. In what sense is modern Indigenous economic development a reconstruction of older economic systems rather than the introduction of economic activity?
  2. Which specific laws and administrative practices turned Indigenous land and resources into limited or inaccessible assets, and how did they do so?
  3. Why did the legal form of a business, whether government department, chartered corporation, or private firm, matter so much for accountability and capital?
  4. Why has access to capital been described as a mechanism through which political autonomy becomes economic capacity?
  5. How do ownership of an enterprise and leasing land to an outside developer differ in who controls decisions and retains benefits?
  6. Why can enterprise revenue rise while household incomes, housing, and infrastructure remain deficient?
  7. How did the institutions of Canada and Mexico produce different forms of Indigenous economic organization from those in the United States?
  8. Should the success of Indigenous enterprise be judged by profit, by sovereignty, by employment, by cultural continuity, or by some combination?
  9. Why do differences in geography and market access produce such different outcomes among Indigenous nations?

Historical Context

Three earlier histories frame this chapter. The first is the precolonial and early colonial economic world described in Volumes I through IV. Indigenous peoples practiced agriculture, fishing, hunting, gathering, manufacture, and trade on scales ranging from household to continental, and in the fur trade (Chapter 46) they were commercial partners with considerable bargaining power. Economic institutions were bound up with political ones. That background matters because it shows that Indigenous nations had economic expertise long before the era of modern enterprise.

The second history is dispossession and administrative control, told in Chapters 82 through 86, 91, and 95. Treaties, removals, reservations and reserves, allotment, and the Indian Act in Canada reduced Indigenous land bases and placed Indigenous property, income, and movement under outside supervision. Chapter 121 summarizes only the economic consequences. It does not retell the policies.

The third history is the reconstruction of political institutions from the 1930s onward: the Indian Reorganization Act (Chapter 97), the termination and relocation era (Chapters 104 and 105), the era of self-determination (Chapter 114), and the legal and territorial gains of Part XX. Economic development took place inside these institutions and often depended on them. A nation that could not secure land tenure, water, or jurisdiction could not easily attract investment, and a nation that could not generate revenue could not fully staff or fund its own government.

Two further contextual points are needed. First, the settler states differ. In the United States, most tribal land is held in trust or restricted status, and federal approval has long attached to many transactions. In Canada, reserve land is held by the Crown for the use and benefit of bands under the Indian Act, with consequences for lending and taxation. In Mexico, much Indigenous territory is communal, held by agrarian communities and ejidos under constitutional arrangements with no direct equivalent to either neighbor. Second, the term "Indigenous economy" covers governments, corporations, households, and individual entrepreneurs, whose interests do not always coincide. The chapter keeps these levels distinct.

Finally, readers should treat the idea of "development" carefully. For some nations and community members it means employment and income; for others it means control over land and resources, cultural continuity, or the capacity to sustain public services. These goals sometimes conflict, as the Multiple Perspectives feature shows. Economic history here is not a story of a single measure of success.

Section 121.1 · Division I — Historical Foundations

Indigenous Economic Systems Before Colonial Dispossession

Before sustained colonization, Indigenous economies across North America were varied, productive, and integrated into regional and continental exchange. Their diversity followed environments and political choices. In the Eastern Woodlands and Southeast, communities cultivated maize, beans, and squash together with sunflower and other crops, a system whose origins and spread are examined in Chapter 13, and the agricultural surpluses supported towns and, at Cahokia, a major urban center (Chapter 22). In the Southwest, Hohokam builders constructed extensive canal networks in the Sonoran Desert (Chapter 17), while Pueblo farmers used floodwater, runoff, and other techniques to produce crops in an arid environment (Chapter 18). Agriculture was one element of broader subsistence systems that included hunting, fishing, and gathering.

Elsewhere, wealth rested on other resources. Northwest Coast societies built economies on salmon and other marine resources, cedar woodworking, and specialized craft, and their ceremonial institutions, including the potlatch, were bound up with the distribution of goods and the confirmation of rank and rights. Along the Columbia River, fishing places such as those near Celilo Falls drew peoples from a wide region for fishing and trade. In California, acorn processing, managed landscapes, and the production of shell beads supported dense populations and extensive exchange. On the Plains, village farmers such as the Mandan and Hidatsa traded agricultural produce with mobile bison hunters. In the Great Lakes, wild rice, maple sugar, fish, and fur supported seasonal economies. Arctic peoples organized maritime and land hunting, with expert knowledge of ice, animals, and weather. Craft production, from pottery to stone tools to textiles, often reached specialized levels.

Exchange connected these economies. Copper, shell, obsidian, and other materials moved across great distances, as the Hopewell networks demonstrated (Chapter 20). Wampum, made from shell, was used in diplomacy and exchange in the Northeast. In Mesoamerica, large marketplaces and tribute systems moved goods on a scale that connected to regions farther north (Chapter 14). After 1492, Indigenous nations did not enter trade as novices: in the fur trade, described in Chapter 46, they supplied and controlled much of the commerce on terms they often shaped.

Two cautions apply. First, the absence of modern corporate forms does not mean the absence of property or commercial initiative. Indigenous legal systems recognized rights to land, fishing sites, hunting territories, tools, ceremonies, and goods, often held by households, clans, villages, or nations, with obligations attached. Forms of ownership differed widely, and some resources were managed collectively while others were held by families or individuals. Second, economic activity was embedded in political and moral relationships. Production and distribution were tied to kinship, reciprocity, leadership responsibility, and responsibilities to land and community. This embeddedness did not prevent trade or accumulation, but it shaped who controlled resources and what obligations accompanied them.

The point for this chapter is continuity of capacity. Contemporary development takes place in a changed legal and market environment, with corporations, banks, and public finance, but it draws on long-standing knowledge of land, resources, and exchange, and on political traditions of collective decision-making. The Continuity and Change feature later in the chapter returns to this comparison.

Section 121.2 · Division I — Historical Foundations

Dispossession, Allotment, and the Disruption of Indigenous Economic Independence

The policies of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries undermined Indigenous economic foundations through specific mechanisms. Treaties and removals (Chapters 65, 66, and 82) reduced territories and separated communities from fisheries, hunting grounds, farmland, and timber. Reservation and reserve policies (Chapter 84) confined peoples to limited areas, often not the best land, and placed the management of resources under federal or Crown supervision. The destruction of the buffalo economy (Chapter 81) ended the basis of Plains subsistence and trade, and restrictions on hunting, fishing, and gathering narrowed older economies elsewhere.

Allotment, examined in Chapter 86, was the most direct blow to land-based production. By dividing tribal land into individual parcels and opening the remainder to settlers, it reduced the Indigenous land base in the United States from roughly 138 million acres in 1887 to about 48 million by 1934, according to the Bureau of Indian Affairs’ historical policy overview and Senate Report 106-361. The economic consequences extended beyond acreage. Allotted land was often leased to non-Indian farmers and ranchers rather than worked by its owners, who frequently lacked capital, equipment, and credit. Inheritance divided parcels among ever more heirs, a process called fractionation that still complicates the use of allotted land. Individual income from leases was handled through federal accounts, whose mismanagement produced the litigation that led to the Cobell settlement of 2009 and 2010, discussed again in §121.7.

Administrative control compounded the loss. Federal agents controlled permits, rations, leases, and the sale of produce and livestock, and the traders who dominated reservation commerce often extended credit on terms that kept households in debt. Restrictions on mobility also complicated economic activity; in Canada, the informal pass system restricted the movement of many Prairie First Nations from reserves, while the "peasant farming" policy of the late 1880s limited the scale and technology of reserve agriculture, and permit requirements restricted the sale of reserve produce. The Indian Act (Chapter 85) then limited the alienation of reserve land and subjected band finances to departmental supervision. In each setting, the state treated Indigenous economic activity as something to be managed.

The resulting dependence on wage labor, government employment, outside merchants, and federal appropriations was not an inherent feature of Indigenous communities. It was a product of policy. Indigenous people adapted continuously during this period, as Chapter 91 describes: they farmed and ranched where they could, took seasonal and wage work, sold crafts, fished and hunted in defiance of restrictions, and sometimes litigated. The Meriam Report of 1928, a major survey of federal Indian administration, documented severe poverty, poor health, and inadequate education, and attributed much of this to the failures of existing policy. Its findings provided contemporary evidence that the poverty of the 1920s was the outcome of institutions and not of cultural deficiency.

Section 121.3 · Division I — Historical Foundations

The Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 and the Reconstruction of Tribal Economic Institutions

The Indian Reorganization Act of 1934, treated as a political reform in Chapter 97, also contained the first broad federal framework for tribal economic institution-building. Its first section ended further allotment of tribal land. Section 5 authorized the Secretary of the Interior to acquire land for Indians and hold it in trust, giving tribes a means of rebuilding land bases, although the scale of acquisition was modest relative to earlier losses. Section 10 authorized a revolving credit fund from which the Secretary could make loans to tribal corporations and, through them, to members. Section 16 allowed tribes to adopt constitutions and bylaws. Section 17 allowed tribes to receive federal charters of incorporation.

The distinction between Sections 16 and 17 matters. A constitution organizes a tribal government: its offices, elections, powers, and procedures. A Section 17 charter creates a corporation, a legal entity that can hold and manage property and conduct business. The original 1934 statute required a petition by at least one-third of the adult Indians and ratification by a majority of adult Indians living on the reservation at a special election. Congress amended Section 17 in 1990 (Public Law 101-301): a tribe now petitions for a charter and its governing body ratifies it. The amendment also changed the maximum lease term specified in the charter provision from ten to twenty-five years for trust or restricted reservation lands. The section preserves limits on alienation and encumbrance, and reserves charter revocation or surrender to Congress. These charter-specific limitations must not be confused with separate leasing authorities enacted later. The intention was to give tribes a recognized business form distinct from the political government, with more flexibility for commercial operations and some protection for property. Primary Source Window 1

Section 17 of the Indian Reorganization Act, 1934 (25 U.S.C. § 5124)

Creator: United States Congress · Date: June 18, 1934 · Type: Federal statute (codified at 25 U.S.C. § 5124, "Incorporation of Indian tribes")

Context: Congress passed the Indian Reorganization Act after the Meriam Report and the work of reform advocates, including Commissioner of Indian Affairs John Collier, who argued that allotment and administrative paternalism had failed. Section 17 was one of several provisions meant to rebuild tribal institutions.

Paraphrase of the section's main provisions: As enacted in 1934, the section required a petition by at least one-third of adult Indians and ratification by a majority of adult Indians living on the reservation at a special election. It authorized property and business powers, subject to restrictions on sale, mortgage, and leases of reservation land exceeding ten years. Only Congress could revoke or accept surrender of a charter. In 1990 Congress replaced the original petition and ratification requirements with a petition by the tribe and ratification by its governing body; the specified lease restriction became twenty-five years for trust or restricted reservation lands. This is a paraphrase of the original and amended provisions, not a quotation. See 25 U.S.C. § 5124 and its amendment notes.

Analysis: Section 17 shows how federal law could create room for tribal commercial organization. It also shows the limits of that room: chartering ran through the Secretary of the Interior, certain land transactions were restricted, and the statute's language reflects an assumption of continuing federal guardianship.

Limitations: A statute records legislative intent and legal structure, not practice. It cannot show how many tribes used Section 17, how tribal members evaluated the option, or what business results followed. Many tribes did not seek charters in the first decades after 1934, and other business forms later became common.

Perspective: The text represents the federal government's view of how tribes should be organized. Tribal perspectives, which varied, appear in votes on constitutions and charters, tribal council records, and later enterprise histories.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the section say about the powers a corporate charter may confer?
  2. Who created the statute and under what circumstances?
  3. What purposes may have shaped the original requirement of a membership vote, its replacement in 1990, and the reservation of revocation to Congress?
  4. What can historians learn from the statute about the legal foundations of tribal enterprise?
  5. What cannot be established from the statute alone about actual business practice?

Other provisions and institutions supported the same aim. The Indian Arts and Crafts Board, created in 1935, sought to expand markets for Indigenous art and craft and later became a regulator against misrepresentation (see Chapter 126 for the arts themselves). Congress extended elements of the reform to Oklahoma and Alaska in 1936. Not all nations adopted the Act: the Navajo Nation, for example, voted narrowly against it in 1935, in the context of controversy over federal livestock reduction, a subject treated in Chapter 97.

The limits were substantial. Federal supervision continued, so decisions about leases, budgets, and contracts often required approval. Credit funds were small relative to need. Many constitutions followed federal models that fit some nations' own political traditions poorly. And the Act's land and credit provisions did not by themselves create markets, infrastructure, or management expertise. It is more accurate to describe the Act as establishing legal and organizational foundations for later enterprise than as producing economic development in the 1930s. Many twentieth-century tribal enterprises, and many later corporations chartered under tribal law, rest on the idea it embodied, that a tribe may hold property and do business through an organization distinct from its political government.

Section 121.4 · Division I — Historical Foundations

From Termination to Self-Determination: The Emergence of Modern Economic Development Policy

The federal policy of the 1950s moved in the opposite direction. House Concurrent Resolution 108 of 1953 and subsequent legislation (Chapter 104) sought to end the federal relationship with selected tribes, and relocation programs (Chapter 105) encouraged Indigenous people to move to cities. The economic results were severe for terminated communities. The Menominee Nation, whose forest and mill had supported a distinctive economy, was terminated in 1961 and the tribal enterprise was reorganized under a state-chartered structure. The community lost services, faced financial strain, and eventually organized politically for restoration, which Congress enacted in 1973. The Klamath Tribes of Oregon, terminated at about the same time, lost their federally managed forest as a tribal asset, and their federal recognition was restored in 1986 without the land base. The lesson drawn by many Indigenous leaders and later scholars was that economic development required political continuity, not the removal of tribal institutions.

The 1960s brought a different set of ideas. War on Poverty programs, including the Office of Economic Opportunity and the Economic Development Administration, reached reservations and gave some tribes access to funds and planning experience, and Indigenous leaders learned to use community action structures. In 1970 President Richard Nixon's message to Congress rejected termination and endorsed greater tribal control. Several enterprises of this period were established with federal incentives and outside partners, including manufacturing ventures. Cherokee Nation Industries began operations in 1969, and the Navajo Agricultural Products Industry began in 1970. Some early industrial ventures did not last, and critics noted that attracting outside plants did not necessarily give tribes control.

Two statutes of the mid-1970s set the framework for modern policy. The Indian Financing Act of 1974 created federal credit tools, discussed in §121.8. The Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975, described in Chapter 114, allowed tribes to contract with federal agencies to administer programs previously run by the Bureau of Indian Affairs and Indian Health Service. Later amendments and self-governance demonstration projects expanded the arrangement into compacts under which tribes receive and allocate funds with fewer restrictions. These laws changed economic decision-making by making tribes employers, purchasers, and managers of public programs.

The central distinction is between assistance and control. Federal economic assistance can leave decisions about priorities with outside administrators when it is administered under externally determined rules; assistance controlled by tribal governments can instead strengthen their decision-making capacity. Indigenous-controlled development places those decisions with the nation. The self-determination era did not eliminate federal oversight or end funding shortfalls, but it created conditions in which tribes could build administrative capacity, accumulate expertise, and plan. Research by the Harvard Project on American Indian Economic Development, founded in 1987, and by the Native Nations Institute, among others, argued that effective governing institutions and real decision-making authority were important conditions for sustained development, an argument students should evaluate rather than assume. Council of Energy Resource Tribes, founded in 1975, offers another example of tribal institution-building aimed at stronger bargaining power over resource leases (see §121.11).

Historical Vocabulary
Tribal Enterprise

Definition. A commercial operation owned by an Indigenous government or community, as distinct from a business owned by an individual.

Historical context. Tribal enterprises existed in several forms before the late twentieth century, but expanded after the 1970s as tribes gained administrative authority and access to credit. Their legal forms include departments of tribal government, enterprise boards, federally chartered corporations, and corporations organized under tribal law.

Why it matters. Ownership allows profits to support public purposes and gives the nation a say in employment and investment.

Potential misunderstanding. "Tribal enterprise" does not designate a single legal or tax status. Legal consequences depend on structure, governing law, and activity.

Section 121.5 · Division II — Enterprise, Capital, and Governance

Tribal Enterprises and the Organization of Indigenous-Owned Businesses

Nations have organized their businesses in several ways, and the choice has practical consequences. The simplest arrangement is a department or program within tribal government, which can start quickly and uses existing administration but exposes government finances directly to business risk and often places business decisions under political pressure. A second arrangement is an enterprise board or authority, a body created by tribal law with its own governing board and budget. A third is a corporation: a federal corporation chartered under Section 17, a corporation chartered under tribal law, or, increasingly, a limited liability company organized under tribal or state law. Nations also form joint ventures with outside firms to obtain capital, technical knowledge, or market access, and some hold a portfolio of subsidiaries under a single holding company.

Why separate commercial operations from government? Nations and their advisers have given several reasons. Professional management with defined authority can make faster and more consistent business decisions. Separate accounts make financial accountability easier to see. A corporation can continue operating through changes in tribal political leadership. And, depending on the legal structure, separation can help manage commercial liability and protect other tribal assets. Researchers associated with the Harvard Project made separating the day-to-day management of enterprises from the political arena a prominent recommendation; its practical forms include independent boards, defined reporting to the tribal council, and rules on dividend payments. Nations have not all adopted the same approach, and some have found that close political involvement serves community goals.

The responsibilities of a tribal government differ from those of a private firm. A private company ordinarily aims to maximize returns to owners. A tribal government has obligations to its citizens for health, housing, public safety, education, culture, and territory, and its enterprises may be asked to pursue goals beyond profit, such as employment for citizens, support for cultural programs, or environmental protection. Balancing these aims is a continuing source of tension, one the Multiple Perspectives feature develops.

Legal and tax status requires care. Whether an enterprise shares its owner nation's sovereign immunity, whether it is subject to federal, state, or tribal taxation, and which court has jurisdiction depend on its organizational structure, its governing law, its location, its activities, and applicable statutes and decisions (see Chapter 118). Students should not assume that every tribally owned business has the same status. For the same reason, claims about taxation and immunity in popular discussion should be checked against the specific entity and situation.

Business ownership can strengthen governmental capacity. Enterprises train managers and workers, create revenue, and give nations experience with contracts, finance, and compliance. Some of the earliest and best-known tribal enterprise histories begin with manufacturing, as in the case of the Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians, whose enterprises from the late 1970s included manufacturing for outside firms, and the Cherokee Nation, examined in §121.13. These were not gaming histories, though several of the nations involved later developed gaming as well, a subject reserved for Chapter 122.

Section 121.6 · Division II — Enterprise, Capital, and Governance

Indigenous Entrepreneurship, Small Businesses, and Local Economic Networks

Alongside tribal enterprises, Indigenous individuals, families, and community organizations own businesses of many kinds. They include retail stores, restaurants, construction firms, trucking and transportation services, lodging, professional services such as law and accounting, manufacturing, farms and ranches, food producers, and artists' and crafts workshops. Some are on reservations, some in nearby towns, and many in cities, a subject Chapter 140 takes up. Cooperatives and community organizations provide another form, particularly where capital is scarce and members pool resources.

The reasons for emphasizing local ownership are economic and political. Employment and income from a locally owned business are more likely to stay in the community, a pattern sometimes described through the idea of a local multiplier, the extent to which money circulates within a community before it leaves. Many reservation economies have historically lost much of their income because residents shopped, banked, and bought services in off-reservation towns. Businesses that sell locally, hire locally, and purchase locally can reduce this leakage, although the size of the effect varies and is hard to measure precisely.

Entrepreneurs have faced concrete obstacles. Trust and restricted land often cannot be mortgaged on ordinary terms, which limits access to loans (§121.7). Banks have been few in many reservation areas, and the 2001 Native American Lending Study identified capital access as a major barrier (§121.8). Geographic isolation raises transportation and communication costs. Infrastructure deficiencies, such as unreliable electricity, poor roads, and limited broadband, constrain business operations. Consumer markets are often small and low-income, and many reservation communities are far from larger population centers. Entrepreneurs may also lack inherited wealth that helps others finance a start-up.

Examples show a range of approaches. Food companies founded by Native entrepreneurs, such as the Oglala Lakota-founded bison-and-fruit snack firm Native American Natural Foods, aimed to combine commercial goals with community and food-sovereignty purposes. Construction, trucking, and tourism firms have grown where tribal and federal projects provided steady demand. Artists and craft producers rely on markets whose integrity depends partly on laws against counterfeit Indian arts, an area of regulation noted in Chapter 126.

Privately owned Indigenous businesses and tribal enterprises differ in ownership, accountability, and revenue structure. A private business answers to its owner and its customers. A tribal enterprise answers, directly or indirectly, to a government and its citizens, and its profits support public purposes. Both contribute to development, and they often reinforce each other, since a tribal enterprise may buy from local firms and a successful tribal economy provides a base of customers. But statistics that combine them can mislead, and policy that favors one may disadvantage the other.

The practical work of business formation illustrates how national enterprise and private entrepreneurship can reinforce each other. A construction firm may begin by repairing houses, but regular purchase orders from a tribal housing authority can enable it to train workers and build a record of performance. A local financial institution can assess the firm’s particular leasehold and equipment arrangements; a larger customer can require insurance, safety procedures, and accounting systems that later qualify it to bid elsewhere. Conversely, if a dominant enterprise buys nearly everything outside the community, much of its spending may leave the local economy. Indigenous procurement and business-support policies therefore need to be evaluated by the suppliers they help develop, as well as by contracts awarded. The point is not to require local purchasing at any cost, but to identify which markets can support viable local capacity and which transactions build longer-term skills.

Section 121.7 · Division II — Enterprise, Capital, and Governance

Land Tenure, Collateral, Banking, and Barriers to Investment

Land tenure is one of the most important and least visible influences on Indigenous economic development. In the United States, three main forms of land status appear on and near reservations. Trust land is land whose legal title is held by the United States for the benefit of a tribe or individual. Restricted fee land is owned by a tribe or individual but subject to federal limits on sale or encumbrance. Fee simple land is held without such restrictions and can be bought, sold, and mortgaged in the ordinary way. Within trust and restricted land, a leasehold, the right to use land for a stated period under a lease, is the usual instrument for business, housing, farming, and energy use.

These arrangements protect land from loss, which was the purpose of restrictions after the allotment experience, but they create barriers to investment. A lender who cannot foreclose on trust land in the usual way may not accept it as collateral. Commercial leases on trust land have historically required federal approval, and delays in title review, appraisal, and environmental clearance have been cited by tribes and in federal reports as obstacles that can cause projects to lose financing or a business partner. Jurisdictional uncertainty, including questions about which law and which court govern a contract, may further deter outside investors, a subject Chapter 118 addresses. Fractionated allotments, which can have many co-owners, are especially difficult to lease or develop. Congress and the Interior Department have taken steps to address fractionation, including the Land Buy-Back Program that followed the Cobell settlement, which used settlement funds to purchase fractional interests from willing sellers and transfer them to tribes.

Historical Vocabulary
Fractionation

Definition. The division of ownership of an allotted parcel among a growing number of heirs, so that each holds a small undivided interest.

Historical context. It arose from allotment (Chapter 86) combined with inheritance rules and the absence of consolidation. A parcel allotted to one person in the early twentieth century could have dozens or hundreds of owners generations later.

Why it matters. Leasing or developing such land can require agreement among many owners, so land that exists on paper may be economically unusable.

Potential misunderstanding. Fractionation is not a cultural preference for shared ownership. It is a consequence of a specific legal arrangement.

The Helping Expedite and Advance Responsible Tribal Homeownership Act of 2012, known as the HEARTH Act, addressed part of this problem. It allows a tribe that has adopted leasing regulations approved by the Secretary of the Interior to approve qualifying surface leases of its trust land, including residential, business, agricultural, and certain other leases, without obtaining separate federal approval for each lease. Earlier, the Navajo Nation had received a similar authority through the Navajo Nation Trust Land Leasing Act of 2000, and some other tribes have had long-term leasing arrangements under earlier statutes. The HEARTH Act does not convert trust land into private property, remove its trust status, or end the federal trust responsibility. It does not authorize mineral extraction leases or leases of individually owned trust allotments merely because those allotments lie within a participating reservation. The Bureau of Indian Affairs published updated approval procedures in March 2026 and lists new approvals in 2026, including the Caddo Nation of Oklahoma; approved regulations are specific to the kinds of leases they cover. It changes who makes certain decisions and how quickly, shifting authority from the Interior Department to tribal governments that qualify. Whether this change increases investment depends on a tribe's ability to administer leasing regulations, which requires staff, legal capacity, and environmental review.

Housing finance shows similar dynamics. HUD's Section 184 Indian Home Loan Guarantee Program, created in 1992, and FHA Section 248 mortgage insurance offer federal credit support that encourage lenders to make mortgages on trust land. The Native American Housing Assistance and Self-Determination Act of 1996 shifted federal housing funds to block grants administered by tribes or tribally designated housing entities. Some tribes have adopted commercial codes, including secured transactions law, to give lenders more confidence, and many use arbitration or tribal court procedures in contracts. These measures illustrate how nations have tried to change the conditions that deter investment without surrendering control over their lands.

Section 121.8 · Division II — Enterprise, Capital, and Governance

Native Financial Institutions, Community Lending, and Access to Capital

Access to capital has been among the most consistent obstacles to Indigenous economic development, and the institutions created to address it deserve close attention. Without credit, an entrepreneur cannot buy equipment or inventory, a farmer cannot plant before harvest, a family cannot finance a home, and a nation cannot build infrastructure. The historical reasons are those described in §§121.2 and 121.7: limits on collateral, scarce bank branches in reservation areas, little local wealth for equity, and lenders' unfamiliarity with tribal law and trust land. Discrimination in lending also appears in the historical record, including in federal agricultural credit, as the Keepseagle class-action settlement discussed in §121.9 illustrates.

Federal policy responded in stages. The Indian Reorganization Act's revolving credit fund, discussed in §121.3, was an early step. The Indian Financing Act of 1974 expanded the federal toolkit. It created a revolving loan fund and provided for loan guarantees and insurance, interest subsidies, and business development grants. The guarantee program, now administered by the Bureau of Indian Affairs, can guarantee a large percentage of a private lender's loan to an eligible Indian-owned business or tribe, which reduces the lender's risk. Indigenous-controlled lenders emerged alongside federal programs. First Nations Development Institute, founded in 1980, supported community economic projects and philanthropy. The Lakota Fund, begun in the 1980s on the Pine Ridge Reservation, made small business loans with training, an example of microlending adapted to a reservation. Banks and credit unions owned in significant part by tribes, Native corporations, or Native individuals were established, including Native American Bank, which was organized to serve Native borrowers. These institutions can combine lending with technical assistance, financial education, and an understanding of tribal legal and land issues that outside lenders may lack.

A major federal turning point came with the Native American Lending Study conducted for the Community Development Financial Institutions Fund and published in 2001. It documented barriers to capital and credit in Native communities, including limited access to financial services, difficulties with trust land, and a shortage of capital. In response, the CDFI Fund began Native-specific initiatives in 2002, with programs supporting the creation and growth of Native community development financial institutions, or Native CDFIs. These are certified, mission-driven lenders that serve low-income and underserved communities. The Treasury Department’s FY 2024 annual report counted 64 certified Native CDFIs at fiscal year-end, compared with 14 in 2001. Its count includes institutions serving Native Hawaiian communities as well as American Indian and Alaska Native communities. This is a dated baseline, not a November 2026 census. These institutions operate in the United States, and they support small businesses, housing, agricultural lending, consumer finance, and community facilities, often with grants for capacity and technical assistance as well as capital. Their reach is limited by scale, and many depend on federal and philanthropic grants.

Why does this matter for sovereignty? Political authority over land and government does not itself supply the financial capacity to build a business or a housing market. Locally accountable lenders can set terms with knowledge of local conditions, keep interest income in the community, and respond to community priorities. But finance cannot substitute for other conditions: a lender needs viable projects, collateral arrangements, and legal certainty, so the institutions described in §§121.5 and 121.7 and finance work together. Evaluating results requires care, because loan volumes and numbers of certified institutions are measures of activity, not of improved household well-being.

Section 121.9 · Division III — Agriculture, Resources, Energy, and Tourism

Indigenous Agriculture, Ranching, and Agricultural Enterprise

Agriculture was central to many Indigenous economies before colonization, and the twentieth-century history of farming and ranching is a history of both loss and rebuilding. Allotment, restrictions on sales, water diversion, and limited credit reduced Indigenous production. Yet nations and individuals continued to farm and ranch, and in recent decades some have built large agricultural enterprises. The development of such enterprises depends on land, water, capital, equipment, labor, marketing, and management, and a shortage of any one can limit the rest.

The Gila River Indian Community in Arizona, home to Akimel O'odham (Pima) and Pee Posh (Maricopa) peoples, illustrates both the history and the possibilities. In the nineteenth century, Pima farmers irrigated fields from the Gila River and sold substantial quantities of wheat and other produce to travelers and to the military. Upstream diversions by settlers beginning in the late nineteenth century reduced the river's flow to the community and devastated its agriculture, a history of water loss that is the background of the Arizona Water Settlements Act of 2004, examined in Chapter 120. The settlement confirmed a large annual water entitlement for the Community, including Central Arizona Project water. The Community has invested in irrigation infrastructure and operates agricultural enterprises, and has also used its water rights in regional drought-conservation arrangements. The case shows how recovering water rights supports agricultural production only when combined with canals, financing, and management, a point developed in Chapter 120.

The Navajo Agricultural Products Industry, or NAPI, was established by the Navajo Nation Council on April 16, 1970 to operate farmland served by the Navajo Indian Irrigation Project in northwestern New Mexico, which draws water from the San Juan River. NAPI is a large tribally owned farming enterprise producing crops such as potatoes, corn, alfalfa, beans, and wheat on tens of thousands of irrigated acres, and it has developed processing and marketing operations. Congress authorized the Navajo Indian Irrigation Project in 1962, with a planned irrigable area of 110,630 acres according to NAPI’s institutional history; this is a project target rather than a verified annual planted or irrigated acreage. The two figures should not be conflated. It also exemplifies tensions common to such projects: federal construction of the irrigation project took decades and was contested, water supplies depend on climate and legal agreements, and the enterprise's benefits to Navajo communities, in jobs, revenue, and food, must be weighed against costs and against the interests of Navajo families who farm and herd on a smaller scale.

Federal data on agriculture require careful reading. The Census of Agriculture, conducted by the United States Department of Agriculture, tabulates farms by the characteristics of their operators, and also publishes information about agricultural operations on reservations. These are different categories. A farm operated by an individual who identifies as American Indian or Alaska Native may be off a reservation and privately owned. A tribally owned enterprise on trust land is a collectively owned operation. Some reservation land is leased to non-Indian operators who appear in the data as the operators. Students should therefore avoid treating a single figure as "the size of Indigenous agriculture" and should check which universe a statistic describes and for what year. The USDA’s 2022 Census of Agriculture identified 78,316 producers reporting American Indian or Alaska Native identity alone or in combination with another race, making decisions on 58,336 farms and ranches. These figures describe producers and operations, not the number of tribal enterprises or federally recognized tribes. The June 2024 USDA highlights also reported approximately 63 million acres operated; that acreage includes rented land and is not a measure of tribal territorial ownership.

Discrimination in federal agricultural credit also belongs to this history. In Keepseagle v. Vilsack, Native American farmers and ranchers alleged that the Department of Agriculture had discriminated against them in farm loan programs, and the case was settled in 2010 for a substantial monetary fund and debt-relief provisions. The settlement, along with similar litigation by other groups, helps explain why many Indigenous producers have relied on specialized organizations such as the Intertribal Agriculture Council, founded in 1987, and on Native lenders. Ranching, bison herds (Chapter 135), cooperatives, food processing, farmers' markets, and community gardens add further dimensions to agriculture, some commercial and some oriented to food security and cultural continuity.

Section 121.10 · Division III — Agriculture, Resources, Energy, and Tourism

Forestry, Fisheries, and Indigenous Natural Resource Enterprises

Forests, fisheries, and wildlife support some of the most consequential Indigenous enterprises, and a basic distinction organizes their history. A nation can receive revenue by leasing resources or selling rights to an outside company, collecting rents or royalties. Or it can own and operate the productive enterprise itself, hiring workers, running mills or processing plants, and retaining profits. The first approach requires less capital and management but gives the nation less control and a smaller share of value; the second requires more capacity and takes on more risk but captures more of the benefits and employment.

The Menominee Nation of Wisconsin is a long-standing example of the second approach. The Menominee forest, roughly a quarter of a million acres, has been managed for continuous yield for well over a century, with selective harvesting that the community has defended as a way of keeping the forest productive across generations. A tribal sawmill at Neopit, begun in the early twentieth century under federal administration, later became the basis for Menominee Tribal Enterprises, which operates the forest and mill and has been independently certified for sustainable forest management. The forest was central to the termination crisis of the 1950s and 1960s (§121.4) and to the movement for restoration. Its economy has not been without difficulty: timber markets fluctuate, mill modernization is costly, and the nation has had to balance employment, revenue, and conservation. Other nations have similar enterprises, such as timber and wood-products operations of the Confederated Tribes of the Colville Reservation and of the Confederated Tribes of Warm Springs.

In the Pacific Northwest, fisheries are a core economic and legal resource. Treaty fishing rights, whose history is told in Chapters 106 and 113, provide the legal basis, and tribes have developed commercial fishing fleets, hatcheries, processing plants, and seafood marketing businesses. The Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission, founded in 1977, coordinates the four Columbia River treaty tribes' fisheries efforts. Individual nations, including the Quinault, Lummi, and Makah in Washington, operate fisheries and related businesses. The legal recognition of rights did not by itself produce income: boats, processing, cold storage, markets, and management were required.

Alaska provides different arrangements. The Western Alaska Community Development Quota Program, established in 1992 and later written into federal fisheries law, allocates a share of Bering Sea fishery quotas to groups representing western Alaska communities, which invest the proceeds in fishing vessels, processing, and community projects. This program is not part of the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act framework discussed in Chapter 115, and its participating groups should not be confused with ANCSA corporations. In British Columbia and elsewhere in Canada, First Nations have pursued commercial fishing licenses, forestry tenures, and revenue-sharing arrangements as court decisions and agreements expanded their roles, topics tied to Chapters 116 and 117.

Natural resource enterprise brings tensions that are explored more fully elsewhere. Commercial production can conflict with conservation, with treaty responsibilities to protect habitat, and with obligations to future generations. Nations may reasonably decide to harvest less than markets would reward. The detailed histories of ecological knowledge, fire management, and treaty stewardship belong to Chapters 133 through 135. For this chapter, the point is that resource-based enterprise shows that Indigenous nations have run long-lived productive businesses, and that management choices embody the nation's own priorities.

Section 121.11 · Division III — Agriculture, Resources, Energy, and Tourism

Energy Development, Tribal Utilities, and Economic Self-Sufficiency

Energy has been a source of both wealth and dispossession for Indigenous nations. In the twentieth century, much of the coal, oil, gas, and uranium on or beneath Indigenous lands was developed by outside companies under leases negotiated with federal approval, often on terms that tribes later regarded as unfavorable and with little tribal control. In response, tribes formed the Council of Energy Resource Tribes in 1975, an organization that helped share information and advocate for stronger terms. Court decisions and statutes subsequently strengthened tribal authority in some respects, including the power to impose certain taxes, though the scope of that authority is complex (see Chapter 118). The conflicts surrounding extractive industries are examined in Chapter 136. This section focuses on enterprise formation and ownership.

The central distinction is between ownership and leasing. A nation that leases land to a developer receives rent and perhaps royalties, while the developer owns the facility, employs most workers, and controls operations. A nation that owns a generating facility, a utility, or a mining company takes on capital costs and operating risk and has more control over the project and its revenue. Intermediate arrangements include equity partnerships and joint ventures. Each arrangement distributes risk, control, and benefit differently.

The Navajo Tribal Utility Authority, established in 1959, is an example of a tribal enterprise that provides essential service. It delivers electricity, water, wastewater, and natural gas to large areas of the Navajo Nation, including remote households that outside utilities had not connected, and it has developed solar projects, including the Kayenta solar projects. The Navajo Transitional Energy Company, formed by the Navajo Nation in 2013, acquired the Navajo Mine in New Mexico and later acquired coal assets in the Powder River Basin, a move that kept the Nation as an owner of coal operations at a time when markets for coal were declining. The closure of the Navajo Generating Station in 2019 illustrated the vulnerability of economies built on a single energy source and renewed debate over transition (see Chapter 138 and Chapter 136). Other examples include the Confederated Tribes of Warm Springs, which hold an ownership interest in the Pelton Round Butte hydroelectric project in Oregon through a tribal enterprise, and the Moapa Band of Paiutes, whose solar project on tribal land supplies power to a Southwestern utility under agreements with outside developers. The Rosebud Sioux Tribe built an early utility-scale wind turbine in South Dakota in the early 2000s.

Federal policy has also shaped this field. Title V of the Energy Policy Act of 2005 created programs for tribal energy development, including Tribal Energy Resource Agreements, which allow qualified tribes to approve certain energy leases and rights-of-way without separate Interior Department approval, and established an Office of Indian Energy at the Department of Energy with loan guarantee and technical assistance programs. Later legislation amended these provisions, and the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 expanded funding for tribal energy loan guarantees and allowed tribal governments to use certain clean-energy tax credits through direct payment, which addressed the fact that tax-exempt governments cannot use ordinary credits. Federal tax and energy policy changed again on July 4, 2025, when Public Law 119-21 accelerated termination of the clean electricity production and investment credits for applicable wind and solar facilities. The general eligibility deadline permits qualifying projects that began construction before July 5, 2026, or are placed in service before January 1, 2028, subject to other requirements. IRS Notice 2025-42 explains the construction rules. Elective payment remains a mechanism for eligible entities to monetize qualifying credits; it does not preserve credits whose underlying eligibility has expired. These changes affect project feasibility, not the inherent authority of tribes to choose energy strategies.

Energy enterprises raise questions of self-sufficiency. Electricity ownership can lower costs for community members, create stable revenue, and provide jobs. At the same time, large energy projects depend on transmission access, power purchase agreements, financing, and regulatory approval, and prices and policy can change quickly. Nations differ in their priorities, with some emphasizing continued fossil-fuel development, others renewable energy, and many a mix. These are decisions for each nation's own political processes, and historians should present the range without assuming a single direction.

Section 121.12 · Division III — Agriculture, Resources, Energy, and Tourism

Indigenous Tourism, Hospitality, and Cultural Enterprise

Tourism has long drawn on Indigenous landscapes and cultures, and the history of Indigenous involvement in it has been shaped by who controlled the presentation. In the early twentieth century, railroads and regional promoters marketed the Southwest and other regions with images of Indigenous peoples that served outside commercial interests, and Indigenous people sold crafts and performed for visitors under conditions they often did not set. In some places, such as the Eastern Cherokee community in North Carolina near the Great Smoky Mountains, tourism became a mainstay of the local economy, but also produced commercialized and stereotyped representations. The central historical change has been the effort by Indigenous nations and individuals to own and direct tourism enterprises and shape how their histories are told.

Indigenous-owned tourism now includes hotels and lodges, guided tours of lands and sites, cultural centers and museums, heritage parks, arts markets, and outdoor recreation. Examples in the United States include the Monument Valley Navajo Tribal Park, established by the Navajo Nation in 1958, guided canyon tours on Navajo lands, and the Hualapai Tribe's Grand Canyon West, which includes the glass walkway known as the Skywalk, opened in 2007 after a development and revenue-sharing agreement with a private partner. In 2012 the Hualapai Tribal Council invoked eminent domain over the partner’s contractual interest, prompting litigation over tribal jurisdiction and contractual remedies. A federal district court order that March addressed exhaustion of tribal remedies rather than deciding the full merits of the commercial dispute. Hotel Santa Fe in New Mexico, developed with partial ownership by Picuris Pueblo, is another often-cited example of hospitality ownership. In Canada, cultural centers such as the Haida Heritage Centre at Kay Llnagaay on Haida Gwaii and the Squamish Lil'wat Cultural Centre in Whistler present Indigenous histories under Indigenous direction, and national and regional organizations promote Indigenous tourism. In the United States, the American Indian Alaska Native Tourism Association, founded in 1999, supports tribal tourism development.

Federal policy has recognized the sector. The Native American Tourism and Improving Visitor Experience Act of 2016, commonly called the NATIVE Act, directs federal agencies involved in tourism and recreation to consider tribal participation, to coordinate assistance, and to include Native American tribes in tourism planning and promotion. Its significance lies chiefly in bringing Indigenous tourism into federal policy, not in creating large new funds, and its practical effects depend on appropriations and agency implementation.

Tourism raises specific questions about cultural integrity. Many communities distinguish between what may be shared with visitors and what must remain private, including ceremonies, sacred sites, and some forms of knowledge. Several Pueblo communities restrict photography and limit access to ceremonial events, and some sites are closed to visitors or open only under conditions set by the community. Decisions about how to present history, and whether to participate at all, involve cultural leaders as well as business managers, and communities have reached different conclusions. Indigenous-controlled tourism differs from commercial activity that relies on stereotyped or externally controlled representations, such as the staged "chiefs" posing for photographs that have operated in some tourist districts. The difference lies in who decides what is shown, who tells the story, and who receives the benefits.

The economic role of tourism is uneven. It can provide seasonal employment and revenue where geography offers scenery or heritage destinations, but it depends on travel conditions, infrastructure, and marketing, and it can strain communities with crowds and environmental pressure. Remote communities often cannot rely on it, and a single event such as a pandemic can interrupt it. Tourism is best understood as one component of diversified economies, linked to the cultural resurgence discussed in Chapters 123 through 126.

The Skywalk dispute is useful precisely because tourism may appear simpler than manufacturing or energy development. A successful attraction still requires roads, transport, maintenance, staff training, liability arrangements, and a clear allocation of ownership and revenue. The Hualapai experience demonstrates the difference between entering a development agreement and retaining control of a business over time. For comparison, a community-operated cultural center may earn less commercial income than a large attraction while exercising far more direct authority over interpretation, visitor behavior, and cultural boundaries. The two models should be assessed according to their own goals. Visitors’ spending is not the same as the net return to the nation, and visitor numbers say little about whether a site is protected or whether employees receive stable year-round work.

Section 121.13 · Division IV — Diversification and Community Development

Cherokee Nation Businesses and the Development of Diversified Tribal Enterprise

The Cherokee Nation, headquartered in Tahlequah, Oklahoma, offers a major case study of a diversified, nationally owned business portfolio. Its modern business history begins with manufacturing. In June 1969, during the appointed principal chiefship of W. W. Keeler, Cherokee Nation established its first manufacturing venture in Stilwell, Oklahoma, which became Cherokee Nation Industries. Keeler was an oil-industry executive; after the Principal Chiefs Act of 1970, he won the Cherokee election in August 1971. The company’s history documents a second plant in Tahlequah producing IBM parts in 1974. The decision came at a time when the Cherokee government was operating under heavy federal restrictions imposed around Oklahoma statehood, despite continued Cherokee political existence, and before the Nation regained a fully elected leadership in the early 1970s. Manufacturing for defense and aerospace customers gave the Nation experience with contracts, quality standards, and employment, and the enterprise became a base from which later businesses grew.

Over the following decades the Nation expanded its holdings. It added further manufacturing and technical operations, entered federal contracting through firms in information technology, engineering, health, and professional services, acquired companies, and developed hospitality, retail, and other businesses. Gaming, which the Nation developed from the 1990s, became an important component of its portfolio and is treated in Chapter 122. The Nation later organized its holdings under Cherokee Nation Businesses, a holding company with subsidiaries in several sectors. In 2020 the Nation, using federal pandemic relief funds, launched a manufacturing operation for personal protective equipment, an example of using industrial capacity to meet a public health need.

Several features of the Cherokee model deserve analysis. First, the Nation's tribal law provides for the business holding company to be governed by a board and to distribute a portion of its earnings to the Nation's government, which its FY 2023 State of the Company report described as a 37 percent allocation of profit to the Nation, with the remaining 63 percent reinvested in employment, business growth, and special projects. These are the company’s reported allocations for the cited period, not a claim that all subsequent distributions necessarily follow the same formula. The Nation’s FY 2025 Annual Comprehensive Financial Report, published in 2026, provides a separate audited government reporting framework for examining actual transfers and finances. This arrangement is an institutional form of the separation of business management from political decision-making discussed in §121.5. Second, reinvestment matters: part of earnings stay in the business to fund acquisitions and operations, and part go to government services. Third, professional management and technical capability, accumulated through decades of contracting, allow the businesses to compete in markets away from the Nation's territory.

The distributions have supported governmental priorities, notably health care. The Cherokee Nation operates one of the largest tribally administered health systems in the country, and in 2019 opened a large outpatient health center in Tahlequah, which the Nation has described as the largest tribal outpatient facility in the United States. The Nation has also reported using business income for housing, education, language programs (Chapter 123), and infrastructure. These claims come largely from the Nation's own annual reports and institutional publications. They are valuable evidence of how the Nation presents and plans its development, and students should evaluate them critically, using independent data from health, education, and economic surveys where possible.

The case also illustrates distinctions this chapter has stressed. Business revenue measures sales and other operating receipts under applicable accounting rules. Operating profit subtracts operating costs, while net income accounts for other applicable income, expenses, and taxes. Neither is equivalent to cash available for distribution. Government distributions are the portion transferred to the Nation's government. And economic benefits include wages paid, purchases from local suppliers, and spillovers, which do not appear in profit figures. A company with large revenues but thin margins may contribute less to public services than a smaller, more profitable one, while a company that employs many citizens may provide benefits not visible in dividends. Because Cherokee citizens live in many places, including outside the Nation's Oklahoma jurisdiction, the geography of benefit is also complicated.

Section 121.14 · Division IV — Diversification and Community Development

Ho-Chunk, Inc. and the Winnebago Tribe's Economic Diversification Strategy

The Winnebago Tribe of Nebraska is a federally recognized nation whose reservation lies in northeastern Nebraska. It is distinct from the Ho-Chunk Nation of Wisconsin, although the two peoples share a language and history, and the name of the corporation reflects the Ho-Chunk language name for the people. The reservation economy, like that of many rural Plains reservations, has historically faced high unemployment, limited private investment, and distance from large markets. In 1994 the tribe established Ho-Chunk, Inc. as an economic development corporation. According to the company's own institutional history, its founding purposes included creating jobs, diversifying revenue, and building tribal economic self-sufficiency.

The founding logic was diversification. The tribe’s casino opened in 1992, and the company’s institutional history identifies the expansion of competing Iowa gaming in 1994 as a reason for diversifying beyond that initial revenue source. Tribal leaders sought to build a portfolio of businesses rather than depend on one industry. The detailed history of gaming remains the subject of Chapter 122. Over time the corporation developed operations in government contracting, construction, information technology, professional and logistical services, real estate, retail, and other sectors, and it created housing and commercial development on the reservation, including a planned community known as Ho-Chunk Village. Its leadership has emphasized the relationship among enterprise expansion, employment of tribal citizens, housing, education, and community investment, and the corporation has supported scholarships and community programs. Gaming preceded the creation of this development corporation and helped motivate diversification; subsequent gaming ventures are reserved for Chapter 122.

Comparison with the Cherokee case is instructive. The Cherokee Nation has a large citizenry, an established administrative apparatus, and proximity to regional markets, while the Winnebago Tribe is small, rural, and remote. The Cherokee Nation's business history began with manufacturing under federal and defense contracts; Ho-Chunk, Inc. built much of its early growth on federal contracting preferences, discussed in §121.15, and on real estate and construction. Both used professional management and structured relationships between business and government, and both reinvested in their communities. The comparison shows that different populations, locations, and resource bases have led to different strategies, though with common institutional features.

A portfolio of businesses under collective tribal ownership has advantages and risks. Advantages include shared management capacity, the ability to move capital among businesses, and reduced exposure to any one market. Risks include dependence on a few government customers, overextension through acquisitions, difficulty monitoring a wide range of businesses, and the burden of political oversight. Programs that give preference to Indigenous firms may change, and a corporation built on them must be prepared to compete without them. The company's own accounts of its achievements should be read with attention to what independent evidence, such as reservation employment and income data and housing statistics, shows.

The company’s FY 2025 annual report, released June 9, 2026, provides a contemporary institutional account of its mission and business development. An additional perspective comes from Jonathan B. Taylor’s 2023 study of the Winnebago reservation between 1990 and 2022, which the corporation links in its housing materials. These sources invite an important methodological distinction: company performance, local economic conditions, and the results of a particular housing assistance program are different measures. A historical comparison should examine the timing of investments, financing sources, and changes in housing or employment before drawing conclusions about causation.

Section 121.15 · Division IV — Diversification and Community Development

Federal Procurement, Government Contracting, and Access to National Markets

Federal contracting has become an important market for some Indigenous enterprises. The government purchases a wide range of goods and services, and preference programs have opened some of this market to Indigenous-owned firms. The oldest is the Buy Indian Act, dating to 1910, which allows the Bureau of Indian Affairs to purchase the products of Indian industry. For much of the twentieth century its use was limited, but later administrative and statutory changes, including Indian preference provisions in self-determination law, increased its application, and recent regulations have addressed its use in construction and other procurement.

The Small Business Administration's Section 8(a) Business Development Program, designed for small firms owned by socially and economically disadvantaged people, has special provisions for firms owned by federally recognized tribes and by Alaska Native corporations. Under 13 CFR § 124.109, Indian tribes receive recognition of social disadvantage but must demonstrate the tribe’s economic disadvantage; qualifying Alaska Native corporations are statutorily deemed economically disadvantaged. Each applicant firm must also meet the applicable ownership, control, size, and business eligibility criteria. Some rules permit larger sole-source awards for qualifying entity-owned firms than for individually owned firms, subject to procurement law and approval requirements. These provisions made it possible for some enterprises to build experience and revenue in federal markets. They have also drawn scrutiny from oversight bodies, which have examined the program's administration and the size of some awards, and the rules have been revised and reviewed repeatedly, including in the mid-2020s. The SBA’s social-disadvantage rule effective September 10, 2026 changed the evidentiary standard for individually owned applicants. Its August 28, 2026 guidance specifically distinguished entity-owned applicants: the new individual social-disadvantage test did not apply to firms owned by tribes, ANCs, Native Hawaiian organizations, or community development corporations. This did not eliminate the other applicable eligibility and compliance requirements.

Contracting has allowed some Indigenous enterprises to operate beyond reservation boundaries and to enter national and, through federal customers, international markets. A tribe with few local customers can sell services to agencies anywhere. This has encouraged investment in professional qualifications, security clearances, quality systems, and compliance capacity. Some firms have partnered with established contractors or acquired other firms to gain capability. The model has risks. Dependence on government contracts exposes a firm to changes in budgets and policy, to competition, and to compliance burdens. Revenue earned from contracts performed elsewhere may not employ many tribal citizens, so the relationship between contracting revenue and local employment should be examined case by case.

Alaska Native corporations deserve separate mention. Created under the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of 1971 (Chapter 115), they are business corporations owned by Alaska Native shareholders, not tribal governments, and they differ from federally recognized tribes in structure and purpose. Their participation in federal contracting has been large in some cases, and a 2021 Supreme Court decision, Yellen v. Confederated Tribes of the Chehalis Reservation, held that Alaska Native corporations qualified as "Indian tribes" for the purposes of a particular pandemic-relief statute, based on the language of the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act. The decision turned on statutory definitions and does not mean that ANCs are governments. For purposes of this chapter, the lesson is that law applies different categories to different Indigenous institutions, and that those categories affect access to funds and markets.

Section 121.16 · Division IV — Diversification and Community Development

Economic Development, Infrastructure, Employment, and Public Revenue

The ultimate question about Indigenous economic development is how commercial activity supports the exercise of self-government and the well-being of citizens. Indigenous governments draw revenue from several sources: distributions from tribally owned enterprises, taxes and fees where their authority and agreements allow, rents and royalties from leases, investment income, and transfers from other governments. Federal appropriations and grants remain a large part of many tribal budgets, and in recent years large one-time programs have been significant, including pandemic-era relief funds, infrastructure legislation, and climate and energy funding. The mix differs greatly among nations, and own-source revenue, which includes internally generated taxes, fees, enterprise distributions, rents, royalties and investment income rather than transfers from other governments, is one measure of fiscal independence.

Historical Vocabulary
Own-Source Revenue

Definition. Government income generated by a nation's own enterprises, taxes, fees, leases, and investments, as distinct from transfers from other governments.

Historical context. As nations took over program administration in the self-determination era, they found that federal funds were often limited to specific purposes. Own-source revenue can be spent according to the nation's own priorities, which is why many leaders treat it as an important component of self-government.

Why it matters. It helps explain why enterprise development is linked to political autonomy.

Potential misunderstanding. Larger own-source revenue does not by itself indicate adequate public services. Needs may be greater than any revenue.

The revenues support roads, housing, utilities, health care, public safety, administration, and education, and the quality of each depends on investment. Infrastructure deficits are a persistent barrier to business as well as a social problem: unreliable roads, lack of water and wastewater service, aging housing stock, and limited broadband constrain enterprises and households alike. Federal programs, including a broadband connectivity program for tribal governments funded in the early 2020s, have addressed some gaps, and nations have built or acquired their own telecommunications and utility systems. Workforce development matters too. The Indian Employment, Training and Related Services Demonstration Act of 1992, known as Public Law 102-477, allows tribes to combine funds from several federal employment and training programs into a single plan under their own management, and Congress later made the approach permanent. Business support services, such as small-business development centers and tribal commerce offices, help local entrepreneurs.

Tribal budgets face competing demands. A government must decide how much to spend on immediate needs, how much to reinvest in businesses, how much to hold in reserve against downturns, how much to devote to repaying debt, and how much to invest for the long term, for example through permanent funds. Citizens may prefer direct payments, services, or growth, and different generations may have different priorities. The 1982 Indian Tribal Governmental Tax Status Act gave tribal governments access to tax-exempt financing for essential governmental projects, an example of a fiscal tool that can support infrastructure, though it comes with limits that have been revised over time. Tribal governments' participation in federal pandemic and infrastructure funding highlighted a data problem: allocation formulas relied on population and employment data that tribes argued did not capture their needs.

Aggregate enterprise revenue cannot by itself measure the well-being of citizens, for at least four reasons. Revenue figures do not show how much reaches government after costs and reinvestment. They do not show how benefits are distributed among citizens, who may live in many places. They do not account for the needs that must be met. And they say nothing about outcomes, such as household income, housing quality, health, and educational attainment, which must be measured by other data, ideally collected or approved by the nations themselves. Federal surveys often show poverty rates for many reservation populations above national averages, with wide variation, and Indigenous scholars have noted the limits of data categories and the importance of nation-controlled data (see Chapter 145).

This section may be the most important in the chapter because it shows how commercial development becomes a means of self-government. When revenue supports a clinic, a police department, a water system, and a school, a nation exercises its authority in practical ways that cannot be achieved by legal recognition alone. When revenue is unavailable, rights may exist on paper while services remain unfunded. That relationship is the reason this chapter treats enterprise, capital, and public finance together.

Section 121.17 · Division V — Continental Economic Development

Indigenous Finance and First Nations Fiscal Development in Canada

Canada's Indigenous financial institutions developed in a different legal setting from those in the United States. Reserve land is held by the Crown for bands under the Indian Act (Chapter 85), and Section 89 of that Act generally shields real and personal property of an Indian or band situated on a reserve from specified enforcement measures by creditors other than Indians or bands, subject to statutory exceptions, which, together with limits on mortgaging reserve land, has made conventional lending difficult. Federal programs of the early 1980s, notably the Native Economic Development Program, and the Canadian Aboriginal Economic Development Strategy launched in 1989, supported the creation of Aboriginal Capital Corporations and related Aboriginal Financial Institutions in the late 1980s and early 1990s. These institutions, now often called Indigenous Financial Institutions, provide business loans, advice, and support to First Nations, Inuit, and Métis entrepreneurs, particularly where banks have not served them. The National Aboriginal Capital Corporations Association, representing them, supports their lending, training, and advocacy. Their development parallels the Native CDFI movement but arose from a distinct federal framework and legal context.

Fiscal institutions for First Nations governments developed through a separate path. In 1988 an amendment to the Indian Act, associated with the Kamloops Indian Band's advocacy and known as the Kamloops amendment, authorized bands to enact property-tax by-laws on reserve land, giving some First Nations a source of own-source revenue from taxation of leaseholds and business property. The First Nations Fiscal and Statistical Management Act, enacted in 2005 and later renamed the First Nations Fiscal Management Act, built on this base. It established a framework of First Nations-led institutions that First Nations choose to join:

  • The First Nations Tax Commission, which succeeded an earlier advisory board and supports and oversees First Nations property taxation and related laws.
  • The First Nations Financial Management Board, which sets financial administration standards and certifies participating First Nations' financial performance and systems.
  • The First Nations Finance Authority, a collective borrowing institution through which participating First Nations can borrow, using designated revenue streams as security, on terms reflecting the pooled credit of members.
  • The First Nations Infrastructure Institute, added by 2023 amendments to support planning, standards, and management of community infrastructure.

The theory is that financial administration standards, revenue-backed borrowing, and institutional accountability can increase investor confidence and lower borrowing costs. A First Nation that adopts approved financial laws and meets certification standards can borrow through the Finance Authority, which raises funds in the bond market, and the Authority issued its inaugural C$90 million debenture in June 2014. Pooling eligible borrowing can spread issuance costs and support infrastructure investment without converting reserve lands into ordinary mortgage collateral. Participants have used these funds for infrastructure such as water systems, roads, housing, and community buildings. Participation is voluntary, and a number of First Nations have chosen not to participate, citing concerns about oversight, the burden of standards, or principle. Critics have also asked whether the framework fully reflects Indigenous governance traditions. Supporters respond that the institutions are led by First Nations and that they expand choice.

These arrangements differ from those in the United States. American tribal governments are governments with inherent powers, and their borrowing and taxation rest on tribal law, federal tax-status statutes, and market access, without a collective statutory finance authority of the Canadian kind. Section 121.18 · Division V — Continental Economic Development

First Nations, Inuit, and Métis Enterprises in Contemporary Canada

Indigenous-owned businesses and development corporations in Canada work in construction, transportation, real estate, fisheries, forestry, tourism, renewable energy, resource services, and professional services. The Canadian Council for Indigenous Business, which originated in 1984 as a council for Aboriginal business, supports relationships between Indigenous and non-Indigenous firms and promotes certification of corporate practices. Federal policy has included the Procurement Strategy for Indigenous Business, begun in the 1990s, and in 2021 the federal government announced a target that at least five percent of the value of federal contracts be awarded to Indigenous businesses. The target is a policy objective. Actual outcomes have varied among departments and depend on how contracts and Indigenous ownership are defined and counted, so students should read official progress reports and independent analyses rather than treat the target as a result.

First Nations enterprises have a wide range. Some are community-owned development corporations that hold urban real estate, such as the Squamish Nation's large rental-housing project at Sen̓áḵw in Vancouver, whose groundbreaking took place on September 6, 2022, on reserve land in partnership with a private developer. The Squamish Nation and Vancouver concluded a services agreement in May 2022, and the federal government announced a C$1.4 billion loan through the Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation at the groundbreaking. Construction continued in 2026. Others operate in resource regions: First Nations in northern Alberta have formed service companies for the oil sands, and a coalition of Indigenous communities purchased an ownership share in pipeline assets in 2022. The Alberta Indigenous Opportunities Corporation, created in 2019, provides loan guarantees to support Indigenous equity in natural resource projects, and in Budget 2024 the federal government announced a national Indigenous Loan Guarantee Program. Its administering corporation was incorporated in December 2024; the Department of Finance’s 2026 financial statements report a C$10 billion guarantee envelope. The first guarantee, C$400 million in July 2025, supported a consortium representing 38 Indigenous communities in acquiring a 12.5 percent interest in Enbridge’s Westcoast natural gas pipeline system. This is a guarantee supporting equity acquisition, not a grant of C$400 million to communities. Equity participation is now a prominent strategy, in which communities seek ownership stakes in projects on or near their territories, but it remains debated, because it can be both an assertion of economic control and a reason for communities to take on debt tied to projects that other community members oppose (see Chapter 137).

Inuit economic institutions follow a different legal and historical path. Land claim agreements, discussed in Chapter 116, created regional corporations and development bodies, including Makivvik Corporation in Nunavik, the Inuvialuit Regional Corporation and its development corporation, and Nunavut Tunngavik Incorporated, which hold funds and assets and invest in airlines, shipping, construction, fisheries, and real estate. Canadian North, an airline serving the North, was jointly owned by Makivvik and Inuvialuit Development Corporation until its acquisition by Exchange Income Corporation was completed in July 2025. On July 15, 2026, the Government of Nunavut announced that it would not exercise an option to acquire a minority stake. The sale illustrates how Indigenous corporations may choose to realize the value of a major holding while governments and communities continue to negotiate over essential service. Earlier cooperatives mattered as well: the first Inuit co-operative was founded at George River in northern Quebec in 1959, and cooperatives later federated into Arctic Co-operatives Limited, an Inuit- and Dene-owned retail and service network serving northern communities. These institutions differ from First Nations band governments and from Alaska Native corporations in legal form.

Métis economic institutions likewise vary. The 1989 Alberta–Métis Settlements Accord was implemented through Alberta legislation in 1990, establishing a new framework for protecting the land base and settlement governance, while other Métis governments and organizations, such as those in Manitoba and Saskatchewan, have established economic development and capital corporations and business programs. Their legal foundations and histories, discussed in Chapters 52 and 83, differ from both First Nations and Inuit. Modern treaties and agreements, such as the Nisga'a Final Agreement, include economic provisions and resource revenue-sharing arrangements. The result is that Canadian Indigenous economic development consists of numerous institutional approaches and not one national model.

The procurement target also supplies a concrete example of the difference between an institutional rule and measured outcomes. In its 2024–25 departmental results report, Public Services and Procurement Canada reported that 3.16 percent of the measured contract value went to Indigenous businesses, while Shared Services Canada reported 9.8 percent for the same fiscal year. The Department of Finance’s 2025–26 report recorded 12.77 percent for that department. These are departmental ratios, not national averages, and the relevant definitions and permitted exclusions must be checked before comparisons. A target therefore provides an administrative lever but not a guarantee of equivalent market access or business results across agencies.

Section 121.19 · Division V — Continental Economic Development

Indigenous Community Enterprises and Economic Development in Mexico

Mexico's economic institutions for Indigenous peoples developed from the agrarian settlement that followed the Revolution of 1910 to 1920. Article 27 of the Constitution of 1917 established the basis for land reform, and during the 1930s and later land was distributed to rural communities. Two forms of collective landholding matter here. Ejidos were typically grants of land to groups of petitioners, while comunidades agrarias, or agrarian communities, were generally the recognition or restitution of lands held by communities with historical claims, a category into which many Indigenous communities fall. Both are administered through assemblies of members, with governing officers, though they differ in legal origin and internal rules. A 1992 reform of Article 27 ended the general land redistribution mandate and expanded opportunities for leasing, partnerships, and qualified conversion of certain individual ejido parcels to full private title, a change criticized by some as a threat to collective holdings and welcomed by others as granting flexibility. The constitutional reforms of 2001 recognized Indigenous self-determination within a framework of autonomy. The amendment to Article 2 published September 30, 2024, went further by recognizing Indigenous peoples and communities as subjects of public law with legal personality and their own patrimony. In June 2026, Mexico published a call and protocol for consultation on proposed general implementing legislation; that consultation was not itself an enacted general law or evidence of completed implementation.

A large share of Mexico's forest land is held by ejidos and agrarian communities, which gave communal tenure a central role in forestry. During the mid-twentieth century the federal government awarded long-term timber concessions to large companies, and communities often received little benefit. When some concessions expired in the early 1980s in Oaxaca and elsewhere, communities organized to run forest operations themselves, forming community forestry enterprises. These are enterprises owned by the community and governed through its assembly.

Ixtlán de Juárez, a Zapotec community in the Sierra Juárez of Oaxaca, is a widely discussed case. Its communal assembly, the highest authority of the community, oversees the forest and its enterprises. The community has operated a sawmill and timber business, developed forest management plans, obtained independent certification of its forest management, and diversified into ecotourism, furniture and wood products, and other activities, according to descriptions by international conservation and development initiatives and by the community. The community's system of communal obligations, including unpaid collective labor known as tequio and a system of service offices, is connected to how enterprise and public life are organized. A 2010 International Partnership for the Satoyama Initiative case study documents investment in equipment, roads, schools and rural medical facilities, alongside distributions to community members. The assembly determines the uses of proceeds. This account describes a particular historical governance arrangement rather than a guarantee of current financial performance. Accounts of Ixtlán's success come largely from the community and its advocates, and students should supplement them with independent studies.

The Mexican case shows both strengths and difficulties. Community assemblies give legitimacy and allow collective control of resources. But challenges are real: access to markets and transportation is limited in mountainous areas, prices are set by larger buyers, financing is hard to obtain, regulatory requirements for forest management are complex, and bargaining power relative to larger firms is unequal. Migration, especially to cities and the United States, has reduced the resident population in many communities and produced remittance economies and hometown associations, which affect labor supply and assembly participation. Other examples include community forestry in Michoacán and Quintana Roo and the Union of Indigenous Communities of the Isthmus Region in Oaxaca, a coffee cooperative organized in the early 1980s that was involved in the origins of fair-trade certification. Large state development projects, including tourism and transport projects, have in some places been contested by Indigenous communities over consultation and benefit.

Comparison with the other two settings clarifies the differences. American tribal enterprises are typically owned by a tribal government or corporation, whose revenue supports government services and, sometimes, citizen distributions. Canadian First Nations rely on a mix of band-owned corporations, financial institutions, and statutory fiscal bodies. Mexican community enterprises are owned collectively by a landholding community and governed by its assembly, with the enterprise, the municipality, and the agrarian authority as distinct institutions that may overlap. None is simply a variation on the others, and each reflects the legal framework through which the state has recognized Indigenous collective rights. Chapter 141 treats the history of Indigenous Mexico and the northern borderlands more broadly.

The distinction between an agrarian community and a municipality is essential in interpreting the Ixtlán example. Membership in the landholding community, rights to participate in resource decisions, and residence in the municipality are not automatically the same status. The historical record of assembly decisions and enterprise accounts must therefore be read alongside evidence about workers, households, and residents who may not enjoy identical rights. Community ownership can secure a collective share of the forest economy, yet it does not by itself establish that every resident receives the same benefit. The Ixtlán case is most useful as an institutional comparison, not as a claim that community forestry provides a uniform economic model for all Indigenous peoples in Mexico.

Section 121.20 · Division V — Continental Economic Development

The Historical Legacy and Future of Indigenous Economic Sovereignty

The history traced in this chapter runs from diverse precolonial economies through dispossession and administrative control to the reconstruction of institutions. Policies of the allotment era and after reduced land bases, restricted commerce, and placed property under outside supervision. The Indian Reorganization Act created legal forms but kept federal supervision. The experience of termination showed the cost of dismantling tribal institutions, and the self-determination era made nations administrators and managers. Since then, Indigenous nations and entrepreneurs have built enterprises, financial institutions, productive resources, infrastructure, and public revenue, using tools that include corporate law, credit guarantees, procurement preferences, tax-exempt finance, community assemblies, and collective borrowing authorities.

These achievements are real and uneven. Nations differ in geography, population, market access, resource ownership, infrastructure, and the length and severity of their dispossession. A nation near a metropolitan area or with abundant natural resources faces different possibilities from a small, remote nation. Research and community experience suggest that governance quality, clarity of authority, and capable institutions matter, but they operate alongside circumstances that no nation controls. The distribution of outcomes within Indigenous nations also matters: citizens in rural reservations, in cities, or in distant diasporas may benefit differently from enterprise revenue.

Emerging opportunities include digital commerce and technology enterprises, renewable energy, manufacturing, infrastructure development, tourism, financial services, and intertribal and intergovernmental commercial cooperation. Each depends on conditions that are changing, including federal and provincial policy, energy markets, climate, and the availability of capital. Federal programs in the United States and Canada have shifted in the mid-2020s, and students should consult current sources, institutional reports, and independent data to evaluate the state of Indigenous economies as of the date they read this chapter. This chapter has used financial statistics sparingly and has identified reporting periods where it has given them, because many published figures are self-reported by the institutions they describe and are updated annually.

The chapter's conclusion rests on a distinction between economic growth and economic sovereignty. Growth means more output, income, or revenue. Sovereignty, in the economic sense, concerns who decides: who controls productive institutions, sets priorities, retains benefits, and finances government. A nation may grow while depending on outside decisions, as when an outside developer controls a resource on its land. A nation may also exercise considerable economic sovereignty with modest growth, if it chooses its development path and keeps its benefits.

Indigenous economic sovereignty is not measured solely by the accumulation of wealth. It also depends on the ability of Indigenous nations to control productive institutions, determine development priorities, retain economic benefits, finance self-government, and improve the material conditions of their communities across generations.

The next chapter turns to gaming, which has been one of the most important revenue sources for many nations and has also raised distinct legal and political questions. Chapters 123 through 126 follow with language, education, repatriation, and cultural production, which together with the economic institutions described here form the broader resurgence of Indigenous nations in the twenty-first century.

Historical Debate

Does Indigenous Enterprise Development Strengthen Sovereignty?

The question: Do commercial enterprises strengthen the political sovereignty of Indigenous nations, or can business success coexist with continued dependence and constraint? Scholars, practitioners, and community members have offered several interpretations.

Interpretation A — Economic self-determination: Enterprise generates resources that strengthen Indigenous governments and reduce dependence on outside institutions. Own-source revenue allows nations to fund services according to their own priorities, and business experience builds administrative capacity. This view draws on cases such as those of the Cherokee Nation and Menominee Tribal Enterprises, where enterprise income and ownership supported public programs.
Interpretation B — Structural limitations: Enterprises remain constrained by inherited inequalities, regulatory arrangements, market conditions, and access to capital. Federal approval requirements, land tenure rules, small markets, and dependence on government contracts mean that business success may not translate into autonomy, and enterprise income cannot substitute for the obligations of treaty partners. This view draws on the history of land loss and on the limits of reform shown in §§121.3 and 121.7.
Interpretation C — Community-centered development: Commercial growth should be evaluated by its contribution to employment, cultural continuity, public services, and the well-being of future generations, not by profit alone. On this view, an enterprise that is profitable but undermines cultural or environmental commitments may weaken a nation's capacity to define its own goals, while a modest enterprise that supports them may strengthen it.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on institutional histories, financial and employment data, studies of governance, and community accounts. Case studies in this chapter, including Cherokee Nation Businesses, Ho-Chunk, Inc., Menominee Tribal Enterprises, NAPI, First Nations fiscal institutions, and Ixtlán de Juárez, offer material for each.

Points of disagreement: The disagreements concern interpretation and measurement: what counts as sovereignty, which outcomes to measure, and how much weight to give structural constraints compared with institutional choices. They are not mainly disagreements about basic facts.

Why it matters: How one answers shapes policy priorities, from credit and land reform to governance support and the treatment of non-commercial goals.

Student task: Choose two case studies from the chapter. For each, identify evidence supporting and complicating each of the three interpretations, and explain what additional evidence would help. Do not assume that profitability and sovereignty are automatically equivalent.

Multiple Perspectives

What Should Indigenous Economic Development Accomplish?

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns that scholars and communities have described. They are not quotations from any named person, and individual people within each group hold varied views.

Tribal government leaders: Enterprise should produce dependable revenue for government services and reduce reliance on outside funding, while keeping the nation in control of its own decisions.
Business executives: Enterprises need professional management, protection from short-term political pressure, and the ability to reinvest earnings in order to survive downturns and compete.
Indigenous entrepreneurs: Individual and family businesses need access to credit, infrastructure, and a fair share of tribal and federal purchasing, and may be concerned that tribal enterprises can crowd out private firms.
Employees: Stable jobs with fair pay, benefits, and opportunities for advancement matter, including for citizens who want to remain in or return to their communities.
Community elders: Development should sustain language, ceremony, land, and obligations to future generations, and should not require compromising what makes the community distinct.
Financial institutions: Lenders and investors look for legal certainty, repayment capacity, and clear governance, and tribes and lenders may differ on acceptable terms.
Environmental advocates, including many Indigenous ones: Resource development should respect treaty responsibilities and ecological limits, and short-term revenue should not override long-term stewardship.

Note on power: These groups do not have equal authority or resources. Tribal councils and boards make the final decisions in most enterprises, while employees and community members may influence them mainly through elections, assemblies, and public debate.

Cause and Effect

From Economic Dispossession to Indigenous Enterprise

Long-term causes

Territorial loss, allotment, loss of fisheries and hunting grounds, and federal administrative control of property and income reduced the capital and land on which Indigenous enterprise could rest.

Short-term causes

Policy reform from the 1930s, the failure of termination, tribal political organizing, and the self-determination laws of the 1970s created legal and administrative openings.

Immediate context

Federal credit tools, tribal capacity-building, financial institutions, preference programs, and, in Canada and Mexico, distinct institutional frameworks provided means to start and finance enterprises.

Transformation

Nations and individuals formed corporations, lenders, farms, forestry and fishing enterprises, utilities, and community businesses, increasingly diversified across sectors.

Immediate effects

New employment, own-source revenue, accumulated management experience, and in some places improved infrastructure and housing.

Long-term consequences

Stronger governmental capacity in many nations, alongside persistent inequality among and within nations and continued exposure to market and policy changes.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the outcomes. Law, finance, geography, leadership, market conditions, and Indigenous political organization interacted, and outcomes were contingent. Different nations with similar legal tools have had different results.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Indigenous Economic Institutions Before and After Colonization

What Changed

  • Legal forms: corporations, limited liability companies, banks, and credit unions replaced or supplemented earlier institutions of exchange and production.
  • Land and resource base: territories shrank and land was held in new legal categories such as trust land and reserve land.
  • Markets and money: wage labor, cash, credit, and national and international markets became central.
  • Relations with states: economic decisions were subjected to outside supervision, then partly reclaimed through self-determination law.

What Continued

  • Collective decision-making and responsibility to community, visible in tribal councils, enterprise boards, and Mexican community assemblies.
  • Agriculture, fishing, forestry, craft production, and trade as economic activities.
  • Responsibility for land and resources across generations.
  • Economic activity embedded in kinship, obligation, and political relationships.

Timing and experience: Change was uneven and often abrupt in the period of dispossession, and more gradual in institutional reconstruction. Communities that lost land most completely experienced change most directly, while communities that retained land and political institutions, such as some in Oaxaca, experienced more continuity. Why the pattern matters: it shows that modern enterprise is neither a break from nor a simple repetition of the past, but a reconstruction under new conditions.

Everyday Life and Social History

Employment, Housing, Families, and Community Prosperity

Economic development reaches households through wages, housing, services, and the ability to stay in or return to a home community. A job at a tribal enterprise can mean a steady income, health coverage, and the ability for a parent to work without leaving the reservation or reserve. A housing program financed through mortgage guarantees or block grants can allow a family to own or rent a home where none was available. A community loan fund can make it possible to start a catering business or buy a truck. Water, electricity, and broadband connection affect schooling, health, and work in ways that do not appear in enterprise ledgers.

Development also has costs and uneven effects. Seasonal and low-wage jobs may not lift households out of poverty. Housing shortages persist in many communities, and young people may leave for education or work in cities, with some returning later. Citizens living away from the home community may receive fewer services. Enterprises may bring new workplace stresses, tension over hiring and promotion, and disputes about the use of revenue. Elders and cultural leaders sometimes worry that economic change alters community life. For these reasons, historians should avoid evaluating development solely through corporate statistics and should consult household surveys, community reports, oral histories, and the accounts of citizens themselves.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 121 has argued that Indigenous economic development is an effort to translate political sovereignty into material and institutional capacity. It is a history of reconstruction, not of invention. Indigenous nations had agricultural, fishing, manufacturing, and trading economies before colonization, embedded in political and kinship relationships, and modern enterprise draws on that experience under new legal and financial conditions.

Dispossession and administrative control disrupted those economies through specific mechanisms. Treaties, removals, and reservations reduced territories. Allotment cut the Indigenous land base in the United States from roughly 138 million acres to about 48 million and produced fractionated parcels that remain difficult to use. Federal agents, permit systems, trader credit, and in Canada the Indian Act supervised Indigenous property and income. The resulting dependence was a product of policy and not of Indigenous incapacity, a point documented in the Meriam Report of 1928.

The Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 ended allotment and created legal foundations for tribal enterprise, including land acquisition in trust, a credit fund, constitutions, and Section 17 charters, but kept federal supervision. Termination in the 1950s and 1960s showed the economic cost of dismantling tribal institutions. The self-determination laws of the 1970s, with the Indian Financing Act of 1974, allowed nations to administer programs and gave them credit tools, building the administrative capacity that later enterprise relied on.

Tribal enterprises take several legal forms, including departments, enterprise authorities, federally chartered corporations, tribally chartered corporations, limited liability companies, and joint ventures, and tribal governments, Alaska Native shareholder corporations, private Indigenous businesses, and cooperatives are not interchangeable. Separating management from political decision-making is widely advocated but not universally adopted, and tax and immunity status depend on the specific entity. Entrepreneurs and small businesses contribute independently and face obstacles of credit, isolation, and infrastructure.

Land tenure and capital access shaped investment. Trust and restricted land are difficult to mortgage, and approval requirements and jurisdictional uncertainty have deterred lenders. The HEARTH Act of 2012 shifted some leasing decisions to qualifying tribes without converting trust land to private property. Native financial institutions, federal guarantees, and the CDFI Fund's initiatives after the 2001 Native American Lending Study widened access to capital, though their scale remains limited.

Productive sectors show different opportunities and risks. Indigenous agriculture, represented by the Gila River Indian Community and NAPI, depends on water, infrastructure, and finance. Forestry and fisheries, from Menominee Tribal Enterprises to Pacific Northwest and Alaska fisheries, show the difference between receiving rents and owning enterprises. Energy ownership, including utilities and solar and coal enterprises, differs from leasing, and is exposed to market and policy change. Tourism offers employment and control over cultural presentation, but requires protection of sacred knowledge.

The Cherokee Nation and Ho-Chunk, Inc. illustrate diversified enterprise under different conditions. Cherokee Nation Businesses grew from manufacturing begun in 1969 into a large portfolio supporting health care and other services, while Ho-Chunk, Inc., founded in 1994 by a small remote nation, built a diversified base through contracting, construction, real estate, and community development. Federal procurement and the Section 8(a) program gave some enterprises access to national markets, with attendant risks and scrutiny. Evidence from corporate reports must be read critically and compared with independent data.

Enterprise matters most when it supports self-government. Own-source revenue, taxation, leases, investment, and intergovernmental transfers fund infrastructure, housing, health care, and education, and competing demands require choices among services, reinvestment, debt, and reserves. Corporate revenue alone cannot measure well-being, because it does not show net income, government distributions, distribution of benefits among citizens, or outcomes such as household income and housing.

The continental perspective shows distinct institutional paths. In Canada, Indigenous Financial Institutions, property-tax by-law authority, and the First Nations Fiscal Management Act with its commission, board, finance authority, and infrastructure institute created a voluntary, standardized regime for financial administration and borrowing, alongside First Nations, Inuit, and Métis enterprises with varied legal foundations and a federal procurement target. In Mexico, ejidos, agrarian communities, and assemblies underlie community enterprises such as the forest enterprise of Ixtlán de Juárez, which faces problems of markets, finance, and migration.

Interpretations differ on whether enterprise strengthens sovereignty. The self-determination view stresses revenue and capacity, the structural-limitations view stresses inherited constraints, and the community-centered view stresses cultural and intergenerational goals. The chapter's conclusion is that Indigenous economic sovereignty is not measured solely by wealth: it depends on the ability to control productive institutions, set priorities, retain benefits, finance self-government, and improve material conditions across generations. Continuing inequality among nations, and changing policy and markets, mean that the story is ongoing.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Tribal enterprise
A commercial operation owned by an Indigenous government or community, with legal and tax status depending on its structure.
Section 17 corporation
A corporation chartered under Section 17 of the Indian Reorganization Act, ratified by tribal members, with federal limits on some land transactions.
Tribally chartered corporation
A corporation organized under a tribe's own law rather than federal charter.
Joint venture
A business partnership between a nation or its enterprise and an outside firm, sharing capital, risk, and control.
Trust land
Land whose legal title is held by the United States for a tribe or individual Indian.
Restricted fee land
Land owned by a tribe or individual but subject to federal restrictions on sale or encumbrance.
Fee simple
Land held without special restrictions, which may be sold and mortgaged in the ordinary way.
Leasehold
The right to use land for a stated period under a lease, the usual instrument for business use of trust land.
Fractionation
The division of an allotment among many heirs holding small undivided interests, complicating use.
Collateral
Property pledged to secure a loan, difficult to arrange on trust land.
HEARTH Act
A 2012 law allowing tribes with approved regulations to approve qualifying surface leases without separate federal approval for each.
Loan guarantee
A promise by a government to repay part of a private loan if the borrower defaults, lowering lender risk, as in the program created by the Indian Financing Act of 1974.
Native CDFI
A certified community development financial institution serving Native communities.
Buy Indian Act
A 1910 statute allowing the Bureau of Indian Affairs to buy products of Indian industry, later applied more broadly to procurement.
Section 8(a) program
A Small Business Administration program for disadvantaged small businesses with provisions for tribally and Alaska Native corporation-owned firms.
Alaska Native corporation
A shareholder-owned business corporation created under the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act, distinct from a tribal government.
Own-source revenue
Government income generated from a nation's own enterprises, taxes, fees, leases, and investments.
Per capita payment
A distribution of tribal revenue to citizens, distinct from funding for public services.
Economic diversification
Spreading a nation's income across several businesses or sectors to reduce dependence on one.
Public Law 102-477
A 1992 law allowing tribes to consolidate federal employment and training funds into a single plan.
Indigenous Financial Institution
A Canadian lender, formerly called an Aboriginal Financial Institution, serving First Nations, Inuit, and Métis businesses.
First Nations Finance Authority
A Canadian collective borrowing institution for participating First Nations, created in the framework of the First Nations Fiscal Management Act.
Ejido
A Mexican form of collective landholding, generally created by land grants to petitioning groups and governed by an assembly.
Agrarian community (comunidad agrarian)
A Mexican form of communal land tenure generally based on recognition or restitution of historical community holdings.
Community forestry enterprise
A forest business owned collectively by a Mexican community and governed through its assembly.
Tequio
In many Oaxacan communities, collective unpaid labor for community projects.
Community Development Quota
An Alaska fisheries program allocating a share of Bering Sea quotas to western Alaska community groups.
Economic sovereignty
A nation's capacity to control productive institutions, set development priorities, retain benefits, and finance self-government.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that sovereignty is exercised through institutions that must be funded, staffed, and sustained. Legal recognition of land, water, and self-government, the subjects of Part XX, does not by itself build a clinic or a water system. Economic development is one means by which Indigenous nations supply the capacity to do so, and its history reveals how law, finance, geography, and political organization interact.

The chapter also reveals continuities across periods. Collective responsibility for land, the embedding of economic activity in political relationships, and long experience with agriculture, fisheries, forestry, and trade appear in precolonial economies and in contemporary enterprises alike. At the same time, the legal forms, markets, and instruments of finance have changed, and different states have produced different institutional paths in the United States, Canada, and Mexico.

The chapter links to later developments. Chapter 122 examines gaming, which became a major revenue source for many nations, and Chapters 123 through 126 examine the cultural and educational dimensions of resurgence that economic capacity helps to support. Chapters 136 and 137 take up conflicts over extraction and infrastructure, and Chapter 139 describes the governments that manage these resources.

The enduring lesson is that no single measure captures Indigenous economic well-being. Profit, employment, revenue, and household income measure different things, and sovereignty depends on who decides. Students who read future reports, headlines, and statistics about Indigenous economies should ask who produced them, what they measure, and what they leave out.
