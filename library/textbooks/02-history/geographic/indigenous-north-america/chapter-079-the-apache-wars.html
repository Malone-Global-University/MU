The Apache Wars

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

In 1861 a dispute over a kidnapped boy at a mountain spring in southeastern Arizona turned a tense but functioning arrangement between a Chiricahua leader and American mail-station operators into more than a decade of open war. By 1883, after campaigns in three countries' worth of mountains and deserts, most Apache communities lived under military or agency supervision, yet Apache families, leaders, and institutions had not disappeared and Apache political questions were not settled.

The phrase "the Apache Wars" suggests a single, long struggle between a unified people and a single army. That impression, reinforced by nineteenth-century newspapers, military memoirs, and twentieth-century films, is misleading. The conflicts treated in this chapter involved several distinct Apache peoples, including the Chiricahua, with their Chokonen, Chihenne (Warm Springs), Bedonkohe, and Nednhi divisions, the Western Apache, and the Mescalero. They involved the Spanish and Mexican governments, the United States Army, Mexican state forces, territorial militias, settlers, miners, and Indigenous neighbors, some of whom were enemies of particular Apache communities and some of whom were allies or relatives. The Yavapai, a Yuman-speaking people whom military records often mislabeled as Apache, were caught up in the same campaigns. The "Apache Wars" are better understood as multiple overlapping conflicts, with different causes, leaders, geographies, and outcomes, taking place across a borderland that three governments claimed and that Apache families knew by other names.

This chapter begins with the Apache peoples themselves: their languages, homelands, seasonal economies, and forms of political authority. That starting point is not decorative. Apache leaders governed by influence and consent, and families and local groups made their own decisions about peace and war. American and Mexican officials repeatedly assumed that a signature from one prominent man bound all Apache people, and the consequences of that assumption, including broken agreements and wars launched against communities that had not been party to the offense, recur throughout the narrative. Understanding who Apache people were, and how they were organized, is therefore a precondition for understanding how the wars began and why they were so difficult to end.

The chapter next places the conflicts in a longer history. Apache relations with Spanish colonial settlements and, after 1821, with independent Mexico had included trade, negotiated peace, raiding, punitive expeditions, captive-taking in both directions, and scalp bounties long before the United States acquired the region in 1848. When American soldiers, miners, mail contractors, and settlers arrived, they inherited this world of existing grievances while introducing new boundaries, commercial projects, and military institutions. The chapter's first division thus insists that Apache resistance was not simply an "American frontier" phenomenon, and that warfare and diplomacy were intertwined from the beginning.

The second division follows what historians commonly call the Cochise wars: the 1861 Bascom Affair at Apache Pass, the 1862 battle there and the founding of Fort Bowie, the killing of the Warm Springs leader Mangas Coloradas in military custody in 1863, and the years of raid and counter-raid that followed. It culminates in the Camp Grant Massacre of April 1871, in which a force organized at Tucson killed a large number of Aravaipa and Pinal Apache people, mostly women and children, who had sought protection near an army post. Camp Grant is treated at length because it exposes, more starkly than any battle, the limits of federal promises to communities that chose peace.

The third division examines the era of negotiated peace and forced concentration. In 1872 Cochise and General Oliver Otis Howard reached an agreement that created a Chiricahua reservation in the Apaches' own country, while General George Crook's campaigns against Western Apache and Yavapai communities in central Arizona pressed other peoples toward agencies. The San Carlos system, the removal of Yavapai and Tonto Apache people from Camp Verde in 1875, and the closure of the Chiricahua reservation in 1876 show that reservation concentration was not a single event but a series of different removals with different destinations and different consequences. Reservations designed as instruments of peace repeatedly became new sources of conflict.

The fourth and fifth divisions turn to the resistance of the late 1870s and early 1880s: the Warm Springs Apache struggle to remain in their New Mexico homeland, Victorio's international campaign of 1879–1880 and his death at Tres Castillos, Nana's raid of 1881, the Cibecue Creek conflict that grew from a religious movement and a mutiny of Apache scouts, the Battle of Big Dry Wash, the 1882 treaty arrangement for cross-border military pursuit, and Crook's 1883 expedition into the Sierra Madre. Throughout, the chapter attends to those whom military narratives marginalize: women such as Lozen and Gouyen, children and elders who traveled with fighting parties, civilians on both sides of the border, Apache scouts with their own reasons for serving, and the Black soldiers of the Ninth and Tenth Cavalry whose service took place within a conquest of Indigenous homelands.

The evidence for this history is unusually uneven. Army reports, agency correspondence, newspapers, diplomatic papers, and officers' memoirs survive in abundance but reflect the priorities and prejudices of their authors, and they often misidentify or misname the people they describe. Apache testimony, much of it recorded decades later through interviews such as those conducted by Eve Ball with James Kaywaykla and others, and the historical accounts maintained by communities such as the Fort Sill Apache Tribe, offer indispensable perspectives but must be read with attention to when they were recorded and what the speakers witnessed personally. Yavapai and Tohono O'odham traditions, Mexican state records, and archaeology at sites such as Apache Pass add further dimensions. The chapter uses these sources comparatively and notes where accounts diverge.

Finally, the chapter has deliberate limits. It carries the narrative through the 1883 Sierra Madre expedition. Geronimo and Naiche's final campaigns of 1885–1886, their surrender, imprisonment, and deportation, and the politics of memory surrounding them belong to Chapter 80. The Diné Long Walk and Bosque Redondo, treated in Chapter 71, were a distinct history and are not interchangeable with Apache experience. The comparative institutional history of reservations across North America belongs to Chapter 84. What Chapter 79 offers is the foundation: an account of how Apache communities defended their homelands and negotiated with three expanding governments, how those governments narrowed Apache freedom of movement without resolving the political questions at stake, and how Apache families sustained themselves through it.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why "the Apache Wars" are best understood as multiple overlapping conflicts rather than one continuous war led by a single commander.
  2. Distinguish among the Chiricahua, Western Apache, Mescalero, Jicarilla, Lipan, and other Apache peoples, and between Apache and Yavapai identities.
  3. Describe Apache political organization, including local groups, extended families, leadership by influence, and the role of women.
  4. Trace Apache relations with Spanish and Mexican frontier authorities and explain why American annexation did not begin Apache warfare.
  5. Analyze how the 1848 and 1853–1854 boundary settlements divided regions through which Apache communities moved.
  6. Reconstruct the Bascom Affair and explain how collective responsibility turned a local dispute into a prolonged war.
  7. Evaluate the Camp Grant Massacre, the competing casualty estimates, and the failure of federal protection.
  8. Compare the 1872 Chiricahua peace settlement with the military campaigns against Tonto Apache and Yavapai communities in central Arizona.
  9. Assess reservation concentration as a series of distinct removals and explain why reservations became new sources of conflict.
  10. Analyze Victorio's war as an international campaign shaped by the Warm Springs removal crisis, water, terrain, and the Mexican and American militaries.
  11. Interpret the Cibecue Creek conflict, the role of Apache scouts, and the 1882 U.S.–Mexico pursuit agreement as evidence of the limits of state control over Apache mobility.
  12. Evaluate army reports, diplomatic documents, and later Apache recollections as historical sources, identifying the perspective and limits of each.

Historical Context

Apache peoples speak Southern Athabaskan languages, part of a language family whose northern relatives are spoken in the subarctic of Alaska and western Canada. Southern Athabaskan speakers, including the Diné (Navajo) and the various Apache peoples, appear in the historical record of the Southwest by the sixteenth century, and the question of when and by what routes their ancestors moved south remains debated among archaeologists, linguists, and Indigenous historians; Chapters 30 and 51 treat this history. What matters here is that by the time Spanish colonists, soldiers, and missionaries pushed north from central Mexico, Apache communities already occupied an enormous arc of mountain, desert, grassland, and river country from the Texas plains and the Pecos Valley west across New Mexico and Arizona, and south into northern Chihuahua and Sonora.

Spanish expansion into this region, discussed in Chapter 42, created a frontier of presidios, missions, mining towns, and ranches, with towns such as Janos, Fronteras, Tubac, and Tucson serving as military and commercial anchors. Apache peoples traded with these settlements, raided their herds, signed peace agreements with their commanders, and were attacked by their expeditions. In the late eighteenth century the Spanish crown adopted a policy that combined offensive campaigns with "peace establishments" in which Apache groups settled near presidios and received rations. That system helped produce a period of relative calm in some districts, but it depended on regular royal funding and on the cooperation of Apache leaders who could not speak for all Apache people. After Mexican independence in 1821 the financial resources available to frontier governments shrank, rations dwindled, and the peace establishments eroded. Raiding, trade, bounty-driven expeditions, and captive-taking continued and intensified.

The United States acquired the northern part of this borderland through the Mexican-American War of 1846–1848 (Chapter 69) and the Gadsden Purchase of 1853–1854. The transfer happened without consultation of any Apache community. For Apache families, the new international line cut across the territories where they hunted, gathered, farmed, visited relatives, traded, and sometimes took refuge from pursuit. Within a decade the new boundary was dotted with mining camps, military posts, ranches, and the stations of the Butterfield Overland Mail, all of which concentrated pressure on springs, passes, and river valleys that Apache people had long used.

Historians must also resist two errors common in popular accounts. The first treats Apache people as inherently predatory, a view that reproduces the rhetoric of nineteenth-century newspapers and bounty proclamations. The second presents the wars as the achievement of a few heroic or villainous individuals, which obscures the ordinary families whose survival decisions drove the conflict. This chapter treats Apache leaders as consequential but constrained political actors, and treats warfare, diplomacy, trade, and the search for a secure place to live as parts of a single history.

Section 79.1

The Apache Peoples: Communities, Languages, Homelands, and Political Authority

Apache peoples are related by language, not united by a single government. Their languages belong to the Southern Athabaskan branch of the Athabaskan family, and the major Apache peoples recognized by scholars and by the communities themselves include the Chiricahua, the Western Apache, the Mescalero, the Jicarilla, the Lipan, and the Plains Apache (sometimes called Kiowa-Apache). The Diné (Navajo) are closely related linguistically but are a distinct people with their own history, treated in Chapter 71. Each Apache people occupied its own country, had its own dialect, and organized its own affairs. Linguistic relationship made communication and intermarriage easier, but it did not produce a common army or a common treaty-making authority. A war involving the Chiricahua was not automatically a war of the Mescalero, and an agreement made by the Jicarilla bound no one in Arizona.

The Chiricahua, whose experiences dominate much of this chapter, were themselves a collection of regional divisions. The Chokonen, Cochise's people, ranged through southeastern Arizona, especially the Chiricahua and Dragoon mountains, and into northern Sonora. The Chihenne, called Warm Springs or Mimbres Apache in American sources, lived in the mountains and valleys of southwestern New Mexico, including the Black Range, the Mimbres country, and the hot springs near Ojo Caliente that gave them one of their names. The Bedonkohe lived in the Mogollon Mountains and the upper Gila region, and the Nednhi, the southernmost division, lived in the Sierra Madre of Chihuahua and Sonora. These names and classifications vary in Apache usage and in scholarly publications; some writers use Mimbreño or Mimbres for the Chihenne, and some treat Bedonkohe and Chihenne as closely linked. The point for students is that "Chiricahua" is a family of communities, and relationships among those communities included marriage alliances, shared campaigns, and occasional disagreement.

The Western Apache, historically sometimes grouped by Americans as "Coyoteros," "Pinals," "Arivaipas," and "Tontos," lived in central and eastern Arizona. Anthropologists commonly divide them into the White Mountain, Cibecue, San Carlos, Northern Tonto, and Southern Tonto groups, with smaller local communities such as the Aravaipa and Pinal bands closely connected to the San Carlos group. The Western Apache combined mobile gathering and hunting with farming along streams and in mountain valleys, and they maintained strong clan and kin ties across large areas. The Mescalero ranged through southern New Mexico and the Trans-Pecos, with their name derived from the roasted agave (mescal) that was central to their diet. The Jicarilla lived in northern New Mexico and southern Colorado, and the Lipan in Texas, where their history intersected with Comanche and Spanish expansion (Chapters 50 and 51).

Apache political life rested on local groups made up of related extended families, often organized around a core of women connected by kinship, with new husbands commonly living with or near their wives' families. Several local groups might associate as a band or division, particularly for seasonal gatherings, ceremonies, and defense. A respected leader, sometimes called a headman or chief in English sources, gained and kept influence through generosity, judgment, knowledge, skill in diplomacy and war, and the ability to persuade others. He or she could not compel obedience, and families who disagreed with a leader's course could separate and follow another. Women played significant roles: they controlled household resources, advised leaders, participated in ceremonial life, and in some cases took part in diplomacy and warfare, as the careers of Lozen and others discussed later in this chapter demonstrate. Ceremonial authorities, including healers and singers whose power came from spiritual relationships, were another form of leadership with influence that could cut across political lines.

The economy that supported these communities depended on detailed geographic knowledge. In the Chiricahua country, families moved seasonally between lower valleys and higher mountain zones to gather agave, mesquite beans, acorns, piñon nuts, cactus fruit, and other plants, to hunt deer and smaller game, and to plant small fields where water allowed. Western Apache families grew maize, beans, and squash along streams and moved to harvest wild foods. Livestock, including horses and mules acquired through trade and raiding and, in some areas, cattle and sheep, added mobility and wealth. Trade with Pueblo, Mexican, Tohono O'odham, and other neighbors provided cloth, metal, corn, and other goods. Mountains offered refuge, springs and tanks provided reliable water, and a family's knowledge of seasonal food and hidden routes was an asset as important as any weapon.

Military and agency records often mislabeled central Arizona's other Indigenous peoples. The Yavapai, who speak Yuman languages and are historically distinct from the Apache, ranged through the Verde Valley, Tonto Basin, and Bradshaw Mountains and had their own bands and leaders. Army officers frequently called Yavapai people "Apaches" (the Smithsonian's materials on the Yavapai make this point explicitly), and in the 1870s the army's "Tonto Apache" campaigns often involved Yavapai communities. When this chapter refers to the Yavapai it does so by name; where a source uses "Apache," the text notes whether the people involved were Apache, Yavapai, or both.

Historical Vocabulary
Apache Peoples, Bands, and Local Leadership

Definition. Four levels must be kept distinct. A linguistic relationship links people who speak related Southern Athabaskan languages. A political affiliation describes shared decision-making among communities, often a division such as the Chokonen or the Chihenne. A local group is a cluster of related households that lived and worked together and was the basic unit of daily life. A leader was a person whose advice and example others chose to follow.

Historical context. American and Mexican officials commonly described "bands" and "chiefs" using the vocabulary of European and United States administrative practice. In practice Apache leadership was typically earned, limited, and revocable.

Why it matters. An agreement made by a leader such as Cochise or Mangas Coloradas did not automatically bind every Apache group, or even every family associated with that leader. This is why peace agreements repeatedly failed to prevent violence by other groups and why army commanders blamed communities for acts they had not committed.

Potential misunderstanding. "Chief" and "tribe" can imply a centralized command. Apache communities were not centrally commanded states.

Section 79.2

Spanish and Mexican Frontier Policies: Trade, Raiding, Diplomacy, and Warfare

Apache resistance to outside authority long predated the United States. Spanish colonists moving north from the late sixteenth century established mining towns, ranches, missions, and presidios, the fortified military posts that formed a defensive line across northern New Spain. In the eighteenth century the line included presidios at places such as Janos, Fronteras, Tubac, and Tucson. Apache people responded to these settlements in varied ways. Some groups traded with colonists and visited settlements. Others raided herds for horses, mules, and cattle that were valuable for food and for mobility. Colonial forces mounted punitive expeditions, destroyed camps and fields, and took captives who were sent into servitude in Mexican households and mines, a practice linked to the wider history of captivity discussed in Chapter 53. Apache communities also took captives, some of whom were adopted into families and some ransomed or traded.

By the late eighteenth century Spanish officials combined military pressure with a policy of dependence. Instructions drawn up for the northern provinces in 1786 encouraged commanders to wage vigorous campaigns while offering peace to Apache groups who settled near presidios. Participating communities, often called "peaceful Apaches" in Spanish documents, received rations of food, cloth, and sometimes other goods, and in return were expected to refrain from raiding and to supply information or fighters against other Apache groups. This arrangement, the "peace establishment," had real effects: it reduced violence in some districts and created settled Apache communities around presidios such as Janos, whose Chihenne and Chokonen residents are among the ancestors of later Chiricahua leaders. But it also divided Apache communities, created dependence on rations, and relied on funds that depended on royal and, later, state treasuries.

Mexican independence in 1821 disrupted these arrangements. The new government inherited the same northern frontier with fewer resources and persistent political turmoil. Rations to participating Apache communities became irregular and, by the early 1830s, collapsed in many places. Some Apache groups returned to raiding as their principal means of acquiring livestock and goods. The Mexican states of Sonora and Chihuahua, responsible for much of frontier defense, adopted bounty systems that paid for Apache scalps, a practice that created incentives for killing and for indiscriminate violence. Records of bounty programs in the 1830s and 1840s show that payments were offered for scalps of men, women, and children, and the system also invited fraud, because other people could be killed and scalped. The notorious episode at Santa Rita del Cobre in 1837, in which a party led by the American trader John James Johnson killed a number of Apache people, including the Chihenne leader Juan José Compá, after inviting them to a feast, illustrates how bounty-driven violence by foreign and Mexican actors could destroy Apache confidence in negotiation.

These policies shaped Apache decisions throughout the following decades. Some Apache families continued to live near Mexican towns, trade, and seek protection when circumstances allowed. Others raided Mexican settlements, sometimes as retaliation for specific killings of relatives and sometimes as an economic strategy, and Apache communities such as the Nednhi in the Sierra Madre used the mountains as bases from which to trade and fight. Mexican officials, for their part, alternated among negotiation, subsidies, expeditions, and bounties as political conditions and resources changed. By the mid-1840s, violence along the northern Mexican frontier was an established feature of daily life for both Apache and Mexican communities.

Students should draw two conclusions from this background. First, neither diplomacy nor violence began in 1846. Apache leaders had long experience of treaty negotiation and of its failure, and many Apache families carried memories of broken agreements, massacre, and captivity. When Cochise, Mangas Coloradas, and other leaders later confronted American officers, they did so with a sophisticated understanding of how frontier governments behaved. Second, any claim that "the Apache" were uniquely warlike is historically unsound. The borderland was a violent environment shaped by competing state projects, commercial interests, slavery and captivity systems, and bounty economies, in which all communities, Apache, Mexican, Pueblo, O'odham, and Anglo-American, were both victims and perpetrators at different times.

Section 79.3

The United States Conquest of the Southwest and the Transformation of the Border, 1846–1860

When the United States Army marched into New Mexico in 1846 under General Stephen Watts Kearny, Apache leaders encountered a new government with no prior history in the region. Kearny's forces moved west toward California, and according to contemporary accounts, the Chihenne leader Mangas Coloradas met American officers and expressed willingness to be friendly. The American assumption, shared by officials who dealt with many Native nations, was that the United States now held sovereignty over the territory and that Native people within it had become subjects or wards. Apache leaders did not accept that assumption, and their behavior reflected their own understanding of their relationship to the land and to the strangers who had begun to cross it.

The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, concluded in 1848, ended the Mexican-American War and transferred to the United States a vast northern part of Mexico, including present-day New Mexico, Arizona north of the Gila River, and California. Apache communities appear in the treaty only indirectly, in an article that obligated the United States to restrain "Indian" incursions into Mexico, an undertaking that was very difficult to fulfill and that became a recurring diplomatic grievance. The Gadsden Purchase, negotiated in 1853 and ratified in 1854, bought additional territory south of the Gila for a southern railroad route, placed Tucson and the country around it under United States control, and released the United States from the earlier obligation. The adjustment established the boundary that, with minor changes, remains today. It cut through the country of the Chokonen, the Nednhi, and other communities who had moved between Sonora and the mountains to the north for generations.

For Apache communities the boundary was not an obvious fact of geography. It was a diplomatic line agreed between two governments, enforced unevenly, and often unmarked. Apache families continued to move across the region according to seasonal food supplies, family ties, and security. In some years Apache groups made peace with Mexican authorities in Sonora while fighting Americans in Arizona, or the reverse, and shifting political conditions on one side of the line influenced choices on the other. Officials of each government charged the other with failing to control Apache movement, a pattern that persisted through the 1880s and explains the 1882 reciprocal pursuit agreement examined in Section 79.19.

The new United States presence brought distinct pressures. Boundary surveyors worked in the early 1850s, and the Santa Rita copper mines and, later, the gold discoveries near Pinos Altos in 1860 drew prospectors into Chihenne country. The army established posts such as Fort Webster near Santa Rita in 1851, later relocated, and the United States signed treaties with Apache leaders in New Mexico in the early 1850s, though their terms and the extent to which they were ratified or implemented were limited. Accounts, some written years afterward, report that miners at Pinos Altos mistreated Mangas Coloradas, and historians differ over how much weight to give those accounts, but the discovery of gold clearly increased contact and conflict. In southern Arizona, American ranchers and miners began to settle around Tucson, Tubac, and the Santa Cruz Valley after 1854.

The most consequential transportation development was the Butterfield Overland Mail, which began service in 1858 and carried mail and passengers between the Mississippi Valley and California along a southern route through El Paso, Mesilla, Tucson, and Yuma. The route passed through Apache Pass, a narrow corridor between the Chiricahua and Dos Cabezas mountains that contained one of the few reliable springs in the region. The company built a station near the spring, and for several years Chokonen Apache and station employees coexisted, with accounts indicating that Cochise's people sold firewood to the station. The arrangement was workable but fragile, because it rested on the continuing cooperation of local actors who lacked authority to bind their superiors. The roads linking Tucson, El Paso, and California made springs like Apache Pass strategically important to the Army and gave small incidents the potential to affect national transportation and military planning.

Thus, by 1860 the United States had inherited existing conflicts along the Sonora border while introducing new ones: a new claim to sovereignty, military posts, mining and ranching interests, and a transportation corridor that ran through Apache land. The territory was organized administratively in ways that mattered little to Apache families but greatly to officials: Arizona was part of the Territory of New Mexico until 1863, and army departments, Indian superintendencies, and later Indian agencies divided Apache people among competing authorities. The administrative map and the map of Apache family movement were fundamentally different, a theme developed in the Geography Study below.

Section 79.4

Apache Strategies of Defense: Raiding, Mobility, Peace-Making, and Leadership

Apache strategies of defense were shaped by demography, geography, and political structure. Apache communities were small compared with the armies and settler populations that eventually opposed them, and they did not field large standing forces. Their strengths lay in knowledge of terrain, mobility, small-unit tactics, and the capacity of families to disperse and reassemble. Parties of men, sometimes joined by women and children in refuge areas, used mountains such as the Chiricahua, Dragoon, Mogollon, and Black Range for concealment, and they relied on known springs, tinajas (rock water-catchments), and hidden stores of food. A small group could cover great distances, exchange mounts, and withdraw into rough country where pursuers lacked water. The Sierra Madre, across the international boundary, provided a refuge that American commanders could not initially enter.

Not all violence was the same. Historians distinguish raids to acquire livestock and supplies, retaliatory attacks to avenge deaths of kin, attacks on military forces and mail stations, and actions aimed at defending territory or preventing the removal of a community. A single party might engage in several of these at different times. Revenge held an important place in Apache moral and political life, particularly after the killing of relatives, and many episodes in this chapter began with grievances arising from earlier killings. Apache communities also used negotiation: leaders traveled to army posts, sought rations, requested reservations, and made agreements designed to protect their families. Diplomacy and warfare were not alternating phases of a single struggle but simultaneous options pursued by different groups in response to the conduct of their opponents.

Leadership, as noted earlier, rested on persuasion. Cochise's authority derived from his record, his kinship ties, and the confidence his followers placed in him; Mangas Coloradas's reputation extended across several communities and included alliances through the marriage of his daughter to Cochise. Victorio, Nana, and Loco among the Chihenne held influence of different kinds and sometimes pursued different strategies, with Loco generally favoring accommodation with the United States and Victorio and Nana resisting removal. When American officers asked a leader to commit "his" people to a treaty, they asked something the leader could not fully deliver, and when a group not party to the agreement acted, officers often concluded that the entire arrangement had been betrayed. This mismatch between American assumptions of centralized authority and Apache political structure was a recurring cause of violence. The historical question is not whether Apache leaders were sincere but whether American officials were willing to recognize the real limits of any individual's authority and to adjust their policies accordingly. Often they were not.

Apache communities also relied on relationships beyond their own. Trading partners among Mexican villagers, Pueblo communities, and Tohono O'odham and Pima neighbors supplied goods and information. Some neighbors were enemies, particularly the O'odham communities of the Tucson region, whose conflicts with Apache groups over raiding and captivity were long-standing and whose participation in the 1871 Camp Grant attack is discussed in Section 79.8. Other peoples served in United States campaigns: Pima, Maricopa, and some Western Apache and Yavapai individuals served as scouts for the Army, and the section on Apache scouts below addresses their independent motives.

Historical Vocabulary
Raiding, Guerrilla Warfare, and Cross-Border Military Pursuit

Definition. Raiding is the taking of livestock, goods, or captives by a mobile party, often for economic or retaliatory purposes. Guerrilla warfare describes irregular warfare by small mobile forces against stronger conventional armies, using surprise, concealment, and local knowledge. Reconnaissance is the gathering of information about enemy positions. Retaliation is violence undertaken to avenge a previous attack. Pursuit refers to an army's attempt to follow and engage an escaping force, here sometimes across an international line.

Historical context. In English-language sources, "raids" often described anything an Apache party did, including defensive action. Army officers commonly used "hostile" for any Apache person not on a reservation, regardless of what they had done.

Why it matters. These categories help students distinguish an economic expedition from an act of retaliation or a defense of a homeland. An international boundary could protect a fighting party, since pursuit might stop at the line, constrain it, since Mexican and American forces could each attack, or divide communities whose relatives lived on opposite sides.

Potential misunderstanding. The term "raiding" is sometimes used as if it explained Apache conflict entirely. It was one practice among several and it occurred in a borderland where livestock theft, captive-taking, and bounty hunting were widespread among many peoples.

Apache Pass and the Geography of Resistance

Everyday Life and Social History

Family, Work, and Ceremony in Apache Communities

Apache daily life was organized around the household and the extended family. Women and girls gathered, processed, and stored food, including agave hearts roasted in earth pits, mesquite pods ground into meal, dried meat, and, among the Western Apache and some Chiricahua groups, maize grown in small fields. They built and maintained dwellings such as the brush-covered wickiup, prepared hides, made baskets and clothing, and cared for children. Men hunted, scouted, and tended and trained horses. Elders taught young people the geography of springs, trails, and seasonal foods, and stories that carried moral instruction and historical memory. Food preservation and the ability to carry belongings quickly were essential in a society that expected to move.

Ceremonial life marked stages of life and sought spiritual protection. The girls' puberty ceremony, celebrated by Chiricahua and Mescalero communities and by Western Apache families with their own forms, was among the most important community events and drew relatives together. Masked dancers representing mountain spirits, called Gáhé by some groups, and healers who acted through spiritual power, held authority in matters of health and protection. Warfare, too, involved ritual preparation, and many Apache people understood success in both fighting and healing to depend on relationships with spiritual power.

Warfare entered this life directly. Attacks on camps destroyed shelters, stores of food, and fields, forcing families to move into rough country in winter or to abandon crops. Children and elders were especially vulnerable. Women accompanied parties in some campaigns and not in others; some took active parts, and others endured the burdens of flight and the care of the wounded. The later chapters of this narrative return repeatedly to these household consequences, which official reports mention only incidentally if at all.

Division II

The Cochise Wars and the Breakdown of Peace, 1861–1874

Section 79.5

The Bascom Affair of 1861: Accusation, Hostages, and the Destruction of Trust

In January 1861 a party of Apache raiders attacked the ranch of John Ward in the Sonoita Valley of southern Arizona, drove off livestock, and carried away a boy, Felix Ward, the stepson of John Ward, who was about ten or twelve years old and of mixed Mexican and Irish parentage. Ward went to Fort Buchanan, the nearest army post, and demanded action. The commander, Lieutenant Colonel Pitcairn Morrison, detailed Second Lieutenant George N. Bascom of the Seventh Infantry, a young officer with little experience of the country, to take troops and recover the boy. Ward and others believed that Cochise's Chokonen Chiricahua, whose country lay to the east, were responsible. The evidence for that belief was thin, and the National Park Service's account of the episode does not support attributing the abduction to Cochise. Historians generally agree that the raiders were from a different Apache group, commonly identified as Western Apache, and that Cochise's people had not been involved.

Bascom's command reached the Apache Pass station of the Butterfield Overland Mail in early February 1861. He invited Cochise to a meeting, and Cochise, apparently confident that he had done nothing wrong and with a tradition of cooperating with the station, came into Bascom's tent accompanied by members of his family, including his brother and two nephews and, according to accounts, his wife and child. Bascom accused Cochise of holding the boy and demanded his return. Cochise denied involvement and offered to find out who had taken Felix and to help obtain his release. Bascom announced that Cochise and his relatives would be held until the boy was returned. Cochise drew a knife, cut through the side of the tent, and escaped under fire, wounded but alive. The others remained prisoners.

The next two weeks transformed a local dispute into a war. Cochise gathered followers and attempted to obtain the release of his relatives by taking hostages, seizing several men connected with the mail station and a wagon train. He offered to exchange his captives for the Apache prisoners, but Bascom insisted that the boy had to be returned first and refused to negotiate otherwise. A surgeon, Bernard Irwin, led a relief party from Fort Buchanan that arrived with additional soldiers and later received recognition for his actions. After the failure of negotiation, the hostages held by Cochise were killed, and the officers at Apache Pass responded by hanging six Apache men, including three of Cochise's relatives, on February 19, 1861. Cochise's wife and child and one other prisoner were later released. Accounts of the specific number and identities of the hostages and the exact sequence of killings differ among sources, and students should treat details cautiously.

The affair turned on two decisions. The first was Bascom's decision to hold Cochise responsible for an offense that Cochise had not committed and to detain him during a meeting that Cochise had entered voluntarily, an act that Apache leaders interpreted as treachery. The second was the refusal of the officers to consider a negotiated exchange. Collective responsibility, the principle that all members of a community may be held accountable for the acts of any part of it, expanded the number of people at risk, and it made it nearly impossible for individual Apache leaders to demonstrate good faith. Although violence had occurred between Apache people and Americans before 1861, the Bascom Affair marked, for Cochise and his followers, the breaking of an arrangement that had worked well enough to allow cooperation with the mail station. In later years Cochise's actions often reflected the desire to avenge his executed relatives.

The outbreak of the Civil War compounded the effects. In mid-1861 the United States Army withdrew its forces from southern Arizona, abandoning Fort Buchanan and other posts. Apache communities, including the Chokonen and Chihenne, interpreted the withdrawal as the result of their own pressure, and violence against settlers, miners, and travelers increased in the following months. Some settlers abandoned ranches and mines. The conflict that grew from Bascom's detention of Cochise became part of a broader war with fluctuating intensity that lasted, in various forms, until the early 1870s. The central question for students is how an incident involving a particular raid could expand into a broader conflict when military authorities imposed collective responsibility and rejected the leader's offer to cooperate. Part of the answer lies in the incentives of the officers, who saw the case as a matter of honor and command authority, and part lies in the structure of power: the Army could impose punishments, whereas Apache leaders could only respond with the means at hand.

Context: The treaty documents were negotiated between governments in Mexico City and Washington without any Apache participation. The reports of the Bascom Affair were written by officers and civilian witnesses to explain their actions to superiors, and later Apache accounts were recorded long after the events, in some cases as family memory.

Paraphrase of the sources (not direct quotation): The 1848 Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo contains an article by which the United States promised to prevent raids by Indians living within its new territory into Mexico and to punish them, an undertaking Mexican officials later invoked repeatedly. The Gadsden agreement of 1853, ratified in 1854, annulled that obligation. Neither document mentions Apache communities as political parties to the arrangements. The contemporary army reports of 1861 describe the meeting at Apache Pass as an attempt to recover a kidnapped boy and portray Cochise as an obstructive suspect; Apache-centered accounts and later historical analysis, including that of the National Park Service, emphasize that Cochise denied involvement, offered to help, and was nevertheless detained.

Analysis: Placed side by side, the sources show the gap between how governments described the Southwest, as territory divided by diplomatic agreements, and how Apache people experienced it, as a homeland crossed by a dangerous new authority. The Bascom reports reveal how an officer's understanding of responsibility, hierarchy, and honor shaped events.

Limitations: The treaty texts do not show how Apache leaders learned of the new boundary or what they thought of it. The army reports are partial accounts by participants with a stake in justifying their actions. Retrospective Apache and Anglo recollections may combine memory, family tradition, and later stories, and they sometimes disagree about names, numbers, and sequence.

Perspective: The surviving record is dominated by United States officials. Cochise's own explanations come to us indirectly, and the perspective of the Western Apache raiders who took Felix Ward is almost entirely absent.

Section 79.6

The Battle of Apache Pass, July 1862, and the Establishment of Fort Bowie

In 1862 Union forces returned to Arizona. A column of California volunteers under Colonel James Carleton advanced from California across the desert toward the Rio Grande, a campaign intended in part to counter Confederate efforts in the Southwest and in part to reassert Union control over the territory. Supplies, including water, were central to the column's movements. By July the column's advance companies, led by Captain Thomas L. Roberts of the Fifth California Infantry, approached Apache Pass, where the only dependable spring lay between the Chiricahua and Dos Cabezas mountains. Roberts's wagons carried provisions and two mountain howitzers.

Cochise and Mangas Coloradas, then allies and relatives by marriage, brought together a force of Chokonen and Chihenne fighters, estimated by officers at roughly several hundred, though numbers are uncertain. On July 15, 1862, as soldiers entered the pass, Apache fighters on the surrounding heights opened fire from concealed positions. The soldiers were pinned down, and the Apaches controlled the approaches to the spring. On the following day, July 16, Roberts's men dragged the howitzers into position and fired into the rocks occupied by the Apache fighters. The artillery forced them from their positions, and the soldiers reached the spring. The National Park Service confirms the role of the howitzers in the engagement.

The outcome of the battle was complex. Tactically, the soldiers held the spring, and the engagement is usually described as a Union victory because it secured the water. Strategically, the Apaches had inflicted casualties on a column that was well armed, they had dispersed in good order, and they still controlled the surrounding mountains. The army's occupation of Apache Pass was nonetheless significant. Carleton ordered the construction of a military post at the site, and the first Fort Bowie was established in late July 1862, named for Colonel George Washington Bowie of the Fifth California Infantry. The first post was a rudimentary installation of stone and sod; a second, larger Fort Bowie was built in the later 1860s on a nearby site, and the post remained a base for operations against Chiricahua communities until 1894, with its history documented by the National Park Service.

The battle also illustrates how water shaped war in this arid country. A spring such as Apache Spring was a fixed point on a landscape where armies needed to resupply; control of it determined whether a column could pass and whether a road could operate. For Apache families, the same spring had long been a source of life, and its loss to a military post represented a loss of a point from which they could move freely. Students should distinguish the battlefield outcome, in which the army won the spring, from the larger question of Apache control over the surrounding countryside, where Cochise's people continued to move and strike for another decade. The establishment of Fort Bowie did not subdue the Chokonen; it provided the army with a fixed base and a new target for Apache pressure.

Section 79.7

Mangas Coloradas, Military Retaliation, and the Expansion of the Cochise Conflict, 1863–1871

Mangas Coloradas was among the most influential Apache leaders of his generation. Born among the Bedonkohe and associated with the Chihenne, he had experience of Spanish, Mexican, and American diplomacy, and by the early 1860s he was elderly, perhaps in his sixties or seventies, though his exact age is uncertain. His alliance with Cochise, reinforced by Cochise's marriage to Mangas's daughter, connected the Chihenne and Chokonen, and the two leaders cooperated at Apache Pass. After the 1862 battle, Mangas, who may have been wounded in the fighting, sought peace, or at least a parley, with American authorities.

In January 1863 he approached a military party in southwestern New Mexico under a flag of truce, and he was taken into custody by troops commanded by Captain Edmond Shirland and brought to Fort McLane. General Joseph R. West was the senior military authority involved in the episode. Soldiers guarding Mangas shot him in the night, and the official explanation was that he had tried to escape. The event is documented in the army's reports, but accounts of what preceded it differ. Later testimony from people who were present or who heard accounts, including a miner's recollection, suggests that the guards had been encouraged to kill him and that he had been tormented before being shot. His body was mutilated after death. Historians generally conclude that he was killed while a prisoner and doubt the official escape story, but students should distinguish the documented circumstances of his capture and death in custody from later claims about the exact sequence of the killing and the identity of those responsible, which rest on retrospective accounts.

The killing of Mangas under a flag of truce became a defining memory for Apache communities. It reinforced a conclusion that many Apache families had already drawn from Bascom's detention of Cochise and from earlier massacres in Mexico: that American negotiators could not be trusted and that meeting soldiers under truce could be fatal. For years afterward Apache leaders were reluctant to approach army posts without assurances, and officers who sought peace had to overcome this inheritance. The killing also brought a younger generation of Chihenne leaders to prominence, including Victorio, whose later actions are examined in Division IV.

The war that Cochise continued after 1863 consisted of raids and counter-raids, ambushes along roads, attacks on stage stations and wagon trains, and army expeditions. The Army's response combined patrols from Fort Bowie and other posts with attacks on Apache camps. Commanders and the territorial press called these operations necessary to establish peace, but their effects were often to drive Apache groups deeper into the mountains and to destroy the supplies on which families depended. Attacks on villages, stored food, and seasonal gathering areas had consequences for women, children, and elders far greater than for fighting men, and soldiers' own reports sometimes acknowledge the destruction of provisions. Settlers likewise suffered: ranches were abandoned, travelers were killed, and soldiers on isolated posts faced shortages and disease. The territory of Arizona was organized in 1863 with its own civil government and a population that demanded military protection.

By the late 1860s the Army kept a substantial force in Arizona, and after the Civil War the Department of Arizona was established to coordinate operations. Yet military operations, even when successful in particular engagements, did not produce lasting security. Apache groups moved across the border into Sonora, where they sometimes traded and sometimes fought, and then returned. Scouts and guides, including some Apache individuals, assisted the Army in some campaigns. The conflict's pattern showed how military operations often increased insecurity even when commanders represented them as necessary to establish peace, and the diplomatic path remained entangled with Apache memories of 1861 and 1863. Many Apache communities sought safer arrangements, and this search led some of them, in 1871, to the vicinity of Camp Grant.

Section 79.8

The Camp Grant Massacre of 1871 and the Crisis of Federal Protection

Camp Grant was an army post on the San Pedro River in southern Arizona, near the confluence of Aravaipa Creek. In early 1871 groups of Aravaipa and Pinal Apache, closely related Western Apache communities, came to the post seeking peace and provisions. They were led by Eskiminzin, an Aravaipa leader who was an established figure in Western Apache politics. The post commander, Lieutenant Royal E. Whitman, accepted them under conditions. He recorded their names, issued rations, allowed them to gather wild foods and to cut hay for contracts, and reported on their conduct to his superiors. By late April, several hundred Apache people, perhaps about 500, were living in camps a few miles from the post, according to Whitman's reports. Many were women, children, and elders.

Not everyone in southern Arizona believed this arrangement would work. Residents of Tucson, who had experienced raiding and who believed, in many cases rightly, that Apache raiders were operating in the area, accused the Apache communities near Camp Grant of responsibility for attacks, and they blamed the army for failing to punish them. In the weeks before the attack, a group of prominent Tucson men, including William S. Oury and Jesus Elías, organized a force to attack the Apaches near Camp Grant. They recruited Anglo-American and Mexican-American residents of Tucson and a large contingent of Tohono O'odham from San Xavier. Estimates of the attacking force vary, but it is commonly described as around 140 men, with the majority being Tohono O'odham and Mexican-American volunteers and a small number of Anglo-Americans.

At dawn on April 30, 1871, the attackers fell on the Apache camp. William S. Oury’s retrospective account of 1879 described 140 assailants—92 Tohono O’odham, 42 Mexican participants, and six Anglo-Americans—while his 1885 version counted 146, including 48 Mexican participants. Neither retrospective enumeration should be mistaken for a verified muster roll. Most of the men of the camp were away, and those present were not prepared for an assault. The attackers killed a large number of people, most of them women and children, burned the camp, and carried off children, some of whom were later sold or held in Mexico and in Tohono O'odham or Mexican households. Whitman’s immediate reporting and the contemporary press distinguished the bodies actually located from the larger number dead or missing: an account based on his May 1 report recorded 63 bodies found and more than 100 people dead or missing. Later estimates differ; Karl Jacoby’s archival synthesis discusses figures approaching 140 to 144 deaths. A total reported as “missing” must not simply be added to an independently confirmed tally of dead. Students should understand that these numbers are estimates, not precise counts, and that the sources disagree about how many were men, how many were captured, and how many escaped.

The reaction in Arizona and Washington was divided. Many Tucson residents and territorial newspapers defended the attack as justified self-defense, while military officers, including Whitman, and Eastern humanitarian groups condemned it as murder. President Ulysses S. Grant called for punishment and for protection of Apache people who had sought peace. A federal grand jury indicted a number of participants, and in December 1871 the trial in Tucson ended with quick acquittals. The legal outcome meant that no one was held to account. It also showed that civil institutions in the territory could not be relied upon to protect Apache lives, and it demonstrated to Apache families that the federal promise of protection was limited by local political power.

The participation of Tohono O'odham men requires careful treatment. The Tohono O'odham of the San Xavier and Tucson area had long suffered from Apache raiding and had their own record of conflict, captivity, and retaliation with Apache groups, with both sides having killed and taken captives. Some of the Tohono O'odham who participated sought revenge for earlier killings of kin, and some may have also sought captives. The nation as a whole cannot be held collectively responsible, and Jacoby's research emphasizes that different communities experienced the event differently and remember it differently. Mexican-American participants likewise had their own grievances and their own precarious position in an increasingly Anglo-American territory. The Anglo-American leaders of the expedition, who organized it and, in some cases, later sought to justify it, bore particular responsibility, as did the political institutions that failed to prosecute them.

Camp Grant revealed the limits of federal protection for Apache communities that had chosen peace. The Army had accepted the Aravaipa and Pinal under its authority, but it did not have enough force at Camp Grant to defend them, and military and civil authorities failed to prevent or punish the civilian assault. Eskiminzin survived and later became an important figure at San Carlos and in the Western Apache community, but the memory of the massacre influenced Apache decisions about whether to trust federal guarantees. It also contributed to President Grant's decision to send peace commissioners to Arizona in 1871 and 1872, which opened the way to the settlement with Cochise. The governing question, what the massacre revealed about the limits of federal promises, did not have a single answer, but it pointed to a persistent gap between a policy of peace and the capacity or willingness of governments to enforce it.

Cause and Effect

From a Local Raid to Prolonged War, 1861–1871

Long-term causes

Spanish and Mexican frontier violence and broken agreements; the 1848–1854 transfer of territory; growing American commercial and military presence along Apache routes; competing claims to springs, mines, and pasture.

Short-term causes

The 1861 abduction of Felix Ward by raiders from another group; the army's decision to hold Cochise responsible; the withdrawal of troops during the Civil War; the return of Union forces in 1862.

Immediate trigger

Bascom's detention of Cochise's relatives at Apache Pass in February 1861 and the refusal to exchange prisoners, followed by the killing of hostages on both sides.

Immediate effects

Open war between the Chokonen and the army; the seizure of Apache Spring and creation of Fort Bowie; the killing of Mangas Coloradas; years of raiding and military retaliation.

Long-term consequences

Persistent Apache distrust of truces; escalation of settler demands for military protection; the Camp Grant Massacre; a shift toward the peace policy and reservation negotiations.

Historical complexity

No single factor produced the war. Collective responsibility, local power, Civil War troop movements, transport corridors, and Apache political structure combined. Different Apache and non-Apache groups had distinct interests, and contingency, such as who raided Ward's ranch, mattered.
Division III

Peace Policies, Reservation Concentration, and Forced Relocation

Section 79.9

Cochise, General Oliver Otis Howard, and the Chiricahua Peace Settlement of 1872

After Camp Grant, the Grant administration, whose "Peace Policy" sought to reduce violence through negotiation, reservations, and appointment of agents, sent special commissioners to Arizona and New Mexico. Vincent Colyer, secretary of the Board of Indian Commissioners, visited Arizona in 1871 and recommended reservations for Apache groups. In 1872 General Oliver Otis Howard, a Civil War general with ties to the Freedmen's Bureau and humanitarian circles, was sent as a presidential envoy. His mission was to negotiate with Apache leaders and to locate places for reservations. Howard understood that peace with Cochise was necessary, and that peace could be negotiated only through someone whom Cochise trusted.

That intermediary was Thomas J. Jeffords, who had carried the mail through Apache country and, according to accounts, had earlier developed a relationship of mutual respect with Cochise. In autumn 1872 Jeffords guided Howard, accompanied by a small party, into the Dragoon Mountains, where Cochise's people were encamped in the rocky natural fortress now known as Cochise Stronghold. Howard met Cochise in October and spent several days in discussions. Contemporary accounts and Howard's own recollections report that Cochise insisted on remaining in his ancestral country in the Chiricahua and Dragoon mountains rather than moving to a distant reservation such as Tularosa in New Mexico, which Howard initially favored. Cochise's position was consistent with Apache political and economic realities: his people knew these mountains, their springs, and their food sources, and a relocation would sever those relationships.

The agreement that resulted was a negotiated arrangement. Howard agreed to a reservation encompassing the Chiricahua and Dragoon mountains in southeastern Arizona, and an executive order in December 1872 established the Chiricahua Reservation. Jeffords, a man Cochise trusted, was appointed agent. The Chokonen were to cease raiding, and the federal government promised rations and protection. In practice the reservation was a mixed arrangement: it kept the Chokonen in their own country and gave them a measure of security, but it was also a means by which the federal government hoped to bring them under supervision. The arrangement was a meaningful diplomatic achievement for Cochise, who had secured recognition of his homeland as a reservation at a time when other communities were being removed to unfamiliar places, but it did not mean that all Apache communities had accepted American authority.

The practical difficulties were serious. Raiders from Mexico, where Nednhi and other groups continued to operate, sometimes crossed into Arizona, and Americans blamed the Chiricahua Reservation for such attacks. Settlers and officials in Tucson were hostile to an agency that they believed sheltered raiders. The army, which was not in charge of the agency, complained about the absence of military oversight, and the Interior Department and the Army disagreed about jurisdiction. Cochise's health failed in 1873–1874. He died on June 8, 1874, in the Dragoon Mountains, and the confirmed fact of his death should be distinguished from uncertain claims about the location of his burial, which was kept secret by his followers and which has been the subject of speculation. His death removed the individual whose authority and relationship with Jeffords had held the arrangement together. His sons Taza and Naiche, whose positions are discussed in Section 79.12, did not possess equal authority, and the reservation entered a period of instability.

Mangas and Howard documentary materials remain edition-level references here, not independently collated transcripts.

Context: The documentary record of these events is divided between reports written close to the events by officers and agents, newspapers that took competing sides, and recollections written years later. For Camp Grant specifically, the documentary project assembled by researchers associated with Brown University provides access to a range of contemporary records.

Paraphrase of the sources (not direct quotation): Whitman's reports describe the Aravaipa and Pinal communities as peaceful, record the issuance of rations, and describe finding the bodies of women and children after the attack. Army accounts of the killing of Mangas Coloradas describe an attempted escape, while later recollections suggest the soldiers were encouraged to kill him. Howard's account of the 1872 meeting emphasizes Cochise's insistence on remaining in his country and the importance of Jeffords's mediation.

Analysis: These sources show three features of the period: officers' reports that were candid about Apache civilian suffering when they wished to condemn civilians, the problem of conflicting narratives of events in custody, and the sophistication of Apache negotiating positions. Cochise's insistence on remaining in his homeland shows that he understood the stakes of relocation.

Limitations: Howard's account is a self-presentation by a participant, and it likely emphasizes his own role. Whitman's reports, while valuable, reflect the view of an officer committed to defending his decision to shelter the Apaches. Casualty figures for Camp Grant vary, and accounts of Mangas's death rely on retrospective testimony.

Perspective: The sources are overwhelmingly American, with Apache voices mediated by interpreters and recorders; the Tohono O'odham and Mexican-American participants in the Camp Grant attack appear mainly through others' descriptions.

Section 79.10

The Tonto War, 1872–1873: Western Apache, Yavapai, and General George Crook

While Howard negotiated with Cochise, General George Crook, who had taken command of the Department of Arizona in 1871, prepared a campaign in central Arizona. Crook was a veteran of Indian wars in the Pacific Northwest and had a reputation for combining tenacity with attention to logistics. He rejected the idea that large infantry columns could pursue Apache or Yavapai groups through rugged country. Instead, he organized mobile columns of cavalry supplied by mule pack trains, and he recruited Indigenous scouts, including Western Apache, Pima, Maricopa, and Yavapai individuals, to find camps and guide soldiers. This approach exploited the fact that Apache and Yavapai families were tied to specific food sources, springs, and seasonal camps, and could be attacked where they were most vulnerable.

The campaign, usually called the Tonto War or Tonto Basin campaign, began in the autumn of 1872 and targeted communities of Tonto Apache and Yavapai in the Tonto Basin, the Mogollon Rim country, and the Salt River canyons. Army columns attacked camps, destroyed food stores and seasonal crops, and pursued small groups relentlessly, so that families could not settle, plant, or gather. Crook's strategy placed the burden of the war on families and food supplies, and the effects fell heavily on women, children, and the elderly. Some Indigenous scouts served for pay and provisions, others for reasons connected to their own conflicts with Tonto or Yavapai groups, and still others in the hope of protecting relatives by cooperating with the army. Their service was not collective Apache support for the army, and these scouts frequently maintained kinship ties with the people being pursued.

One of the best-known episodes was the attack at Skeleton Cave on December 28, 1872, in the Salt River canyon. A Fifth Cavalry column led by William H. Brown, accompanied by Captain James Burns and Indigenous scouts, located Yavapai people sheltering in a cave. The army opened fire and the ricocheting of bullets within the cave and the subsequent fighting killed a large number of people. The National Park Service describes a death toll of approximately fifty to seventy-five, whereas a Sharlot Hall Museum account gives seventy-six; these are not interchangeable confirmed totals. The identification of the victims is contested: army accounts classified the engagement as part of the campaign against the Tonto Apache, but Yavapai descendants, including those of the Fort McDowell Yavapai Nation, identify the victims as their people, as the National Park Service acknowledges. Students should resist the military's classification of all resistance in central Arizona as Apache and should recognize the Yavapai as a distinct people with their own historical memory of the event, often described as a massacre.

A further engagement at Turret Peak in March 1873 reinforced the army's advantage. In the spring of 1873, several Yavapai and Tonto Apache leaders, among them Cha-lipun and Delshay, surrendered or came to the army posts, and survivors moved toward reservation settlements established near Camp Verde and elsewhere. The surrenders were driven by hunger, exhaustion, loss of family members, and the army's relentless pressure, rather than by acceptance of American sovereignty. Crook's reputation grew, and in 1873 he was promoted to brigadier general. The campaign also provided a template for later operations: use of scouts, mobile columns, attacks on subsistence, and the combination of military pressure with offers of reservation life. It also reveals a distinction central to this chapter. Cochise negotiated peace on terms preserving his homeland, whereas the communities of central Arizona were subjected to war until they accepted concentration on reservations.

Section 79.11

The San Carlos Reservation, Apache Agencies, and the Federal Policy of Concentration

The reservation system that emerged in Arizona and New Mexico in the early 1870s was not a single institution. It consisted of several reserves and agencies established by executive order and administered by the Bureau of Indian Affairs, within the Interior Department, with the Army's involvement in security and, at times, in management. The White Mountain reservation and its agency near Fort Apache served mainly White Mountain and Cibecue Apache families. The San Carlos agency on the Gila River, established in the early 1870s, became a larger site where officials sought to concentrate groups from different regions. The Chiricahua Reservation in the Chiricahua and Dragoon mountains was separate, the Hot Springs or Ojo Caliente reservation in New Mexico was intended for the Chihenne, and the Mescalero reservation near Fort Stanton in southern New Mexico served the Mescalero and, at times, others. The Camp Verde reservation housed Yavapai and Tonto Apache people until 1875. San Carlos was therefore only one of several reservation communities, and it should not be treated as the sole Apache reservation.

The federal policy that became known as concentration aimed to bring previously autonomous communities under the supervision of agents, to distribute rations and supplies, to encourage farming, and to restrict movement. Officials argued that concentrating communities at fewer sites would reduce administrative costs, make surveillance easier, and promote assimilation. John P. Clum, who became agent at San Carlos in 1874, used the policy aggressively, consolidating Apache groups from several reservations and establishing an Apache police force and a tribal court of limited authority.

The conditions of reservation life were often harsh. San Carlos lay in low desert country along the Gila River, with extreme summer heat, poor soils, and problems of malaria and other diseases that the people found unfamiliar. Water was available but not always reliable, and irrigation schemes depended on administrative decisions. Rations were frequently inadequate or of poor quality, and corruption, including the diversion of supplies by contractors and agents, was widespread in the Indian service nationally and recurred in the region. People accustomed to hunting, gathering, and farming in higher, cooler areas found that the new environment could not support them, and restrictions on movement prevented them from seeking food elsewhere. Communities that had long lived in different environments were also brought together on the same ground, sometimes with histories of conflict, which increased tensions.

Experiences varied. The Chiricahua from the Chokonen division had one set of arrangements under Cochise's settlement and later suffered closure of their reservation. The Chihenne at Ojo Caliente faced the closure of their reservation in 1877 and removal to San Carlos. Western Apache communities at Fort Apache and Cibecue lived on or near their own territory, and for some, the reservation overlapped with their homeland, a circumstance that offered more continuity. The Mescalero had a reservation in their own country but experienced pressure from settlers and from the movement of other Apache groups. Yavapai and Tonto Apache people from the Verde Valley were forcibly relocated to San Carlos. These distinctions are essential to understanding why Apache responses differed: groups that could remain on or near ancestral land often adapted more readily than those taken to unfamiliar places.

Authority was contested. The Army and the Bureau of Indian Affairs disagreed over who should control Apache communities, over the use of soldiers as police, and over the role of agents. Frequent changes of agents and of army commanders produced inconsistent policies, and Apache communities complained that promises made by one official were not honored by the next. The Army wished to hold Apache people accountable for raids by individuals, while agents resisted punishing entire communities. Political pressure from settlers and contractors, who profited from reservation supplies and who coveted reservation land, added further instability. Reservations built to secure peace thus depended on administrative consistency and resources that were frequently lacking, and the central question remains why such an institution became a new source of conflict.

Historical Vocabulary
Peace Agreements, Reservations, and Forced Concentration

Definition. A military peace arrangement is an understanding between an army commander and Native leaders to suspend hostilities, often with rations. An executive-order reservation was established by presidential order rather than by treaty or act of Congress. A reservation agency was the administrative office, headed by an agent, that managed supplies and relations. Forced relocation is the compulsory movement of a community to a different place, and concentration is the policy of gathering several groups into fewer reservations. Federal supervision describes the agent's and army's authority over residents.

Historical context. After the 1870s, the Apache reservations were created largely by executive order, which meant that boundaries and status could be changed quickly by administrative decision, as the closure of the Chiricahua Reservation in 1876 demonstrated.

Why it matters. Consent to a peace did not equal consent to permanent confinement. Cochise agreed to peace and to a reservation in his homeland; he did not agree to be moved elsewhere. Officials who treated the first as consent to the second created conflict.

Potential misunderstanding. Reservations are sometimes described as places Native people "were given." For many Apache communities they were places where the government sought to confine them, often away from their homelands.

Section 79.12

The Camp Verde Exodus of 1875 and the Closure of the Chiricahua Reservation in 1876

Two episodes of the mid-1870s illustrate how concentration worked and how it differed from place to place. The first involved Yavapai and Tonto Apache people at Camp Verde. After the Tonto campaigns of 1872–1873, many survivors had settled on the Rio Verde reservation, where they built an irrigation ditch and cultivated crops with the help of the agency and army. The settlement was a significant achievement, and the farms were successful enough to produce surplus crops. But the success attracted the attention of contractors and officials who preferred to concentrate Apache communities at San Carlos, and who, according to many historians, saw the Verde reservation's lands and supply contracts as obstacles to other interests. In early 1875 the federal government decided to close the reservation and move its residents.

Beginning February 27, 1875, approximately 1,450 to 1,476 Yavapai and Tonto Apache people were forcibly marched from Camp Verde to San Carlos. The National Park Service describes a route of approximately 150 miles across difficult country; in testimony to the United States Congress in 2024, the Yavapai-Apache Nation described its ancestors’ forced removal as a journey of approximately 180 miles. The two distances reflect different route descriptions and measurements rather than a securely established single figure. The families traveled in winter, over rugged terrain and across cold, swollen streams, leaving cultivated fields and irrigation works behind. The National Park Service states that at least 100 died along the route, while the Yavapai-Apache Nation testified that more than 100 people died from exposure, trauma, childbirth, and drowning. These deaths were a central human consequence of coerced relocation, not an incidental hardship. The Yavapai and Tonto Apache who survived the journey were placed in an unfamiliar environment, where they were sometimes held in conditions of near-imprisonment. The removal is remembered by Yavapai and Tonto Apache communities as a profound loss, and it is an example of forced concentration separating families from cultivated lands.

The second episode concerned the Chiricahua. After Cochise's death in 1874, the Chiricahua Reservation faced mounting pressure. Taza, Cochise's elder son, became the leading figure among the Chokonen, and his brother Naiche was also prominent. Taza continued Cochise's policy of peace, but his authority was less secure, and Chiricahua people on the reservation were divided. Geronimo, a Bedonkohe man with a family history of loss at the hands of Mexican forces, was influential among some communities but did not hold the status that later narratives sometimes assign him in this period, a subject for Chapter 80. In 1876, after two station employees were killed at Sulphur Spring Station in an attack attributed by authorities to a small Chiricahua party, and amid broader settler pressure, the administration decided to concentrate Chiricahua residents at San Carlos. The removal was undertaken in the summer while the 1872 reservation order technically remained in effect. Only on October 30, 1876 did President Ulysses S. Grant issue the formal executive order cancelling the Chiricahua Reservation and restoring its lands to the public domain. The decision to remove residents and the legal act revoking the reservation must therefore be dated separately.

The agent John Clum, assisted by Apache police and soldiers, moved several hundred Chiricahua people to San Carlos in the summer of 1876. Not all went. Some families, including Nednhi people, fled to Mexico or avoided removal, while some others entered the Sierra Madre, so that the closure scattered the community. Taza, traveling to Washington in the fall of 1876, died there of pneumonia in September, which deprived the Chokonen of a leader and raised Naiche to the head of the Chokonen. At San Carlos, the Chiricahua lived among Western Apache and others in an environment unlike their mountain homeland, a source of continuing friction. They disagreed among themselves: some wished to comply with the new arrangements, others sought to escape to Mexico, and others sought alternative arrangements, such as a return to their old country.

These two episodes demonstrate that reservation concentration was not a single, uniform event. The Verde residents were removed from a working farm settlement to a distant agency; the Chiricahua lost a reservation in their homeland. Both removals were administrative decisions that ignored the preferences of the affected communities, and both contributed to the instability that produced later resistance. The Warm Springs removal of 1877, examined in Division IV, followed the same pattern.

Historical Debate

Did Reservation Policies Resolve Conflict or Reproduce Its Conditions?

The question: Historians debate whether the reservation system of the 1870s produced peace or created new causes of war.

Interpretation A — Reservations were a necessary and partly successful alternative to endless war: Some scholars, especially those writing from the perspective of military and administrative history, argue that reservations, combined with rations and the Peace Policy, offered Apache families a way to survive and brought an end to many raids. They point to the success of the Chiricahua Reservation under Cochise and to farms at Camp Verde and among the Western Apache.
Interpretation B — Reservations reproduced the conditions of conflict: Others argue that policies of concentration, forced removal, inadequate supplies, corruption, and administrative inconsistency created new grievances, as the Camp Verde removal, the Chiricahua closure, and the Warm Springs removal show, and that Victorio's war and later resistance grew directly from them.
Interpretation C — The answer varied by community and time: A third approach holds that outcomes depended on whether a reservation lay in the community's homeland, whether supplies were reliable, who the agent was, and whether the government kept its commitments. On this view, Cochise's reservation and San Carlos in the late 1870s should be compared as different cases, not as variants of one policy.

Evidence: Annual agency reports, army correspondence, accounts of Apache leaders' requests, records of flights from reservations, and later Apache testimony.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns causation and interpretation: how much weight to give reservation conditions versus settler pressure and individual leaders' choices, and whether the closures were avoidable.

Why it matters: If reservations bred conflict, then later military campaigns were partly products of earlier policy. If they brought peace where honored, then the failures lie in administration and bad faith.

Student task: Using the sections of this chapter on Cochise's reservation, the Verde removal, and the Warm Springs removal, write a short assessment identifying which interpretation the evidence best supports and what additional evidence would clarify the problem.

Section 79.13

The Warm Springs Apache, Ojo Caliente, and the Breakdown of Reservation Arrangements

The Chihenne, the Warm Springs or Mimbres Apache, regarded the mountains, valleys, and hot springs of southwestern New Mexico as their homeland. Ojo Caliente, in the Warm Springs country of present-day Sierra County, was a place of particular importance, associated with healing waters, farming, and winter residence. (It must not be confused with the similarly named locality in Texas near Fort Quitman.) After Mangas Coloradas's death, Chihenne leadership passed to a group of men and women including Victorio, Loco, Nana, and Lozen. Victorio, born around 1825 according to common estimates, was an experienced leader who had fought in the earlier wars. Loco, a leader with a reputation as a peacemaker, represented an approach that emphasized accommodation. Nana, a skilled elder warrior, and Lozen, Victorio's sister, were important members of the community, and each held influence of a different kind.

In the early 1870s the federal government created a Warm Springs reservation arrangement, establishing an agency at or near Ojo Caliente where the Chihenne could receive rations and live in their own country. The arrangement was fragile. It depended on federal funding and on settlers' acceptance, and it was affected by the wider Apache policy, including the push to concentrate communities at San Carlos. In 1877 the Interior Department decided to close the Warm Springs agency and to transfer the Chihenne to San Carlos. In April and May of 1877, agent Clum, accompanied by Apache police, arrived at Ojo Caliente, arrested Geronimo and others who were present, and moved a large number of Warm Springs people, several hundred, to San Carlos. This removal separated them from their homeland, placed them with groups with whom their relations were uneasy, and exposed them to the harsh conditions of the San Carlos agency.

Not all Chihenne accepted removal. Victorio and a group of followers left San Carlos in September 1877, traveling back toward New Mexico. They sought a place to live that would be acceptable to them and to the authorities. For roughly the next two years, they moved among army posts, negotiated with officers and agents, and sought permission to return to Ojo Caliente. At various times they were at Fort Wingate in New Mexico and at the Mescalero reservation near Fort Stanton, where Mescalero Apache relatives and friends gave them refuge. The Mescalero reservation was thus a temporary haven for Warm Springs people, but it was not a secure one, since the reservation lay under the supervision of an agent who was under pressure from settlers and from the Army to return the Chihenne to San Carlos or to otherwise act against them.

The historical record shows that Victorio and his followers were repeatedly attempting to obtain an acceptable place to live, not engaged in uninterrupted warfare in these years. They presented petitions to officers, accepted rations when they were offered, and left when they believed arrest or relocation was imminent. In 1879, local authorities in Doña Ana and Grant counties pursued charges against Victorio related to earlier incidents, and rumors of imminent arrest circulated. The effect of these conditions was to make any residence at Mescalero precarious. Victorio had learned from Bascom's detention of Cochise, from the killing of Mangas Coloradas, and from the removal of 1877 that submission to arrest could be lethal. When the army attempted to detain him in the summer of 1879, he left the reservation, and the war known by his name began. The distinction between the long period of attempted compliance and negotiation and the subsequent period of armed resistance is central to understanding the events of 1879–1880.

The Warm Springs experience also demonstrates the limits of the labels used in official reports. The Chihenne were Chiricahua, but they were not the same as the Chokonen of Cochise or the Bedonkohe of Geronimo, and their leaders had their own goals. Loco's followers, who stayed at San Carlos, and Victorio's followers, who sought another refuge, made different choices from the same set of pressures. The decisions of Warm Springs families to live at Mescalero, to return to Ojo Caliente, or to fight reflect, again, Apache political decentralization and the many ways that Apache people responded to American policy.

Section 79.14

Victorio's War, 1879–1880: Military Resistance across New Mexico, Texas, and Chihuahua

In the late summer of 1879, as the threat of arrest grew, Victorio left the Mescalero reservation with a group of followers. On September 4, 1879, his party attacked a detachment of the Ninth Cavalry near Ojo Caliente, killing several soldiers and taking the horses of the unit. This event is usually treated as the beginning of Victorio's war, though Apache accounts emphasize the sequence of events that led him to this point. In the following months Victorio's following grew and changed. It included Warm Springs families and Mescalero and other participants who joined for various reasons. The fighting parties were accompanied, at times, by women and children, and the group's size fluctuated, with estimates in army sources ranging from perhaps a hundred or so fighters to larger numbers including noncombatants.

Victorio's strategy resembled earlier Apache defensive practice but operated at larger scale. His forces moved quickly across the mountains of southwestern New Mexico, the Black Range, and the San Andres Mountains, ambushed patrols, attacked ranches and stagecoach routes to obtain horses, supplies, and ammunition, and used mountain refuges. They crossed the border into Chihuahua, where they used the Sierra Madre and the desert ranges for refuge, and returned to New Mexico and Texas when pressure eased. The international line was a major resource. American troops could not follow beyond it without risking diplomatic trouble, while Mexican forces were slower to respond and had their own jurisdictional and political concerns. Victorio used this seam between jurisdictions to elude pursuit, restock, and rest.

The American response was extensive. Colonel Edward Hatch, commanding the District of New Mexico, mobilized troops from the Ninth Cavalry and other units, and in the spring of 1880 operations in the San Andres Mountains, including fighting at Hembrillo Basin in April, tested Victorio's force. The Army also disarmed the Mescalero on the reservation in April 1880, which was an effort to prevent them from aiding Victorio, and which resulted in disruption and hardship for Mescalero families who had not been in revolt. Texas, too, became a battleground as Victorio crossed into the Trans-Pecos region, and the Texas Rangers participated alongside army forces. Residents of the region, Anglo-American, Mexican-American, and Indigenous, experienced the war as a series of attacks, rumors, and flights, and settlers and travelers were killed, while mail and stage services were disrupted.

American descriptions of the campaign portrayed it as the suppression of raiding by a violent band. Victorio and his followers understood the war differently: they were defending their right to live in their homeland and refusing to be moved to a place that had proved deadly. The dangers of forced relocation were not abstract for them. The experience at San Carlos, where Warm Springs people had suffered from sickness and hardship, was known to the community, and the history of arrests and killings of leaders under truce was fresh. Apache accounts later recorded by interviewers, such as those collected by Eve Ball, stress the determination of Victorio's followers to avoid a return to San Carlos, while acknowledging the heavy cost to families.

The campaign placed severe burdens on the families traveling with the fighting parties. Provisioning a group that included children, elders, and women required continual raiding for livestock and food, careful use of water sources, and the ability to move quickly even with those unable to travel fast. Women organized camps, prepared food, cared for the wounded, and in some cases fought. Civilians on both sides suffered: ranch families in New Mexico, Texas, and Chihuahua lost property and lives, and Mexican villagers in Chihuahua, who had their own memories of Apache raids, were alarmed and demanded protection. Apache noncombatants were exposed to cold, hunger, and disease as they moved through rugged country with limited supplies. The war therefore combined armed struggle with the continuing task of keeping a community alive.

The campaign had international dimensions. American officials sought Mexican cooperation, and the Mexican state of Chihuahua mobilized its own forces. Though American troops were barred from entering Mexico except under limited arrangements, the possibility of coordinated action led to negotiation and to a strategy of pushing Victorio toward Mexican forces. Chihuahua's government, in turn, had its own priorities and had earlier engaged in bounty-driven violence, and its commanders pursued Victorio with a mixture of local militia, auxiliaries, and regular troops. By mid-1880 Victorio had brought together a mobile force operating across three jurisdictions, and the next phase of the war was shaped by the efforts of American troops to control the waterholes of western Texas.

Section 79.15

Buffalo Soldiers and the Campaign against Victorio, 1880

African American soldiers played a major role in the campaign. The Ninth and Tenth Cavalry, created in 1866 as segregated regiments with white officers, and the Twenty-fourth and Twenty-fifth Infantry, served throughout the West. They were later known as "Buffalo Soldiers," a name said to have originated with Native peoples, though its exact origin is debated. In New Mexico and Texas in 1879–1880, the Ninth Cavalry under Colonel Hatch and the Tenth Cavalry under Colonel Benjamin H. Grierson, along with units of the Twenty-fourth Infantry, bore much of the burden of fieldwork: reconnaissance, pursuit, guarding of water sources and supply lines, and escorts.

Hatch's operations in New Mexico involved patrolling the Black Range and the San Andres and other mountains and attempting to intercept Victorio's parties in the spring of 1880. The campaigns taxed men and horses, with long marches through country where water was scarce. Grierson, who was posted in western Texas, concluded that chasing Victorio's trail was inefficient, since Victorio could move faster and farther. Instead, Grierson planned to control the waterholes on which Victorio's force had to depend as it crossed the arid Trans-Pecos. Using the telegraph, the railroad then approaching, and rapid movement of detachments, he positioned troops at springs and tinajas, including at Rattlesnake Springs and near Tinaja de las Palmas, in the hope of cutting Victorio off from water and forcing him back toward Mexico.

The strategy bore fruit in the summer of 1880. On July 30, soldiers of the Tenth Cavalry fought Victorio's party at Tinaja de las Palmas, near the Rio Grande, and the Apaches were forced to withdraw. On August 6, 1880, at Rattlesnake Springs, Grierson's command, reinforced by units that arrived in time, again confronted Victorio, who sought to take the water and was driven off. (The Texas State Historical Association gives August 6, 1880; an NPS webpage giving 1886 appears to contain a typographical error.) Victorio withdrew into Mexico. The engagements did not destroy his force, but they demonstrated that control of water could deny a mobile opponent the freedom of movement that was the foundation of his strategy, and they contributed to pushing the campaign toward Chihuahua.

The service of Black soldiers in this campaign must be understood in several dimensions. These men enlisted for reasons that included steady pay, opportunities unavailable in civilian life in the postwar South, and a desire for status, and they served with discipline and endurance under officers who often had low opinions of their ability. They faced discrimination in the Army and in the communities near their posts, and they experienced the same hardships of the campaign as other soldiers. At the same time, they were agents of the United States in the conquest of Indigenous homelands, and the Apache families they pursued experienced them as part of a military force that threatened their survival. Historians therefore examine the military service and sacrifices of Black soldiers without losing sight of their participation in the conquest, a tension that cannot be resolved by choosing either a purely celebratory or a purely critical narrative. The individuals involved had their own complicated motives, and their service took place in a system built on racial hierarchy and Indigenous dispossession.

Section 79.16

Tres Castillos, October 1880: The Death of Victorio and the Survival of His Followers

After the Texas engagements, Victorio and his people moved into Chihuahua. The Mexican government, with its own interest in ending Apache raiding, mobilized forces under Colonel Joaquín Terrazas, a member of the powerful Terrazas family of Chihuahua. Terrazas commanded a force of Chihuahuan militia and auxiliaries, including Indigenous troops from the Tarahumara (Rarámuri) region and other allies, who tracked Victorio's party through the desert ranges of northern Chihuahua. Victorio's people were short of ammunition and supplies and were pursued from several directions.

The decisive battle occurred on October 14 and 15, 1880, at Tres Castillos, a set of low hills northeast of Chihuahua City. Mexican forces surrounded and attacked Victorio's camp. According to Mexican reports, the fighting lasted into the second day, and Victorio and many of his followers were killed. Figures attributed to Terrazas’s report describe 78 Apache people killed—62 identified as fighters and 16 as women and children—and 68 taken prisoner, many of them women and children, with some others escaping. These are the victorious commander’s reported categories, not an independently corroborated census of the dead or an original report collated here from manuscript. Students should treat these figures cautiously, since they derive from a report by the victorious commander, and other accounts differ. There is also disagreement about how Victorio died. Mexican accounts credit a particular soldier, while some Apache accounts hold that he took his own life rather than be captured. The evidence does not permit certainty.

The consequences for families were grave. Captured women and children were taken to Chihuahua City and elsewhere, where some were held as prisoners, some placed in households as servants, and some separated from relatives, with the loss of kin networks that had held Warm Springs society together. The practice of holding Apache captives in Mexican households continued a long history of captivity (Chapter 53), and later efforts to locate and recover relatives continued for years. Some Apache people who escaped, including a small number of men and women, made their way back toward the Mescalero reservation or to groups led by Nana.

The experiences of women, children, and survivors are documented most vividly in Apache testimony. James Kaywaykla, who was a child with Victorio's following, recounted his memories to the writer Eve Ball in interviews decades later, published in 1970 as In the Days of Victorio. His narrative, along with the accounts of other survivors preserved by communities such as the Fort Sill Apache Tribe, offers a view of displacement and survival centered on family, grief, and endurance. Kaywaykla's account must be handled with care: he was a young child at the time, and the book combines what he personally saw with what he learned from elders in later years. The National Park Service places the October 1880 defeat within the wider international campaign, which is the context in which it must be read.

Victorio's death did not end Apache resistance. Nana, an elder Chihenne leader who was with the survivors or rejoined them, emerged as a successor, and Lozen and other women remained central to the survival of the community. Warm Springs people who had stayed at San Carlos, under Loco's leadership, or who lived at Mescalero, continued their own lives and made their own choices. For many Apache families, Tres Castillos was a catastrophe, and its memory persists in Apache communities, but it was also followed by reorganization and continued struggle, as the next division shows.

Division V

Renewed Resistance, Cross-Border Campaigns, and the Road to 1886

Section 79.17

Nana, Lozen, and the Continuing Resistance of 1881

In the months after Tres Castillos, small groups of Warm Springs and Mescalero survivors moved through the mountains of Chihuahua, southwestern New Mexico, and the borderlands, reassembling kin and seeking safety. Nana, an elder of the Chihenne believed to have been in his seventies, took a leading role. He had been associated with Victorio, with whom he had fought for years, and he had the experience, knowledge of routes, and credibility to lead a party. In the summer of 1881 Nana, with a small band of fighters often estimated at a few dozen at most, crossed into New Mexico and conducted a campaign that army officers found startling for its range and speed.

Over roughly two months, Nana's party moved through southwestern New Mexico, attacking ranches, taking horses and supplies, and fighting detachments of soldiers, including units of the Ninth Cavalry. Reports from the period describe long marches, engagements in the Black Range and nearby mountains, and a return to Mexico. Army accounts credit the party with covering a very long distance and evading pursuit by larger forces, though the specific mileage and the number of engagements vary in the sources and should be treated with caution. The campaign demonstrated that the death of Victorio had not ended Warm Springs resistance, and that Apache mobility, knowledge of terrain, and willingness to take risks remained formidable.

Nana's reputation rests on these abilities of age, experience, and mobility. Yet the campaign should not be understood as simply the exploit of an individual. It was a community effort: provisioning, communication with relatives, and shelter in the mountains depended on a network of families, some on reservations, some in Mexico. The campaign's purpose, as far as Apache testimony indicates, was both to avenge losses and to demonstrate that the Warm Springs people remained a force, and it also served to draw attention from vulnerable groups, including the survivors of Tres Castillos.

Lozen, Victorio's sister, is among the most remembered Apache women of this period. Apache testimony, particularly that recorded by Eve Ball and preserved in the Warm Springs community, describes her as a woman with spiritual power who contributed to the safety of her people, helped guide parties, and took part in warfare. Accounts attribute to her a power to detect the position of enemies, which is a matter of Apache religious understanding rather than a claim that historians can verify. She is also associated with assistance to women and children, and a frequently told episode concerns her role in helping a woman, Gouyen, cross hostile country to give birth. The detailed chronology of such episodes often comes from later recollections, and the chapter qualifies them accordingly. Historians should avoid both dismissing Lozen as legend and embellishing the record with unsupported detail.

Gouyen and other Apache women similarly appear in accounts of the period as actors who endured loss, made decisions about survival, and sometimes acted at great personal risk. The Fort Sill Apache Tribe's historical accounts, which preserve the memory of Nana, Gouyen, and other figures, offer a counterweight to army-centered narratives. The survival and reorganization of families after Tres Castillos, who found each other, rebuilt households, protected children, and kept ceremonies and relationships alive, were as historically significant as any individual military engagement. They are the reason Apache communities existed to confront the events of 1882 and 1883.

Section 79.18

The Cibecue Creek Conflict of 1881: Religious Authority, Apache Scouts, and Military Violence

In 1881 a religious movement emerged among the Cibecue Apache, a Western Apache group living in the valley of Cibecue Creek, north of the White Mountain reservation. Its leader was Nock-ay-det-klinne, whose name appears in many transliterations in the sources. He was a healer and a dancer with a following, and he taught that the dead would return and that conditions would change. The details of his teaching are documented mainly through the accounts of observers and later testimony, and some contemporary reports are hostile or sensational. It is clear that the movement drew energy from the conditions of reservation life: confinement, shortages of food, dissatisfaction with American administration, and fear of the future. Religious movements of this kind, discussed more broadly in Chapter 89, were a way for communities to respond to cultural and political crisis.

Army and agency officials at Fort Apache viewed the movement as a threat. They feared that it might stir unrest and they were alarmed by reports of large gatherings. In August 1881 Colonel Eugene A. Carr, commanding at Fort Apache, was ordered to arrest the prophet. Carr led a column of troops of the Sixth Cavalry, along with a company of Apache scouts enlisted at the post, toward Cibecue Creek. On August 30, 1881, Carr's troops arrested Nock-ay-det-klinne and began to withdraw with him. Fighting broke out, as Apache followers of the prophet gathered near the camp and as some of the scouts turned against the soldiers. In the ensuing fighting, Captain Edmund Hentig and several other soldiers were killed, the prophet was killed, and a number of Apache people died. Casualty counts vary by source. The National Park Service's documentation identifies August 30 as the principal encounter.

The role of the scouts was extraordinary. Scouts enlisted by the Army, whose service had been a tool of campaigns like Crook's, now fired on their commanding officers. Their motives were not uniform and are not fully documented. Some scouts were related to followers of the prophet; some shared the movement's beliefs or hopes; some were reacting to the arrest and the threat of violence against a spiritual leader. Their action shows that service as scouts did not signify allegiance to the United States, and that their loyalty to kin and community could override military discipline. Following court-martial proceedings at Fort Grant in November 1881, three enlisted Apache scouts—known in military records as Dead Shot, Dandy Jim, and Skippy—were executed there on March 3, 1882; two other scouts received prison sentences. Published accounts drawing from the proceedings preserve divergent details of the fighting, but the trial and execution dates are independently documented.

After the fighting, Apache groups attacked Fort Apache, and fear spread through the Arizona Territory. Troops were rushed to the area, and the army's attention turned to the White Mountain and Cibecue communities. The conflict damaged confidence in reservation administration among Apache and non-Apache people. Many Apache families feared reprisals, and the episode shaped the choices of the Chiricahua at San Carlos, several groups of whom left the reservation in the weeks after, an action the National Park Service's documentation connects to the Cibecue events. Chapter 80 treats the subsequent history of those Chiricahua leaders.

Cibecue demonstrates how military responses to Indigenous religious movements could produce renewed conflict. An attempt to arrest a spiritual leader, in the midst of his followers, in a community with grievances and with armed scouts in the column, was a recipe for violence. It also shows that Apache communities used religious and ceremonial authority as a resource in difficult times, and that American officials often misread such authority as political conspiracy. The episode belongs to the history of Western Apache people on their own lands and should not be absorbed into the story of Chiricahua resistance.

Section 79.19

Big Dry Wash, 1882, and the Internationalization of the Apache Campaigns

After Cibecue, tension remained high in the White Mountain country. In the summer of 1882, a group of Apache fighters associated with a leader named Natiotish (also written Nan-tia-tish) left the reservation, and clashes with settlers and troops followed. On July 17, 1882, in the broken country along the Mogollon Rim in central Arizona, cavalry under Captain Adna R. Chaffee and others, assisted by Apache scouts, overtook the group at a canyon known as Big Dry Wash. Scouts detected the party and the soldiers surrounded its position, and in the fighting, army accounts reported that a number of Apache fighters were killed, with figures in army reports on the order of twenty, and that a few soldiers died. Casualty figures from army accounts should be used cautiously, since Apache losses were not independently recorded and the dead were often counted by the victors.

Military authorities interpreted the engagement as breaking the capacity of certain Western Apache groups for organized resistance in this region. In the following years, Western Apache people experienced reservation life, with scouting and wage labor and farming, sometimes with continued friction, and the territory of the White Mountain and Cibecue communities remained part of their reservation. The scouts again played an important role, as detectors of ambush and as fighters, and their service highlights the complexity of Western Apache politics: scouts served in the same period in which other Western Apache people fought.

Within two weeks of Big Dry Wash, the governments of Mexico and the United States signed an agreement that reflected the larger problem of the Apache campaigns. On July 29, 1882, representatives of the two states agreed to allow reciprocal crossing of the international boundary by regular military forces in pursuit of Indian groups, under specified conditions, including limits related to unpopulated areas and procedures for notification when approaching settlements. The text is preserved in the State Department's Foreign Relations of the United States collection. A supplemental protocol signed on September 21, 1882 revised the duration provision in Article VIII; the original agreement and its modification should be distinguished from the 1883 campaigns conducted under these diplomatic arrangements.

The agreement reveals several things. First, neither government could confine Apache mobility within the borders it claimed. Apache groups moved across the line as part of established patterns of seasonal movement, kinship, and refuge, and each state's inability to control the movement produced diplomatic friction. Second, the agreement treated Apache people as a problem of military pursuit rather than as communities with territorial authority. Neither state acknowledged in the text any Indigenous claim to the land across which the pursuit would occur; the question was one of sovereignty between governments. Third, the agreement made explicit the logic of the preceding decade: the Apache had used the boundary as a resource, and the two governments now sought to neutralize it. Mexico's willingness to agree reflected its own concern with Apache raiding and also anxiety about American troops on Mexican soil, and the conditions written into the agreement reflect that anxiety.

For Apache communities living on both sides of the line, such as Nednhi and Chokonen families in the Sierra Madre, the agreement meant that the border no longer offered the same protection. It did not, however, resolve the underlying political question. The Apache who lived in the Sierra Madre had their own political authority, their own relationships with Mexican communities, and their own assessment of risk. The difference between international agreements between states and the political authority asserted by Apache communities was precisely what the agreement ignored, and it would become important in 1883.

Section 79.20

Crook's Return, the Sierra Madre Expedition of 1883, and the Changing Character of the Apache Wars

In September 1882 General George Crook returned to command the Department of Arizona. His experience in the Tonto campaigns and his reputation for effective use of scouts made him the army's choice to deal with the crisis. Crook found that conditions at San Carlos and the White Mountain agency had deteriorated, that the Army and Interior Department were again at odds, and that groups of Chiricahua who had left in 1881 or earlier remained in Mexico. He reorganized operations, enlisted Apache scouts, established a more systematic scouting corps under officers such as Al Sieber (the chief of scouts) and Lieutenant Charles B. Gatewood, and sought authority to operate across the border under the 1882 agreement.

In March 1883, a party of Chiricahua men under Chato and others crossed from Mexico into Arizona and New Mexico, killing a number of settlers, including the family of Judge H. C. McComas near Silver City, New Mexico, and taking the couple's young son. The raid caused public outrage and intensified pressure on Crook to act. He prepared an expedition that would enter the Sierra Madre with a large force of Apache scouts, about two hundred, and a small number of soldiers. In May 1883 the column crossed into Mexico, accompanied by pack mules, and moved into the mountains.

The expedition's success rested on scouts, who found the camps of Chiricahua groups in the rugged country. In mid-May, scouts attacked a Chiricahua camp, and in the aftermath, the leaders, whose families were in a vulnerable position and whose relationships with the scouts included many kin ties, entered negotiations with Crook. Several Chiricahua leaders, including Chato and Bonito, and then others, agreed to return to the United States. In the weeks that followed, a large number of Chiricahua people, men, women, and children, several hundred in total, accompanied the column north and returned to San Carlos in the summer of 1883. John G. Bourke, Crook's aide, recorded the expedition in his published account, and the State Department's 1883 correspondence provides diplomatic documentation.

The scouts' role was essential and requires careful interpretation. Apache scouts in Crook's force served for varied reasons: pay, provisions, status, the possibility of influencing the outcome for relatives, and, in some cases, longstanding conflicts with the people being pursued. Some retained cultural and family connections to the groups they sought. Their assistance to the Army should not be read as collective Apache support for conquest, any more than the resistance of the Chiricahua could be read as the position of all Apache people. Their situation also illustrates the pressures of the reservation: service in the army provided income and status in a community where both were scarce.

The negotiated returns of 1883 were vulnerable. The terms under which the Chiricahua came back left unresolved questions of punishment for earlier killings, the jurisdiction of Army and civilian authorities, the conditions of confinement, the treatment of families, and where the Chiricahua would live. Some people returned with understandings that were later disputed. Civilian officials in Arizona wanted trials; the Army and the Indian Office disagreed about authority; and Apache leaders who had returned remained aware of the histories of arrest and killing under truce. These were the unresolved matters that would shape the events of 1884 through 1886. The chapter's concluding question concerns how military campaigns and reservation concentration limited Apache freedom while leaving unsettled the questions of political authority, family survival, and the legitimacy of American control. Chapter 80 carries this story forward through the final campaigns of Geronimo and Naiche, the surrender of 1886, the imprisonment and deportation of the Chiricahua, and the politics of memory; the narrative of Chapter 79 ends in 1883.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Apache Autonomy Before and After Reservation Concentration

What Changed

  • Freedom of movement across seasonal territories contracted sharply as communities were placed under agents and soldiers, and as reservations were created, reduced, and closed by executive order.
  • Subsistence shifted from gathering, hunting, farming, and raiding toward rations, wage labor, scouting, and reservation farming.
  • Leadership acquired new forms, including agency police, scouts, and men and women recognized by agents, alongside older forms of influence.
  • Communities from different regions were brought together, and families were separated by removal, flight, captivity, and the later events of Chapter 80.

What Stayed the Same

  • Extended-family networks, kinship obligations, and local groups continued as the center of social life.
  • Ceremonies, healing practices, and religious authority, as the Cibecue movement shows, remained sources of community power.
  • Detailed knowledge of land, water, and plants endured and was used by scouts, fighters, and families alike.
  • Apache communities continued to make independent decisions about compliance, flight, negotiation, and resistance.

When and how. Change was uneven and abrupt for communities removed by force (the Yavapai and Tonto Apache in 1875, the Warm Springs in 1877), more gradual for Western Apache people who stayed near their homelands, and contested everywhere. Who experienced what. Removed communities experienced the greatest rupture; those living on or near ancestral lands experienced more continuity.

Why the pattern matters: Treating reservation concentration as the end of Apache independence erases the continuing political, cultural, and familial life that survived it, while treating Apache life as unchanged ignores the real limits placed on freedom.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Apache Wars were multiple overlapping conflicts, not one continuous war under a single commander. Apache peoples, including the Chiricahua with their Chokonen, Chihenne, Bedonkohe, and Nednhi divisions, the Western Apache, and the Mescalero, were related by language but politically distinct, organized around extended families and local groups led by people whose authority rested on persuasion. The Yavapai, often mislabeled Apache in military records, were a separate people who shared in the campaigns of central Arizona.

Apache resistance did not begin with the United States. Spanish presidios, peace establishments, expeditions, and captive-taking, followed by the Mexican states' bounty systems and the collapse of rations after 1821, created a frontier in which diplomacy and violence were intertwined. Events such as the 1837 killing at Santa Rita taught Apache leaders to fear negotiation under false pretenses long before American soldiers arrived.

The American conquest of the Southwest, confirmed by the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo and the Gadsden Purchase, imposed a boundary through Apache country and brought miners, ranchers, surveyors, mail contractors, and soldiers. Apache Pass, with its spring, became a strategic corridor on the Butterfield route. The administrative maps of the United States and Mexico bore little relation to the movements of Apache families.

The Bascom Affair of 1861 turned a raid by another group into a war with Cochise's Chokonen, because an officer imposed collective responsibility on a leader who had denied involvement and offered help. The Battle of Apache Pass in 1862 gave the Army the spring and Fort Bowie without giving it control of the countryside, and the killing of Mangas Coloradas in custody in 1863 reinforced Apache distrust of truces. Years of raid and counter-raid increased insecurity for Apache families and settlers alike.

The Camp Grant Massacre of April 30, 1871, in which a force from Tucson including Anglo-American, Mexican-American, and Tohono O'odham participants killed a large number of Aravaipa and Pinal Apache people, mostly women and children, exposed the limits of federal protection. Casualty estimates vary, with Jacoby's research suggesting a toll that may have approached 140, and the acquittal of participants showed that local politics could defeat federal promises.

The peace policy produced the 1872 settlement between Cochise and General Howard, which kept the Chokonen in their own country, while Crook's campaigns in central Arizona subjected Tonto Apache and Yavapai communities to coercion, as at Skeleton Cave, where Yavapai descendants identify the victims as their people. Reservation concentration was not uniform: the 1875 Verde removal, the 1876 closure of the Chiricahua Reservation, and the 1877 Warm Springs removal were distinct decisions with distinct effects, and San Carlos was only one of several reservation communities.

Victorio's war of 1879–1880 grew from the Warm Springs removal crisis, after years in which Victorio's followers had sought an acceptable place to live. It was an international campaign across New Mexico, Texas, and Chihuahua, met by Black soldiers of the Ninth and Tenth Cavalry, who controlled waterholes under Grierson, and by Mexican forces under Terrazas, who defeated Victorio at Tres Castillos in October 1880. Casualty figures and accounts of his death remain uncertain, and the experiences of women, children, and survivors, preserved in testimony such as Kaywaykla's, are central to the history.

Resistance continued. Nana's 1881 campaign, the Cibecue Creek conflict with its religious movement and mutiny of scouts, and the Battle of Big Dry Wash showed that reservation conditions and military responses repeatedly produced violence. The 1882 reciprocal pursuit agreement revealed that neither government could confine Apache mobility within its borders, and neither acknowledged Indigenous territorial authority.

Crook's 1883 Sierra Madre expedition, carried out largely by Apache scouts with their own motives, returned many Chiricahua people to San Carlos but left unresolved the questions of punishment, jurisdiction, and confinement that would fuel the events treated in Chapter 80.

Throughout, Apache communities sustained political independence by mobility, negotiation, and family and ceremonial continuity. Military campaigns and reservation concentration restricted their freedom without ending Apache sovereignty or cultural life.

Chapter Reflection

The Apache Wars matter because they show how conquest in the American Southwest was actually carried out: not by a single army against a single enemy, but through overlapping treaties, local confrontations, campaigns, reservation orders, and international arrangements, each with its own actors and its own victims. Distinguishing among Apache peoples, and between Apache and Yavapai, is not a matter of tidiness. It is the only way to see why agreements failed, why collective punishment was so destructive, and why different communities responded differently.

The chapter also shows that borders are historical constructs with consequences. The line drawn in 1848 and 1854 divided Apache homelands, gave Apache communities both a refuge and a hazard, and eventually produced the 1882 agreement in which two states sought to control a mobility they had not created. Apache communities' own understandings of territory, kinship, and authority continued across that line, reminding students that the political map of North America has always been contested by Indigenous peoples (see also Chapters 141 and 142).

Reservation concentration, treated more broadly in Chapter 84, emerges here as a set of specific decisions with specific human costs: farms abandoned at Camp Verde, a homeland reservation closed, a hot-springs community sent to an unfamiliar desert. These decisions left behind questions that the campaigns of 1883 did not answer. Chapter 80 takes up those questions through the final campaigns of Geronimo and Naiche. Students who finish this chapter should be able to read that history without reducing it to a biography, with attention to families, women, scouts, civilians, and the sources that preserve their voices.

Finally, the chapter illustrates how historians work with uneven evidence. Army reports, diplomatic documents, tribal histories, and recorded testimony each tell part of the story. Reading them together, and being clear about what each can and cannot establish, is the method this textbook asks students to practice, and it is the reason that Apache survival, community decision-making, and continuing sovereignty can be seen as central to the history rather than as an afterthought to conquest.
