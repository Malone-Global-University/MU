Indigenous North America on the Eve of the New Deal

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History · Primary chronological scope: c. 1925–1933

Chapter Opening

By 1933, decades of allotment, compulsory schooling, trust administration, and statutory control had reduced Indigenous land bases and constrained Indigenous economies across the continent. They had not produced the disappearance of Indigenous nations that the architects of those policies had once predicted.

On April 1, 1930, the United States Census Bureau began its fifteenth decennial enumeration. Among the people counted were Indigenous families on reservations, in rural districts, and in towns and cities, recorded on schedules whose categories had been designed by federal administrators rather than by the communities being counted. Fourteen months later, on June 1, 1931, Canadian enumerators carried out that country's own census under different rules, and the Department of Indian Affairs continued to maintain its separate registers of people with legal status under the Indian Act. In both countries, two different kinds of official knowledge about Indigenous people existed side by side: the knowledge of the census, which attempted to classify the whole population, and the knowledge of Indian administration, which tracked those whose lives were governed by statute. The two did not always agree, and the disagreement itself is part of the historical evidence.

Chapter 95 examined the campaigns, investigations, and reform organizations that, by the late 1920s, had made the failures of assimilationist policy a matter of public argument. This chapter asks a different question. Setting reform proposals aside, what had decades of those policies actually produced? By 1930 the major instruments of assimilation had been in place for roughly forty years. The reservation and reserve systems had been consolidated, allotment had transferred enormous acreages out of Indigenous ownership in the United States, compulsory schooling had separated generations of children from their families for long periods, and administrators on both sides of the border exercised wide authority over land, money, movement, and in some cases ceremony. The chapter therefore functions as an assessment at a turning point: a documented baseline against which the changes of the 1930s can later be measured.

The central argument can be stated plainly. By 1933, Indigenous nations had endured extensive territorial dispossession, economic marginalization, administrative intervention, and educational disruption. Nevertheless, they retained political institutions, kinship networks, cultural knowledge, spiritual traditions, and forms of collective organization that assimilationist policy had failed to destroy. The Great Depression intensified inequalities that already existed, making the limits of federal administration harder to ignore, while Indigenous communities entered the coming reform period with their own institutions, grievances, and competing visions of the future. The chapter's governing distinction is between material impoverishment, which was widespread and well documented, and political or cultural disappearance, which did not occur.

The evidence for this assessment is uneven, and the chapter treats that unevenness as part of its subject. Two bodies of evidence stand at its center. The first is the 1930 United States Census, which recorded 332,397 American Indians in the continental United States, excluding Alaska, and with Alaska Native and Canadian populations counted under different systems. The second is the 1928 Meriam Report, formally The Problem of Indian Administration, the survey conducted by investigators of the Institute for Government Research, which documented serious economic and social difficulties in the communities it visited. A census and a survey are different instruments. One counts people by official category; the other describes conditions through the eyes of investigators with their own assumptions. Neither is a neutral window onto Indigenous life, and the chapter does not treat them as interchangeable measures.

The chapter is also continental. Its chronological reference point is the American New Deal, but the Indigenous world of 1930 did not stop at the Canadian border or at the edge of the continental United States. First Nations, Métis, and Inuit communities lived under Canadian arrangements that differed from American ones in law, administration, and consequence. Alaska Native peoples lived under yet another set of circumstances, and the territories of nations such as the Tlingit, Haida, Blackfoot, and Tohono O'odham, or the Indigenous peoples of the northern borderlands, had been cut by lines drawn without their consent. South of the United States, Indigenous communities in postrevolutionary Mexico were subject to their own distinct policies and were not part of the American story that this chapter anticipates.

Finally, the chapter attempts to avoid two opposite errors. One is to write the early 1930s exclusively as a story of poverty, disease, and dispossession, which would reduce Indigenous communities to the conditions imposed on them. The other is to treat survival as proof that policy had little effect, which would minimize real and lasting harm. Indigenous governments, families, ceremonial societies, farmers, herders, fishers, wage workers, and political organizers were active participants in the economies and institutions of 1930. Their choices, adaptations, and disagreements are central to what historians know about the period. The chapter closes on a question that Chapter 97 will take up: whether a new federal policy would strengthen Indigenous authority or merely reorganize federal control.

The sections that follow are organized in five divisions: population, territory, and political status; economies and the Depression; health, education, and family life; political and cultural continuity, including Canada and Alaska; and the administrative and environmental threshold of change between 1930 and 1933. Throughout, students are asked to read national statistics with care, to distinguish what the evidence documents from what it suggests, and to remember that the Indigenous North America of 1930 was a continent of many different conditions rather than a single condition.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Indigenous population totals around 1930 depend on the classifications and methods used by census bureaus and Indian administrations rather than reflecting a single objective count.
  2. Distinguish among tribal lands, trust allotments, fee-patent lands, leased lands, and non-Indigenous holdings within reservation boundaries, and describe how inheritance fractionation complicated each.
  3. Compare American allotment with the reserve system established under Canada's Indian Act, identifying differences in land tenure and administration.
  4. Analyze what the Indian Citizenship Act of 1924 did and did not change, and explain why citizenship coexisted with federal guardianship and uneven access to voting.
  5. Assess the economic position of Indigenous agricultural, pastoral, wage-earning, and subsistence households entering the 1930s, distinguishing problems that predated 1929 from those the Depression created or deepened.
  6. Evaluate the role of women, households, kinship obligations, and community institutions in sustaining Indigenous families, and the limits of wage and income statistics for describing that economic life.
  7. Interpret health, mortality, and education evidence from the Meriam Report and contemporary government records, including the limits of those sources.
  8. Describe the diversity of Indigenous political arrangements in 1930, including hereditary, clan-based, village, statutory, and elected governments, and the pressures federal agents placed on them.
  9. Explain how Indigenous languages, ceremonies, arts, and knowledge systems persisted under restriction, without reducing cultural life solely to resistance.
  10. Compare conditions among First Nations, Métis, and Inuit communities in Canada and Alaska Native communities with those in the continental United States.
  11. Analyze primary sources, including census material, administrative reports, the Meriam Report, and Indigenous-authored political writing, for authorship, purpose, evidence, and limitation.
  12. Assess the state of federal Indian administration in 1932–1933 and explain why Indigenous nations entered the New Deal with institutions, grievances, and competing expectations rather than as a uniform constituency for reform.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What had assimilationist policy actually produced by 1933, and how do historians distinguish what policy intended from what it accomplished?
  2. How did census categories, tribal enrollment, and legal status shape the numbers historians use to describe Indigenous populations?
  3. In what ways did allotment and its consequences change Indigenous economic life, and which economic activities were not captured by official statistics?
  4. Why did American citizenship in 1924 not end federal guardianship, and how did Canadian status under the Indian Act differ?
  5. Did the Great Depression create a new crisis in Indigenous communities, or did it expose and intensify problems that already existed?
  6. How should historians weigh government investigators' descriptions of Indigenous households against Indigenous testimony and community records?
  7. What forms of political authority did Indigenous communities retain in 1930, and how did federal agents constrain or work around them?
  8. What does cultural continuity mean when languages, ceremonies, and arts also changed over time?
  9. Why does no single national statistic adequately represent Indigenous North America around 1930?
  10. What different expectations did Indigenous leaders, federal officials, and reform advocates bring to the coming period of policy change?

Historical Context

The conditions of 1930 were the product of developments examined in earlier chapters, and only the outline is needed here. In the United States, the General Allotment Act of 1887 authorized the division of reservation land into individual parcels, held in trust for a period, with surplus land opened to non-Indigenous purchase. Later legislation, including the Burke Act of 1906, allowed fee patents to be issued earlier to individuals judged competent, which exposed their land to taxation and sale. Compulsory schooling, an expanding system of boarding schools, restrictions on ceremony, and a growing body of administrative regulation accompanied these land policies. Chapter 86 treats allotment and Chapter 87 the schools. Chapter 91 examined Indigenous economies after conquest, Chapter 93 citizenship, and Chapter 95 the reform movement that rose through the 1920s.

In Canada, the Indian Act of 1876 and its many amendments created a different structure. Land was set aside as reserves held by the Crown for the use of bands, individuals could receive location tickets, and the legal category of "status Indian" governed access to reserve membership and treaty annuities. Enfranchisement provisions offered a path out of status, and the department's authority over band funds, band councils, schooling, and many aspects of community life was extensive. The 1920 amendments reinforced compulsory school attendance for covered First Nations children, with day, industrial, and boarding schools operating under distinct arrangements; the statute did not require every child to attend a residential school specifically. Chapter 85 examines the Indian Act, and Chapter 98 will treat First Nations politics between the wars.

The 1920s also produced forms of Indigenous organizing and criticism of the existing system. Intellectuals and political leaders examined in Chapter 92 argued for citizenship, claims, and community control. In the United States, the Pueblo response to the Bursum Bill, the formation of reform organizations, congressional attention to Indian affairs, and the Meriam survey drew new public scrutiny to federal administration. In Canada, organizations such as the League of Indians of Canada and the Allied Tribes of British Columbia pursued claims and rights, even as federal law narrowed the space for that political work. Those developments form the background to this chapter, but the chapter's own task is different: to describe the Indigenous world that these arguments were about.

Historians approaching 1930 should avoid two assumptions. The first is that the period is simply the lowest point of a continuous decline, after which federal reform brought improvement. That framing is retrospective and teleological, and it neglects Indigenous initiative both before and after 1933. The second is that the evidence is transparent. Much of what is known comes from reports written by officials, investigators, and reformers, each with institutional purposes. Census schedules record how enumerators classified people, not how those people understood themselves. Agency reports record what the agency wished to report. Indigenous-authored letters, petitions, council records, oral histories, and community archives are essential, though fewer survive in collections that are easy to consult. This chapter uses official evidence carefully and treats it alongside Indigenous voices wherever the record allows.

A final point of context concerns scale. In 1930, the Indigenous population of the continental United States was a small fraction of the national total, and Indigenous people in Canada were likewise a small minority. Yet their situation concerned very large areas of land and a considerable share of the rural West and North. The variety among Indigenous communities was also very great. A Pueblo village governed through religious societies, a Plains reservation divided into allotments, a Pacific fishing community, a Cree or Dene hunting group in the northern interior, and an urban Indigenous household in a midwestern city faced different laws, environments, and opportunities. The chapter's comparative method is designed to keep that diversity in view.

Section 96.1

Indigenous North America in 1930: Survival after Dispossession

Approximately four decades after the consolidation of the reservation, reserve, allotment, and compulsory-schooling systems, Indigenous peoples across North America remained numerous, present, and politically active. That statement may seem obvious, but it contradicts a once-common expectation. In the late nineteenth century, many officials, writers, and reformers in both the United States and Canada described Indigenous peoples as vanishing populations, and they designed policy accordingly. Allotment assumed that communal landholding would give way to individual farming. Schools assumed that children removed from home communities would adopt new languages, religions, and livelihoods. Citizenship and enfranchisement were intended, in different ways in each country, as steps toward absorption. By 1930 these expectations had not been met. Indigenous communities were functioning, governing, farming, fishing, herding, trading, worshipping, and raising children in ways that continued earlier practice while also changing it.

Survival did not mean ease. The assessment that follows documents substantial losses. Land bases had contracted. In many communities, income was low and unstable, health services were inadequate, schools were overcrowded or distant, and administrators held authority over matters that other citizens managed for themselves. The chapter's central analytical distinction is between material impoverishment and political or cultural disappearance. The first was widespread and is well documented. The second did not occur. A community could be poor and still maintain a council, a language, a ceremonial calendar, and networks of kinship that structured daily life. Conversely, an official report could note progress in school attendance or farm acreage while missing the institutions through which people made their own decisions.

Government expectations also contrasted with the actual diversity of Indigenous experience. Federal administrators in the United States organized their work through agencies and superintendencies, each responsible for particular reservations, but the people under an agency's jurisdiction might be one nation or several, and their circumstances might differ from those of the next agency by hundreds of miles of terrain and by entire economic systems. The Diné of the Southwest depended heavily on sheep and goats across a very large and arid land. Pueblo communities along the Rio Grande and elsewhere practiced irrigated agriculture within village governments and religious societies that predated Spanish rule. On the northern Plains, reservation lands had been divided into allotments, and many were leased to non-Indigenous operators. In Oklahoma, the Five Tribes and the Osage had distinct histories of allotment and, for the Osage, oil income. On the Pacific coast and in southeastern Alaska, fisheries structured economic and political life. In northern Canada and Alaska, hunting, trapping, and fishing remained fundamental. A single narrative of reservation decline cannot describe all of these.

The chapter's continental scope matters here. A reader limited to American sources would see only the policies of the Office of Indian Affairs. Indigenous people in Canada lived under the Indian Act, with its separate rules for status, reserves, and band governance, while Métis and Inuit stood in different legal relationships to the Canadian state. Alaska Natives, whose land had not been reserved in the same way as lands in the continental United States, lived under yet other arrangements. The peoples whose homelands crossed the Canada–United States boundary, or the boundary between the United States and Mexico, experienced those lines as impositions that affected movement, employment, kinship, and ceremony. Viewing Indigenous North America as a continental system helps explain why policy change in one country could not simply be read onto another, and why some comparisons are informative while others mislead.

Finally, an assessment of 1930 should not be written as though the New Deal were already visible. Historians know that major legislation followed within a few years, but Indigenous people and officials in 1930 did not. They lived in a present where the future of federal policy was uncertain, where Depression conditions had not yet reached their full depth, and where both continuity and change were possible. Taking that uncertainty seriously is part of the discipline of avoiding teleology. The sections that follow therefore describe conditions as the evidence documents them, and they hold the question of what came next for the final division of the chapter.

Section 96.2

Indigenous Population, Demographic Change, and the Limits of Government Enumeration

Any account of Indigenous population around 1930 must begin with the question of who was counting whom and for what purpose. The United States Census of 1930 recorded 332,397 American Indians in the continental United States, excluding Alaska. The Census Bureau's categories followed instructions to enumerators that treated "Indian" as a racial classification. Under those instructions, a person of mixed Indian and white ancestry was ordinarily to be recorded as Indian if the Indian ancestry predominated or if the person was accepted as Indian in the community where he or she lived. Enumerators in Indigenous communities were also directed to record tribal affiliation. In short, the census combined ancestry, community reputation, and the judgment of an enumerator, and it did so on a form that offered a small number of racial categories.

The Office of Indian Affairs kept its own records, built around different principles. Its counts followed jurisdiction, trust status, and tribal enrollment. A person enrolled with a tribe and living on a reservation would appear in agency records, while a person of Indigenous ancestry who had left the reservation, held fee land, or was not enrolled might be absent from them. Conversely, a person might appear in agency records yet be enumerated differently by the census because of residence or classification. Enrollment itself was shaped by tribal rolls compiled for allotment and by tribal and federal rules about descent, residence, and eligibility for payments, rules examined further in Chapter 144. The differences between the Census Bureau and Indian Affairs counts are therefore not simple errors. They reflect two administrative logics: one counting by race and residence, the other by legal relationship to the federal government.

These differences complicate attempts to read demographic change from census totals. Between censuses, a rise or fall in the recorded Indian population could reflect births, deaths, and migration, but it could also reflect changed instructions, different enumerator practice, shifting community willingness to be counted as Indian, and the reclassification of people of mixed ancestry. The 1930 total was higher than earlier twentieth-century counts, and the Census Bureau's own analysis cautioned that part of the apparent change might reflect more complete enumeration and the way mixed-ancestry persons were treated. Historians generally accept that the Indigenous population of the United States had passed its nadir around the turn of the twentieth century and was beginning slow recovery, but the pace of recovery cannot be derived from raw census totals alone. Treating a classification change as demographic growth, or the reverse, would be an error of method.

Canadian evidence presents a parallel problem. The 1931 census published Indian population figures in several tabulations that used different categories and combined groups in different ways. One published tabulation reported 122,911 persons classified as Indian. Other contemporary tables employed different category schemes, and the Department of Indian Affairs maintained its own registers of status Indians connected with bands and treaty payments. The census, therefore, did not simply measure the same population the department administered. Métis people were not status Indians under the Indian Act, and their enumeration depended on census categories that did not correspond to Métis communities' own understandings of identity. Inuit, too, were enumerated in ways that reflected administrative convenience in the north. These distinctions are part of the historical problem, and this chapter does not silently reconcile them.

Where Indigenous people lived is also part of demography. Most Indigenous people in both countries lived in rural settings, many on reservations or reserves, but significant numbers lived in towns and small cities, and some lived in metropolitan areas. Census schedules and Meriam Report observations about "migrated Indians" suggest that the urban Indigenous presence was real, if small relative to later decades. Reservation populations were also young in comparison with national averages, which reflects both the high mortality of the period and its birth patterns, and which had consequences for schooling and employment. The key lesson for students is that population numbers, however vivid, depend on category definitions, and that the persistence of Indigenous people is better described by the existence of continuing communities than by any single census figure.

Historical Vocabulary
Census Enumeration and Administrative Classification

Definition. Enumeration is the process of counting a population according to defined categories. Administrative classification is the sorting of people into legal or bureaucratic groups, such as enrolled tribal member, status Indian, ward, or fee-patent owner, for the purpose of applying laws and programs.

Historical context. The United States census treated "Indian" as a race category, while the Office of Indian Affairs administered a legal relationship. Canada's census and the Department of Indian Affairs likewise used different frameworks. These systems overlapped but did not coincide.

Why it matters. Numbers produced by one system cannot be assumed to describe the population defined by the other, and neither necessarily corresponds to Indigenous national membership as defined by Indigenous nations themselves.

Potential misunderstanding. Students sometimes treat census totals as direct measures of how many people "were" Indigenous. They are measures of how many people officials recorded under particular rules.

Section 96.3

Indigenous Landholding after Allotment: Reservations, Reserves, and Fragmented Ownership

The territorial consequences of nineteenth- and early twentieth-century land policy were among the most important conditions of 1930. The Bureau of Indian Affairs identifies a reduction from approximately 138 million acres of Indian land in 1887 to about 48 million in 1934. These are endpoint figures. The 1934 number should not be read as a 1930 measurement, and neither should the total be taken as a simple record of sales, since land left Indigenous ownership through several mechanisms. Surplus land was opened to non-Indigenous purchase after allotments were assigned. Allotments received fee patents and were sold or taxed. Heirs sold or leased trust parcels. Land was lost through tax foreclosure, mortgage, and, in some cases, fraud or irregular administration, and some reservations were reduced through agreements and statutes. The loss occurred over decades and at different rates in different regions.

Within reservation boundaries in 1930, several categories of land coexisted. Some land remained tribally owned, held in common. Individual allotments remained in trust, meaning the United States held legal title for the allottee's benefit and approval was needed for sale or long leases. Other parcels had been issued fee patents and were subject to taxation and ordinary sale. Some parcels were leased, often to non-Indigenous farmers or ranchers, with rental income flowing to the Indigenous owner, sometimes through agency accounts. And significant tracts within reservation boundaries were owned outright by non-Indigenous people who had purchased surplus land or former allotments. The resulting "checkerboard" pattern complicated jurisdiction, road building, schooling, law enforcement, and community planning, because the same reservation might include land governed by trust restrictions, county tax systems, and private non-Indigenous ownership.

Inheritance made fragmentation worse. When an allottee died, trust land passed to heirs, and with each generation the number of owners of a parcel could multiply while the parcel's size remained the same. Dividing an 80-acre allotment among many heirs produced fractional interests too small to farm individually, which led to leasing or sale as the practical means of using the land. Administering such parcels required agency staff to track ownership, collect and distribute lease payments, and approve decisions, a burden that grew faster than agency capacity. Taxation of fee lands and the financial pressures facing owners compounded the difficulty. The practical outcome was that land which had been meant to support individual farm families often supported them only as a source of small rental income, if at all.

Canadian reserves worked differently. Reserve land was held by the Crown for the use and benefit of bands. Individuals could hold location tickets entitling them to particular parcels, but the land itself was not alienable in the way allotted land was in the United States, and reserve lands could be surrendered only through a formal process that required band consent under the Indian Act, though the department's role in obtaining and administering surrenders was powerful, and surrenders and reductions did occur, particularly on the prairies and in British Columbia. The Canadian system avoided the heirship fractionation produced by American allotment, but it concentrated authority over land use, leasing, timber, and band funds in the department, and it was accompanied by contested claims over lands never treated with, notably in British Columbia. Comparison shows two settler states reaching different legal forms that nonetheless limited Indigenous control over land.

Land loss affected agricultural capacity, grazing, timber access, community institutions, and economic opportunity. Farm families without adequate acreage could not sustain diversified agriculture. Pastoral communities needed grazing range that allotment could not easily provide. Communities that lost timber or water access lost resources central to local economies. Loss of land also affected institutions, since councils, ceremonial grounds, schools, and burial places depended on a shared land base. Yet land was not entirely lost to Indigenous control. Many nations retained large and strategically important territories, defended boundaries through litigation and lobbying, and in some cases, such as the Navajo Nation and the Pueblos, kept communal tenure in ways allotment never reached. The loss was substantial, but it was neither total nor uniform.

Historical Vocabulary
Trust Tenure, Fee Ownership, and Fractionation

Definition. Trust tenure describes land whose legal title is held by the United States for the benefit of a tribe or individual, restricting sale and requiring federal approval for many transactions. Fee ownership (fee simple) is ordinary property that can be sold, mortgaged, and taxed. Fractionation is the division of ownership interests among multiplying heirs.

Historical context. Allotment created individual trust parcels intended to become fee property after a trust period. Inheritance rules and leasing practices then produced fractional ownership.

Why it matters. These categories determined who could use, lease, sell, or lose land, and they created administrative work that overloaded agency capacity.

Potential misunderstanding. Trust status did not mean the land was inalienable or secure. Trust land could be leased or sold with approval, and the trust relationship coexisted with extensive federal control.

Section 96.4

Citizenship, Federal Guardianship, and the Political Status of Indigenous Nations

The Indian Citizenship Act of 1924 declared that all non-citizen Indians born within the territorial limits of the United States were citizens. Many Indigenous people already held citizenship through earlier statutes, treaties, military service, or allotment, so the act extended a status to others rather than creating it. Its practical effects in 1930 were more limited than the word suggests. Citizenship did not end trust restrictions on land, did not dissolve tribal governments or treaty relationships, and did not automatically remove the authority of agents and superintendents over matters ranging from leasing to schooling. A person could be a United States citizen and a member of a tribal nation, and also remain subject to federal administration of property held in trust. Chapter 93 treats the legal history in detail; the question here is what these arrangements meant in practice.

Voting rights illustrate the gap between status and practice. Under the Constitution, states regulated much of voting qualification, and several used guardianship, residency, literacy, tax, or other criteria to exclude Indigenous people. In 1928, the Arizona Supreme Court in Porter v. Hall held that Indians under federal guardianship were not entitled to vote in that state, a ruling that reflected a broader pattern. In other states, access varied, and officials in some places discouraged or obstructed Indigenous voting regardless of citizenship. Indigenous leaders and their allies contested these barriers, but the contest continued for decades. In Canada, status Indians were not enfranchised federally under ordinary rules; those who wished to vote could do so only by giving up Indian status through enfranchisement, which many refused as a surrender of community membership. The comparison with Canada shows that formal membership in a state's political community could be conditioned on very different terms.

Federal guardianship in the United States rested on the doctrine that Indians were wards of the government, a legal characterization with a long history. In 1930 it justified administrative control over individual funds, lease approvals, and in some places personal affairs. Critics, including the Meriam Report's authors, argued that it had produced dependency and poor administration. Defenders in the Office of Indian Affairs saw it as protection for people who might otherwise lose property to exploitation. The tension between protection and control is a recurring theme of the period, and it was felt differently by different communities. Wealthy Osage headright owners, for example, were subject to guardianship systems that were both protective and, as contemporary criticism made clear, vulnerable to abuse by appointed guardians.

Under Canada's Indian Act, legal status, reserve administration, and political representation operated under different constitutional rules. Status Indians came under the federal government's constitutional responsibility for "Indians, and Lands reserved for the Indians." The act defined who was an Indian for legal purposes, governed band membership, and gave the department authority to approve band council decisions and regulate band funds. Band councils existed, either elected under the act's provisions or retained in custom, but their powers were narrow, and the department could depose elected councillors in certain circumstances. A 1927 amendment barred soliciting or collecting funds for the prosecution of claims without departmental consent, constraining the legal organizing of claims such as those pursued by British Columbia nations. Political rights and legal status, in other words, were tied together in ways that discouraged some forms of Indigenous political activity.

Indigenous governments nonetheless continued to exercise authority. Councils decided matters of land use among their members, mediated disputes, managed ceremonial calendars, and negotiated with agents and officials. In some places the agent's approval was a formal necessity but the council's consent was the practical foundation of any decision. In others, agents overrode community preferences, appointed or recognized friendly leaders, or ignored traditional authorities. The next divisions return to these arrangements. For now, the point is that citizenship, guardianship, status, and tribal membership were overlapping but distinct relationships. Understanding the early 1930s requires holding them apart and recognizing that Indigenous people lived within all of them at once.

Section 96.5

Agriculture, Pastoralism, and the Economic Limits of Reservation Land

Many Indigenous households entering the 1930s depended on farming, herding, or both, but they did so on land bases and with infrastructure that limited what they could produce. The Meriam Report and agency records describe common constraints: soils that were thin or arid, irrigation systems that were incomplete or poorly maintained, limited access to water, shortages of livestock and equipment, restricted credit, and long distances to markets over poor roads. These conditions varied. Where allotments were small, farmers could not easily expand. Where reservations lay in semi-arid country, dry farming was risky, and rainfall variation could ruin a season. Where irrigation existed, it often served non-Indigenous landholders as much as Indigenous farmers, and water rights were frequently contested.

The Diné provide the clearest pastoral example. Navajo households in 1930 relied substantially on sheep and goats, with wool, lambs, and woven textiles as sources of cash and trade, and with livestock also serving as wealth and as the basis for kin obligations and ceremonial exchange. The reservation covered a very large, arid area, and the population and livestock numbers had grown in earlier decades, increasing pressure on range. Federal officials were already concerned about overgrazing and erosion, though the later livestock reduction programs belong to Chapter 97 and subsequent chapters and are not part of this chapter's narrative. What the evidence of 1930 shows is a pastoral economy of real productivity and cultural centrality that operated under environmental stress and under administrative arrangements, including agency control over grazing districts and trading posts, in which Diné herders had limited formal power.

Pueblo communities practiced irrigated agriculture on small, intensively used lands along the Rio Grande and its tributaries and in the western Pueblos, where dry farming and runoff techniques supported maize, beans, and squash. Water was both a material resource and a matter of governance, managed through village officials and shared labor on ditches. Pressure on water came from upstream and downstream non-Indigenous users, and the Pueblos' land titles had been clouded by non-Indigenous claims until the Pueblo Lands Act of 1924 created a process for resolving them. That resolution addressed title but did not fully compensate for lost land and water, as later sections discuss.

On the Plains, farming and ranching on allotments met the difficulties of fragmentation and semi-arid climate. Many allottees leased their land to non-Indigenous operators, receiving rent that supported households but left them as landlords rather than producers. Others farmed small plots or kept a few cattle. Dry-land conditions and falling prices after 1920 affected small farmers generally, and Indigenous farmers had the added disadvantages of restricted credit, because trust land could not easily be mortgaged, and supervision by agents who sometimes substituted their own judgment for the farmer's. In Oklahoma, the Five Tribes and other allotted peoples confronted a mix of fee and restricted land, with a strong pressure toward sale, and the Osage experience was shaped by oil headrights that created wealth for some and also exposed them to the guardianship abuses noted earlier.

It would be a mistake to read these constraints as evidence that Indigenous farmers and herders were unskilled or passive. Indigenous agriculture drew on generations of environmental knowledge, selection of drought-tolerant seed, careful water management, and communal labor practices, and these continued. Navajo weavers and herders adapted breeds and markets. Pueblo farmers maintained ditch systems and field rotations. Plains families combined gardening, haying, wage work, and lease income. Many farms did well in good years. The distinction to hold is between problems rooted in the land base and administration, which predated the Depression, and those added by falling prices and weather after 1929. The next sections develop that distinction.

The character of agricultural risk can be seen by considering how a farm family's needs were distributed across a year. Seed, fencing, irrigation maintenance, livestock feed, and transportation often demanded resources before any crop could be sold. An allottee with a legally recognized title but insufficient working capital could be forced to lease the parcel rather than farm it. A family with access to water might produce food for household use even when sales brought little cash, whereas a household cut off from reliable grazing could not simply convert its land into another productive use. Such differences complicate the tendency of official reports to compare the acreage assigned to an individual with the acreage of an economically viable farm. The first figure establishes a property category, not a livelihood. Water rights, market access, shared labor, and agency decisions determined what that property actually made possible. Pastoral families could also respond flexibly to seasonal conditions through mobility and exchanges of animals, yet fencing, jurisdictional boundaries, and restrictions on access to customary pasture narrowed the options available. The central economic question was therefore not whether Indigenous families had been given land on paper; it was whether they retained effective control of a sufficient and usable resource base.

Section 96.6

Wage Labor, Natural Resources, and Indigenous Participation in Regional Economies

Indigenous people participated in regional economies as workers in many industries, and many households combined wage labor with subsistence production, moving between them according to season and opportunity. In the Pacific Northwest, Indigenous men and women worked in logging, sawmills, canneries, and fisheries, and in Washington's hop fields and other harvests whole families migrated seasonally for work. In the Southwest, Indigenous workers found jobs on railroads, in mines, on ranches, and in agricultural labor, and some took part in the growing market for crafts sold to tourists and traders. On the Plains and in the Great Lakes region, wage work in farming, construction, road building, and towns supplemented farm income. In Canada, Indigenous workers took part in commercial fishing on the Pacific coast, farm labor on the prairies, logging and mining in the Shield country, and the northern fur trade.

Natural resources provided another source of community income. Timber from reservation forests, as on parts of the Menominee and Colville reservations, produced revenue and employment, sometimes under tribal enterprise and sometimes through leases to outside companies. Minerals and oil were leased, with royalties held in trust accounts. Fishing remained central to many Pacific communities. In each case, the question of who controlled the resource and who received its income was decisive. Where companies leased resources under government-approved arrangements, income might flow primarily to the lessee, with Indigenous owners receiving royalties set by administrative standards that some contemporaries considered low. Where Indigenous communities controlled the enterprise, as in some tribal timber operations, they retained more of the value but also faced administrative approval requirements.

Restrictions on resource access affected income as well. Fishing and hunting regulations, particularly state game laws and conservation measures, limited traditional harvests, and treaty rights to fish were contested in Washington and elsewhere. Commercial fishing technologies such as fish traps in Alaska and large-scale canneries on the Pacific coast competed with Indigenous fishers, a theme developed in §96.16. In the north, the fur trade shaped the economy of Cree, Dene, Ojibwe, and Inuit hunters, tying them to prices set in distant markets and to the credit practices of trading companies. Indigenous workers were thus participants in markets but seldom controlled the terms.

Regional comparison shows how different the patterns were. In communities near industrial centers, such as parts of the Great Lakes, the Northwest, and the Southwest, wage employment could provide a significant portion of household cash. Communities whose economies remained primarily agricultural, pastoral, or subsistence-based, as in much of the interior Southwest, the northern Plains, and the north of Canada, had less access to wage work and relied on a mix of livestock, farming, lease income, and hunting. Indigenous labor was often hired at lower wages, in seasonal or insecure positions, and in jobs that offered little advancement. Discrimination by employers and unions also limited access in some places.

A central question is whether participation in regional markets increased autonomy or intensified dependence on outside employers and administrators. The evidence supports both answers. Wages and crafts sales gave households cash and choices that subsistence alone could not provide, and some Indigenous entrepreneurs, traders, ranchers, and fishers built successful enterprises. At the same time, dependence on a small number of employers or a single commodity made communities vulnerable to price changes, and administrative restrictions limited the ability to invest or borrow. Seasonal labor also required travel that could disrupt family and community life while strengthening networks across regions. Historians increasingly emphasize that Indigenous people chose among these opportunities for their own reasons, and that wage work did not by itself imply assimilation.

A wage worker's relationship with a reservation community was often cyclical rather than a single departure. A person might leave for a railway job or the fruit harvest, send part of the earnings home, and return for planting, religious occasions, or a winter season without paid work. Wages could purchase tools, food, and school supplies, but travel costs and layoffs made this income uncertain. Employers sometimes treated Indigenous workers as a temporary labor reserve to be hired at moments of peak demand and released when prices fell. The administrative record of such work is consequently fragmentary: a payroll names an employer and a worker but seldom records how the earnings circulated through a household or helped meet kinship obligations. Nor can an annual figure for employment show the amount of unpaid labor required to sustain a family between jobs. Regional resource industries brought an additional question of authority. Fishing restrictions could interrupt a customary livelihood while permitting industrial harvests; timber leases could produce revenue for an agency account without giving a community equivalent control of its forests. Distinguishing access to wages from control of production prevents market participation from becoming a misleading synonym for economic independence.

Section 96.7

Household Economies, Indigenous Women, and Community Systems of Support

Official statistics on farm acreage and wage employment describe only part of Indigenous economic life. Households in the late 1920s and early 1930s drew on a wide range of activities: growing and gathering food, hunting and fishing, tending livestock, preparing and preserving provisions, making and selling crafts, trading, caring for children and elders, and performing the household labor that sustained everything else. Women were central to many of these activities. They farmed in communities with agricultural traditions, gathered plants, processed hides and fish, wove, made pottery and baskets, managed household stores, raised children, and often controlled the sale of craft goods. In Pueblo, Diné, Northwest Coast, Great Lakes, and many other communities, women's work had both economic and cultural significance, and in several matrilineal societies women held the household and land-use rights through which families were organized.

Kinship obligations structured the circulation of goods and labor. Extended families shared food, livestock, work, and housing. Grandparents cared for children, and older relatives passed on knowledge. Clans and societies assigned responsibilities at births, deaths, and ceremonies, which often required collective contributions of food and goods. Such systems redistributed resources within communities and provided support during hardship, in ways that cash income did not capture. A household with little cash might still have access to food, shelter, and labor through relatives, while a household with wages might be obliged to share them. This is why comparing cash incomes between Indigenous and non-Indigenous households can mislead: the same cash supported different numbers of people and was embedded in different obligations.

The Meriam Report devoted a chapter to family and community life and is a useful source on household conditions, but it must be read critically. Its investigators visited communities, observed housing, diet, and household management, and described what they judged to be deficiencies, such as crowded and poorly equipped dwellings. Their descriptions are valuable evidence of material conditions, including inadequate housing and diets. But they also reflected assumptions about proper domestic organization drawn from white middle-class norms of the period, including expectations about the nuclear family, home furnishings, and the role of women in the household. Practices such as extended-family living, communal food sharing, or the division of labor by gender in Indigenous societies could be misread as disorganization. Historians therefore use the report for observed conditions while questioning its interpretive framework.

Craft production illustrates how household economies combined cultural and commercial value. Navajo weaving and silverwork, Pueblo pottery, Pacific Northwest and Great Lakes basketry, beadwork across many regions, and other crafts were sold to traders, tourists, and collectors, with increasing attention from art markets and museums in the 1920s. Income from crafts could be a significant share of household income for some families, especially women, while the objects also carried aesthetic and ceremonial meaning. Traders and dealers controlled much of the market, and prices for makers were often low, but crafts gave families a source of income that did not depend on land or wages, and they sustained skills across generations.

Household accounts also require historians to attend to the use of time. In many communities the same person moved among food preparation, cultivation, care for children and elders, fuel gathering, trading, and paid work within a single season. A federal investigator who assigned only one occupation to that person recorded neither the range of work nor its importance to others. The Meriam survey's discussion of family and community life recognized that women supplied income through handicrafts and seasonal harvesting, but its authors also filtered such activities through contemporary expectations about domesticity and economic modernization. The record is useful precisely because it makes visible both the activity and the investigator's assumptions. Households were not isolated units. Shared gardens, exchanges of meat or fish, care provided by grandparents, and collective observances redistributed burdens when illness or unemployment reduced a family's resources. These systems were not perfect safeguards against scarcity; demands on relatives could increase just as everyone's material circumstances deteriorated. Nevertheless, they constituted forms of economic organization that income tables did not fully register. Students should therefore resist treating low reported cash earnings as proof that a family produced nothing, while also avoiding the opposite error of romanticizing reciprocity as a complete substitute for adequate land, housing, wages, or medical care.

Cause and Effect

Allotment and Changing Household Economies

Long-term causes

Federal assimilation policy that treated individual landownership and farming as the path to citizenship, and pressure from settlers and states for access to Indigenous land.

Short-term causes

Early twentieth-century acts allowing earlier fee patents, leasing of allotments, and sales of inherited land, along with falling farm prices after 1920.

Effects on households

Smaller land bases, fractional heirship interests, reliance on lease income, more wage work, and a shift of some farming families into landlord status, while kinship sharing and household production continued.

Longer consequences

Persistent administrative burdens, fragmented parcels that remained a problem for decades, and a foundation for later debates over land consolidation and tribal control.

Historical complexity: Outcomes depended on region, climate, and local administration. Allotment did not mechanically produce poverty or farming success. It interacted with credit, markets, and community decisions, and some households adapted effectively.

Historical Vocabulary
Subsistence Economy, Market Dependence, and Economic Relief

Definition. A subsistence economy meets household needs primarily through direct production such as farming, hunting, fishing, gathering, and herding. Market dependence describes reliance on sales, wages, and purchased goods. Relief refers to aid from governments or private organizations to people in need.

Historical context. Indigenous households in 1930 typically combined all three, in proportions that varied by region and year.

Why it matters. Employment and income statistics capture market activity but omit subsistence production and kin-based exchange, so they can misstate both poverty and resilience.

Potential misunderstanding. Subsistence production is not the same as lack of development, and market participation is not the same as prosperity.

Section 96.8

The Great Depression, 1929–1933: Unequal Economic Consequences

The Great Depression reached Indigenous communities through several channels. Farm prices, already weak in the 1920s, fell further after 1929, reducing returns on crops and livestock. Demand for wool, timber, minerals, and fish dropped, and with it employment in extractive industries and seasonal labor. Wages fell and jobs disappeared in towns and cities, so migrants who had left reservations in search of work sometimes returned to relatives. Credit tightened, which hurt people who already had limited access to it. Lease income from allotments declined as non-Indigenous lessees struggled or defaulted. The effect was an overall contraction in the cash economy on which many households had come to depend, layered onto conditions the Meriam Report had already documented in 1928.

The Depression did not begin Indigenous poverty. Evidence from the 1920s, including the Meriam survey and agency reports, shows low incomes, poor health, and inadequate services well before 1929. The proper characterization is that the Depression intensified existing problems and added new ones, such as unemployment among wage workers and sharp price declines for market sellers. In some regions weather compounded the effect. The Board of Indian Commissioners' 1932 annual report describes economic disruption made worse in some western communities by poor weather, crop failures, and livestock losses. Drought and low prices together could devastate farm and ranch households, while communities with more diversified subsistence systems could draw on those systems to buffer the shock.

Comparison among communities reveals unequal consequences. Households heavily dependent on market sales and wage labor, including many in lumber and fishing communities and in the vicinity of industrial towns, often faced abrupt loss of income. Communities with stronger subsistence systems, such as many Pueblo villages and some Diné herding districts, had food from fields and flocks, though prices for wool and lambs fell, and drought could reduce yields. Remote northern communities were affected through fur prices and the credit systems of trading companies, though hunting and fishing continued to provide food. The Depression therefore did not hit all Indigenous regions identically, and generalization is risky.

Relief arrived unevenly. Federal Indian administration in 1930–1932 had limited emergency resources, and agents often distributed small amounts of food and clothing or used existing funds for work projects. Counties and states sometimes excluded Indigenous people from local relief on the grounds that they were a federal responsibility, while federal agencies stressed constraints on appropriations. As a result, people could fall between systems. Indigenous families, councils, and local organizations responded through traditional mutual aid, sharing of food and labor, and community-run efforts, as well as through petitions and pressure on agents and legislators. Churches and private charities provided some assistance in places. In Canada, relief was likewise unevenly extended to reserves, and some provincial authorities treated status Indians as a federal responsibility.

For the historian, this section's main task is to hold together continuity and acceleration. Hardship was not new, but the scale of unemployment and price decline was, and officials who had previously treated Indian economic conditions as a slowly improving matter now confronted urgent need. This helped create the policy environment in which reform proposals found a hearing in 1933, a connection §96.19 examines without anticipating later legislation. It also reminds us that Indigenous people were not simply victims of an economic crisis. They faced it as farmers, workers, parents, and leaders who used the resources available to them, and their responses shaped the conditions that later reformers encountered.

The reports published during the Depression reveal how relief could also become an instrument of supervision. The Board of Indian Commissioners' report for fiscal 1932 described food distributions, special appropriations, and emergency responses to crop and livestock losses. It also commended the Indian Service for asking able-bodied recipients to perform useful labor when practicable in exchange for government assistance. The report thereby documents both tangible relief and an administrative belief that dependency had to be disciplined. Its authors did not write as neutral witnesses: they evaluated whether federal employees and programs deserved support. For an Indigenous household, the practical meaning of relief depended on how promptly provisions arrived, whether recipients could meet work requirements, and whether food or livestock feed was appropriate to local needs. The decline of seasonal employment also led some workers to return to home communities, increasing the claims on land and resources already supporting relatives. These pressures differed sharply between a fishing village dependent on a damaged market, a household able to store or produce some food, and a pastoral community confronting drought. The Depression did not erase those distinctions, but made each one more consequential.

Section 96.9

Health, Disease, Nutrition, and Medical Services in Indigenous Communities

Health conditions around 1930 were among the most serious problems documented by contemporary investigators. The Meriam Report devoted a detailed chapter to health, and its authors, along with government physicians and public health officials, described high rates of tuberculosis, trachoma, and other infectious diseases, elevated infant and maternal mortality, inadequate nutrition, and limited access to professional medical care. Indigenous death rates in the United States were higher than those of the general population, and tuberculosis in particular was recognized as a major cause of illness and death in many communities. Canadian officials likewise reported tuberculosis as a persistent health problem on reserves and in residential schools. These findings are consistent across several independent kinds of sources, which gives historians reasonable confidence in the general picture even where exact numbers are uncertain.

The causes were multiple and rooted in conditions the previous sections have described. Poverty limited diet and housing quality. Overcrowded dwellings and institutions allowed contagious diseases to spread. Water supplies and sanitation were often inadequate, and in many places the nearest hospital or physician was far away over difficult roads. Federal health services were underfunded and understaffed, and the Meriam Report criticized the quality of many agency hospitals and the preparation of some staff. Boarding schools, where large numbers of children lived together on limited diets, were singled out for conditions that facilitated infection. Health disparities, then, were not simply the result of Indigenous cultural practice, as some contemporaries assumed, but of poverty, administrative neglect, and environmental conditions.

Environments differed. In the rural and reservation settings of the Southwest and Plains, distance and poverty were major barriers. In urban settings, Indigenous residents might have access to municipal services but faced discrimination and poor housing. In remote northern areas of Canada and Alaska, medical services were sparse, and epidemics such as influenza and measles could reach isolated communities with severe consequences, as occurred in earlier decades. Indigenous communities also used their own medical knowledge. Healers, herbalists, midwives, and ceremonial practitioners continued to provide care, often alongside or instead of agency physicians, and Indigenous people made choices about whether and when to use federal services, sometimes distrusting institutions that had separated families or ignored their practices.

Statistics on Indigenous health must be handled with caution. Mortality and morbidity reporting was uneven. Vital registration was incomplete in many Indigenous communities, deaths and births could go unrecorded, and causes of death were assigned by people with varying training. Registration areas for national statistics did not always include reservations, and reporting systems in Canada and the United States differed. As a result, published rates may understate or misstate actual conditions, and comparisons between sources require attention to coverage. The best approach is to use the Meriam Report and government reports to establish documented conditions, while recognizing their limits and avoiding both exaggeration and dismissal.

The health record also shows Indigenous participation and initiative. Some communities lobbied for hospitals and clinics, supported day schools with health instruction, and managed their own sanitation and water improvements where possible. Some Indigenous people worked as nurses, field matrons, and health aides, and Indigenous leaders raised health issues with agents and legislators. At the same time, the limits of federal capacity meant that the gap between need and service was large, a gap that the reform discussion of 1933 would address in its own terms. For the purposes of this chapter, health conditions form a central part of the inherited situation, one that cannot be separated from land, economy, and institutions.

The opening pages of the Meriam Report's health chapter provide unusually specific evidence of institutional shortcomings. On printed pages 189–192, investigators criticized low medical salaries, high personnel turnover, inadequate hospitals, weak preventive services, and the absence of reliable vital statistics. They further identified crowding, limited food appropriations, and demanding work routines in boarding schools as threats to children's health. These were observations about the operation of a service, not a complete epidemiological census, and the survey itself cautioned against relying on defective government records. Its concern with tuberculosis and trachoma cannot be translated into an exact national prevalence without specifying who was examined and how cases were classified. A family encountering disease might have access to a traditional healer, a missionary medical facility, a distant agency physician, or no regular practitioner; those possibilities varied even within one administrative jurisdiction. Their coexistence should not be framed as a contest in which one medical tradition simply replaced another. The historical finding is more concrete: health depended on housing, diet, transport, public expenditure, and the ability of Indigenous families to secure attention, and the federal system was frequently unequal to those demands.

Section 96.10

Boarding Schools, Day Schools, and Educational Conditions around 1930

Education was one of the most extensive points of contact between Indigenous families and government. In the United States, the Office of Indian Affairs operated reservation and nonreservation boarding schools and day schools, while many Indigenous children also attended public schools and mission schools. In Canada, residential schools, operated mostly by churches under federal funding and regulation, and day schools served First Nations children, with attendance compelled by law. Chapters 87 and 88 examine these systems in depth. This section assesses their condition around 1930, immediately before the reforms of the 1930s.

The Meriam Report offered a sharp critique of many boarding schools in the late 1920s. It described crowded dormitories, diets that were inadequate in quantity or variety, heavy labor assigned to students to maintain the institutions, and staff who were often poorly paid and insufficiently trained for work with children. It argued that young children should not be sent far from home and recommended greater emphasis on day schools and community-based education. By 1930, federal officials had begun to respond, with some improvement in staff standards, diet, and school curricula under the leadership of the Rhoads administration, though change was uneven and constrained by appropriations. The distance between recommendation and implementation is a theme of §96.19.

Enrollment and attendance patterns varied. Many children in the United States attended school, whether in boarding schools, day schools, or public schools, but attendance could be irregular, particularly in remote areas or where families needed children's labor. School location mattered: in some regions, the nearest day school was too far to walk, so boarding was the only option. Funding limits meant crowding. In Canada, residential schools relied on per-capita grants and student labor, and chronic underfunding, disease, and overcrowding were documented in reports and by critics. The relationship between formal education and employment was weak. Graduates of vocational courses often found few jobs matching their training, and curricula emphasized manual and domestic skills over academic preparation, while returning students sometimes found that they were out of place in their communities and in the labor market.

Language suppression and cultural restriction were part of school life. Many schools prohibited Indigenous languages, enforced English-only rules, and discouraged ceremonial practices, and students could be punished for speaking their languages. Institutional separation from family weakened language transmission in some households, though children also maintained languages among themselves and returned to communities where elders continued speaking them. The comparison between the countries shows different legal structures. American schools were federal institutions or contractors under federal oversight, with a growing public school component, while Canadian residential schools operated through a partnership of church and state under Indian Act authority, with compulsion strengthened by the 1920 amendment. The consequences for families were serious in both systems.

Indigenous parents and leaders were not passive. They sought better education: schools closer to home, more adequate facilities, instruction that respected community values, and a role in school decisions. Some families withdrew children or resisted removal. Others embraced educational opportunities, valuing literacy and skills as tools for dealing with the surrounding society. Indigenous leaders, many themselves educated in boarding schools, used their education to advocate for their communities. Chapter 95 covered the reform campaigns. Here the point is the actual condition of the schools and the continuing, varied efforts of Indigenous families to shape what education would mean.

The 1932 Board of Indian Commissioners' report offers a useful view of how proposed educational improvements differed from completed reform. Its authors endorsed, in principle, schooling closer to Indigenous homes and acknowledged proposals to convert some boarding facilities to day-school use. They also argued that boarding schools would remain necessary in certain circumstances and warned against weakening those institutions by withdrawing funds too quickly. The same report discussed federal support for Indigenous students entering higher education. None of these positions proves that a particular child could choose the school most suitable for the family, that an agency offered a functioning day school nearby, or that school administrators recognized Indigenous language and authority. The distance between a reform policy and its implementation could be measured in buildings, teachers, transportation, and the continued power to compel attendance. In Canada, statutory school attendance rules reinforced in 1920 likewise did not create one uniform residential-school experience: day, industrial, and boarding schools operated under distinct arrangements. Comparing school systems requires attention to those institutional differences and to Indigenous parents' own stated preferences, rather than assuming that a change in administrative terminology transformed children's daily lives.

Section 96.11

Indigenous Families, Children, and the Consequences of Administrative Intervention

Family life extended well beyond the institutions of formal education, though it was shaped by them. Boarding-school removals, migration for work, restricted employment, and economic insecurity affected households in different ways. Children away at school were absent from the household for months or years at a time, which altered the flow of labor, the transmission of knowledge, and the emotional life of families. Parents who relied on children's work in herding, farming, or fishing lost that help. Siblings might be sent to different schools. For some families, schooling was a hardship to be endured, for others an opportunity, and for many both. The experiences were not uniform, and generalizing from either the most harmful or the most positive accounts would distort the record.

Extended families remained central. Grandparents provided childcare, taught language, stories, and skills, and served as authorities in family and community matters. Clan and kin systems defined obligations and relationships, often independent of the nuclear household that outside observers expected. In matrilineal communities, descent and inheritance followed the mother's line, and in others, bilateral or patrilineal systems prevailed. Marriages, births, naming ceremonies, and funerals were occasions when kin networks were activated. Children and young adults contributed to the household economy by herding sheep, tending gardens, carrying water, fishing, gathering, caring for younger siblings, and doing seasonal wage work, and these roles were often seen by families as part of education in the broader sense.

The practical consequences of federal and provincial intervention in family life included the removal of children to schools, the regulation of marriage and residence in some places, agency scrutiny of households receiving relief or lease income, and the enforcement of laws on matters such as ceremony and dress. Some administrators also intervened in child placement and guardianship. In Canada, legal status rules affected family membership, including the loss of status by Indian women who married non-status men, a provision of the Indian Act with long consequences. These interventions were real and intrusive, yet Indigenous families were not merely recipients. They negotiated with agents, evaded or ignored rules, kept children at home when they could, and structured their own household decisions according to kinship obligations.

Students must distinguish documented experiences from reconstructed illustrative cases. Documented experiences come from sources such as census schedules, agency records, school records, oral histories, and published testimony, which identify specific people and places. Illustrative reconstructions combine such evidence to portray typical circumstances, and they should be labeled as such. The feature below is an illustrative reconstruction of a household in 1930, built from the kinds of evidence just named. It does not describe a particular real family, and students should treat it as a way of reading sources rather than as a primary document.

Family separation also had a political dimension. When agents or school officials interpreted a parent's objection as resistance to education, they could discount an equally serious dispute over who possessed authority to decide what happened to a child. Conversely, a parent might actively seek instruction, vocational preparation, or English literacy while rejecting the removal of a child to a distant institution. Those positions were not contradictory. School correspondence that lists only admission, attendance, and discipline can flatten such negotiations into the actions of an official bureaucracy, whereas council petitions or family testimony reveal disagreements about distance, language, health, and access to children. Generational change was similarly complex. Young adults educated away from home might return with skills useful in interpreting legal correspondence or negotiating with officials while also having experienced ruptures in language transmission and kinship routines. The same individual could participate in both the preservation and alteration of community institutions. A responsible synthesis must therefore describe administrative damage without treating Indigenous families as structurally incapable of adaptation. It must also acknowledge that the institutional archive generally preserves the decisions of officials more fully than the words of parents and children.

Section 96.12

Indigenous Migration, Urban Communities, and New Social Networks

Indigenous people moved to towns, cities, industrial centers, and seasonal employment districts well before the large-scale relocation programs of the post-1945 era. Their reasons included wage work, schooling, military service in the First World War, marriage, and the search for opportunity or relief from reservation conditions. Census schedules show Indigenous residents in many cities, and the Meriam Report included a chapter on what it called migrated Indians, which examined the circumstances of Indigenous people living away from reservations and the attitudes of officials toward them. The urban Indigenous population of 1930 was small compared with later decades and should not be exaggerated, but it was real, and historians have documented communities in places such as Minneapolis, Chicago, Los Angeles, and the cities of the Pacific Northwest.

Migrants found employment in construction, railroads, domestic service, factories, and service trades, and in seasonal agricultural labor that moved whole families across regions. Housing discrimination, wage discrimination, and uneven access to schools limited opportunity. Officials sometimes viewed migrants as having left the Indian service, which meant reduced access to agency services, while others saw them as people to be assisted with advice and placement. Yet leaving a reservation did not mean abandoning Indigenous national membership or cultural identity. Migrants maintained ties through visits, remittances, participation in ceremonies, and the return of family members, and many returned to home communities during the Depression when urban jobs disappeared. Membership in a nation was a political and kinship relationship that did not end with a change of address.

The Meriam Report's treatment of migrated Indians reflects how officials understood urban Indigenous populations. It described their economic conditions, and it argued that the government had some responsibility toward them, particularly in the form of assistance in finding work and in the transition. It also reflected an expectation that moving off the reservation was consistent with assimilation. Such assumptions framed urban Indigenous people as individuals joining the larger society, while the migrants themselves often saw their movement as an extension of kinship and community networks rather than a departure from them.

New social networks and institutions emerged. Indigenous people in cities formed associations that provided mutual aid, social events, and political voice, such as intertribal clubs and councils in midwestern and western cities. Personal networks, including relatives who had settled earlier, helped newcomers find lodging and work. Churches and boarding school alumni associations also served as meeting points. These institutions were modest in scale and varied from city to city. They were not the large urban Indigenous communities and organizations of the 1950s and later, and projecting the scale or institutions of the relocation era backward into 1930 would be an anachronism. They were, however, early forms of urban Indigenous community-building that anticipated later developments.

The Meriam Report's chapter on migrated Indians, examined separately from its discussions of reservation economy, illustrates another evidentiary problem. Researchers paid attention to Indigenous people living away from recognized agencies, but their treatment often followed the question of how former reservation residents were adapting to the dominant labor market. An Indigenous household in a city might appear in census schedules through occupation and address but not through the relationships that tied its members to a nation, home community, or seasonal gathering. Urban residence itself could be temporary, repeated, or multigenerational. For these reasons, it is misleading to divide people into permanently separate categories of traditional reservation residents and assimilated city dwellers. Connections sustained by relatives, correspondence, remittances, and visits could cross that administrative boundary. At the same time, urban residence created new vulnerabilities: unfamiliar institutions, discriminatory housing practices, and difficulty securing help from an agency that no longer regarded a household as within its normal service area. This experience anticipates later developments but must be assessed on its own modest prewar scale rather than through the much larger federal relocation policies introduced after 1945.

Section 96.13

Indigenous Governments, Traditional Councils, and Community Political Authority

Indigenous governments in 1930 took many forms, and no single arrangement was universal. Pueblo communities were governed by village officials, including governors and councils whose roles were interwoven with religious societies and caciques, in systems that combined older Pueblo institutions with offices adopted during the Spanish and American periods. The Haudenosaunee retained clan mothers, chiefs, and council traditions, along with elected councils on some reserves and reservations imposed by the Canadian and American governments, producing contested claims to legitimacy. Plains communities often had a mix of traditional societies, headmen, and agency-recognized business committees. Pacific Northwest and Alaska communities relied on clan and house leadership, hereditary roles, and newer organizations. In Oklahoma, the Five Tribes had seen their national governments dissolved by federal law in the early twentieth century, though principal chiefs continued to be appointed and community institutions persisted.

Federal agents, statutory councils, and local political practice interacted in complicated ways. Agents in the United States could recognize or ignore councils, control access to funds, and approve or veto decisions, and they sometimes encouraged business committees or councils that would cooperate with federal programs. Where councils were created to approve leases, as with the Navajo Tribal Council of 1923, they could serve as vehicles for resource leasing while also becoming a basis for later political development. In Canada, the Indian Act provided for elected band councils where the department applied the system, with a three-year term and limited powers, while many communities retained hereditary or customary systems that the department sometimes recognized only unofficially or sought to displace. The result was frequent dual structures, one formal and one customary, with communities deciding which to follow on which questions.

Treaty diplomacy and collective decision-making persisted. Communities invoked treaty provisions in claims and in dealings with agents, and councils met to deliberate on leases, land, schooling, and ceremony. Leaders traveled to Washington, Ottawa, and provincial capitals to present grievances, and delegations to Congress and the Senate Indian affairs committees in the late 1920s and early 1930s provided occasions for testimony. Efforts to control land and institutional affairs were persistent, from Pueblo defense of land titles and ceremony to Plains leaders' objections to leases and the Allied Tribes of British Columbia's attempts to press land claims, which a 1927 parliamentary committee rejected and which the amended Indian Act then made harder to fund.

It helps to distinguish communities operating under externally imposed governing structures from those retaining greater control over political decisions. Some communities were governed largely by councils that the agent had designated and that lacked legitimacy among many members, while others kept customary systems intact, with the agent's role limited to a formal review. Between these poles lay a range of hybrid arrangements. This variety is essential to understanding the debates that followed. Reformers who later proposed new tribal constitutions would encounter communities with deeply different political traditions and with differing views on whether federally structured governments would enhance or erode their authority. Chapter 97 examines those debates. The point for 1930 is that Indigenous political life was neither absent nor uniform.

The meaning of representation deserves special scrutiny. An elected council established for the convenience of a federal leasing program could have procedures that appeared familiar to outside administrators without possessing the authority of older community institutions. The Navajo Tribal Council created in 1923 in connection with mineral leasing is an example of an institution whose early function cannot simply be equated with the full range of Diné political life. Elsewhere, imposed elections coexisted with continuing claims by hereditary or traditional authorities; the 1924 intervention at Six Nations of the Grand River demonstrated that an election could itself be an instrument of colonial interference. Yet it would be equally misleading to classify every elected Indigenous leader as an agent of assimilation, because communities also used electoral bodies to press claims and manage affairs. The proper inquiry is who established the rules, whose consent they reflected, which decisions they could make, and what alternative sources of authority survived. These questions reveal why an administrative list of councils is not a complete inventory of Indigenous self-government.

Section 96.14

Language, Religion, Ceremonial Life, and Cultural Knowledge

Indigenous cultural institutions in the early 1930s were under pressure and remained vital. Languages were spoken in homes and communities across the continent, though transmission to children had weakened in some regions as a result of schooling and economic change. Oral histories, songs, and stories continued to be told. Ceremonies, from Pueblo and Hopi calendar ceremonies to Plains dances, Great Lakes and Southeastern ceremonial traditions, Pacific Northwest winter dances and potlatches, and the Native American Church in some regions, were performed in ways that adapted to circumstance. Arts and crafts continued, and community knowledge systems, including agricultural, medicinal, and environmental knowledge, were passed on through families and societies.

Schools, missionaries, agents, and laws attempted to restrict particular practices. A 1921 circular of the Office of Indian Affairs urged limits on certain dances, and agents in some places prohibited or discouraged ceremonies, with the Sun Dance targeted on parts of the Plains and Pueblo ceremonial life drawing special scrutiny and public controversy in the early 1920s. In Canada, the ban on potlatch ceremonies and restrictions on dances were enforced through the Indian Act, which also led to prosecutions and confiscations of ceremonial objects. Missionary organizations sought conversion and criticized Indigenous religious practice, while many Indigenous people became Christians, often blending or combining Christian and Indigenous practices. Such blending was a choice made by Indigenous people for their own reasons, not simply a result of coercion.

Indigenous communities maintained knowledge through adaptation, selective concealment, intergenerational instruction, and institutional continuity. Ceremonies might be held at different times or in different places, performed under cover of another occasion, or shortened. Religious societies controlled access to specialized knowledge, and secrecy was both a protective strategy and an inherent feature of many traditions. Elders trained younger people in songs, languages, and ritual roles, often outside the school context. Communities used organizations such as the Pueblo All Pueblo Council and Native American Church chapters to defend practice. The result was neither simple preservation nor simple loss but a continuing process of transmission and change.

It would be a mistake to frame cultural life exclusively as resistance to colonialism. Ceremonies, languages, and community institutions had purposes and meanings of their own: relationships with the land and with other beings, the ordering of the seasons and the life cycle, social obligation, and the transmission of understanding. They would have existed in the absence of settler intervention, and they were practiced for those reasons. Resistance was one dimension of their meaning in the 1920s, when restriction made practice a political act, but it was not the whole. Equally, cultural survival did not mean unchanging tradition. Languages absorbed new words, songs and designs responded to new materials and markets, and ceremonies adjusted to new conditions. The best description is of cultures that were both persistent and changing.

Loss was also real. Some languages had very few speakers, some ceremonies had been interrupted, knowledge had been lost when elders died without having passed it on, and the separation of children from families had weakened some forms of transmission. Students should keep both realities in view. The evidence of the early 1930s shows communities that possessed substantial cultural resources and also bore real damage, and the future of language and ceremony would depend heavily on what communities themselves chose to protect, a theme that returns in Chapter 123 and Chapter 146.

Cultural knowledge was maintained through relationships as well as through named ceremonies. A language spoken while planting, fishing, making tools, telling stories, or visiting relatives could transmit practical knowledge and obligations in forms that a school inspector would not count as formal instruction. Community members also made deliberate choices about visibility. Under restrictions on certain dances, gatherings might be altered, relocated, or defended in correspondence; that does not mean all cultural practices were identical or equally vulnerable. The 1921 circular and subsequent federal directives against some dances in the United States illustrate administrative pressure, but the existence of a directive cannot prove complete compliance across the continent. Canadian prohibitions likewise had uneven local consequences and did not eliminate ceremonial life. Care is required with the term revival: some practices were renewed after interruption, others persisted continuously, and still others developed new forms in changing social environments. The evidence of survivance is therefore not the preservation of a perfectly fixed past. It is the continued capacity of Indigenous peoples to determine meanings and relationships, even where outsiders exercised power over schools, property, or public gatherings.

Section 96.15

First Nations, Métis, and Inuit Communities in Canada around 1930

Canadian Indigenous communities in 1930 lived under arrangements that differed from those in the United States in law, administration, and consequence. Status First Nations people lived mostly on reserves administered by the Department of Indian Affairs under the Indian Act, with Indian agents responsible for local administration, and with the department controlling band funds, approving council decisions, and overseeing schooling and health services. The department's annual reports for these years record reserve populations, expenditures, school enrollments, agricultural statistics, and departmental activity, and are central evidence for how the department understood and presented its work. Compared with the United States, Canada had no policy of allotment of reserves in severalty, but the act's provisions on enfranchisement, band membership, and the regulation of ceremony and politics gave the department wide authority.

Métis and Inuit stood in different legal relationships. Métis people were not status Indians under the Indian Act and, with exceptions such as those who had taken treaty or scrip arrangements, were largely outside federal Indian administration, which left them with fewer services but also fewer formal controls. In the prairie provinces, many Métis lived in impoverished settlements on road allowances and marginal lands, and Métis political organization developed in response. The Métis Association of Alberta was founded in 1932 and began to press for land and services. Inuit were administered through arrangements connected with the federal government's northern branch rather than under the Indian Act, with trading companies, missions, and police posts shaping local life, and the question of federal responsibility for Inuit was left unsettled until later decisions. These differences meant that the experiences of First Nations, Métis, and Inuit people cannot be treated as one.

Regional differences were strong. On the prairies, reserves supported farming and ranching, and the department promoted farming under what it described as the permit system, restricting sales of produce without the agent's permission, a practice that critics saw as hindering Indigenous farmers. In British Columbia, fisheries and canneries, logging, and coastal and interior ranching structured the economy, while unresolved land claims shaped politics. In Ontario and Quebec, woodland and agricultural communities combined farming, hunting, fishing, and wage work, and the Haudenosaunee communities at Six Nations and elsewhere contended with a dispute over band council authority, with the department's 1924 imposition of an elected council on the Six Nations of the Grand River remembered by many as a violation of their hereditary government. In the north, trapping and hunting persisted as the basis of the economy, linked to fur prices.

Schooling, health, and political restrictions were prominent features. Residential schools enrolled many First Nations children, with compulsion backed by law, and the standard of care and funding was widely criticized, as the system's own inspectors and some church and medical observers had documented. Health services on reserves were limited, and tuberculosis and other diseases were serious. Political restrictions included the potlatch ban, limits on organizing claims, and the pressures of enfranchisement, and status Indians could not vote federally without surrendering status. The Depression affected communities differently: prairie farmers faced drought and collapsing grain prices, coastal fishing communities faced shrinking markets, northern hunters faced falling fur prices, and relief arrived unevenly. Indigenous networks, including family ties, religious organizations, and political groups such as the League of Indians of Canada, continued to operate under restrictive government policies. Chapter 98 treats the political history in detail; this section supports the continental scope of the assessment.

The Canadian department's annual report for the fiscal year ended March 31, 1931 reveals the need to read official judgments critically. In its opening assessment, the deputy superintendent acknowledged unemployment and depressed prices but suggested that many reserve residents had experienced a less abrupt deterioration than their non-Indigenous neighbors because of housing arrangements and some government-provided services. This statement is evidence of how a senior official evaluated conditions; it does not establish that hardship was minor across Canada. The report itself then organized information by health, schooling, lands, and provincial or territorial agency operations. Such organization allows careful regional comparison, but it also reinforces the administrative boundaries of the Indian Act. Métis families and many Inuit communities did not occupy the same legal or institutional categories as status First Nations. A genuinely continental account must compare the report with the separate census and with Indigenous community sources, rather than using its administrative total or generalizations as a substitute for all Indigenous Canadians.

Section 96.16

Alaska, the Northern Regions, and Indigenous Communities across International Borders

Alaska Native peoples, including the Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshian, Iñupiat, Yupik, Unangax̂ (Aleut), Athabascan peoples, and others, lived in circumstances that differed from those of Indigenous communities in the continental United States. Alaska had been a district and then a territory, with no general reservation system, with some Native communities and lands recognized through various statutes and with other lands unresolved, and the unsettled status of Native land claims remained a feature of the period. Federal responsibilities were divided among agencies, including the Office of Indian Affairs for education, and the territorial government played a role in schooling for some residents. Access to public institutions depended on legal classification and on local practice, including educational segregation in some places and uneven access to voting.

In southeastern Alaska, the Tlingit and Haida developed political organization that responded to these circumstances. The Alaska Native Brotherhood, founded in 1912, and the Alaska Native Sisterhood, founded in 1915, became continuing institutions that promoted citizenship, education, and economic and political rights, and that connected communities across the region. Their organizing in the 1920s, along with the advocacy of William Paul Sr., a Tlingit attorney who served in the territorial legislature, provides well-documented evidence of Indigenous institutional continuity. The organizations addressed fisheries, land claims, access to schools, and voting, and they used the legal and legislative systems available to them. Fisheries were especially important, since Native fishers faced competition from large canneries and from fish traps, which controlled access to salmon runs, and Native peoples saw this as a threat to livelihood.

Northern communities elsewhere confronted different circumstances. Inuit communities in the Canadian Arctic, administered through the federal northern branch and not under the Indian Act, depended on hunting, trapping, and fishing, linked to trading posts and fur prices, with missions and police posts shaping local life. Alaska Native subsistence activities were affected by game laws and by commercial competition. In both countries, jurisdiction over northern peoples was less settled than over reserve communities, which allowed communities some practical autonomy but also left them with limited services. Epidemic disease and food shortages could be serious. At the same time, subsistence systems provided resilience, since hunting, fishing, and sharing networks could cushion price shocks to a degree that cash-dependent households could not.

Indigenous nations whose territories crossed the boundary between Canada and the United States illustrate the difference between Indigenous geography and settler-state boundaries. The Haudenosaunee, Anishinaabe, Blackfoot, Salish, Tlingit, and other peoples had kin, trade, and ceremonial relationships across the line. The two governments treated them under different laws, with differing implications for citizenship, taxation, and customs, and border officials could complicate movement. These nations experienced the border as a recent imposition on older networks. Their cases are important reminders that national policy narratives capture only part of the Indigenous experience.

Indigenous communities south of the United States border were experiencing distinct policies under postrevolutionary Mexico, where the government promoted rural education, land reform, and a developing ideology of indigenismo that treated Indigenous peoples as a national concern in ways that differed from American and Canadian approaches. These policies and their consequences are not subject to the American New Deal, and this chapter does not imply otherwise. They are noted here to preserve the continental scope of the narrative, and later chapters, including Chapter 141, return to the Mexican borderlands.

Section 96.17

Land, Water, Environmental Pressures, and the Future of Indigenous Resource Economies

Economic insecurity in 1930 was connected not only to income but to control over the resources necessary for community survival: grazing range, water, fisheries, forests, and arable land. A household's wages could fall or rise, but a community that lost access to a river, a salmon run, a forest, or a range lost something more basic. This section therefore treats resources as the material foundation of the economies described earlier, and asks how land fragmentation, federal conservation policy, settler economic interests, and environmental degradation affected communities differently.

In the Southwest, the central resources were range and water. Diné grazing lands were large but arid, vulnerable to drought, and shared with an expanding sheep and goat population, so that erosion and range condition had become concerns of both officials and herders. Federal engineers and soil specialists in the late 1920s and early 1930s raised questions about the relationship between livestock numbers and the carrying capacity of the land, as did the building of Boulder (Hoover) Dam, which drew attention to Colorado River sedimentation. Those arguments would become major disputes during later conservation programs, and this chapter introduces them without treating subsequent policies as already implemented. For Pueblo communities, water was the vital issue, with irrigation works and rights contested by upstream and downstream users and by public conservancy projects that assessed costs against Pueblo lands.

On the Plains, land fragmentation and leasing limited the ability to organize grazing and farming sensibly. Parcels too small and scattered for effective use were leased to outsiders, and the consolidation of holdings into units large enough to sustain a family or a community enterprise was difficult under existing law. Drought and wind erosion in the early 1930s, culminating in the dust bowl years, heightened the vulnerability of the region's farms, including Indigenous ones. In the Pacific Northwest, fisheries and forests were the key resources. Treaty fishing rights were contested by state regulations and commercial fishing, and timber on reservations such as Colville and Menominee was subject to government-administered logging with disputes over management and returns. In the far north, caribou, fish, and marine mammals sustained subsistence, with fur prices shaping the trapping economy and game laws potentially restricting harvest.

Federal conservation policies and settler economic interests could cut in different directions. Conservation measures sometimes protected habitat or stabilized range, yet game and fishing regulations also restricted Indigenous harvests, and resource development, including dams, mining, and commercial fishing, often benefited non-Indigenous interests more than Indigenous communities. Communities experienced these pressures differently. A group with strong treaty rights and an active council, as on parts of the Northwest Coast, might contest regulation, while a group with fragmented land and little political leverage might simply see income decline. Environmental degradation, including erosion, overgrazing, and depleted fisheries, interacted with these political and legal conditions and was rarely simply natural.

Environmental problems were inseparable from jurisdiction. A stream crossing reservation land could be governed by prior appropriations, court decrees, federal projects, and local agricultural need; the right to live beside water did not necessarily ensure the right to divert it. Pastoral households faced similar conflicts when grazing areas were divided by fencing, leases, or restrictions that did not correspond to customary seasonal movement. In fishing regions, a community's access to customary places could be constrained by industrial harvesting equipment and regulations formulated without its consent. William Paul Sr.'s 1929 argument concerning southeastern Alaska made this relationship between law and livelihood explicit. He maintained that federal fisheries administration displaced Native fishers while protecting economically powerful commercial operators; his statements were political evidence requiring evaluation, not independently verified estimates of every loss. These cases show why conservation should not automatically be understood as either benevolent protection or simple dispossession. Its consequences depended on who could use a resource, which communities participated in making the rules, and who bore the costs of enforcement.

Section 96.18

Regional Case Studies: The Uneven Condition of Indigenous North America

No single national statistic adequately represents Indigenous North America around 1930. Three case studies, analyzed with a common framework, show why. Each examines land, employment, health, education, political authority, and cultural continuity. They are selected for contrast and are not claimed to be representative of all communities.

The Diné Nation: Pastoral Livelihoods in a Large Arid Territory

Land. The Navajo Reservation was among the largest in the United States and was held communally rather than allotted, though its boundaries had been enlarged by executive orders and contested with settlers and the state governments, and checkerboard lands lay along some edges. Employment. Livestock, wool, and weaving supported most households, supplemented by trading, wage labor on and off the reservation, and, from the 1920s, income from oil leases that went to the tribe through its council. Health. Tuberculosis and trachoma were serious, and medical services were scarce relative to population and distance. Education. Schools could not accommodate all children, and many families were reluctant to send them away. Political authority. Traditional leadership was local and kin-based, and the Tribal Council of 1923, created to approve leases, had limited legitimacy among many Diné. Cultural continuity. Language use, ceremonial life, weaving, and silversmithing remained strong. The Diné case shows a large, culturally cohesive nation with pastoral livelihoods, environmental stress, and a governing structure in transition.

A Pueblo Community in New Mexico: Communal Landholding and Water

Land. Pueblo lands were held communally under older grants and recognized in federal law, small in area, and surrounded by non-Indigenous settlement. The Pueblo Lands Act of 1924 created a board to adjudicate non-Indigenous claims within Pueblo grants, which resolved titles but, in the view of many Pueblo leaders, did not adequately compensate for losses. Employment. Irrigated agriculture, livestock, craft sales such as pottery, and wage work shaped livelihoods. Health. Tuberculosis and infant mortality were concerns. Education. Pueblo children attended day and boarding schools, and some communities negotiated over schooling and ceremonial obligations. Political authority. Village governments, religious societies, and the All Pueblo Council, formed in 1922, gave Pueblos a collective voice with federal authorities. Cultural continuity. Ceremonial life remained central and was protected by secrecy and organization, despite external pressure. This case shows durable communal institutions defending land and ceremony in a small territory.

A Northwestern Community: Fisheries and Subsistence on the Pacific Coast

Land and resources. Many coastal and river communities held small reservations or lands and depended on fishing, shellfishing, hunting, and gathering, with treaty rights to fish contested by state regulation and commercial competition. Employment. Seasonal combinations of fishing, logging, cannery work, and agricultural labor were common, and the Depression reduced all of them. Health. Poverty and crowding fostered disease, with limited services. Education. Children attended boarding schools, mission schools, and local schools in varying proportions. Political authority. Councils, hereditary leadership, and organizations such as the Alaska Native Brotherhood in the north combined older and newer institutions. Cultural continuity. Languages, winter ceremonies, art traditions, and fishing knowledge continued despite restrictions such as the Canadian potlatch ban. This case shows a resource-dependent economy exposed to external regulation and market change, with strong organizational continuity.

Placed side by side, the cases make several points. Land tenure differed fundamentally, from communal pastoral and village lands to allotted and fee holdings. Economic risk differed by resource and market. Political institutions ranged from older village and clan systems to recently created councils. Yet all three communities retained cultural knowledge and collective institutions, and all faced administrative constraints. A national average of income, schooling, or health would conceal these differences, which is why the chapter's method is comparative.

The value of regional comparison lies partly in identifying which apparent similarities arise from different mechanisms. Two communities may have had low cash incomes, but one might have faced drought and restricted range while another faced the collapse of fish prices or exclusion from commercially valuable waters. A distant boarding school might serve children from many communities, but the political question for a Pueblo village defending its local institutions differed from that for an Alaska Native family negotiating territorial education authorities. Even demographic statistics demand care: a state or provincial total may conceal migration, shifts in classification, and sharp differences in age structure. Rather than rank these communities by an assumed scale of modernization, students should ask identical questions of each: Who controlled land and water? What work produced food and income? Who decided questions of schooling and government? What connections extended beyond local territory? This approach makes comparison an exercise in identifying historical relationships rather than declaring one community representative of the whole.

Section 96.19

Federal Indian Administration in 1932–1933: The Limits of the Existing System

The American Office of Indian Affairs immediately before the New Deal was a large bureaucracy with limited resources. Agencies were staffed by superintendents, clerks, teachers, farmers, physicians, and field workers, many underpaid and some inadequately trained. Appropriations lagged behind needs, especially after the Depression reduced federal revenues and increased demands. Administrative authority was broad but thinly spread, and agents were responsible for tasks from approving leases to distributing rations. Educational services, health care, and employment programs existed, but, as the Meriam Report and the Board of Indian Commissioners had observed, they often fell short of need. As economic conditions worsened, agents faced mounting requests for assistance with few means to respond.

To what degree had the Meriam Report influenced practice by 1932? Historians distinguish reform proposals from enacted policy, and enacted policy from actual implementation. The Hoover administration appointed Charles J. Rhoads as commissioner in 1929 and Henry Scattergood as assistant commissioner, and these officials, with Interior Secretary Ray Lyman Wilbur, expressed sympathy with parts of the Meriam program: improving boarding-school conditions, expanding health services, and upgrading personnel. Some changes occurred, including measures on school diet and staffing. Yet deeper recommendations about community-based education, reorienting allotment, and increasing Indigenous participation were not enacted, and budget limits constrained implementation. The Senate subcommittee investigation of Indian conditions, begun in 1928, kept pressure on the agency by gathering testimony that publicized shortcomings.

The transition from Commissioner Rhoads to John Collier in 1933 was a significant administrative development. Collier, who had been a prominent critic of federal policy through the American Indian Defense Association and had been involved in the Pueblo land controversy of the early 1920s, was appointed by the new Roosevelt administration. His appointment signaled a different orientation, and some Indigenous leaders welcomed it, while others were wary. This section considers it only as evidence of a changing policy environment. The first emergency measures of 1933 point the same direction: the National Archives dates the creation of the Indian Emergency Conservation Work administration to May 22, 1933, linking federal relief and conservation work to Indigenous communities. These measures are noted here as the earliest signs of a new environment. The Indian Reorganization Act and its implementation belong to Chapter 97 and are not narrated here.

The limits of the existing system were therefore visible to many by 1933. Administrators recognized shortages and inadequacies. Reformers pointed to structural defects, notably guardianship and allotment. Indigenous leaders raised grievances about land, schools, and authority. Yet the existing system also remained in place, and the sources show an agency that could neither meet emergency needs nor satisfy critics. What replaced it would depend on choices not yet made, a point that supports this chapter's insistence on avoiding teleology.

Administrative reform was also constrained by the scale of crisis. The Board of Indian Commissioners' fiscal 1932 report discussed emergency relief for communities affected by unemployment, poor weather, and livestock losses, even as it noted pressure on federal appropriations. It commended efforts to provide aid but maintained assumptions about work requirements and the danger of dependence. Its discussion of education simultaneously recognized improvements and recommended greater use of day schooling while retaining a role for boarding institutions. Thus the record of 1932 contained both practical responses to immediate suffering and a continued conviction that federal personnel should direct fundamental choices. Commissioner Charles J. Rhoads, assistant commissioner J. Henry Scattergood, and Secretary of the Interior Ray Lyman Wilbur held different offices; their roles should not be conflated when describing departmental policy. Nor should the creation of the Indian Emergency Conservation Work administrative division on May 22, 1933 be confused with either the broader emergency conservation legislation or the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934. The chronology matters because it separates short-term relief, changes in administration, and later structural legislation that remained unsettled in 1933.

Section 96.20

Indigenous North America on the Eve of the New Deal: Conditions, Continuities, and Competing Futures

An assessment of Indigenous North America in 1933 must hold together an inventory of inherited conditions and an inventory of persistent institutions. The inherited conditions were substantial: reduced land bases, fragmented ownership, unequal economic opportunities, inadequate health services, educational disruption, restrictive administration, and the effects of the Depression. Each of these has been documented in the preceding sections, and none should be minimized. Combined, they meant that many Indigenous households lived with scarce cash, uncertain resources, and limited control over key decisions.

Balanced against this was the persistence of Indigenous governments, cultural institutions, family networks, land-defense movements, and political organizing. Councils and village governments continued to deliberate. Languages and ceremonies were transmitted. Kinship networks distributed resources and labor. Pueblo leaders had defended land and ceremony through the All Pueblo Council, the Alaska Native Brotherhood and Sisterhood had sustained organization across southeastern Alaska, Canadian organizations had pressed claims despite legal restriction, and Métis activists were organizing in the prairies. Indigenous farmers, herders, fishers, and workers made their living in difficult conditions and often skillfully. The central claim of the chapter follows: material impoverishment did not amount to political or cultural disappearance.

Different actors held different expectations about what would come next. Federal officials in the outgoing and incoming administrations differed on the pace and direction of change. Reform advocates such as Collier and his allies sought an end to allotment, protection and consolidation of land, community-based services, and support for Indigenous culture and self-government. Indigenous leaders held a range of views. Some wished for stronger recognition of treaty rights and community authority, some sought rapid release from federal supervision, and some were skeptical of any new program devised in Washington. Others emphasized land recovery, schooling reform, or the redress of particular claims. In Canada, Indigenous leaders and organizations pressed for recognition of rights, claims, and political voice within a legal framework that gave them little formal leverage.

Indigenous nations therefore entered the New Deal period neither as communities without political institutions nor as uniformly organized supporters of federal reform. Some communities had strong customary governments, others had councils of recent origin, and still others were divided over the legitimacy of existing authorities. These differences would shape responses to the proposals of 1933 and 1934, the subject of Chapter 97. The question moving forward is whether a new federal policy would strengthen Indigenous authority or merely reorganize federal control.

The historical baseline can be stated in institutional as well as material terms. A land inventory could show trust acreage but not who decided to lease or conserve it. A school table could show enrollment but not whether a family trusted the school or could keep its children close to home. A health report could count hospital beds but miss distances, language barriers, and the value of Indigenous caregiving. Likewise, an official description of a council could reveal its legal powers without proving that it represented all forms of community authority. The central evidence of this chapter therefore demands two simultaneous readings: conditions within the systems created by settlers, and the continuing relations through which Indigenous nations organized their own lives. Reformers who concentrated only on better agency performance risked leaving the underlying balance of authority untouched. Officials who treated all Indigenous communities as equivalent overlooked the very differences that should inform public decisions. Entering 1933, Indigenous peoples were not merely subjects of an impending program; they were political actors whose existing practices and claims shaped the terrain on which new federal proposals would be disputed.

Historical Debate

Did the Great Depression Create a New Indigenous Crisis?

The question: Did the Great Depression create a new Indigenous crisis, or primarily expose and intensify problems generated by decades of dispossession and administrative control?

Interpretation A, a new crisis: Some historians emphasize that the collapse of prices, wages, and employment after 1929 removed income sources that many Indigenous households had come to depend on, producing a sudden deterioration that differed in kind from earlier hardship, particularly for wage-dependent and market-dependent communities.
Interpretation B, intensification of prior conditions: Others emphasize that the Meriam Report documented severe conditions in 1928, and that the Depression operated on a base of dispossession and administrative control, so that its main effect was to expose and deepen existing vulnerabilities.

Evidence: Both rely on the Meriam Report, agency and Board of Indian Commissioners reports, census material, and regional records. They differ in how they weigh the pre-1929 baseline against the scale of post-1929 decline, and evidence on income and mortality is limited.

Points of disagreement: Mainly interpretation and emphasis, plus regional variation. Evidence may support both views in different places, since some subsistence-based communities were buffered while wage-dependent ones suffered sharply.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians explain the receptivity to reform in 1933 and whether the Depression is treated as a cause of policy change or a catalyst.

Chapter Summary

This chapter assessed the condition of Indigenous North America immediately before the major American Indian policy changes of the 1930s. Its central argument is that decades of allotment, schooling, and administrative control had produced extensive dispossession, economic marginalization, and institutional disruption, but had not produced the disappearance that policy once anticipated. Indigenous nations retained political institutions, kinship networks, cultural knowledge, and forms of collective organization, and they entered the 1930s with their own grievances and competing visions of the future.

Division I established the demographic and territorial baseline. The 1930 United States Census recorded 332,397 American Indians in the continental United States, excluding Alaska, and Canadian statistics presented several tabulations, one reporting 122,911. These figures depend on classification rules, and the differences between census and administrative counts are part of the historical evidence. The BIA's endpoint figures of about 138 million acres in 1887 and 48 million in 1934 summarize large territorial loss, which occurred through several mechanisms and left reservations with a pattern of trust, fee, leased, and non-Indigenous holdings, complicated by heirship fractionation. Canadian reserves, by contrast, avoided allotment but concentrated authority in the department.

Citizenship, guardianship, and status were distinct relationships. The Indian Citizenship Act of 1924 did not end trust restrictions or secure voting, as Porter v. Hall in Arizona demonstrated, and Canada's Indian Act tied legal status to political rights in different ways. Indigenous councils nonetheless exercised authority in practice, though often under federal or departmental constraint.

Division II examined economies. Agricultural and pastoral communities faced limited land, water, credit, and equipment, but they drew on deep environmental knowledge and collective practice. Wage labor and resource work connected Indigenous people to regional economies, with benefits and vulnerabilities. Household economies, especially the work of women and the obligations of kinship, supported families in ways that cash statistics do not capture. The Great Depression intensified existing inequalities with uneven regional effects, and the chapter rejected the view that it began Indigenous poverty.

Division III turned to health, education, and family life. The Meriam Report and government records document serious health problems, notably tuberculosis and high infant mortality, rooted in poverty, overcrowding, and inadequate services, with data limits that require caution. Boarding, residential, and day schools faced crowding, underfunding, and cultural suppression, while Indigenous families continued to seek better education. Family networks, including grandparents and clans, remained central. Indigenous migration to towns and cities was real but small in comparison with the post-1945 period, and migrants kept ties to home communities.

Division IV emphasized political and cultural continuity. Indigenous governments took many forms, and their interaction with agents and statutory councils produced hybrid and contested arrangements. Languages, ceremonies, arts, and knowledge persisted through adaptation, concealment, and instruction, under restriction, and cultural life had meanings beyond resistance. Canadian First Nations, Métis, and Inuit stood in different legal relationships to the state, and Alaska Native organizations such as the Alaska Native Brotherhood and Sisterhood show durable Indigenous institutions. Border-crossing nations underlined the difference between Indigenous geography and settler boundaries.

Division V examined the threshold of change. Control over land, water, fisheries, and forests underlay economic security, and environmental pressures anticipated later conservation disputes. Three case studies, the Diné, a Pueblo community, and a northwestern community, demonstrated why no single statistic represents the continent. The federal Office of Indian Affairs in 1932–1933 had limited resources and had implemented only part of the reform agenda, and the move from Rhoads to Collier and the creation of emergency conservation work in May 1933 signaled a changing environment.

The chapter's features offered methodological lessons. The primary source windows showed how census, administrative, survey, and Indigenous-authored materials each capture different aspects of reality and omit others. The historical debate on the Depression emphasized that interpretation depends on how historians weigh baselines against change. The continuity-and-change comparison from 1887 to 1933 showed a cumulative process rather than a sudden collapse.

Throughout, the chapter distinguished conditions from the policies meant to improve them, used national statistics with care, and gave weight to Indigenous institutions and initiative. It kept the American New Deal as a chronological reference while preserving the Canadian, Alaskan, and borderland dimensions of Indigenous North America and noting Mexico's separate path.

The chapter's closing question is the one that Chapter 97 takes up: whether a new federal policy would strengthen Indigenous authority or merely reorganize federal control. What decades of assimilation policy had done was reduce material resources and disrupt institutions. What Indigenous nations still possessed were governments, kin networks, knowledge, and a continuing political claim to self-determination, resources that would shape how they met the next era of federal intervention.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 96 matters because it establishes a baseline. Without a documented account of conditions around 1930, later developments, including reorganization, termination, relocation, and the sovereignty movements of later volumes, cannot be evaluated. The chapter shows what decades of assimilationist policy had done to land, livelihoods, health, schooling, and administration, and it shows that those effects, though severe, did not extinguish Indigenous nations.

The chapter also reveals larger processes: the interaction between legal categories and lived experience, the way statistics are made rather than simply found, and the unequal ways economic crisis moved through regions and communities. It reminds readers that policy outcomes depend on the institutions, resources, and decisions of those governed as well as those governing.

What survived beyond the chapter matters most for what follows. Councils, village governments, kin networks, ceremonial societies, and Indigenous organizations were the institutions that federal reformers would encounter, and their varied histories explain why responses to reform differed. The chapter also leaves open questions about the relationship between federal authority and Indigenous self-government, questions that continued through the rest of the century.

In the textbook's larger design, the chapter links the assimilation era of Volume VIII to the reorganization era of Volume IX, keeping a continental view in which Canada, Alaska, and the borderlands remain part of the story. It supports the book's central argument that Indigenous nations are continuing political and historical communities and not remnants of an earlier North America.
