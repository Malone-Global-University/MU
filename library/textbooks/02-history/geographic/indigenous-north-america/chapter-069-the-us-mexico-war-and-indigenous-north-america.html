The U.S.–Mexico War and Indigenous North America

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

On February 2, 1848, two republics negotiated the future of vast territories whose Indigenous nations had not agreed to surrender their sovereignty. A change of international claims was not the same as the disappearance of Indigenous governments, homelands, and political choices.

On February 2, 1848, representatives of Mexico and the United States signed a treaty at Guadalupe Hidalgo that transferred Mexico’s claims over a vast northern territory to the United States. They were not the only governments with interests in that country. The political and economic worlds of Comanche, Kiowa, Apache, Diné, Pueblo, Ute, O’odham, Quechan, and numerous other Indigenous peoples extended across the lands being discussed. Their leaders had not entered the negotiations. The treaty would change international borders without immediately changing all the communities or relationships within them.

The difference between a map and the political world on the ground is the starting point of this chapter. A government might identify a river as a boundary, and an army might occupy a provincial capital, while Indigenous nations continued to govern territories, trade with neighbors, conduct diplomacy, and defend their interests. For the Mexican and American governments, victory, defeat, and territorial cession were defining events. For people living beyond or within the provincial towns, the arrival of new authorities raised more specific questions: Would land and water rights be respected? Which officials would make promises? Would new soldiers protect a village, demand its provisions, or provoke new conflict?

This chapter connects directly to the history developed in Chapter 68. Comanche and Apache power had shaped the northern Mexican frontier long before American soldiers marched south. Indigenous commerce, raiding, local diplomacy, and contests over land formed conditions that cannot be explained by Mexican national politics alone. Texas annexation added an immediate diplomatic crisis, but the war was also part of a longer continental history in which peoples contested what a state could claim and what it could actually govern. The movements of armies would bring this problem to Santa Fe, Taos, California, the Gila River corridor, and the treaty table.

The war produced real and consequential American military victories. The United States seized strategic ports, occupied New Mexico, fought into Mexico City, and secured an international agreement that changed its position on the continent. Nothing in this chapter minimizes those results. Its argument is instead that a victory against Mexico was not identical to a conquest of all the Indigenous political systems in the transferred territories. The American army had not secured the consent of their nations, and in many areas it had barely encountered the people whose homelands its maps now described as American.

Treaties are particularly revealing sources for these problems. In November 1846, U.S. Colonel Alexander Doniphan negotiated with named Diné leaders at Bear Springs, producing a document about peace, trade, and captives. In February 1848, different negotiators, acting for two republics, agreed to clauses concerning Mexican residents’ property, citizenship, and an American obligation to restrain Indigenous incursions into Mexico. The first document shows Indigenous people participating in diplomacy, although the representativeness of the signatories remains an issue. The second shows Indigenous people described as objects of interstate security policy rather than invited as treaty parties.

The chapter therefore follows a sequence of interstate war, local resistance and negotiation, formal treaty settlement, and the uncertain beginnings of an expanded American territorial order. Students should distinguish what an archival document proves from what it merely asserts, particularly when its authors sought to justify conquest. The history after 1848 was neither preordained nor free of consequences: an increasingly powerful United States now had new legal claims and growing means to impose its will, while Indigenous nations continued to act politically and defend their homelands. Chapters 70–72 examine several of the major confrontations that followed.

Historical Context

The territorial background of the war lies in the Mexican Republic’s northern provinces and the Indigenous political systems within and beyond them. Mexico inherited Spanish presidios, settlements, legal traditions, and obligations to negotiate with Indigenous neighbors. As Chapter 68 established, the state’s resources were uneven, and its capacity to protect rural communities or control distant routes varied sharply. The political influence of Comanche and Kiowa bands on the southern Plains, the diverse local governments of Apache peoples, and the Diné’s relationships with New Mexican towns were not recent products of U.S. annexation.

Texas declared independence in 1836; Mexico did not recognize it. The Republic of Texas claimed lands far beyond its effective settlement and military reach, while its relations with Indigenous nations ranged from diplomacy to war. Annexation by the United States in December 1845 changed the interstate dispute but did not eliminate the historical rights or interests of nations inhabiting the contested country. Polk’s expansionist policies turned these tensions into war in spring 1846.

New Mexico and California had histories distinct from Texas. Pueblo towns maintained recognized communal lands under Mexican rule and their own institutions, while Diné, Apache, and Ute communities interacted with New Mexican settlements through trade, conflict, and negotiation. In California, mission history, secularization, ranchos, Indigenous agriculture, labor, and community survival shaped the world American military forces entered in 1846. The conquest therefore affected several political and social systems rather than one undifferentiated Mexican frontier.

This chapter examines the U.S.–Mexico War and its 1848 settlement in that broad setting. It does not repeat the origins of Comanchería in Chapter 50, Apache worlds in Chapter 51, or Texas borderland politics in Chapter 68. Nor does it undertake the later California catastrophe, Diné Long Walk, or Pacific Northwest conflicts assigned to Chapters 70–72. Its task is the political transition created by war, treaty, and the continuing Indigenous sovereignty that the interstate settlement could neither describe adequately nor extinguish.

Division I — Indigenous Sovereignty and the Road to War
Section 69.1

Indigenous North America on the Eve of the U.S.–Mexico War

In 1845 the maps on the desks of officials in Washington and Mexico City represented northern Mexico as a field of colored jurisdictions. Such maps recorded diplomatic claims, but they scarcely conveyed the authority exercised in the same places by Indigenous governments. The Comanche and Kiowa pursued diplomacy, bison hunting, commerce, and warfare across the southern Plains; Apache communities occupied mountain ranges, deserts, and valleys extending into northern Mexico; the Diné defended a homeland centered west of the Rio Grande; and Pueblo villages maintained distinct institutions and agricultural lands within New Mexico. Farther west, Ute communities moved across the plateau and mountain country, O’odham peoples cultivated river valleys and deserts, and Quechan communities inhabited the strategic lower Colorado crossing. The existence of Mexican and American claims did not make these communities subjects who had relinquished the power to decide their own affairs.

The political landscape had several layers. Mexico maintained provincial capitals, presidios, towns, courts, tax arrangements, and commercial relationships, yet it could not reliably impose its will across the immense distances between them. Indigenous nations could affect who crossed those distances, where livestock could graze, and which settlements received trade or faced raids. Even in towns where Mexican jurisdiction was widely accepted, the law interacted with longstanding village rights and local political institutions. American travelers tended to see the country through the routes by which they entered it, but those routes were already organized by Indigenous knowledge of water, forage, trading partners, and seasonal movement. The difference between a capital’s claim and a nation’s effective capacity is the first analytical problem of this chapter.

Indigenous power did not always mean uncontested control of an entire region. Comanche bands negotiated with one another and sometimes disagreed; Apache local groups had distinct leaders and relationships with settlements; Pueblo towns could disagree about whether accommodation or resistance offered greater protection. Indigenous territorial boundaries were often understood through access to places, kinship connections, use, and political relationships rather than through a line surveyed across the landscape. None of this makes Indigenous authority less real. Rather, it requires students to distinguish forms of government instead of measuring every political community by the institutions of nineteenth-century republics.

The United States approached the war with little experience governing most of these western societies. Its officers and politicians drew on older practices of treaty-making, military posts, and frontier trade, but they also carried assumptions about racial hierarchy, private property, and the inevitability of national expansion. Officials could announce that an area had changed hands without knowing who held its farms, who maintained its irrigation works, or who could enforce peace along nearby routes. Mexican officials possessed more experience with particular Indigenous neighbors but also disagreed among themselves about policy, and many local settlements resented the inability of distant authorities to supply protection. The conquest would not simply replace one unified sovereignty with another.

One additional category complicates the map: the great number of people living in mixed commercial and kinship worlds. Traders, interpreters, captives, and households with connections to more than one nation could make political decisions according to the protection available at a particular moment. The borderlands were not cleanly separated ethnic blocks. The authority of a trading partner, a village council, or a respected headman might matter more immediately than a distant governor's proclamation. Attention to these connections allows the chapter to discuss Indigenous independence without depicting societies as isolated from their neighbors or frozen in place before conquest.

The years 1845–1848 therefore offer an unusual vantage point. A treaty would end a war between two republics and change the international description of vast territories, yet it would not end the political careers of the Indigenous nations within them. That distinction should not minimize the consequences of conquest: a stronger and increasingly populous United States acquired the diplomatic position from which to extend garrisons, commissions, roads, and settlement. The question is what changed immediately and what remained subject to further conflict, bargaining, and resistance. Chapter 68 traced the older borderlands system; Chapter 69 follows it through war and treaty without mistaking state paperwork for the disappearance of Indigenous political life.

Section 69.2

Texas Annexation, the Border Dispute, and the Crisis of 1845–1846

The immediate diplomatic crisis began with Texas. The Republic of Texas declared independence in 1836, but Mexico did not recognize that separation. Texas leaders asserted a southern boundary at the Rio Grande, extending the claim far beyond their settled communities and into portions of New Mexico. Mexican authorities disputed this claim and treated the Nueces River and the region beyond it as part of their own political space. For Indigenous nations, neither line adequately described the locations of communities, grazing areas, hunting grounds, or routes of movement. The dispute nevertheless mattered because it would draw American troops and national resources into territory where several forms of authority intersected.

Congress approved annexation in 1845, and Texas formally entered the Union in December. President James K. Polk pursued further expansion toward California and New Mexico while insisting that the United States inherited Texas’s Rio Grande claim. Mexico severed diplomatic relations over annexation. Negotiations were further strained by questions of compensation for American claims, the legitimacy of annexation, and whether Mexico would agree to sell additional territory. These were interconnected controversies rather than a single simple quarrel over a river. Politicians in both countries also faced domestic divisions, which shaped the positions their negotiators could sustain.

Early in 1846 Polk directed General Zachary Taylor’s force to advance from Corpus Christi toward the Rio Grande. American soldiers established positions opposite Matamoros, a movement Mexican authorities interpreted as an invasion of disputed territory. Fighting began in late April; Congress declared war on May 13 after Polk reported that American blood had been shed on American soil. This formulation was itself part of the political controversy. Opponents challenged the premise that the location of the first fighting was unquestionably American territory. Distinguishing the sequence of troop movements from the administration’s account of them is necessary for explaining both the beginning of the war and later arguments about its justification.

An Indigenous-centered account also asks what the competing boundary claims left outside the conversation. Comanche and Kiowa communities operated north and west of many Texan settlements; Lipan Apache and other Indigenous groups maintained relationships that crossed the claimed boundary; and communities along the lower Rio Grande possessed their own histories of allegiance, commerce, and local landholding. A diplomatic crisis framed in Washington as a dispute over national honor, and in Mexico as defense against aggression, could bring economic disruption to people who had never consented to either government’s assertion. Military roads and supply depots also drew labor, food, animals, and traders into the conflict.

Texas annexation thus serves two roles in the chapter. It explains the immediate road to formal war, but it also demonstrates the central theme: a state may inherit a claim by annexing another state without inheriting effective authority over all the communities and lands included within that claim. The Republic of Texas had already discovered the limits of its jurisdiction when dealing with Comanche, Cherokee, and other nations. American annexation enlarged the state resources available to contest that independence. It did not extinguish it in December 1845.

Indigenous Sovereignty and Interstate Sovereignty

Sovereignty

Definition. The capacity and claim of a political community to govern itself and maintain relations with others; in this history the term describes both Indigenous political authority and the international claims of Mexico and the United States.

Historical context. The republics recognized one another as treaty-making states but often failed to treat Indigenous nations as equal negotiating parties. Indigenous communities possessed governing institutions independently of foreign recognition.

Why it matters. Students must distinguish the transfer of one government’s claim from consent by the people whose homeland it claimed.

Potential misunderstanding. It does not follow that every Indigenous nation had identical institutions or that its political authority was free of outside pressure.

Section 69.3

Indigenous Warfare and the Weakening of Northern Mexico

The war between the United States and Mexico did not begin in a peaceful northern region suddenly disrupted by invasion. Throughout the 1830s and early 1840s, northern Mexican communities experienced repeated conflicts with Comanche, Kiowa, and different Apache groups; warfare also marked relationships among Diné communities, New Mexican settlements, and neighboring peoples. The conflicts had distinct causes and patterns, and no single Indigenous alliance directed them all. Nevertheless, the cumulative effects were profound. Raids removed horses, mules, cattle, and sometimes captives; counterraids, militias, and punitive expeditions brought further destruction; and families moved toward fortified towns or more defensible districts. The security problem could not be separated from the economic problem because horses and mules were essential to transport and agriculture.

Mexico’s fiscal and political problems made the effects of war harder to contain. Military units in the northern states were often short of pay, mounts, supplies, and experienced officers. Governments in Chihuahua, Sonora, Coahuila, Durango, and New Mexico faced different conditions and adopted varying responses. Some officials pursued negotiations and trade; others financed militias, offered rewards, or authorized retaliatory operations. Local communities sometimes made temporary accommodations with one Indigenous group while remaining at war with another. Describing this as a uniform Mexican frontier policy would conceal the very variation that shaped the history.

Comanche operations could reach deep into northern Mexico and cause substantial losses in districts far removed from the Plains. Apache warfare in Sonora and Chihuahua followed different ecological and political patterns, involving mountain refuges, settlements, and shifting relations with presidios and local communities. For the Diné, relations with New Mexicans combined commerce, raids, livestock movements, and captivity, with cycles of retaliation that different participants remembered differently. The term 'Indian raids' in contemporary official writing often compressed these separate experiences into one threatening category. Students should restore the distinctions among nations, groups, objectives, and local agreements.

Historian Brian DeLay has argued that Indigenous warfare was a major, often neglected condition shaping Mexico’s vulnerability in 1846. That interpretation corrects accounts that treat Indigenous actors as merely passive victims of the interstate war. It does not claim that Indigenous nations planned the American invasion, desired Mexico’s defeat as a common objective, or acted as an army on behalf of Washington. Their actions came from their own territorial, economic, kinship, and diplomatic interests. The weakening of the northern Mexican frontier could be an indirect consequence of their military activities without being their intended service to an expansionist United States.

Mortality and property losses in these conflicts are difficult to measure. Mexican provincial reports recorded raids and requests for assistance, but officials had incentives to emphasize danger, and documents from communities devastated or abandoned are often incomplete. Indigenous losses in punitive expeditions are even less consistently documented. Counting only Mexican losses would make the conflict appear one-sided; inventing a symmetrical death toll would be equally misleading. A responsible account compares the types of records available, identifies the geographical scope of particular estimates, and recognizes that the war's consequences for both frontier residents and Indigenous families were serious without assigning unsupported numerical certainty.

There is an important limit to the argument. Mexico’s defeat also depended on national finances, political fragmentation, military organization, the relative resources of the two republics, American strategy, and the battles of 1846–1847. Indigenous warfare was neither the sole cause of Mexican weakness nor merely a footnote to it. The northern states became harder to defend because multiple pressures interacted over time. In several areas the American army encountered a population that already knew the failures of distant governments, while Indigenous nations saw an opportunity—or a danger—in the arrival of another external power. The paradox is that independent Indigenous activity helped shape a state war whose outcome would expose many Indigenous homelands to new forms of military pressure.

Section 69.4

Manifest Destiny, Territorial Expansion, and Indigenous Political Futures

The language of Manifest Destiny became prominent in American political discussion in 1845, when advocates depicted expansion as the natural or providential future of the republic. The phrase did not describe a single coherent program, but it joined ideas of national mission, commercial opportunity, strategic power, and racial hierarchy. Many expansionists assumed that American institutions were entitled to spread across the continent. Their arguments frequently reduced Mexican political standing and Indigenous independence to obstacles on a map. The ideological construction mattered because it could make taking control of lands seem legitimate before the inhabitants had been consulted and before American officials possessed the means to govern them.

Political support for expansion was uneven. Some Americans welcomed annexation and war because they expected access to Pacific ports, agricultural land, new trading routes, or a stronger national position. Others opposed the conflict as an unjust war of aggression or feared its impact on the balance of free and slave states. The connection between slavery and expansion was especially contentious. Texas had preserved slavery during its independence, while the prospect of acquiring extensive western territory renewed disputes over whether slavery could expand. These arguments were conducted largely without treating Indigenous sovereignty as a decisive political consideration.

Mexico’s defenders did not share one view either. Some emphasized national territorial integrity and resistance to foreign invasion; others were frustrated by weak institutions and regional inequities. Northern Mexican settlers often combined a sense of Mexican citizenship with demands for local defense or freedom to negotiate with Indigenous communities. The same rancher who feared an Apache raid might resent national officials or seek a treaty with an Apache leader. A representative of a Pueblo town might weigh the shortcomings of Mexican provincial administration against the unknown consequences of U.S. occupation. National labels did not settle local political choices.

Indigenous leaders also confronted competing possible futures. Comanche bands could continue to negotiate with multiple markets and governments, but the arrival of a stronger eastern republic threatened to increase settlements and garrisons. Diné communities could consider whether American promises of peace offered protection or merely replaced one set of uncertain neighbors with another. Pueblo leaders could hope for recognition of existing lands yet fear disruption to institutions that had survived colonial changes. O’odham and Quechan communities controlled important corridors and crossings, but a new military interest in those routes might bring travelers, merchants, and settler pressure. Different groups could make different decisions without sharing a single position for or against Mexico.

For students, ideology should be studied as a cause, not as an explanation that makes later outcomes inevitable. The language of destiny encouraged expansion, but American control had to be pursued through actual campaigns, diplomacy, surveying, legislating, treaty-making, and often coercion. Indigenous nations could impose costs, withhold cooperation, negotiate, or resist. The gap between ambition and capacity structures the remainder of this chapter. In 1845 the United States had a vision of the Pacific; in 1848 it had new international titles to vast territories; possession and governance of Indigenous country were far from settled.

Division II — American Invasion and the Transformation of the West
Section 69.5

The Outbreak of War in 1846 and the Northern Borderlands

Fighting in the disputed country between the Nueces River and the Rio Grande escalated in spring 1846. After clashes with Mexican forces, General Zachary Taylor fought at Palo Alto and Resaca de la Palma in May, and his army advanced toward Monterrey later in the year. In the public narrative of the war these engagements became tests of national strength. For a chapter concerned with Indigenous North America, their significance also lies in the way military operations moved food, horses, wagons, labor, and money through existing borderlands networks. Troops required roads, water, forage, contractors, translators, and guides; campaigns could interrupt commerce even far from a battlefield.

The war operated on multiple fronts. An American naval presence challenged Mexican ports; Taylor’s force campaigned in northeastern Mexico; another army moved westward along the Santa Fe Trail; detachments and ships would occupy California; and in 1847 Winfield Scott would lead an invasion toward Mexico City. The campaigns were coordinated only imperfectly, with great dependence on communication over long distances. This fragmentation matters for Indigenous history because an officer’s proclamation in Santa Fe might not determine conditions in a distant mountain district, while a military march through the Gila valley exposed an army to societies whose political relationships it barely understood.

Borderlands economies adapted as well as suffered. Traders supplied armies and used new commercial opportunities, but requisitions could strip a community of draft animals or grain. Some inhabitants welcomed troops because they expected business or protection against older enemies; others feared seizure, illness, or competing claims to their land. Indigenous guides sometimes sold geographical knowledge or negotiated terms of passage. Such cooperation was neither automatic allegiance to the United States nor proof that a community surrendered control of its territory. People could provision travelers and still insist on rights in land and water that foreign officials had not recognized.

Several conflicts between Indigenous nations and Mexican settlements continued through the war, largely independently of the formal campaigns. Comanche and Kiowa operations did not halt when Congress declared war; Apache communities continued to make decisions in relation to local Mexican and Indigenous neighbors. American military leaders sometimes interpreted these conflicts as evidence that the new United States could protect inhabitants better than Mexico had. The claim created expectations that would later be codified in Article XI of the peace treaty. Yet in 1846 American forces had neither established broad authority over the southern Plains nor reached most Apache homelands. A promise of protection could run far ahead of available soldiers or accepted jurisdiction.

The presence of Indigenous peoples in army descriptions also raises the problem of naming. A report might distinguish 'friendly Indians' from 'hostiles' according to whether particular individuals supplied a military party. Such terms describe an officer's immediate interests, not a reliable taxonomy of nations or lasting political loyalties. A person serving as a guide could remain accountable to family and community authorities not represented in the officer's paperwork. The textbook therefore treats military classifications as evidence of American perceptions while reconstructing Indigenous relationships independently wherever possible.

The distinction between the international war and the varied local wars is therefore essential. Mexico and the United States fought over governments, boundaries, and military objectives. Indigenous nations defended, expanded, traded, or negotiated in political worlds that had existed long before Polk’s declaration. Their actions intersected with the formal war but cannot be reduced to a side in it. Chapters 69.6–69.12 examine what happened when American forces entered western settlements and approached the independent nations around them. The question is not only where an army planted a flag, but how people and governments responded after it moved on.

Section 69.6

Stephen W. Kearny and the Conquest of New Mexico, 1846

The Army of the West under Brigadier General Stephen Watts Kearny entered the upper Arkansas and Santa Fe Trail corridor in the summer of 1846. Its march depended on caravans, animals, experienced travelers, water, and the established commercial relationships of the route. On August 18 it occupied Santa Fe without a major battle after the Mexican governor, Manuel Armijo, withdrew his forces. This comparatively bloodless entry should not be confused with consent from all residents. Mexican officials had made calculations about resistance, and local families faced a new army whose staying power was still uncertain. Kearny raised the American flag, announced that New Mexico would be governed under U.S. authority, and sought to secure the appearance of an orderly transfer of administration.

His proclamations promised security for property and religion while inviting inhabitants to recognize the new political order. American commanders understood that an occupying force could not govern an entire province through military threats alone. They sought continuity from courts and local officials, appointed administrators, and attempted to maintain public order. The legal compilation commonly called the Kearny Code was prepared under this provisional government. It combined borrowed local and American forms and signaled a claim to govern people not yet transferred by an international treaty. Such proclamations reveal American aspirations and official promises; they do not by themselves demonstrate that inhabitants trusted the promises or that the new institutions could protect all existing rights.

The largest gap between assertion and enforcement appeared outside the principal towns. New Mexico was not simply Santa Fe and its governmental offices. Pueblo towns held lands and local authorities; Hispano communities managed irrigated fields, grants, churches, and grazing; Diné, Apache, and Ute communities exercised political authority beyond many of the settlements. Roads could be patrolled, but the authority of a garrison thinned as soldiers traveled into mountains and river valleys where local knowledge was decisive. In practice, American officers had to negotiate with many communities rather than act as though the conquest of the capital settled regional diplomacy.

Kearny left a part of his force to secure the province and proceeded toward California, while other units prepared to move south and east. The campaign therefore scattered U.S. manpower at the same moment it enlarged the areas where commanders claimed jurisdiction. In the autumn of 1846 dissatisfaction grew among some New Mexican residents over taxes, authority, political appointments, and the uncertain future of property. A planned revolt in late 1846 was disrupted, but discontent did not disappear. In January 1847 armed opposition erupted around Taos. The political problem of conquest had not been solved by the absence of fighting at Santa Fe in August.

Religious and communal institutions were central to New Mexican responses. Parish networks, confraternities, Pueblo ceremonies, agricultural irrigation communities, and local councils shaped belonging in ways military proclamations could not quickly remake. When Kearny promised religious freedom, inhabitants could measure that pledge against the conduct of soldiers and officials, not merely its wording. The later destruction at Taos Pueblo illustrates the distance between an announced protection and the experience of a community under attack. In this sense, administrative history and Indigenous social history cannot be neatly separated.

The American entry into New Mexico demonstrates a recurrent pattern: a declaration of annexation or occupation was a beginning, not an ending. New institutions had to operate through human communities whose histories were older than the new state claim. For Indigenous nations the appearance of U.S. soldiers offered risks and opportunities: a possible new bargaining partner, a threat to established claims, and an armed force that might side with one set of local interests against another. The ensuing diplomacy with the Diné and conflict at Taos make those uncertainties concrete.

Section 69.7

Pueblo Communities and the Taos Revolt of 1847

The Pueblo towns of New Mexico had lived through Spanish colonial government, the Pueblo Revolt of 1680 and its aftermath, and then the Mexican period. Each community maintained political institutions, agricultural lands, and cultural practices, even while interacting with provincial authorities, missions, courts, and markets. Mexican citizenship and recognition of village property offered an important legal context, but they did not dissolve the towns into undifferentiated communities of individual citizens. The U.S. occupation in August 1846 placed a new government over this established world. Some Pueblo and Hispano inhabitants adapted pragmatically; others viewed American rule, taxation, officials, and prospective land claims with hostility.

Resistance was neither exclusively Indigenous nor uniformly Pueblo. In northern New Mexico different individuals and groups combined grievances about occupation, local rivalries, and fears for their political standing. In mid-January 1847 an uprising at Taos brought together participants from Taos Pueblo and Hispano New Mexican communities. Governor Charles Bent, appointed under the American occupation, was killed on January 19, along with other figures associated with the new government. Pueblo participant Tomás Romero, known in some accounts as Tomasito, and Hispano figures such as Pablo Montoya appear in accounts of the insurgency; the surviving descriptions vary in how they distribute leadership and responsibility. It would be misleading to infer from these events that all Pueblo towns or all Hispano residents supported the revolt.

American troops under Colonel Sterling Price marched north and engaged insurgents in a series of encounters. In early February the confrontation culminated in an assault on Taos Pueblo, where defenders took refuge in and around the church of San Geronimo. Artillery was used against the position, causing major destruction and loss of life. The attack on a Pueblo religious building carried significance beyond immediate military objectives. It demonstrated how quickly a government promising to protect religion and property could use force against a community whose members contested its authority. The subsequent arrests, trials, and punishments were part of the attempted reimposition of American order and raise questions about military occupation and due process.

To reconstruct the revolt, historians rely heavily on reports produced by American officers and officials. Those reports describe troop movements, the danger posed to garrisons, and the suppression of resistance; they are less capable of conveying every participant’s motives. Local traditions, New Mexican records, and Pueblo historical perspectives complicate the official account. Some rebels may have opposed foreign rule outright, some sought the return of particular officials or institutions, and some reacted to immediate local grievances. A serious account must distinguish documented actions from motives inferred long afterward. The word 'revolt' describes a rising against an occupying government, but the choice of term should not conceal the participants’ own political understandings.

The aftermath of the fighting became a struggle over narrative authority as well as security. Trial records and official statements classified participants as criminal rebels or enemies of the United States, although the occupation's legitimacy remained disputed. Where legal proceedings were conducted by the very authorities against whom resistance had been directed, the categories of treason and loyalty were themselves political issues. Students should not assume that a court's surviving records convey the views of those accused. At the same time, identifying the coercive context does not eliminate the need to account carefully for the killings committed during the uprising and the casualties inflicted in its suppression.

The Taos episode also clarifies the different scales at which the U.S.–Mexico War was fought. Even after an army occupied a capital, local communities could contest the legitimacy of its government; the state’s claim to sovereignty did not guarantee obedience. Nor did the suppression of an uprising resolve Pueblo land rights or the legal standing of Indigenous governments. Those questions would become more complicated when the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo transferred Mexico’s international claims. Chapter 69 treats the revolt as evidence of continuing political agency and the violence that occupation could bring, not as proof that Pueblo communities all adopted one position on the war.

Section 69.8

The American Conquest of California, 1846–1847

California entered the war with a different colonial history from New Mexico. Spanish missions had concentrated, baptized, and subjected many Indigenous people to coercive labor and cultural disruption. After Mexican secularization in the 1830s, ranchos and local authorities appropriated former mission resources in ways that frequently left Indigenous workers with little land or economic independence. At the same time, many Indigenous communities maintained autonomy beyond the ranching districts, preserved knowledge and social networks, and pursued forms of subsistence suited to varied ecologies. By 1846 there was no single 'California Indian' political position, and Mexico’s claim to Alta California did not imply complete governmental authority over its numerous peoples.

American naval operations seized key coastal positions during 1846. The Bear Flag Revolt began in the Sonoma area in June; American naval authorities took Monterey in July and asserted control over the province. These events intersected with ambitions of American settlers, Californio politics, and the activities of U.S. military detachments. A fleet’s capture of a port did not end resistance farther south. Californios challenged the occupiers, while American forces under Kearny moved toward the coast after occupying New Mexico. At San Pasqual in December 1846 Kearny’s force suffered a difficult encounter with Californio lancers. Further campaigns and agreements brought an end to organized Californio military resistance in January 1847.

For Indigenous peoples, changes in the identity of coastal administrators were not the only events that mattered. War created uncertain demands for horses, labor, provisions, information, and guides. Communities in mission-influenced valleys faced the social power of rancheros and soldiers, while inland societies encountered changing trade routes and increasing movement through their lands. Some Indigenous individuals served as scouts or laborers for military parties; others avoided the fighting or defended local interests. Evidence about these choices is often fragmentary because the written sources concentrate on American and Californio military leaders. Students should not equate silence in official reports with inactivity among Indigenous inhabitants.

A useful comparison with New Mexico reveals both similarities and differences. In both regions American officers proclaimed protection and continuity as they sought local compliance. In both, occupation of towns preceded an internationally recognized transfer of territorial claims. Yet California included a coastline of immediate commercial value and a wide distribution of Indigenous languages and communities, as well as a history of mission labor and rancho landholding that shaped later dispossession. Military conquest brought American officials into these relationships without giving them either the capacity or necessarily the willingness to protect all Indigenous interests.

The process of secularization before the war helps explain why the change of flag was not the only disruption Indigenous Californians faced. Mission lands and herds had been redistributed in ways that frequently favored ranchero elites, while Indigenous workers were expected to supply labor under unequal terms. American occupation arrived within this existing social order. New legal institutions and migrants could then interact with older dispossession rather than replace it. This is why the next chapter's account of California should begin with the structures already present in 1846 and not treat the Gold Rush as the first external pressure on Indigenous communities.

The years after 1848 transformed California more violently than a brief account of the military campaign can convey. Gold discovery and accelerated migration, state policies, forced labor, attacks on communities, and demographic catastrophe belong in Chapter 70. Chapter 69 should not narrate those later developments prematurely. Its responsibility is to show the legal and military transition that made a new wave of expansion possible. By the time the treaty was signed, American forces had subdued principal Californio opposition, but the political future of California’s Indigenous nations had not been negotiated with those nations. That absence is fundamental to understanding the conquest.

Section 69.9

The Diné and the American Arrival in New Mexico

The Diné, or Navajo people, occupied an extensive homeland of mesas, mountains, river valleys, and grazing areas west and northwest of the Rio Grande. Their households developed economies combining agriculture, livestock raising, trade, and crafts. Diné political organization rested on local communities and respected leaders rather than a central government able to issue binding orders to every household. Relations with New Mexican towns included exchange, marriage and kinship ties, disputes over livestock, and raids and retaliations that affected both sides. Captive-taking also formed part of this violent and commercially connected borderlands world. The United States inherited these relationships when it occupied Santa Fe; it did not create them and could not simply order them to end.

Kearny’s commanders quickly discovered that proclamations to New Mexican towns would not settle relations with the Diné. In the autumn of 1846 an American military expedition moved toward Diné country and sought an agreement with leaders who could be persuaded to attend. Colonel Alexander W. Doniphan and Diné representatives reached a memorandum at Ojo Oso, commonly translated Bear Springs, on November 22, 1846. The document is valuable because it is an identifiable text with a date, named signatories, and specific articles, rather than merely an officer’s report of an informal conversation. Some names recorded in English spelling include Sarcilla Largo, Narbona, and Manuelito. The list itself reveals the attempt to give a written form to a relationship between different systems of political representation.

The agreement promised peace, freedom of movement for trade, mutual protection, restoration of prisoners, and restitution of property taken after the American occupation of Santa Fe. Article 2 explicitly included New Mexicans and Pueblo people within the expression 'American people.' This wording is especially revealing. From the American military perspective it drew populations already living under different relationships into a category governed by the occupying force. From the Diné perspective, peace with the army did not necessarily erase separate experiences with Pueblo villages, Hispano settlements, or local raiding. The text reveals what the negotiators said they would do; it does not establish that every community agreed with their terms or possessed an identical understanding of the English-language memorandum.

The prisoner and property clauses were difficult to implement. People taken in earlier raids might have been sold, adopted, married, moved away, or incorporated into households under circumstances that did not fit a simple exchange of captives. Livestock could pass through several transactions and across large distances. The stipulation concerning property taken since August 18, 1846, also exposed a gap between American administrative dates and a much longer history of local grievances. Even parties that sincerely desired peace might disagree about who owed restitution to whom. The surviving treaty text is therefore best treated as evidence for the creation of a diplomatic framework, not as proof of immediate social transformation.

The wording of the 1846 agreement also makes clear why a documentary source window is essential. The treaty's Article 3 envisions mutual travel by Navajos, Americans, Mexicans, and Pueblo peoples, but that reciprocal promise presupposes safe conduct across communities with longstanding conflicts. Article 4's language on prisoner exchange requires the parties to arrange for people who could not simply be swapped one for one. These were demanding obligations, not formulaic statements. Their practical difficulty helps explain why continued local conflict does not automatically establish that all negotiators entered the agreement in bad faith.

Diné communities retained the capacity to trade, negotiate, and use armed force after 1846. American officials sometimes interpreted continued violence as evidence of broken promises; Diné communities might instead view a particular action through local disputes or the failure of a different party to honor terms. Later agreements in 1848 and 1849 show that a single treaty did not establish lasting peace. This chapter ends before the military campaigns and forced movement that produced the Long Walk and incarceration at Bosque Redondo, subjects reserved for Chapter 71. The 1846 episode is central here because it shows how occupation brought the United States into an existing political world that could not be reduced to the sovereignty of Mexico or the authority of the new army.

Section 69.10

Apache Nations and the Changing International Frontier

Apache peoples occupied a vast and varied region stretching from parts of present-day Texas and New Mexico to Sonora, Chihuahua, and other northern Mexican districts. Chiricahua communities in mountainous country, Mescalero groups in the Pecos and southern New Mexico region, Lipan communities in the Texas borderlands, and other Apache local groups possessed distinct political relationships. The collective term 'Apache' did not designate a single government capable of choosing a common side in the U.S.–Mexico War. Leadership frequently depended on persuasion and local affiliations, and a community could negotiate with one garrison while another maintained conflict with different neighbors. Such distinctions were routinely lost in official reports that attributed all violence to a generic Apache enemy.

In the decades before 1846 Spanish and Mexican authorities had used presidios, negotiated peace arrangements, trade, rations, military patrols, and sometimes rewards for violence against Apache people. These policies varied across provinces and changed with finances and local politics. Where reciprocal arrangements functioned, they could provide goods and a measure of security; where they failed, warfare might resume. The American conquest threatened to reorganize these relationships. Officers new to the region often had to depend on Mexican residents or Indigenous intermediaries for basic geographical and political information. The arrival of American soldiers did not remove older grievances over stolen livestock, murdered relatives, or broken promises.

For some Apache leaders, the new power might appear to offer different trading opportunities or an alternative counterparty to Mexican officials. Others could regard American military parties as unfamiliar intruders whose long-term intentions were unknown. Evidence does not justify generalizing one response to all Apache communities. Local decisions depended on proximity to garrisons, access to markets, competition with other groups, and the treatment of particular leaders and families. Even the meaning of a temporary peace was contingent: a leader might agree to safe passage without giving an army jurisdiction over all nearby people or land.

Geography made the limits of occupation particularly apparent. American columns could follow the Rio Grande, the Santa Fe Trail, or the Gila corridor, but reaching and holding remote ranges required supplies, routes, intelligence, and continuing local cooperation. Apache communities already familiar with mountain water sources and seasonal movement did not become subjects of a new state because a treaty assigned territory to it. Mexican villagers likewise remained in complicated relationships with their Apache neighbors after a diplomatic boundary changed. A person raiding across a line recognized in Washington might be moving through a region in which kinship, ecological, and political connections predated the line.

An analytical exercise can compare two hypothetical reports from the same year without inventing a specific event. An American officer might record a meeting as a successful agreement with 'the Apaches,' while a local Apache community might understand it as a limited arrangement concerning one trail or garrison. A Mexican rancher might describe the same period in terms of stolen livestock and a desire for protection. The historian must ask for names, locations, signatories, and the conditions under which each statement was recorded. This method guards against turning the politically useful shorthand of officials into an inaccurate account of Apache nations.

The conquest did create an important long-term change. The United States now had international claims and military incentives to patrol areas previously contested by Mexico, and it increasingly approached relations with Apache nations through its own treaty and military systems. Yet these ambitions would have to be enacted through further negotiation and conflict. Chapter 69 explains the transition rather than importing the later Apache wars into 1846–1848. The principal conclusion is that interstate sovereignty changed more swiftly than local relations: diplomacy, trade, mobility, and warfare continued according to the decisions of multiple Apache nations and their neighbors.

Section 69.11

Comanche, Kiowa, and Southern Plains Power during the War

Comanche and Kiowa political and economic power remained an essential feature of the southern Plains throughout the U.S.–Mexico War. Their communities moved among bison ranges, seasonal camps, trading centers, and allied or rival settlements. Comanche bands possessed different leaders and priorities; Kiowa communities maintained their own political life while cooperating with Comanches in commerce and warfare. The area often called Comanchería was not a state with a capital and fixed frontier, but it was a region in which Indigenous decisions shaped movement, markets, and security. Texas annexation, the American advance, and eventual Mexican defeat changed the surrounding state system without instantly changing that fact.

Trade linked Plains societies to New Mexico and to American markets along the Arkansas and Red rivers. Horses, mules, bison products, cloth, metal goods, and other commodities circulated through both direct exchange and intermediaries. Raiding and the acquisition of captives also formed part of regional political economies, although they should not be used as a shorthand for all Indigenous activity. A community might seek peace with traders while conducting military operations against a distant settlement. State reports often portrayed raids as proof of general hostility; a regional history instead asks which bands, which towns, and which agreements were involved at a particular moment.

American victory over Mexico created opportunities and uncertainties for these networks. New federal authorities might promise to restrain incursions into Mexico, as the treaty of 1848 would require, but doing so meant asserting practical authority over Comanche and Kiowa activities on a scale U.S. officers had never achieved. Mexico’s earlier negotiations, commercial ties, and defensive failures could not simply be transferred to the American government as though they were property. Indigenous leaders retained choices about whether to engage American agents, continue existing exchanges, or confront a growing settler frontier.

The distinction between Comanche and Kiowa activity and the American campaigns helps explain why some borderland residents experienced little immediate security improvement in 1848. The movement of regular armies toward Mexican cities did not place garrisons at every ranch, river crossing, and winter camp. American troops could defeat Mexican forces on a battlefield while remaining unable to prevent an independent raiding party from crossing a vast region. Such limitations did not mean the United States lacked expansionist capacity; rather, it would devote increasing resources to frontier posts and coercive policies over subsequent decades. The ending of the war accelerated those later confrontations by changing diplomatic responsibility and the perceived need to protect new territories.

Trade also allowed some Plains leaders to leverage rivalry among outside governments. A decision to redirect horses or bison products toward a different market could influence prices and political relationships without being a military attack. Yet the opportunities were not limitless: disease, changes in bison ranges, and pressure from neighboring nations constrained choices. The chapter's account of Comanche and Kiowa autonomy should neither romanticize absolute freedom nor reduce their power to raiding. It should explain the combination of negotiation, kinship, commerce, ecology, and armed capacity on which their regional influence rested.

Students should not treat the presence of independent Plains nations as evidence that Mexican or American sovereignty was simply fictitious. Each government exercised real authority in particular places and over particular populations. The historical question is how those spheres of authority overlapped and competed. In 1848 Comanche and Kiowa communities remained significant actors capable of imposing costs on settlers and influencing state policy. Chapter 69 must make that continuity visible, while leaving the later Plains campaigns and reservation order to the appropriate subsequent chapters.

Section 69.12

Ute, O’odham, Quechan, and Other Nations of the Western Borderlands

The international transfer of 1848 affected a west far more diverse than the northern Rio Grande and the southern Plains. Ute communities lived across mountain valleys, plateaus, and river systems in regions that American cartographers would call Utah, Colorado, and New Mexico. O’odham communities inhabited desert and river landscapes farther south, including agricultural villages in the Gila basin; Quechan people maintained an important homeland at the lower Colorado River crossing. Other societies occupied the Mojave, Colorado Plateau, and California approaches. Their distinct languages, governing practices, and economic systems caution against writing as though the American army entered one undifferentiated 'Indian territory.'

Ute relationships with New Mexican traders, settlers, and neighboring Indigenous nations included commerce, alliances, conflict, and changing arrangements for access to animals and goods. Some Ute groups were more directly connected to the Rio Grande settlements, while others lived farther from American military routes. The conquest of Santa Fe had unequal consequences across these communities. An officer's claim of authority over New Mexico might affect access to markets before it affected a remote political leader's practical freedom of action. Here as elsewhere, treaty lines and trade connections operated on different maps.

The Gila corridor provides a concrete view of the western transformation. American forces moving toward California depended on established Indigenous settlements, cultivated fields, routes, and water sources. The Pima, known today among the Akimel O’odham, and neighboring Maricopa or Piipaash communities were not simply stops on an empty road. Their agriculture and trade made the corridor traversable for outsiders. The Mormon Battalion, marching toward California in 1846, reached villages along the Gila in December and depended on arrangements for movement and provisions. Transactions of this kind required local negotiation and could bring both economic opportunity and risk. They did not constitute an agreement to relinquish Indigenous land to the United States.

At the lower Colorado River, the Quechan controlled a crucial crossing used by travelers connecting the inland Southwest and California. Their location would later attract increasing traffic and federal interest. A map might draw a new international boundary along a river farther south, but that line could not settle who owned the ferrying places, fields, and paths used by Indigenous communities. The strategic importance of these corridors was visible even before extensive settler migration followed conquest. Military parties relied on geography that Indigenous people understood and managed, while written army accounts often described the same country primarily in terms of its utility to American expansion.

The different histories of Akimel O’odham, Tohono O’odham, Piipaash, and Quechan communities should remain visible in terminology. Names widely used in older American military records do not always correspond to contemporary self-identifications or to the relationships among communities at the time. Likewise, the label 'Maricopa' in a report might refer to Piipaash people living in the Gila region rather than to a generalized category of all river peoples. Using each name carefully prevents an account of the march to California from erasing the people who made travel through the region possible.

The position of these nations also reveals a chronological trap. The present international border was not completed in its modern configuration by the treaty of 1848 alone; later negotiations, most notably the 1853 Gadsden Purchase, altered its course. Students should not project a contemporary line backward onto the O’odham and other homelands of 1846. More generally, Ute, O’odham, Quechan, and neighboring nations did not experience one uniform 'American arrival.' Some dealt with soldiers immediately; others confronted settlers, merchants, or surveyors later. Chapter 69 establishes this differentiated transition while reserving the longer histories of dispossession and resistance for subsequent chapters.

Division IV — Conquest, Law, and the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo
Section 69.13

Military Occupation and the Problem of Governing Conquered Territory

The occupation of New Mexico and California forced American commanders to confront a question that followed every successful military advance: who would administer conquered places, and under which laws? An army could issue orders, guard a customs house, and demand supplies, but ordinary life required courts, property records, schools, irrigation arrangements, municipal offices, and systems of local mediation. Commanders therefore often kept parts of Mexican administrative practice while asserting a new supreme authority. This produced a hybrid and frequently unstable government. The promise to preserve familiar practices could ease occupation, but residents had reason to ask whether the promise would survive new political demands from Washington or incoming settlers.

Kearny's provisional government in New Mexico illustrates the problem. His proclamations appealed to religion, property, public security, and the benefits of American institutions; his administration worked with local officials while announcing the new government's authority. The Kearny Code attempted to provide an organizing legal text. Nevertheless, a military governor appointed by an occupying army was not an elected representative of all the population now subject to American orders. Moreover, occupation in 1846 preceded the treaty in 1848; an officer's declaration that people were citizens of the United States was not identical to the later legal rules negotiated between the republics.

Military occupation also rearranged coercive power. Garrison commanders could arrest suspected opponents, regulate travel, requisition supplies, or undertake punitive expeditions. Such actions affected local populations differently. A merchant might gain new customers; a farmer might lose draft animals; a Pueblo village might confront officials who did not understand communal landholding; and an Indigenous group beyond the settlement district might be approached as an enemy or as a potential treaty partner. A war of conquest creates incentives for commanders to interpret resistance as disorder rather than as a contest over legitimate political authority. The revolt at Taos exposes the stakes of this interpretive conflict.

The legal distinction between occupation and cession is central. Occupation is a condition of military control during war; cession is an agreement between states transferring their recognized claims to territory. Neither is identical to consent by Indigenous nations. A treaty can change the rules among the signatory governments while leaving existing Indigenous claims unsettled. Nor did cession automatically erase local property rights and obligations: the 1848 treaty contained provisions specifically addressing people, rights, and property in the ceded territory. Disputes over how to enforce those provisions would last for generations. Students should therefore separate the date a place was occupied, the date a treaty was signed, the exchange of ratifications, and the actual establishment of civil administration.

These distinctions prevent a deceptively smooth narrative of conquest. The United States did gain enormous geopolitical advantages: ports, roads, mineral regions, and future opportunities for settlement. But it had to negotiate the limits of its power amid Indigenous sovereignty, local governments, and a diverse population. Military occupation could be swift in a capital; building an accepted order across the wider region required further political acts. This chapter treats those acts as historical processes rather than assuming they occurred automatically when an American flag appeared over a town square.

Section 69.14

Military Mapping, Exploration, and the Geography of Conquest

American conquest was accompanied by a project of describing the West in military and scientific terms. Army topographical engineers recorded routes, rivers, mountain passes, vegetation, settlements, and the locations of water and forage. Such information could keep a column alive, shape a later road, and inform congressional decisions about territory. Lieutenant William H. Emory traveled with Kearny's advance guard toward California in 1846–1847 and prepared a detailed reconnaissance subsequently published in 1848. His report belongs to a wider body of military travel writing that combined empirical observation with the purposes of an expanding state. It is indispensable evidence for the campaign route, but it is not a transparent account of the full society through which soldiers traveled.

Mapmaking was collaborative, even when published maps gave principal credit to an American officer. Military parties depended on Indigenous guides, interpreters, local traders, and residents familiar with river crossings, trails, agricultural villages, and reliable springs. Knowledge gathered through such encounters could be incorporated into a map without recording its Indigenous sources. Engineers also worked within the limits of the instruments and conventions available to them: a measured point or recognizable river could be placed more confidently than a broad zone of seasonal movement. The resulting maps were selective representations of a complex political landscape, not complete inventories of ownership or sovereignty.

Compare the Santa Fe Trail with the route west from the Rio Grande to the Gila and Colorado rivers. The former passed through long-established trading relationships that connected Missouri and New Mexico. The latter required travelers to follow water in arid country and navigate Indigenous agricultural and trading networks that American officers often poorly understood. An army that could cross a landscape had accomplished a formidable logistical task, but a line of march should not be confused with administrative control. Military maps frequently highlighted the progress of one column while leaving Indigenous territorial histories less visible.

The production of knowledge also changed the incentives for settlement. A recorded pass, a reliable river crossing, or an assessment of agricultural land could make a region appear newly accessible to policymakers and migrants. Scientific observations of plants, soils, and minerals helped recast the country as a set of resources to be evaluated. This does not mean every mapmaker personally intended dispossession. It means that mapping formed part of the institutional infrastructure through which expansion became practicable. The route could precede the garrison, the garrison could precede the settler, and the settler could then make claims that generated further conflict with Indigenous owners and communities.

The sources themselves can be treated as layered objects. Emory's published reconnaissance includes narrative observations, measurements, geographical descriptions, and illustrations; later editions and composite congressional reports may also contain the work of other officers. A citation should specify the author, report, publication year, and relevant part rather than attribute every illustration or observation to Emory personally. Students comparing the publication with a modern map should identify which places appear precise, which are approximations, and whose place names survived into the published form.

For historical analysis, juxtapose a U.S. reconnaissance map with an Indigenous-centered reconstruction showing homelands, movement routes, political relationships, and community sites. Ask what each map makes easy or difficult to see. The lesson is not that all state maps were simply false: they often conveyed accurate geographical details. It is that geographical accuracy can coexist with a misleading political implication. Drawing a frontier or naming a province does not prove the land was ceded by all its inhabitants. The difference between recording the ground and claiming authority over it is essential to the history of 1848.

Section 69.15

Negotiating Peace: Mexico’s Defeat and the Treaty Settlement

By 1847 the military campaign had placed Mexico under intense pressure. American forces fought through northeastern and central Mexico, while a separate invasion under Winfield Scott advanced from Veracruz to Mexico City. The occupation of the capital in September 1847 provided the United States a strong bargaining position, but it did not resolve the diplomatic problem of making peace with a government divided by war and political crisis. Mexico's leaders had to assess what territory and autonomy could be preserved, while U.S. policymakers differed about how much land to demand. The eventual settlement emerged from negotiation under coercive conditions, not from two republics bargaining from equal military positions.

Nicholas P. Trist, the American peace commissioner, negotiated with Mexican commissioners Luis G. Cuevas, Bernardo Couto, and Miguel Atristáin. President Polk had become dissatisfied and recalled Trist, yet Trist chose to continue the effort because he believed a workable peace could still be concluded. The resulting Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo was signed on February 2, 1848. Its importance extended beyond the battles: it specified a new international boundary, provisions concerning residents and property, monetary obligations, and a controversial attempt to regulate Indigenous movement. The treaty's legal force depended on the subsequent action of both governments, and the American Senate altered the original text before ratification.

The territorial settlement recognized the Rio Grande as the Texas boundary and transferred extensive Mexican claims in the Far West, including the territories that would become California, Nevada, Utah, New Mexico, and portions of several other states. The United States agreed to pay Mexico $15 million and assumed specified claims obligations under other treaty provisions. It is useful to describe this as a transfer of Mexico’s claims to a vast territory, rather than the straightforward sale of unoccupied land. Some districts contained established Mexican settlements and institutions; others were inhabited primarily by Indigenous nations who had not been represented in the negotiations and whose political rights remained unresolved.

Mexico’s negotiators had reasons to seek guarantees for residents and property in territories that would no longer be under Mexican jurisdiction. Articles VIII and IX provided important protections, though their later interpretation was contested. The United States also made commitments regarding incursions by Indigenous peoples into Mexico—an extraordinary provision because its language assumed future American control over nations that were not parties to the treaty. The juxtaposition is revealing. In some articles the treaty recognized the rights of persons already resident in the ceded territories; in another it treated Indigenous communities primarily as a problem of border security to be managed by the acquiring state.

After signing, the treaty proceeded through the U.S. Senate on March 10, presidential ratification on March 16, exchange of ratifications at Querétaro on May 30, and American proclamation on July 4, 1848. These steps cannot be collapsed into one 'treaty date.' Equally, the treaty's ratification could not complete every survey, court dispute, or negotiation that the new boundary would require. Its achievement was to end the formal state war and define a new legal relationship between Mexico and the United States. Its central limit was that peoples whose political worlds covered much of the transferred land had not negotiated its terms.

Section 69.16

The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo: Territory, Property, and Citizenship

The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo did more than describe a line on a map. It contained rules for the diplomatic transfer of territory and attempted to preserve certain rights of people already living in the ceded lands. Article V established the new boundary, beginning at the Rio Grande and proceeding through points defined by the contemporary geography of New Mexico, the Gila River, and the Colorado River toward the Pacific. The precise language matters because the boundary would still have to be surveyed, mapped, marked, and sometimes renegotiated. A line defined by rivers and a map used in diplomatic negotiations was not necessarily a line whose course could be determined without controversy in mountainous or poorly surveyed country.

Article VIII addressed Mexican residents in the territories transferred to the United States. It gave them the option to remain or to depart for Mexico while retaining or disposing of their property. Those who stayed could choose to retain Mexican nationality or acquire U.S. citizenship within the period established by the treaty; a person who remained without declaring a preference to retain Mexican nationality after one year was deemed to have elected American citizenship. The article also protected property owned by Mexicans not resident in the ceded area. These guarantees were significant, yet they did not resolve every question about what counted as valid title, how courts would enforce grants, or how communities would maintain collective landholding traditions.

Article IX, in the form that survived U.S. Senate amendment, addressed the incorporation of Mexican residents who did not retain Mexican nationality. It envisaged admission to the enjoyment of rights of U.S. citizens at a time judged appropriate by Congress and protected liberty, property, and religious exercise in the meantime. This language differed materially from the original negotiated wording. The change demonstrates that there was no single uncontested 'treaty text' between the negotiators’ agreement of February 2 and the final ratified settlement. For close reading, students must use the amended form while explaining why the earlier draft was changed. The distinction is a question of historical evidence, not an editorial technicality.

The U.S. Senate also struck the original Article X, which had specifically addressed recognition of Mexican land grants. Its removal did not erase the property protections elsewhere in the treaty, but it narrowed the explicit textual guarantee concerning grants and contributed to later disputes. In the 1848 Protocol of Querétaro, American representatives explained that deletion should not be understood as an intention to annul legitimate land grants. Whether later courts and administrations honored that position consistently is a separate historical question. Community grants and claims in New Mexico remained especially complex, as differing documentation, surveys, and legal institutions confronted one another under the new government.

Pueblo peoples demonstrate why general language about citizenship must be used carefully. Pueblo communities had held recognized village lands and a form of citizenship under Mexican law while maintaining their own governments. Their position after the treaty could not be reduced to that of either non-Indigenous Mexican residents or Indigenous nations with different histories of U.S. relations. Subsequent federal and territorial decisions interpreted Pueblo status and lands in conflicting ways. That later legal evolution should not be projected back as though 1848 automatically decided every question of Pueblo sovereignty and property. The treaty supplied a framework whose operation depended on courts, lawmakers, communities, and further disputes.

The chronology of treaty approval is a valuable exercise in legal reading. The diplomats signed a document on February 2; the U.S. Senate advised ratification with amendments on March 10; President Polk ratified on March 16; and ratifications were exchanged at Querétaro on May 30. The American proclamation followed on July 4. A sentence saying simply that the treaty 'was ratified in February' would be wrong. Likewise, quoting the original Article X as if it remained in force would give students a false account of the settlement. Documentary precision affects the substantive interpretation of rights.

Nor should one infer that the treaty conferred identical rights on all Indigenous inhabitants. Comanche, Apache, Diné, Ute, O’odham, Quechan, and other nations were not parties to the agreement, and their legal relationships with Mexico had never been uniform. Cession between republics could change the state asserting ultimate authority without being a consensual extinguishment of Indigenous political identity, land claims, or local governance. In many places those relationships remained to be contested through negotiation and force. The central lesson of Articles V, VIII, IX, and the deleted X is that a treaty could simultaneously establish new international claims, acknowledge some residents’ rights, and leave the rights of Indigenous nations fundamentally unresolved.

Section 69.17

Article XI: Indigenous Nations in a Treaty They Did Not Negotiate

Article XI is one of the clearest pieces of evidence for the contradiction at the heart of the 1848 settlement. Its opening characterized much of the newly acquired territory as inhabited by Indigenous peoples whom the treaty described, in the discriminatory language of its signatories, as 'savage tribes.' The wording assumed that they would thereafter be under the exclusive control of the United States. No Comanche, Kiowa, Apache, Diné, or other affected Indigenous government signed the treaty. The provision therefore records what Mexico and the United States claimed about the future authority of the acquiring state, rather than an act of consent or a compact with the peoples whose conduct it purported to regulate.

The article bound the United States to restrain incursions by Indigenous peoples from the newly American territories into Mexico, punish them when they could not be prevented, and seek redress with energy comparable to that used when American citizens were threatened. It also prohibited inhabitants of the United States from buying certain captives or stolen Mexican property taken by Indigenous raiders. Additional clauses required the recovery and return of Mexican captives when the U.S. government learned of them and could exercise its influence and power. These were substantial promises, reflecting Mexico’s fear that the international boundary would leave the northern states exposed to raids originating north of the new line.

The provision is remarkable because it turned complex histories of raiding, captivity, trade, and local diplomacy into a bilateral security obligation. In northern Mexico, these interactions involved different Indigenous nations and local communities with different aims and past agreements. A captured person could have moved through households and markets long before American officials learned of the situation. An animal taken from one district might be exchanged repeatedly over great distances. Article XI presumed that the United States could restrain practices rooted in such networks, even though at the time of signature it had only recently occupied the most accessible settlements and knew relatively little about the political organization of many Indigenous groups.

The last part of Article XI deserves particular attention. It obligated the United States, when contemplating removal of Indigenous people or settlement of the acquired territories, to avoid placing those communities in conditions that might drive them into incursions against Mexico. The clause reveals how interstate security and prospective Indigenous dispossession were explicitly connected in the treaty. The two republics anticipated that movement of settlers and displacement of Indigenous people might create cross-border violence, yet the article considered the problem primarily in terms of the duty of one state to protect the other. Indigenous land rights and political consent did not form the foundation of the bargain.

A historian must therefore read Article XI at two levels. At the level of diplomatic intention it sought to reassure Mexico and make the United States responsible for security along its expanded frontier. At the level of effective power it revealed a startling gap: the American government was making promises about communities it had not conquered or governed. This gap created the possibility of repeated Mexican complaints, retaliations, disputes over damages, and disagreement over which government was responsible. The language was also unstable as policy. Article II of the Gadsden Purchase treaty of 1853 subsequently released the United States from Article XI obligations and expressly abrogated that article.

The treaty's language concerning captives invites further critical analysis. It prohibited certain transactions by inhabitants of the United States but did not directly regulate every form of captivity, adoption, or servitude existing among Indigenous and Mexican communities. Those practices varied in meaning and coerciveness. The provision should not be presented as though the republics had negotiated a universal emancipation agreement. Rather, it sought the return of Mexican captives and the disruption of specific markets the signatories believed encouraged raids. The humanitarian language and the security objective need to be considered together.

The change in 1853 confirms that the 1848 arrangement was not a final resolution of Indigenous geopolitics. The American government acquired a duty it struggled to carry out and later negotiated away. Indigenous nations retained their own political and military capacities throughout the period. Article XI is thus not merely an obscure clause at the end of a peace settlement; it is a direct record of how two expanding or contracting states interpreted independent Indigenous nations as a problem to be managed by international law without inviting those nations into the negotiation. Its text must be read alongside evidence of Indigenous authority, not mistaken for proof that authority had vanished.

Section 69.18

The New International Boundary and Indigenous Homelands

The boundary agreed in 1848 changed the international legal classification of territories from the Rio Grande through the Southwest to the Pacific. For governments, the line defined new areas of jurisdiction and new responsibilities for security, trade, and customs. For Indigenous nations, it crossed much older networks of movement and affiliation. Diné, Apache, O’odham, Quechan, and other peoples had developed relationships around river valleys, mountain ranges, routes, and neighboring communities that were not organized around an international frontier. The creation of a line on a diplomatic map could not immediately replace those relationships with passport-like distinctions between Mexican and American space.

There was also an important difference between a treaty boundary and a surveyed boundary. The treaty used geographical descriptions and a map associated with the negotiations, but the actual course of the line required fieldwork, measurement, agreement among commissioners, and monuments on the ground. Technical errors and competing interpretations could produce political disputes even between the two governments that had signed the treaty. The Gila River and the approach to the Colorado made geography important to the division of territory, while the later Gadsden Purchase altered the southern boundary in 1853. Students should avoid depicting the present U.S.–Mexico border as though every segment was already fixed in 1848.

The new line influenced Indigenous affairs unevenly. It might change which garrison claimed authority over a trading post without altering the leaders who regulated local movement. In other locations, the arrival of customs officers, surveyors, military escorts, or settlers could transform an established route into a contested corridor. Some Indigenous communities maintained cross-border ties; others found that a new jurisdiction attempted to regulate trade or patrol a river crossing. Such changes unfolded over years. A boundary mattered even before it was regularly enforced because it changed the claims officials made, the legal language they used, and the resources they might later devote to expanding control.

Borderland households experienced these transformations in social rather than merely cartographic terms. Kin networks could extend across a river or mountain range, as could trading partnerships and obligations concerning captives. People might carry goods between settlements without regarding the route as international commerce; a new customs regime could reclassify the same exchange. The growing distinction between 'American Indians' and 'Mexican Indians' could obscure an Indigenous nation's own identity and territorial relationships. Examining these lived consequences helps explain why the treaty's formal language cannot by itself tell us how the new boundary operated on the ground.

Boundary surveys themselves could become sites of encounter. Survey parties needed water, labor, escorts, and local knowledge, and they sometimes encountered Indigenous communities who had not been informed of the diplomatic settlement. Surveyors might identify a point as strategically vital because it offered water or access through a pass—precisely the reasons an Indigenous community had long valued it. Reading survey reports alongside Indigenous histories helps students see how apparently technical choices about lines and monuments could affect the geography of later territorial disputes.

The boundary also changed the responsibilities of Mexico and the United States in ways that affected Indigenous diplomacy. The American government would increasingly insist on negotiating as the predominant external state north of the line; Mexican authorities would seek security south of it. But the actual choices of Indigenous leaders—whether to make peace, trade, resist, move, or remain—continued to shape regional politics. The transformation of the boundary thus offers a clear test of the chapter's main argument. International claims changed sharply in 1848, while Indigenous political and economic geographies persisted and were contested under new conditions.

Section 69.19

From Mexican Frontier to American Territorial Expansion

The close of the U.S.–Mexico War opened a new period of American territorial administration. Military posts, survey parties, roads, and newly organized civil institutions extended the government's reach into parts of the Southwest and Far West, but not at a uniform speed. New Mexico remained under changing provisional arrangements before a formal territorial government was created in 1850. California experienced a rapid influx of migrants and a political transition accelerated by the discovery of gold. These developments grew from the geopolitical position acquired in 1848, but they should not be described as though the new state could simply command every community and landscape within the cession.

Administration required categories, and categories shaped policy. Officers and agents attempted to identify Indigenous leaders, population sizes, land use, treaty commitments, and regions they considered threatened. Such information was often incomplete or based on outsider assumptions. Indigenous nations with local or dispersed political organization did not necessarily fit the model of a centralized tribe represented by one signatory. American officials who overlooked this fact could secure an agreement with some leaders and then assume it applied to everyone. Disagreements arising from such assumptions would become an enduring feature of federal–Indigenous relations in the newly acquired lands.

Territorial acquisition also encouraged travel and settlement. Military reconnaissance helped describe routes; merchants saw opportunities to connect Pacific ports and interior markets; migrants pursued farms, ranches, and mineral wealth. The expansion of civilian settlement could produce new pressure even where no major military campaign had occurred during the war. Surveyed land, planned roads, and claims under American property law increasingly competed with Indigenous patterns of occupation and use. The legal conversion of territory into a space for land sales or private claims required further political acts, and those acts could threaten preexisting Indigenous institutions as well as the property protections guaranteed to some Mexican residents.

Different regions experienced the transition differently. California's immediate postwar history included catastrophic violence and coercion against Indigenous peoples and belongs principally in Chapter 70. The military campaigns and forced displacement of the Diné culminating in the Long Walk are the responsibility of Chapter 71. Resistance and treaty-making in the Pacific Northwest follow in Chapter 72, and the later wars and reservation order of the Plains receive attention in the following volume. Chapter 69 should make these trajectories intelligible without prematurely narrating their full chronology or assuming that all emerged mechanically from a single treaty clause.

A second transformation concerned the flow of information to Congress. Officials who had previously discussed Mexico's northern frontier as foreign territory now presented it as an American administrative problem requiring appropriations, military roads, agents, and sometimes new treaties. That change in institutional responsibility could have practical consequences even before a new fort was built. Requests for money, surveys, and reports began constructing a federal vocabulary for communities whose political relationships were far more complex than a list of tribes in a budget document could convey.

What connects these later stories is the change in international context. The United States no longer needed to contest Mexico's legal claim to many western territories before attempting to extend its own institutions there. That altered the balance of power. It did not settle the moral or political question of Indigenous consent, nor did it produce immediate possession of every homeland. In many places the subsequent history would consist precisely of attempts to turn a claim asserted in 1848 into authority that Indigenous nations had not surrendered. The distinction between those two processes is the historical legacy of the territorial transfer.

Section 69.20

The Meaning of Conquest: Indigenous Sovereignty after 1848

In conventional diplomatic history the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo marks the end of the U.S.–Mexico War and the acquisition by the United States of enormous western territories. That statement is correct at the level of interstate relations. It is incomplete as a history of Indigenous North America. The new international boundary crossed or surrounded the homelands of nations that had their own political institutions, laws, ceremonies, markets, and histories of negotiating with outside powers. Those nations did not sign the treaty; its transfer of Mexico's claims was not equivalent to the transfer of their consent. This chapter's central distinction has been between changes in recognized international title and changes in effective, locally legitimate authority.

The war altered the geopolitical environment decisively. The United States gained access to Pacific ports, acquired a stronger position in western trade, and assumed opportunities and responsibilities that Mexico could no longer exercise in the transferred territories. American forces had occupied Santa Fe and important Californian settlements, and policymakers could now pursue roads, garrisons, commissions, settlement, and further treaties without needing to defeat Mexico in another state war. Those advantages gave the United States far more capacity to pressure Indigenous nations over time. Yet they were capacities and ambitions to be deployed, not evidence that all Indigenous political life had already been absorbed.

The responses of Indigenous nations show the limits of a single narrative of conquest. Diné leaders negotiated with American commanders in 1846, but local relations and disputes continued. Pueblo participants at Taos resisted occupation, while other Pueblo communities took different positions; later legal controversies showed that their status was not conclusively settled in 1848. Comanche and Kiowa bands continued to shape commerce and security on the southern Plains. Apache local groups made their own decisions within a frontier that now included a new government. O’odham, Quechan, Ute, and other nations encountered the American state through different routes and at different times. The war did not produce one uniform Indigenous experience.

The treaty's legal language captures both the scale of the transition and its contradictions. Articles VIII and IX sought to protect specified rights of Mexican residents, but implementation would involve contested claims and unequal institutions. The deleted Article X illustrates the importance of distinguishing a negotiated draft from the ratified treaty. Article XI required the United States to control Indigenous incursions and captives while treating Indigenous peoples as objects of negotiation between states. Its assumptions about American power were not realized simply because the article existed. That provision, and its later abrogation, exposes the uneasy effort to use international law to regulate nations outside the negotiation.

Historical interpretation should resist two opposite simplifications. It would be wrong to say that nothing important changed for Indigenous nations because many retained territory and agency after 1848. The new international order made future military and settler expansion much more likely and gave the United States greater institutional capacity to pursue it. It would be equally wrong to say that Indigenous sovereignty ended in 1848. Governments, kin networks, trading systems, and communities continued to act and to defend their rights. Their persistence was not a footnote after the treaty; it was part of the unfinished process that followed.

The long-term meaning of sovereignty also calls for precision. Indigenous sovereignty in this account is not a claim that every nation exercised identical legal authority or never faced external domination. It is a recognition that Indigenous peoples had political communities with governing capacities, relationships to land, and claims that were not created by the treaty between Mexico and the United States. Some rights were recognized imperfectly; others were ignored or attacked. Describing continuity after 1848 is therefore compatible with acknowledging major losses, vulnerability, and unequal power. The task of history is to show both realities in their chronological relationship.

The chapter therefore ends with a change of frame rather than a completed conquest. The defeat of Mexico removed one claimant from vast regions and installed another with formidable ambitions. The political future of Indigenous North America, however, would still be made through the choices, resistance, accommodation, and survival of Indigenous nations themselves. The next chapters follow the widening gap between American claims and Indigenous rights into California, Diné country, and the Pacific Northwest. The war of 1846–1848 was a turning point in that history, not the moment when its outcomes became inevitable.

Chapter Summary

The U.S.–Mexico War arose from the annexation of Texas, rival boundary claims, expansionist politics, and the decision of American leaders to pursue territorial control by force. The history of that conflict cannot be understood independently of Indigenous North America. Comanche, Kiowa, Apache, Diné, Pueblo, Ute, O’odham, Quechan, and other nations occupied and governed large parts of the territories that the republics claimed, and their military and diplomatic activity influenced the conditions in which the interstate war occurred.

American military forces occupied Santa Fe and key Californian positions in 1846. In New Mexico, Kearny’s proclamations and provisional government asserted new authority while attempting to secure local compliance. Resistance erupted at Taos in 1847, involving some Pueblo and Hispano participants, and American forces suppressed it with severe violence. The conquest of California had its own political and social background; its later Indigenous catastrophe is treated in Chapter 70.

The Diné–American agreement at Bear Springs in November 1846 shows Indigenous representatives negotiating a specific diplomatic relationship with a new military power. Its terms concerning trade, prisoners, property, and Pueblo and New Mexican residents reveal both the possibilities and limits of wartime diplomacy. Other nations, including Comanche, Kiowa, Apache, Ute, O’odham, and Quechan communities, continued to act in political worlds that did not become American simply because a treaty later said that the surrounding territory belonged to the United States.

The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo was signed in February 1848, approved with U.S. Senate amendments in March, effective upon exchange of ratifications in May, and proclaimed by the United States in July. Article V defined a new boundary; Articles VIII and IX addressed Mexican residents and property; original Article X was deleted; and Article XI attempted to make the United States responsible for restraining Indigenous incursions into Mexico. The treaty was an act of diplomacy between republics, not an Indigenous land-cession treaty. Its provisions reshaped regional politics, but they did not resolve the independent claims of Indigenous nations.

The central conclusion is both change and continuity. American victory dramatically strengthened the position of a state seeking continental expansion, and later dispossession would follow in many regions. Yet Indigenous communities retained political agency, kin networks, local governments, trading systems, and claims to homelands after 1848. The treaty's territorial settlement was a major turning point in a history that remained unfinished.

Original proposed land-grant article removed from the ratified treaty by the U.S. Senate.

Treaty provision assigning the United States responsibility concerning Indigenous incursions, captives, stolen property, and displacement-related risks.

Formal exchange of governments’ completed approvals; for the 1848 treaty, May 30 at Querétaro.

Political authority of Indigenous communities and nations, independent of a foreign state’s recognition.

Provisional legal framework established under American military occupation in New Mexico.

Nineteenth-century American expansionist language linking continental growth to national mission and racial assumptions.

Control asserted by armed forces during wartime, distinct from consensual transfer of sovereignty.

Northern Mexican province occupied by the United States in 1846; home to Pueblo and other Indigenous communities.

1848 diplomatic explanation addressing some U.S. Senate amendments, including interpretation of deleted Article X.

Military and geographic observation, route recording, and mapping for strategic and administrative use.

U.S.–Mexican peace treaty signed February 2, 1848, which transferred Mexican territorial claims and contained legal obligations.

Interstate war of 1846–1848 that transformed the political position of Indigenous nations in western North America.

Indigenous people of the lower Colorado River crossing, an important corridor for military and commercial movement.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter Reflection

Consider an American map printed after the U.S.–Mexico War. It may present a clear new frontier, with a vast region colored as part of the United States. Now ask how a Diné household near a grazing route, a Pueblo village defending irrigation land, a Comanche community trading on the southern Plains, or an O’odham agricultural settlement would have described the same political world. A treaty did change the international position of the republics, but it did not substitute their words for the choices and histories of all the people on the map.

The lesson of Chapter 69 is neither that conquest was imaginary nor that it was complete. American victory placed an increasingly powerful state in a new position to pursue settlement, military expansion, and laws that would reshape Indigenous North America. At the same time, Indigenous political life continued. Communities negotiated, adapted, resisted, and survived under conditions of profound inequality. Historical understanding requires describing both the changed balance of power and the authority that the treaty’s signatories did not create and could not simply declare extinct.

Before proceeding to California, the Diné Long Walk, and the Pacific Northwest, return to the chapter’s central question. What precisely did the United States gain in 1848? Which rights had Mexico been able to transfer between governments, and which claims and obligations remained unsettled because Indigenous peoples were not parties? Answering that question accurately is the foundation for understanding the next stage of continental conquest and resistance.
