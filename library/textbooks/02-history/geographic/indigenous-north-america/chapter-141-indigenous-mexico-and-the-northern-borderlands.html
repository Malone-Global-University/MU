Chapter Opening

Imagine a homeland first mapped by rivers, mountain ranges, seasonal rains, and the walking routes of families, and then crossed, in the middle of the nineteenth century, by a line drawn in distant capitals. The line divided an Indigenous landscape between two states. It did not create two peoples.

This chapter begins with that problem. The international boundary between the United States and Mexico was established through a war, a treaty negotiated in 1848, and a further territorial purchase agreed in 1853 and ratified in 1854. These were agreements between states. The Indigenous peoples whose homelands lay along and across the new boundary were not parties to them in any form that either government has ever treated as a negotiation among equals. For the Tohono O'odham, whose communities, ceremonial geography, and farming and water-gathering places extended across the Sonoran Desert, and for the Kumeyaay, whose homeland reaches from the coast and mountains of present-day southern California deep into northern Baja California, the boundary cut through places that had long been connected by kinship, language, ceremony, and exchange. The effect was not the disappearance of those connections but their reorganization under two sets of laws, two administrative systems, and, over time, two national societies.

The Kickapoo present a different historical problem, and the contrast is essential to the chapter. Kickapoo ancestors were not desert peoples divided in place. They were Algonquian-speaking people whose historical homelands lay far to the north and east, in the Great Lakes region, and whose movements through the interior of the continent in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries were shaped by warfare, treaty pressure, and settler expansion. Part of the Kickapoo people eventually established a community in northern Mexico, and the transnational Kickapoo community that exists today is the product of migration and political choice rather than of a boundary drawn through an old homeland. Both Kickapoo and Tohono O'odham communities now live across the international line, but they arrived at that circumstance by different historical routes. The chapter treats that difference as a central analytical point.

Chapter 141 is also a chapter about Indigenous Mexico as a historical subject in its own right. A continental history cannot treat the peoples of northern Mexico as an appendix to the history of Native communities in the United States. The Yaqui (Yoeme), Mayo (Yoreme), Rarámuri, Comcaac, Guarijío, and other peoples of Sonora, Chihuahua, Sinaloa, and Baja California have histories shaped by Spanish missions and mining frontiers, by independent Mexico's land policies, by revolution and agrarian reform, and by Mexico's distinctive legal arrangements for communal land and Indigenous recognition. These histories are connected to those of Native nations in the United States, but they are not reducible to them, and several of the peoples examined here have their principal historical communities south of the boundary.

The chapter has a central argument. The international boundary reorganized Indigenous homelands without eliminating the peoples, relationships, and political communities that existed before it. Contemporary Indigenous nationhood in the northern borderlands reflects centuries of resistance, adaptation, migration, institutional reconstruction, and cultural continuity. It would be as mistaken to describe this history as a story of loss alone as to describe it as a story of unbroken tradition. Indigenous communities in the borderlands have lost land, water, people, and political autonomy, sometimes through violence on a large scale. They have also rebuilt towns, reestablished ceremonial and governmental institutions, moved in order to survive and then moved again, and sustained family and community ties across jurisdictions that were not designed to accommodate them.

The evidence for this history is varied and unevenly distributed. Spanish missionary reports, military correspondence, and administrative records survive in abundance for the colonial frontier, but they were written by outsiders with their own purposes. Mexican and American treaties, congressional and legislative records, land-grant and agrarian-reform files, and court proceedings document the legal transformation of Indigenous territory. Archaeology and historical linguistics illuminate the long background. Oral histories, community-authored publications, ceremonial calendars, and the testimony of Indigenous people themselves provide knowledge that written colonial records often omit or distort. Some knowledge, especially ceremonial knowledge, is restricted by the communities that hold it, and this textbook does not seek to disclose it.

Students should also keep in mind a boundary of another kind, the boundary between this chapter and its neighbors in the textbook. The precolonial Southwest was treated earlier, in Chapter 30, and the colonial northern frontier in Chapter 42. The nineteenth-century transformations of the borderlands appear in Chapters 68 and 69, the Apache Wars in Chapters 79 and 80, and water rights in Chapter 120. Urbanization was the subject of Chapter 140. This chapter draws on those discussions and summarizes them only where necessary. It is primarily a people-centered regional history. Chapter 142 will turn to the border itself as a political, legal, and administrative institution, including border-crossing rights and enforcement, and those subjects are reserved for it.

To organize comparison across very different peoples, the chapter introduces an MGU instructional framework, the Six-Dimension Borderlands Indigenous Continuity Framework, presented below. It is a teaching tool for asking consistent questions, not a scholarly theory, a statistical index, or a measure of sovereignty, and it assigns no scores to any Indigenous people. The chapter's twenty sections move from the historical geography of the borderlands, through the peoples of northern Mexico and the peoples whose homelands were divided, to contemporary institutions and the question of what sovereignty means when Indigenous communities live under more than one state.

One note on names. Many peoples in this chapter are known by both outsider names and their own. Yaqui is a familiar name; Yoeme is the people's own term in their language. Mayo corresponds to Yoreme. Tarahumara is the older exonym for the people who call themselves Rarámuri, and the Comcaac are widely known by the exonym Seri. The Tohono O'odham were formerly widely called Papago, a name the nation no longer uses. Kumeyaay, Kumiai, Ipai, Tipai, and Diegueño overlap in complicated ways discussed in Section 141.9. This textbook uses self-designations wherever they are established and notes familiar exonyms where students will meet them in sources. Orthography varies among communities and publications, and the chapter does not claim to settle questions that belong to the communities themselves.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why northern Mexico is indispensable to a continental history of Indigenous North America, and why Indigenous Mexico is treated as a historical subject in its own right.
  2. Describe the environments of the northern borderlands, including the Sonoran and Chihuahuan deserts, the Sierra Madre Occidental, the Colorado River, the Gulf of California, and Baja California, and explain how Indigenous economies related to them.
  3. Trace the transformation of Indigenous communities through Spanish colonial expansion, Mexican independence, and the nineteenth-century territorial transfers.
  4. Distinguish changes in international sovereignty from the continuing relationships of Indigenous peoples to their homelands.
  5. Compare the historical experiences of the Yaqui, Mayo, Rarámuri, Kumeyaay, Tohono O'odham, and Kickapoo, explaining why each should be understood on its own terms.
  6. Contrast peoples whose homelands were divided by the boundary with peoples, such as the Kickapoo, whose transnational community resulted from migration.
  7. Analyze Mexican communal landholding, including the ejido, and compare it with reservation and trust-land arrangements in the United States.
  8. Evaluate the relationship among legal recognition, territorial control, and practical governing capacity in Mexico and the United States.
  9. Explain how work, seasonal migration, and multilocal households coexist with Indigenous community membership and cultural continuity.
  10. Assess the roles of women, elders, families, and young people in reconstructing Indigenous communities.
  11. Apply the Six-Dimension Borderlands Indigenous Continuity Framework as an analytical tool while recognizing its limits.
  12. Evaluate whether Indigenous nationhood can be adequately understood within the territorial framework of modern states.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What does it mean to describe the northern borderlands as an Indigenous historical region rather than as the frontier of two national histories?
  2. How did Indigenous communities in desert, mountain, river-valley, and coastal environments experience Spanish colonization differently?
  3. In what ways did independent Mexico's land policies and state-building threaten Indigenous communal territory, and how did Indigenous communities respond?
  4. Why is a change in international sovereignty not the same as a change in Indigenous relationships to a homeland?
  5. How did the Yaqui experience deportation and dispossession, and how did they rebuild community and territory?
  6. Why does the Kickapoo case differ historically from the Tohono O'odham and Kumeyaay cases, even though all three involve communities on both sides of the boundary?
  7. How do Mexican arrangements such as the ejido and constitutional recognition differ from federal recognition and trust land in the United States, and what are the limits of each?
  8. How do households and communities sustain themselves economically and culturally when work requires movement?
  9. What evidence can historians use to study peoples whose knowledge is partly restricted, and how should restricted knowledge be respected?
  10. Can Indigenous nationhood be adequately understood within the framework of modern states, and what would be lost or gained by trying?

Historical Context

The northern borderlands of this chapter are a set of linked environments rather than a single landscape. The Sonoran Desert stretches across southern Arizona, northwestern Sonora, and parts of Baja California and southeastern California. East of it, the Chihuahuan Desert covers much of northern Chihuahua and extends into Coahuila, Texas, and New Mexico. The Sierra Madre Occidental rises between them and the Pacific, a high, deeply cut range of pine-oak uplands and canyons that includes the barrancas of the Sierra Tarahumara. Westward from the Sierra, rivers descend toward the Gulf of California, among them the Yaqui, Mayo, and Fuerte, and their lower valleys have long supported farming communities. The Colorado River once reached the Gulf through a broad delta, and the Baja California peninsula separates the Gulf from the Pacific. Each of these environments supported distinct Indigenous economies, from floodwater farming and desert foraging to marine harvesting, mountain agriculture, and river-valley cultivation.

Long before European arrival, these environments were inhabited by peoples with elaborate economic and ceremonial systems, whose histories are developed in Chapters 15 through 18 and Chapter 30 for the Southwest and in Chapter 14 for relationships with Mesoamerica. What matters here is the pattern of relationships rather than a reconstruction of the whole precolonial region. Agricultural communities in river valleys and along desert washes exchanged goods with mobile hunting and gathering populations. Shell from the Gulf of California and the Pacific moved inland, and goods and ideas moved along routes that linked the Southwest to northwestern Mexico. Languages of the Uto-Aztecan family, which Chapter 1 introduced, were spoken across much of the region, from the Tohono O'odham and their relatives to the Yaqui and Mayo (Cahitan languages) and the Rarámuri, while Yuman-family languages were spoken along the lower Colorado River and in southern California and northern Baja California. Language relationships imply deep historical ties but do not prove political unity.

Spanish expansion into the north began in the sixteenth century, driven by the discovery of silver, by the search for labor, and by the aims of the Crown and the missionary orders. As Chapter 42 explained, the northern frontier of New Spain was organized around missions, presidios (garrisons), and mining settlements, and the Jesuit order established mission districts among the peoples of the Sonoran coast and the Sierra Tarahumara in the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. In Baja California, mission founding came later and involved first Jesuit, then Franciscan, and then Dominican orders. Colonial rule was uneven. Some communities were incorporated into mission pueblos and subjected to labor demands and epidemic disease. Others negotiated, withdrew into rougher terrain, rebelled, or maintained autonomy at the margins of Spanish control.

Mexican independence in 1821 ended Spanish rule but did not end pressure on Indigenous land. The new state, and the regional elites who dominated northern politics, inherited colonial categories while adopting liberal ideas about land and citizenship that treated corporate Indigenous landholding as an obstacle. Mexico also lacked the military and fiscal capacity to control all of its northern territory, and Indigenous peoples such as the Apache and Comanche, together with autonomous communities elsewhere, remained powerful actors on the frontier, a subject treated in Chapter 68. The Texas revolt and the U.S.–Mexico War of 1846 to 1848, described in Chapter 69, led to the cession by Mexico of vast territories, and the Gadsden Purchase of 1853 adjusted the southern boundary of what became Arizona and New Mexico.

The boundary that resulted was not a single event but a long process of surveying, marking, regulating, and eventually policing. For many decades it was crossed with little restriction. Only gradually, through customs regulation, immigration law, and twentieth- and twenty-first-century enforcement, did it become the heavily administered line that many students know today. This history matters because Indigenous communities experienced the boundary differently in different periods. In the nineteenth century it was in some ways more a line on a map than an obstacle. Later, as the two national societies consolidated, it increasingly shaped questions of citizenship, land tenure, schooling, and access to services. The detailed legal and administrative history of the boundary belongs to Chapter 142.

The twentieth century brought distinctive transformations on each side. In Mexico the Revolution of 1910 and the agrarian reforms that followed expanded and transformed the ejido, a landholding institution with colonial antecedents, into a principal instrument of rural redistribution, and in the 1930s redistributed land to many communities, including, in the Yaqui case, a significant territorial restitution. Large-scale irrigation and commercial agriculture developed in the Sonoran and Sinaloan river valleys, dams altered rivers, and mining, forestry, and later tourism and industrial development reshaped the Sierra. In the United States, federal Indian policy shifted from allotment and assimilation to the Indian Reorganization Act, termination, and self-determination, as earlier chapters describe, and tribal governments gained recognition and, over time, administrative capacity. The two countries thus produced very different legal environments for Indigenous communities.

By the late twentieth century, Indigenous political organization across Mexico had also changed. Mexican constitutional recognition of Indigenous peoples was introduced in the early 1990s and was substantially developed in a reform of 2001, and further reforms, including one in 2024 noted in Chapter 1, have continued to alter the legal framework. These developments are examined in Sections 141.14 and 141.18, with attention to the gap between formal recognition and practical authority. Today Indigenous peoples of the borderlands include communities in rural towns, mountain settlements, and cities on both sides of the line, and many households combine agriculture, wage labor, and migration. Their histories offer a particularly clear view of the limits of any approach that treats modern states as the natural units of Indigenous history.

The Six-Dimension Borderlands Indigenous Continuity Framework

MGU Instructional Tool

Chapter 141 introduces a comparative framework designed to help students ask consistent questions of very different Indigenous peoples operating under different political jurisdictions. It is applied throughout Divisions II through V. The framework is an MGU instructional tool. It is not an established scholarly theory, a statistical index, or a universally accepted measure of Indigenous sovereignty, and it is not used to rank or to assign numerical scores to any Indigenous people. Indigenous nations define their own sovereignty and continuity, and the dimensions below are prompts for historical questions, not criteria that a community must satisfy.

DimensionHistorical questions
Territorial continuityHow do communities maintain relationships with ancestral lands, including lands they no longer legally control?
Political and legal authorityWho governs, and what authority can community institutions exercise under each state's law and under community norms?
Economic capacityHow do communities sustain households and collective institutions?
Cultural and linguistic continuityHow is knowledge transmitted between generations?
Family and kinship networksHow do families sustain geographically distributed communities?
Transboundary relationshipsHow are relationships maintained across different national jurisdictions?

The six dimensions overlap. A language program is a cultural institution, but it may also rest on kinship ties, require funding, and depend on a government's recognition of a school. Students should expect to find the dimensions interacting and should resist treating any one of them as decisive.

Athabaskan-speaking peoples whose histories extend across the Southwest and northern Mexico; see Chapter 51.
Cucapá (Cocopah)
A Yuman-speaking people of the lower Colorado River and delta, with communities in Mexico and in Arizona.
Comcaac (Seri)
A people of the Sonoran coast of the Gulf of California, with a language that has no established relatives and a marine economy.
Guarijío
A Uto-Aztecan-speaking people of the mountains and canyons of southeastern Sonora and southwestern Chihuahua.

Sonoran Desert
The desert region of southern Arizona, Sonora, and parts of California and Baja California.

Division I

The Historical Geography of the Northern Borderlands

Section 141.1

Indigenous Homelands Before the International Border

A region is not a natural object. It is an interpretation of relationships that people made across space. The northern borderlands of Mexico and the southwestern United States are best understood in that way, as a region organized by Indigenous peoples, ecological systems, and the exchange of people and goods, long before the nineteenth-century boundary was drawn through it. To say so is not to claim that the region was uniform or unchanging. It contained mobile foragers and sedentary farmers, river towns and mountain settlements, coastal harvesters and desert water managers, and these populations moved, married, traded, fought, and reorganized themselves over many centuries. The point is that the principal organizing lines of this world were rivers, mountain ranges, deserts, seasons, languages, and kin ties, not the political lines later laid over them.

Historical Vocabulary
Borderlands

Definition. A borderland is a region whose history has been shaped by the presence of one or more political boundaries, together with the societies, economies, and relationships that exist on either side of them and across them.

Historical context. Historians of North America have used the term for the northern frontier of New Spain, for the zone between rival empires, and for the region along the present U.S.–Mexico boundary. Each usage emphasizes that frontier regions are places of exchange, conflict, and negotiation, not merely edges of centralized states. For Indigenous history, the term carries an additional and specific implication: for many peoples the borderlands were not the edge of anything but the center of their own world.

Why it matters. The term is useful because it directs attention to relationships that cross lines, but it can mislead if it implies that the boundary is the primary fact of Indigenous history. This chapter uses "borderlands" for the region and "homeland" for a people's own territorial relationships.

Potential misunderstanding. A "border" is not a "frontier." A frontier is a zone of contact; a modern border is a legally defined and administered line. Indigenous peoples experienced both, in different periods.

Deserts, Mountains, Rivers, and Sea

The environmental core of the region is the Sonoran Desert, a warm desert with two rainy seasons, one in winter and one in the summer monsoon, that supports a rich community of plants including the saguaro, mesquite, and agave. Indigenous peoples of the desert, notably the O'odham, developed farming methods suited to unreliable rainfall, including the use of seasonal floodwater at the mouths of washes, and they also relied on the harvest of wild plant foods such as saguaro fruit and mesquite pods. These activities were organized seasonally. Families and communities moved between summer field villages and winter villages near more dependable water, and the movement of people between ecological zones was part of the economy, not a departure from settled life.

East and south of the Sonoran lowlands, the Sierra Madre Occidental creates a very different environment. High forested plateaus are cut by deep canyons, and elevation brings differences in temperature and rainfall within short distances. Peoples of the Sierra, among them the Rarámuri and Guarijío, practiced agriculture in small, scattered plots of maize, beans, and squash on mountain slopes and in canyon bottoms, kept stock after the arrival of Europeans, and used knowledge of seasonal movement between higher and lower elevations. Westward, rivers such as the Yaqui, Mayo, and Fuerte descend to coastal plains where annual flooding and irrigation could sustain concentrated agriculture, and the Yaqui and Mayo settled in these valleys. Along the Gulf of California and the Pacific coasts, people such as the Comcaac relied on fishing, shellfish gathering, and the hunting of sea turtles and other marine animals alongside desert foraging.

Farther north and west the Colorado River, with its seasonally flooding lower valley and delta, supported riverine farming among Yuman-speaking peoples such as the Cucapá, who planted in the silt after floods, fished, and gathered. Across the Baja California peninsula and into the coastal and mountain country of what is now southern California, the Kumeyaay and their neighbors drew on coastal resources, oak woodlands whose acorns were a staple food, and seasonal resources of the interior desert margins. Chaparral, oak, pine, coastal sage, and desert plants all lay within the movements of a single people.

Networks of Exchange and Ceremony

These environments were linked. Shell from the Gulf of California and the Pacific coast moved inland as ornament, and goods from the interior moved toward the coasts. Salt, plant materials, stone, pigments, feathers, and agricultural products also moved through exchange relationships that rested on kinship and ceremonial ties as well as on trade. Ceremonial geography added further connections. Mountains, springs, and other places held religious significance for particular peoples, and journeys to gather salt or other sacred materials could connect widely separated communities. These networks were not centrally managed, and they changed over time, but they gave the region an interconnected character that the later boundary would cut across.

Students should be careful not to read this description as a map of fixed, exclusive "tribal territories." Historical relationships were more complex than a single line around a people. Communities shared resources, borderlands between peoples were zones of interaction, and populations moved in response to drought, warfare, disease, and opportunity. The schematic maps in this chapter show approximate areas of principal association only.

The waterways also illustrate why the modern boundary is an inadequate unit of environmental analysis. The Colorado is a continental river, and the water available in its delta depends on decisions made far upstream. The Yaqui and Mayo rivers, by contrast, descend through Mexican territory from upland catchments toward the Gulf. In all three cases water management connects people who live in different ecological zones. A community's fields may be downstream from a reservoir, but its claim to a river may encompass places that cannot be reduced to an irrigation allotment. Distinguishing a watershed from a political jurisdiction is therefore essential for understanding the later histories of resource conflict. The chapter returns to this relationship in Section 141.13.

Section 141.2

Spanish Colonialism and the Northern Frontier

The Spanish colonization of northern Mexico is treated in Chapter 42, and this section summarizes only what students need to understand the nineteenth- and twentieth-century histories that follow. The key point is that colonization was not experienced uniformly. Spanish presence was thick where silver and large Indigenous populations lay together, thinner in mountain and desert interiors, and uneven even within a single region. Indigenous peoples' experience of Spain depended heavily on the ecology of their homelands, the density of colonial settlement, the labor demands placed upon them, and the strategies they adopted.

Missions were central to the colonial effort in the northwest. A mission district was an institution that combined religious instruction with the concentration of Indigenous people into towns (pueblos) around a church, with agricultural production, and with the supervision of labor. Jesuit missionaries began work among the Yaqui and Mayo of the river valleys in the early seventeenth century, and the Yaqui in particular entered into an arrangement with the Spanish that left them substantial control over their own communities and territory. Among the Rarámuri, Jesuits and later other clergy established missions in the Sierra Tarahumara, but dispersed mountain settlement meant that many Rarámuri lived beyond the daily reach of missionary oversight. In the Sonoran Desert, missionaries working among the O'odham established missions at selected sites, and the Jesuits' successors continued to rely on a mixture of persuasion, gifts, and military force. In Baja California, Jesuit, then Franciscan, then Dominican missions were established on the peninsula, and from 1769 Franciscans established missions in Alta California that reached the Kumeyaay.

Mines drew Indigenous men into labor systems and drew Spanish settlers who sought land and water for ranches and farms. These pressures created recurring conflict. Colonial demands for labor, the spread of epidemic disease, and encroachment on land provoked rebellions across the region, including revolts among the Rarámuri in the seventeenth century and large uprisings in the Yaqui and Mayo country in the eighteenth. Some rebellions were suppressed with great violence. Others resulted in negotiated adjustments. Indigenous communities also used colonial institutions, such as petitions to colonial authorities and the pueblo's formal officers, to defend land and privileges, so that accommodation and resistance often operated together.

Colonial rule left a durable institutional legacy. Many Indigenous communities came to organize themselves through a civil-religious structure of offices, a cabildo or town council with annual or periodic officers, a church-centered ritual calendar, and a system of obligations that combined community service with religious devotion. These forms were colonial in origin, but communities appropriated and reshaped them, and they came to be understood as the communities' own. Some peoples also kept earlier institutions and practices alongside them. Historians therefore avoid treating either "colonial imposition" or "unbroken precolonial tradition" as the whole story.

The expulsion of the Jesuits from Spanish dominions in 1767 disrupted the mission system in the northwest, and secular clergy, military officers, and civilian settlers took on greater roles in the late colonial period. The Bourbon reforms of the eighteenth century increased the Crown's efforts to extract revenue and to extend control over the frontier, and pressure on Indigenous land and labor grew. By 1821, when Mexico gained independence, Indigenous communities in the north occupied a range of positions: some largely incorporated into rural colonial society, some holding a defended autonomy, and some still outside Spanish control.

Mission histories help students distinguish institutional concentration from territorial conquest. A village could accept missionaries and continue to control access to its surrounding fields and gathering places. Conversely, the establishment of a church could be followed by demographic loss and the increasing intervention of military and civil officials. The Jesuit work along the Fuerte and Mayo preceded the entry of missionaries into the Yaqui valley, and the sequence shows how news of missions traveled between Indigenous communities. Yaqui leaders made decisions with knowledge of developments among their neighbors rather than simply reacting to a sudden Spanish intrusion. The contrast between the river valleys and the mountains also matters: maintaining a church in a dispersed Sierra community did not give a missionary the same day-to-day authority that officials hoped to exercise in a concentrated settlement. [4], [5]

The economic institutions of the frontier were connected. Mining settlements needed workers, food, animals and transport; agricultural missions could produce supplies and furnish labor; presidios offered military protection for colonial settlements. Yet Indigenous families also had their own priorities and could use trade or employment to acquire goods without accepting permanent relocation. Colonial records frequently describe such exchanges from the point of view of officials who wanted predictable labor and revenue. Reading them alongside community histories changes the question from whether a mission was administratively successful to what the encounter meant for the people whose labor and territory sustained it. [4], [5]

Section 141.3

Mexican Independence and Indigenous Territorial Struggles

Independence in 1821 ended Spanish rule and inaugurated an independent monarchy; the First Mexican Empire fell in 1823, and the federal Constitution of 1824 established a republic, but it did not simply return land or autonomy to Indigenous communities. The new state abolished the colonial legal categories that had distinguished "Indians" as a separate legal group, with their own protections and obligations, and declared all citizens formally equal. For Indigenous communities this had mixed consequences. Equal citizenship removed some forms of colonial discrimination, but it also removed the legal basis on which communities had defended their lands and weakened the corporate identity that colonial law had acknowledged.

The nineteenth century brought the rise of liberal land policy. Liberal reformers, who saw communal landholding by corporations such as Indigenous communities and the Church as an obstacle to economic progress and to a society of individual citizen-owners, passed laws in the 1850s, such as the Ley Lerdo of 1856, and incorporated related principles in the Constitution of 1857, requiring the breakup of corporate landholdings. In practice, the privatization of communal land often benefited those with capital and political connections, and many Indigenous communities lost land through sale, forced sale, and fraud. In the north, the expansion of commercial agriculture and ranching, the growth of mining, and the ambitions of regional elites increased pressure on land and water, particularly in fertile river valleys.

The Yaqui provide the clearest example of Indigenous resistance to these processes, and Section 141.5 examines their history in detail. Their valley was fertile and increasingly attractive to speculators, and Yaqui leaders resisted the efforts of state and national authorities to survey, divide, and open the land. Yaqui resistance involved both armed struggle and political organization, and in the 1870s and 1880s it produced a brief period in which Yaqui authorities exercised extensive control over their territory. Mayo communities, Rarámuri communities, and other northern peoples also resisted, petitioned, withdrew, or adapted. The state, for its part, was uneven in its capacity: central authority was weak in many periods, regional governors and landowners were powerful, and the Mexican army was at times engaged in other conflicts.

The northern frontier also faced external pressure. Apache and Comanche raiding, treated in Chapter 68, contributed to insecurity in northern Mexico and to the militarization of parts of the frontier, and governments in Sonora and Chihuahua at times offered bounties for the scalps of Apache people, a policy of extreme violence directed at one set of Indigenous communities by the state. This aspect of frontier history reminds students that "Indigenous peoples" in northern Mexico were not a single party: some Indigenous communities were victims of state-sponsored violence while others, such as Opata and O'odham auxiliaries, served in frontier militias, and Indigenous groups themselves competed for resources and raided one another.

The constitutional chronology matters for understanding this transformation. Between the achievement of independence and the later liberal reforms, Mexico experimented with different forms of state organization. The federal republic established in 1824 did not settle the distribution of authority between national and regional governments. In northern Mexico, the ability of governors, military commanders and landholders to make decisions about the frontier was often as important as national declarations. Indigenous political strategies accordingly depended on the changing balance of power: a community could seek protection through a regional arrangement while rejecting the terms imposed by another government. Neither federalism nor centralism automatically guaranteed local Indigenous autonomy. [3], [4], [13]

The legal idea of individual equality should also be distinguished from the material ability to exercise rights. To register property, contest a survey or obtain access to a court required information, time, political connections and often money. A landholding institution could have legal protection at one moment and become vulnerable when another administration applied new rules. For families accustomed to using shared pasture, woodland or water, an individually registered field did not necessarily provide an equivalent livelihood. The pressure to convert land to a marketable asset therefore involved not only ownership but the reorganization of collective decision-making. Yaqui and Mayo histories offer contrasting examples of the ways communities resisted, negotiated and survived these changes. [3], [4], [13]

Cause and Effect

State Formation and Indigenous Territorial Dispossession

Long-term causes

Colonial concentration of land and labor, the legal category of the "Indian" that independence abolished, and the economic importance of northern river valleys, mines, and ranches.

Short-term causes

Liberal land laws of the mid-nineteenth century, the desire of regional elites for land and water, and the need of the state to assert authority over the north.

Immediate effects

Surveys, sales, and partition of communal land; the weakening of corporate Indigenous legal standing; armed conflicts with communities that resisted.

Long-term consequences

Loss of land and water for many communities; later, revolution and agrarian reform in the twentieth century that reversed some losses and created new legal forms.

Historical complexity: The liberal state did not have a single purpose or capacity. Some of its policies were driven by ideology, some by private interest, and some by security concerns. Indigenous communities were not passive: they litigated, negotiated, allied with factions, and fought. The pattern should not be read as a story of inevitable dispossession.

Section 141.4

The Creation of the U.S.–Mexico Border

The war between the United States and Mexico, treated in Chapter 69, ended with the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, signed in 1848. Mexico ceded to the United States a vast area, including present-day California, Nevada, Utah, and most of Arizona and New Mexico, together with parts of other states, and the boundary was set at the Rio Grande and westward to the Pacific, south of San Diego. For the Kumeyaay, the treaty meant that part of their homeland, including the coast around San Diego, would lie in the United States, and part, in the Baja California peninsula, would remain in Mexico. A few years later, in 1853, the Gadsden Purchase transferred additional territory south of the Gila River, and the agreement was ratified in 1854. This line crossed the Sonoran Desert homelands of the O'odham.

The treaties were agreements between two states. Neither the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo nor the Gadsden Purchase involved the consent of the Indigenous peoples whose homelands were transferred or divided. Both governments, like other states of the period, claimed authority over territory and populations on the strength of their own legal systems and military power. This is a key distinction for students: a change in international sovereignty is different from a change in the relationship between a people and its homeland. The treaties changed which state claimed jurisdiction over particular places. They did not end Indigenous presence, kinship, ceremonial attachment, or, in many cases, historical claims to the land.

The border region was at first very lightly governed. Surveying the line took years, boundary commissions marked it with monuments at intervals, and large stretches were remote. Customs regulation was limited. People moved across the line for family, work, ceremony, and trade, and for many Indigenous communities the boundary had little immediate practical effect. At the same time, the treaties contained provisions that treated Indigenous raiding as a problem for the two states: the 1848 treaty obliged the United States to restrain Indigenous groups from raiding into Mexico, and this was later superseded by the Gadsden agreement. These provisions show how the two governments conceived Indigenous peoples as security problems to be managed across the line, and they affected the Apache and others in ways examined in Section 141.12.

Over time, different jurisdictions developed on either side. In the United States, Indigenous communities were affected by reservation policy, treaty-making and its end, allotment, and the extension of federal administration. In Mexico, communities were subject to the state's land policy, municipal governance, and later agrarian law. Differences in how each state recognized, regulated, and funded Indigenous communities gradually produced differences in the institutions, resources, and legal standing of communities that had once been closely linked. These divergences are developed in the later sections of this chapter, and the administration of the boundary itself is the subject of Chapter 142.

A close reading of the two treaties clarifies their different functions. Article V of the 1848 treaty delineated the international boundary. Articles VIII and IX addressed the property and political status of Mexican residents in territories acquired by the United States, but they did not establish an equivalent nation-to-nation settlement with the Indigenous peoples living there. Article XI was a separate undertaking concerning raids into Mexico. In Article II of the Gadsden treaty, the United States was released from that undertaking. The change did not eliminate Indigenous political authority in practice; it changed how the states allocated responsibility for frontier violence. The 1848 agreement and the later treaty should therefore be read together rather than treated as one undifferentiated act of border creation. [1], [2]

The treaties also illustrate the difference between a diplomatic map and a map of effective control. Surveyors had to interpret the agreed line, identify landmarks and mark the boundary on the ground. The ability of governments to regulate territory then depended on local institutions, transportation and enforcement. Indigenous people continued to use familiar routes and resources even when the paper status of those places changed. In the long run the boundary acquired greater importance because each state developed separate systems for registering property, administering citizenship and providing public services. The emergence of these systems, rather than the treaty date alone, explains the increasing divergence in community circumstances. [1], [2]

Section 141.5

The Yaqui: Territory, Resistance and Nationhood

The Yaqui, who call themselves Yoeme, are among the best-documented Indigenous peoples of northern Mexico, and their history shows with unusual clarity how territory, community institutions, and political resistance were connected. Their homeland lies along the lower Yaqui River in Sonora, a fertile river valley and coastal plain that supported farming, fishing, and gathering. Their language belongs to the Cahitan branch of the Uto-Aztecan family, closely related to that of the Mayo. Before sustained Spanish contact, Yaqui communities were organized in rancherías along the river, and Yaqui people later described the identity of their people as tied to the river valley and to the pueblos and institutions that developed within it.

The Eight Pueblos and Colonial Encounter

In the early seventeenth century, the Yaqui reached an accommodation with the Spanish. After armed encounters in which Yaqui forces repulsed Spanish expeditions, Yaqui leaders agreed to receive Jesuit missionaries, and the Jesuits organized the Yaqui into eight principal pueblos along the lower river: Tórim, Bácum, Cócorit, Huírivis, Belem, Pótam, Ráhum, and Vícam. The pueblos became the basic units of Yaqui social and political life. Each had a church, a plaza, and a set of civil and ceremonial officers, and the system of eight pueblos came to symbolize the unity of Yaqui territory. The Yaqui maintained a high degree of control over their land, in part because the Spanish needed their labor and cooperation and could not easily displace them. They also adopted elements of Catholic practice and combined them with their own ceremonial and ritual forms, in ways examined in Section 141.16.

The relationship was not harmonious. Yaqui people were drawn into colonial labor, including work in mines and on farms outside the valley, and Yaqui leaders led or joined rebellions, including a major uprising in 1740 in which Yaqui and Mayo participants rose against Spanish authority and abuse. After the Jesuits' expulsion in 1767, Yaqui authorities took on a greater role in running their own communities, and the pueblos developed a form of self-government that combined civil, military, and ceremonial offices.

Dispossession and War in the Nineteenth Century

After Mexican independence, pressure on the Yaqui valley grew as landowners, speculators, and state officials sought its fertile land and water. Yaqui leaders resisted, and in the 1820s and 1830s there were Yaqui revolts and negotiated settlements. In the 1870s and 1880s a Yaqui leader known as Cajeme, who had served in the Mexican military and in local government, led a period of organized Yaqui self-rule and military resistance, reorganizing the valley's institutions and defending Yaqui territory against the Mexican state. Mexican forces defeated Cajeme in the 1880s and he was executed in 1887. Resistance continued under other leaders, including Tetabiate in the 1890s, with fighters using the rugged Bacatete mountains as a refuge.

Deportation and Migration

The government of Porfirio Díaz, which ruled Mexico for most of the period from 1876 to 1911, sought to open northern lands to commercial agriculture and to end Yaqui resistance. The campaigns of the Porfirian state against the Yaqui, especially in the early twentieth century, included military operations and the deportation of Yaqui people from Sonora to other parts of Mexico, including plantations in Yucatán and other regions where they were forced into harsh labor. Historians agree that the deportations involved thousands of people, but estimates of the numbers deported and the numbers who died vary, and this chapter does not give a figure. Many families were separated. Some Yaqui fled to the mountains. Others moved north into Arizona, where they settled in communities such as those near Tucson and elsewhere, creating the foundation of the Yaqui communities in the United States.

Historians use the language of "deportation" and "forced displacement" for these events, and some have argued that the violence and intent of Porfirian policy warrant discussion in terms of ethnic persecution or genocide. The classification is debated, and this textbook notes the debate without resolving it here; what is not in dispute is that the Mexican state forcibly removed large numbers of Yaqui people from their homeland and subjected many to coerced labor.

Reconstruction and Contemporary Life

The Mexican Revolution brought upheaval, and many Yaqui participated in the fighting while seeking the return of land. President Lázaro Cárdenas’s agreement of October 27, 1937, published October 30, recognized agricultural land and associated resources. The final titling resolution published October 22, 1940, specified the boundaries of the restored territory and provided for irrigation from the La Angostura reservoir. These were separate stages of restitution. The restoration was important but did not settle every boundary, land-use dispute or water claim; subsequent dams and agricultural development altered the river system itself. [6], [7]

In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, Yaqui authorities have continued to struggle over water, particularly over the diversion of water from the Yaqui River basin to supply cities and agriculture. A long-running conflict over a project to transport water to the city of Hermosillo became a focus of legal and political action by Yaqui authorities and drew attention to Yaqui claims. In May 2013 the Supreme Court affirmed protection for members of the Vícam community in amparo review 631/2012, requiring a hearing through recognized community representatives in relation to environmental authorization. Yaqui authorities and community members have also faced violence and criminalization during conflicts over land and water, and they have called on Mexican and international institutions for protection.

In the United States, the Yaqui community near Tucson was recognized as the Pascua Yaqui Tribe by Public Law 95-375 on September 18, 1978, giving the community government-to-government status, and the tribe has since developed institutions of governance, education, and economic enterprise. Yaqui communities in Arizona and in Sonora maintain strong ties of kinship and ceremony, and families and ceremonial specialists cross the boundary for the ceremonial cycle, particularly Lent and Holy Week observances. Not all Yaqui communities in Mexico and the United States share the same legal status or governing institutions, but the Yaqui view the traditional pueblos and the unity of their people as the center of their identity.

The eight pueblos remained a focus of territorial reconstruction, although actual settlements and their locations changed through dispossession and rebuilding. The 2021 decree names the communities through their contemporary institutional designations, including Loma de Bácum and Cócorit–Loma de Guamúchil. A student comparing this list with the historical pueblo names should distinguish an enduring political community from the exact location or name of a settlement at a given date. In the United States, the recognition of the Pascua Yaqui Tribe likewise created a new legal relationship for a particular political community rather than a blanket recognition of every Yaqui person or institution in Sonora. Its development of health services following recognition illustrates the practical consequences of a legal change: an acknowledged tribal government could negotiate institutional arrangements to serve its members. Community identity and governmental service eligibility remain distinct questions. [6], [11], [12]

The 1994 amendments to the Pascua Yaqui legislation expressly acknowledged the tribe as a historic, federally recognized Indian tribe possessing the inherent attributes of sovereignty except where removed by Congress or inconsistent with tribal status. This statutory clarification is distinct from the original 1978 recognition. [36]

Yaqui Territorial Resistance and Community Reconstruction

The problem. Historians reconstructing Yaqui history rely on a mixture of sources that differ sharply in perspective.

Spanish and Mexican official records include missionary reports, military correspondence, state and federal decrees, and agrarian files. They document campaigns, deportations, and legal decisions, but they were written by those who sought to govern, control, or exploit the Yaqui, and they often describe Yaqui resistance as rebellion or banditry rather than defense of territory.

Legal and community documents, including land restitution decrees and records of Yaqui governing authorities, document the reconstruction of territory in the twentieth century.

Section 141.6

The Mayo or Yoreme: Agriculture, Community and Cultural Continuity

South of the Yaqui, in the valleys of the Mayo and Fuerte rivers in southern Sonora and northern Sinaloa, live the Mayo, who call themselves Yoreme, "the people who respect the tradition." Their language, like Yaqui, belongs to the Cahitan group, and the two languages are closely related, which led early colonial observers to treat the peoples together. The comparison is useful, but the Yoreme have a distinct history, and the section explores both the relationship and the differences.

Yoreme communities historically lived in dispersed rancherías along the river valleys, farming maize, beans, squash, and cotton on the fertile floodplain, fishing, and gathering wild resources. The Jesuits began work among the Mayo in the early seventeenth century, before their work among the Yaqui: Jesuits worked along the Fuerte River by 1605, entered the Mayo River Valley in 1614, and began their Yaqui mission in 1617. The missionaries organized Mayo settlements into mission pueblos. As with the Yaqui, the mission towns became centers of both colonial religious instruction and Indigenous community life, and the Mayo developed a system of civil and religious offices with ceremonial leaders who continue to play a role in community life.

The Mayo participated in the uprisings of the eighteenth century, and in the nineteenth century they too faced pressure on land and water from commercial agriculture. In contrast to the Yaqui, however, the Mayo did not sustain a prolonged territorial war of the same duration, and their land was incorporated more extensively into regional commercial agriculture, so that Mayo communities today often live in a landscape of intensive irrigated farming shared with non-Indigenous farmers and with large agricultural enterprises. The reasons for the difference are debated and are likely to include geography, the particular leadership and political decisions of each people, the strategies of regional elites, and contingent events, not a single cause.

Twentieth-century agrarian reform created ejidos in the region, some of which included Mayo communities, and irrigation systems fed by dams on the Mayo and Fuerte rivers transformed agriculture. Many Mayo people work in agriculture as farmers and wage laborers, and some migrate for work. Community institutions, including traditional authorities, ceremonial societies, and mutual obligations, continue to organize community life, and Mayo ceremonial life, with its Lenten observances and fiestas, is a major expression of identity. Language use varies, with the Mayo language declining in some communities and supported through community and educational programs in others.

The Mayo River Valley and the Fuerte River region must be treated as distinct centers within a wider Yoreme historical geography. The early mission districts at Camoa, Navojoa and Etchojoa linked settlements through religious administration and agricultural production. Mining in the nearby Álamos district created markets and labor demands, which brought Indigenous farmers into relationships with the colonial economy. The resulting social geography was not simply a network of missionary establishments: fields, rancherías, community centers and places of worship continued to connect people beyond the administrative boundaries of each district. The 1740 uprising shows that these connections could also become networks of political resistance. [4], [13], [14], [9]

After independence the pressure on Yoreme land came not only from a national policy of privatization but from the gradual expansion of neighbors' farms, commercial investment and regional transport. The transformation of the valley created situations in which an Indigenous family might reside in a longstanding community but depend on wages earned elsewhere. When land was distributed through agrarian reform under Cárdenas, Yoreme communities obtained parcels and common lands in a fragmented pattern; in some localities the beneficiaries included non-Indigenous residents. Thus an ejido could be an important resource without reproducing the territorial unity of the earlier community. Its membership and assembly need not coincide exactly with the cultural and ceremonial community. [4], [13], [14], [9]

The contemporary significance of these differences can be seen by comparing the Yoreme and Yaqui justice plans. Federal documents treat the Yoreme as a distinct people with their own demands and agreements. Their concerns include recognition of authorities, land, infrastructure and the conditions necessary for community life. A program intended to strengthen Indigenous institutions must work with the particular authorities and settlements involved; it cannot merely transfer the administrative pattern of the eight Yaqui pueblos to the Yoreme. The Navojoa and Etchojoa area, for instance, contains towns and agricultural settlements in which Yoreme and non-Yoreme residents may use the same schools, markets and roads while maintaining different institutional and ceremonial relationships. [4], [13], [14], [9]

Language is another important dimension of this history. Yoreme and Yoeme are related languages, but the relationship does not make their speakers one political people. A school program or dictionary developed for one community does not automatically meet the language needs of another. Younger speakers may have different opportunities for learning and using the language depending on residence, employment and school provision. A sound comparison must therefore ask how specific institutions sustain language transmission and where the pressures of agricultural labor and Spanish-dominant public administration have changed the circumstances of that transmission. These questions explain why the Yoreme deserve a substantive history of their own, not merely a paragraph appended to the history of Yaqui resistance. [4], [13], [14], [9]

The Rarámuri: Mountain Homelands and Indigenous Autonomy

The Rarámuri, formerly widely known by the exonym Tarahumara, live primarily in the Sierra Madre Occidental of southwestern Chihuahua, in a region of high plateaus, forested uplands, and deep canyons known as the Sierra Tarahumara. Their language belongs to the Uto-Aztecan family. The name Rarámuri is generally rendered by the people themselves as referring to the people, and it contrasts with the term used for non-Rarámuri, discussed in community sources. Consult the linked documentary evidence for the relevant historical details.

Environment, Settlement, and Community

The Rarámuri world is a world of dispersed settlement. Families traditionally live in scattered homesteads, often moving between highland and lowland residences over the year, and farming small fields of maize, beans, and squash on slopes and in canyon bottoms. Pastoralism, based on animals introduced by Europeans such as goats, sheep, and cattle, became an important part of the economy. Settlement clusters around a church and a community center, where gatherings and ceremonies take place, and neighborhoods and communities are linked by kinship and by obligations of mutual assistance. Community organization includes a system of officers, among them governors and others who adjudicate disputes and oversee community affairs, and gatherings in which community decisions are discussed.

Rarámuri environmental knowledge is detailed. It includes the management of fields and water, knowledge of plants for food and medicine, and the interpretation of seasonal and ecological cycles. Ceremonial life follows a calendar and includes gatherings for planting, harvest, healing, and Holy Week observances, often involving communal meals of maize beer (tesgüino) and dance. Some ceremonies are publicly observed; others are restricted, and visitors and researchers have an obligation to respect Rarámuri decisions about what may be recorded or shared.

Colonial Missions and Beyond

Spanish contact with the Rarámuri intensified in the seventeenth century with the discovery of silver in the region and the arrival of Jesuit missionaries. Missions encouraged concentration into mission towns, and mining demanded labor, and many Rarámuri responded by moving deeper into the mountains, a strategy sometimes described as retreat but better understood as a deliberate use of terrain to preserve autonomy. The seventeenth and eighteenth centuries saw a series of Rarámuri revolts against colonial demands, and the colonial authorities responded with military force and negotiation. After the expulsion of the Jesuits, colonial and later Mexican rule continued to push at the edges of Rarámuri territory.

In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, new pressures arrived: the extension of ranching, the building of railroads, commercial logging, mining, and, more recently, tourism and the expansion of other economic activities. Rarámuri communities have faced loss of land and forest and conflicts with non-Indigenous settlers, loggers, and others, and drought, food insecurity, and violence connected with organized crime in parts of the Sierra have affected communities in recent decades. Many Rarámuri have also migrated to cities and agricultural regions, including Chihuahua City, Ciudad Juárez, and agricultural zones elsewhere in Mexico, seeking work and services, and urban Rarámuri communities now exist alongside rural ones. Rarámuri leaders and organizations have pressed for land rights, the protection of forests, education in Rarámuri languages, and public services.

Popular portrayals of the Rarámuri often portray them as isolated, timeless "runners" living in harmony with nature. Students should be wary of such descriptions. The Rarámuri have long histories of contact with Spanish, Mexican, and other communities, adopt and adapt new technologies and economic practices, and participate in the wage labor economy. Long-distance running is a significant part of some Rarámuri traditions, involving games and communal competition within a broader cultural context, but reducing the Rarámuri to that single practice distorts their history and obscures the political and economic pressures they face.

The Sierra Tarahumara is not an isolated cultural island. The highlands around places such as Norogachi, the commercial and transport centers associated with Creel, and the lower canyon environments offer different livelihood opportunities. A road can improve access to schooling and trade while also increasing the commercial value of timber and land. The development of tourism can provide employment and markets but can also change which institutions control valuable sites. These are historically contingent consequences, not properties inherent in mountain geography. A community's position relative to road networks, markets and municipal centers helps explain why neighboring settlements may experience development very differently. [15], [16], [31]

Rarámuri political organization must also be understood alongside the other peoples of the Sierra. The national Indigenous catalogue describes the region as shared with Ódami, O'ob and Guarijío communities. Federal education and justice-plan programs in the Sierra have therefore been organized around more than one people. This does not imply that their governing arrangements are identical. A school or community dining facility may serve a particular locality, whereas a regional development program may involve several communities and municipal jurisdictions. Evaluating the success of such institutions requires knowing who participates in their design, whether services can be accessed in the relevant language, and whether the resources reach dispersed settlements rather than only administrative centers. [15], [16], [31]

Migration can alter the distribution of responsibilities between the Sierra and cities without ending the community itself. A household with members working in a city may maintain fields in a mountain settlement, or return for family responsibilities while relying on urban earnings for supplies. These arrangements vary by household; they must not be assumed for every Rarámuri person. The combination of local environmental knowledge and new economic strategies is better understood as historical adaptation than as evidence of a transition from an allegedly traditional to a modern society. [15], [16], [31]

Geography and Everyday Life

Environment, Settlement and Community Organization in the Sierra Tarahumara

A Rarámuri household may maintain fields at different elevations, move animals according to the season, and travel to a central community for church services, assemblies, and celebrations. Household labor is divided by age and gender, and cooperative work parties exchange labor for communal meals. Women's work includes food preparation, weaving, the care of children and animals, and ceremonial food preparation, and women also hold property and participate in community decisions in ways that vary by community. Young people increasingly spend periods away for schooling and work.

The Indigenous Diversity of Northern Mexico

The three peoples examined so far are only part of a larger pattern. Northern Mexico is home to many Indigenous peoples with distinct languages, economies, and histories, and treating the region as culturally uniform would obscure more than it reveals. This section introduces several others.

The Comcaac, widely known as the Seri, live on the Sonoran coast of the Gulf of California, principally in the communities of Punta Chueca and El Desemboque, and their historical territory included the coast and Tiburón Island, the largest island in the Gulf. Their language is not demonstrably related to other languages in the region and is generally treated as a language isolate or the sole surviving member of a small family, a classification that specialists continue to discuss. Historically the Comcaac were mobile, harvesting marine resources such as fish and sea turtles and gathering desert plants, and they resisted Spanish and later Mexican incursions for centuries. In the twentieth century, they won recognition of rights to land that included the island and parts of the coast, and Comcaac communities have since become involved in the management of marine resources and in the cultural and ecological tourism economy, though conflicts over fishing, conservation, and access continue.

The Guarijío, whose own name for themselves is Makurawe or Warihó in different renderings, live in the canyon country of southeastern Sonora and southwestern Chihuahua. Their language belongs to the Uto-Aztecan family, closely related to that of the Rarámuri. They farm, raise livestock, and gather in a rugged landscape, and their communities have been relatively small and isolated from the main routes of the region, though not outside the reach of colonial and national institutions. Land titles, development projects, and access to services have been central issues for Guarijío communities.

The Pima or O'ob of Sonora and Chihuahua, the Pima Bajo, speak an O'odham-related language and live in smaller communities whose language and customs have been eroded through centuries of contact and pressure. They are related to but distinct from the O'odham north of the boundary, a relationship examined further in Section 141.10. Other communities in the region include the Opata, historically a major people of central and eastern Sonora, whose language and community identity were heavily affected by colonial and national policies, and who are today the subject of renewed efforts at cultural recovery, and the Tepehuán and others in the Sierra.

The Baja California peninsula was home to several Indigenous peoples. In the north, the Pa ipai, Kiliwa, Kumiai (the southern Kumeyaay), and the Cucapá of the Colorado River delta region speak Yuman languages, while in the central and southern peninsula, the Cochimí and other peoples whose languages are largely extinct faced severe population decline following the establishment of missions and the spread of introduced disease. Today, small Indigenous communities in northern Baja California sustain language and cultural efforts, while the peninsula also hosts large populations of migrants from other Indigenous regions of Mexico, such as Mixtec and Triqui people from Oaxaca who work in agriculture and urban industries, a reminder that borderlands Indigenous communities include newcomers as well as ancient residents.

These examples show different adaptations: marine harvesting, mountain agriculture, desert foraging, river-valley farming, and, in the modern period, migration for wage labor. They also show different relationships to the state: some communities holding recognized territory, some struggling for title, some with language vitality, and some with language loss. The unity of "Indigenous northern Mexico" lies in the shared experience of colonial and national pressure, not in a shared culture.

A further comparison concerns names and their geographical use. Guarijío communities in Sonora and Chihuahua use self-designations recorded in the national catalogue as Macurawe and Warihó, with spelling variants. Their distinct regional relationships reflect a history of interaction with Yoreme and Rarámuri neighbors. A linguistic classification is useful for describing such connections but does not authorize a historian to collapse these communities into larger peoples. The same applies to the O'ob of the Sierra and the O'odham communities of the desert: related languages are not evidence that a single contemporary government represents both. The national atlas is a valuable starting point for exploring these distinctions, but it is organized around linguistic categories and must be supplemented by community accounts. [17], [18]

The Kumeyaay: A Homeland Divided

The Kumeyaay homeland extends from the coast and valleys around present-day San Diego, through the mountains and into the desert edges of southern California, and south into northern Baja California. Their languages belong to the Yuman family and are sometimes described as a dialect continuum, and scholars and communities use several overlapping names. Kumeyaay is widely used as an inclusive term, Ipai and Tipai refer to northern and southern groupings, and Kumiai is commonly used in Baja California for communities of the southern group. The older term "Diegueño," derived from the mission of San Diego, is an exonym. Distinctions among these names reflect both linguistic variation and the history of colonial and national categories, and they should not be read as proof of separate peoples or as a settled classification.

Environment and Community

Kumeyaay people lived in extensive environments: coastal lagoons and shores, river valleys, oak woodlands, chaparral slopes, mountain pine and oak country, and the desert margin. Communities, often organized around patrilineal clans or lineages with leaders who held authority within specific territories, moved seasonally to harvest acorns, agave, grasses, shellfish, game, and other resources, and they traded with neighbors. Knowledge of plants, water sources, and routes allowed people to use these environments through the year, and ceremonies, including mourning ceremonies and gatherings that drew people from many communities, reinforced relationships.

Missions, Dispossession, and the Boundary

In 1769 the Spanish established the first mission of Alta California at San Diego, and the mission system extended into Kumeyaay country. In 1775 Kumeyaay people attacked and burned the mission of San Diego de Alcalá in a major revolt, which is among the earliest large-scale Indigenous uprisings against the California missions. The mission period brought disease, labor demands, and disruption, though many Kumeyaay communities remained in the interior beyond direct mission control. Mexican independence, secularization of the missions, and the rancho economy brought new pressures, and after the U.S.–Mexico War and the California Gold Rush, American settlement and violence devastated Native communities in California, as Chapter 70 describes.

The boundary of 1848 placed the coast around San Diego in the United States and the peninsula to the south in Mexico, and it ran through Kumeyaay country. Over the following decades federal executive orders, legislation and land acquisitions established reservations for Kumeyaay communities in San Diego County, often on small and marginal parcels, and the lands were later affected by federal policy on allotment and termination, and then by a revival of tribal government in the late twentieth century. Several Kumeyaay bands today have federally recognized governments, and some operate economic enterprises. In Baja California, Kumiai and related communities hold land under Mexican arrangements, including communal land, and live in small communities in the mountains and valleys of the northern peninsula and in towns and cities. The two sides differ in legal status, resources, and relations to government.

Despite the divergences, Kumeyaay people maintain relationships across the line. Families have kin on both sides, communities share songs and ceremonies, and cultural and language programs, such as those that bring together speakers from different communities, work across the boundary. Kumeyaay leaders have also been involved in advocacy about the protection of sacred sites and ancestral places in the border region and about the effects of border infrastructure on those places. Detailed treatment of border infrastructure and crossing rights belongs to Chapter 142.

The federal reservation history offers a concrete illustration of the difference between territorial recognition and territorial restoration. Grant's December 1875 executive order set aside specific parcels in San Diego County, including lands associated with Sycuan, Capitan Grande and Santa Ysabel. It did not recognize the full historical extent of Kumeyaay territory. Moreover, the legal history of each community differed; the same order cannot stand for the establishment history of every reservation. Indigenous advocacy, including petitions and efforts to defend access to land, was important to the creation and retention of these holdings. The Viejas Band's own historical account places the order in the context of earlier land loss and the failure of the United States to ratify the California treaties. [21], [20], [22], [19]

In the twentieth century another change illustrates the continuing vulnerability of even recognized lands. Reservoir development in the Capitan Grande area displaced people who subsequently established homes at Barona and Viejas. The history of those communities thus includes both nineteenth-century reservation policy and twentieth-century relocation. To describe these events as the simple survival of an ancient reservation system would obscure the choices and hardships through which communities rebuilt their institutional base. The history of Jamul, involving a small land base and its own institutional development, further demonstrates that different Kumeyaay governments emerged through distinct processes. [21], [20], [22], [19]

South of the international line, communities such as Juntas de Nejí and San José de la Zorra retain ties to particular places in northern Baja California. INPI records also identify Kumiai families living in settlements with other populations. The relationship between the geographic homeland and the actual distribution of people is therefore not reducible to a single cluster of villages. Comparative study must distinguish the sovereign tribal governments recognized in the United States from Mexican Indigenous communities operating through different institutions. A relationship of language, kinship or ceremonial exchange does not automatically confer the same legal membership or access to services in each polity. It nevertheless remains an important historical relationship in its own right. [21], [20], [22], [19]

The Tohono O'odham: Desert Society and Territorial Continuity

Tohono O'odham means "Desert People." The O'odham, including the Tohono O'odham, Akimel O'odham (River People or Pima), Hia-Ced O'odham, and other related communities, speak related languages of the Uto-Aztecan family and share a cultural world across the Sonoran Desert of southern Arizona and northern Sonora. The formerly used outsider name "Papago" is no longer used by the Nation. The historical O'odham historical region was a world of desert agriculture, water management, seasonal mobility, and ceremony.

Desert Agriculture and Seasonal Life

Rainfall in the Sonoran Desert is scarce and uneven, and O'odham farmers developed methods of using seasonal runoff, directing floodwater from washes into fields at the mouths of arroyos, a technique often described by the O'odham term ak-chin. Villages shifted seasonally. In summer, families lived in field villages near the arroyo mouths where crops were planted after the monsoon rains began, and in winter they moved to mountain villages near springs and wells. Harvesting the fruit of the saguaro cactus in early summer was a major ceremonial and economic event, and communities also gathered mesquite, cholla buds, and agave, hunted, and traded. Water was central to settlement: springs and tanks, including those at places such as Quitobaquito Springs, were vital and carried sacred significance.

Communities were organized around villages, with village leadership and ceremonial specialists, and kin ties linked villages across large distances. Ceremonial geography included sacred mountains and other places that structured the people's relationship with the land, and ritual calendars connected communities. This world was not a single political unit, and the O'odham lacked a centralized government before colonial rule, but they were linked by language, kinship, and shared practice.

Colonial Encounters and the Boundary

The O'odham met Spanish missionaries in the late seventeenth century, when Jesuit missionaries including Eusebio Francisco Kino established missions in Pimería Alta. The O'odham experienced mission work, labor demands, and disease, and they participated in the Pima revolt of 1751. Mexican independence changed the framework of authority, and in 1853 the Gadsden Purchase divided the O'odham homeland. The line left some communities and sacred places in the United States and others in Mexico.

In the United States, the Nation dates the establishment of its main reservation to 1917; other reservation lands have distinct histories. The Nation’s constitutional chronology identifies its original constitution in 1937 and its new constitution and name in 1986. In the twentieth century, the Tohono O'odham Nation organized a constitutional government, and today it is a federally recognized tribe with its own legislature, courts, and a large land base in southern Arizona, which includes approximately 62 miles of the international boundary, as reported by the Nation. On the Mexican side, O'odham communities in Sonora live in settlements such as those near Sonoyta and Caborca, and O'odham in Mexico have not had the same degree of government recognition, land base, or institutional resources, and their communities are small and in some cases face the loss of language and land. The Tohono O'odham Nation and communities in Sonora do not share a single governmental arrangement, and the O'odham in Mexico are subject to Mexican municipal, state, and agrarian institutions.

Relationships across the boundary have continued. People visit relatives, take part in ceremonies and pilgrimages, and gather resources on both sides, and for many decades crossing was relatively simple. As border enforcement intensified, especially from the late twentieth century, the Nation has contended with the effects of surveillance, vehicle barriers, a border wall, enforcement activity, and the deaths of migrants in the desert on its lands, and has negotiated and at times contested federal border policy. Members of the Nation have also reported difficulties in crossing and in bringing Mexican relatives to ceremonies. These administrative and legal questions will be taken up in Chapter 142.

The Nation's contemporary territorial structure is not identical to the wider O'odham homeland. Its main reservation, smaller reservation areas, and neighboring O'odham governments represent different institutional histories. For example, the Akimel O'odham communities of the Gila River region cannot simply be described as subordinate districts of the Tohono O'odham Nation. The latter's own account identifies approximately 62 miles of its lands along the international boundary. This is a measure of the length of the present reservation boundary adjoining Mexico, not a measurement of the whole Indigenous homeland, the extent of O'odham presence in Mexico or the length of all culturally significant routes. [23]

The modern political significance of desert water management follows from the contrast between seasonal use and fixed administrative boundaries. A community could historically make use of fields, springs, gathering places and temporary settlements in several parts of the desert. The availability of a legally titled parcel does not ensure the continued availability of all those resources. Roads, groundwater withdrawal, private property, protected areas and the international boundary can each affect access. This is why the Nation's public statements on its territorial relationships include environmental and cultural concerns as well as questions of governmental jurisdiction. A source published by the tribal government is especially useful for understanding its declared institutional position, though it is not a substitute for the accounts of individual families or communities in Sonora. [23]

In this comparison, recognition and kinship are independent dimensions. A person with relatives on both sides of the border may have an important role in community life without automatically possessing the same legal status on both sides. The ability to travel, to use a public service or to hold an elected position may be governed by different rules. The continuing existence of the O'odham as a people cannot be inferred solely from any of those administrative categories. It is sustained through the relationships that people maintain and the institutions through which they govern their own affairs. [23]

The Nation’s own constitutional history dates the adoption of the original Papago tribal constitution to 1937 and the adoption of a new constitution and name to 1986. These are specific moments of institutional change, not dates marking the creation of the O’odham people. The distinction between earlier community authority and later constitutional government is important for interpreting the history of the Nation. [34], [35]

The Kickapoo: Migration, Autonomy and Transnational Community

The Kickapoo case differs fundamentally from the Tohono O'odham and Kumeyaay. The boundary was not drawn through an old Kickapoo homeland. The Kickapoo are an Algonquian-speaking people whose historical homelands lay in the Great Lakes region, in present-day Michigan and Wisconsin, and whose movements over the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries took them through Illinois, Indiana, and Ohio and later across the Mississippi into Missouri, Kansas, and Texas. Their history of movement belongs to the broader story of Indigenous peoples in the Great Lakes and the interior, examined in Chapters 45 through 47 and 61 through 63, and the pressures of treaty cession, warfare, and settler expansion drove their displacement.

In the early nineteenth century, Kickapoo communities were divided among groups that accepted removal and groups that resisted it, and different bands settled in different regions. Some groups moved south into Texas and then, in the middle of the nineteenth century, into northern Mexico, where Mexican authorities, concerned with frontier defense and raiding, granted them land and, in return, expected military service on the frontier. After an initial land arrangement around 1850, an exchange of land in 1852 secured a permanent Kickapoo land base at El Nacimiento near Múzquiz, Coahuila. Black Seminole people, known in the region as Mascogos, also settled in the area, reflecting other displacements and alliances.

The Kickapoo in Mexico were not isolated from the United States. In 1873 U.S. military forces crossed the border and attacked Kickapoo villages at Remolino, an episode that reflects the pressure of U.S. frontier policy on communities that sheltered in Mexico, and U.S. officials pressed many Kickapoo to return to Indian Territory. Some did, and today there are Kickapoo communities in Oklahoma and Kansas, with governments recognized by the United States. A group of Kickapoo stayed in or returned to Coahuila, and the El Nacimiento community remained an important center. The resulting dispersion produced separate political institutions and continuing family relationships.

The Kickapoo of Coahuila and the Kickapoo of Texas, who are associated with a reservation near Eagle Pass, maintain close ties. Historically, Kickapoo families traveled seasonally between Mexico and the United States, working in agriculture and other seasonal employment in the United States and returning to their communities in Mexico for ceremonial and family obligations. Congress enacted the Texas Band of Kickapoo Act on January 8, 1983 (Public Law 97-429) to recognize the Texas Band of Kickapoo and to address the status of Kickapoo who were crossing the border and holding connections on both sides. The Kickapoo maintain a strong ceremonial and kinship life, and they maintain the use of the Kickapoo language, an important feature of their continuity. Their political independence, their reputation for resistance to assimilation, and the strength of their cultural institutions are often noted, but outsiders' characterizations should be balanced with Kickapoo accounts.

The Mexican land arrangement should be understood in the political circumstances of the mid-nineteenth century. Kickapoo groups entering northern Mexico sought distance from the pressures of American removal and an opportunity to sustain their own political and economic life. Mexican authorities, in turn, sought allies and security in a frontier where their own ability to control territory was limited. The 1852 exchange of an earlier grant for land at El Nacimiento produced a more durable settlement. It illustrates how migration could be a political strategy rather than simply a consequence of dispossession. The land agreement was made by people confronting constrained alternatives; the fact that it involved negotiation does not mean that previous displacement had been voluntary. [24], [25]

El Nacimiento became a center for community life, while other Kickapoo communities developed distinct relationships with the United States. The Mascogos in the same region have their own history, involving Black Seminole migration and alliances. Their geographical proximity to the Kickapoo is evidence of the varied migrations that made northern Coahuila a borderlands society, not evidence that the two communities were one political group. Comparative history should preserve that distinction while recognizing that Mexican frontier policy offered opportunities for communities seeking refuge from slavery, dispossession and violence elsewhere. [24], [25]

The Texas Band of Kickapoo Act was enacted in 1983. It established a statutory framework for the Band's relationship with the federal government, including an enrollment process, provision for services and provisions concerning the status and mobility of qualifying members. Congress distinguished the Band from the Kickapoo Tribe in Oklahoma while recognizing the relationship between them. The act's special provisions concerning eligible members must not be generalized to all Kickapoo people. Neither tribal membership nor a family relationship with El Nacimiento automatically grants dual national citizenship. The detailed legal questions of admission and crossing are reserved for Chapter 142; here the law is significant because it illustrates how a state eventually adapted its administrative categories to a community whose historical circumstances did not fit the ordinary categories. [24], [25]

The modern community is sustained through more than residence on one parcel. Work, family obligations, political administration and community membership connect places such as Eagle Pass and El Nacimiento, while separate Kickapoo governments exist elsewhere. A contemporary study must ask which governmental institution it is describing and which people its evidence concerns. The source on the history of migration and the federal statute answer different questions: the former describes the development of a transnational community, while the latter defines rights and responsibilities within one legal system. Together they demonstrate why the Kickapoo case is distinct from the history of a homeland divided in place. [24], [25]

Historical Vocabulary
Transnational Community

Definition. A transnational community is a community whose members, institutions, and relationships extend across the boundaries of more than one state.

Historical context. In the borderlands, transnational Indigenous communities arose through different processes. Some, like the Tohono O'odham and Kumeyaay, were divided in place when a boundary was drawn through a homeland. Others, like the Kickapoo, became transnational through migration and political choice. Still others, like the Yaqui in Arizona, became transnational through flight and displacement.

Why it matters. The term helps students see that the same outcome, a community on both sides of the line, can result from very different historical routes, with different implications for territory, legal claims, and identity.

Potential misunderstanding. "Transnational" does not mean "stateless" or "rootless." Transnational Indigenous communities are typically deeply rooted in particular places, and they sustain relationships across states by means of kinship, ceremony, and organization.

Section 141.12

Apache and Other Transboundary Indigenous Histories

Apache peoples, whose general history is treated in Chapter 51, lived across a wide area of the Southwest and northern Mexico, including southern Arizona, southwestern New Mexico, and the Sierra Madre of Sonora and Chihuahua. Chiricahua Apache groups in particular moved across what became the international boundary, and their economy involved hunting, gathering, farming in some groups, trade, and raiding. Spanish and Mexican officials regarded Apache power as a major obstacle to settlement in northern Mexico, and the northern Mexican states waged war against Apache communities, including through bounty policies.

The 1848 and 1853–1854 agreements did not immediately end Apache movement across the area. Apache groups continued to move between Mexico and the United States, using the mountains and deserts as bases and refuges, and U.S. and Mexican military forces eventually concluded arrangements to allow pursuit of Apache groups across the boundary. The narrative of the campaigns, treated in Chapters 79 and 80, ended with the surrender of Chiricahua leaders in 1886 and the imprisonment of Chiricahua people as prisoners of war for more than two decades. The effect on Apache communities in Mexico was severe, and today Apache descendants live in the United States, with the Chiricahua and Mescalero in New Mexico and other Apache nations elsewhere, and the history of Apache communities in northern Mexico is less well documented and in some places remembered through family and community accounts. Here the point is that the boundary did not immediately terminate Apache territorial relationships; it became part of the political geography of conflict and, ultimately, of the colonial and military campaigns that ended Apache independence.

The Cucapá, or Cocopah, are another people whose homeland was divided. Their territory lay along the lower Colorado River and in the delta, and the boundary placed some communities in what became Arizona, where the Cocopah Tribe is federally recognized, and others in Mexico, in Baja California and Sonora. The Colorado River itself changed profoundly in the twentieth century: dams and diversions upstream drastically reduced flows reaching the delta, and the river that had supported floodplain farming and fishing no longer reached the sea in many years. Cucapá in Mexico have also been affected by the establishment of protected areas in the delta and the Gulf, and by regulations on fishing that have limited traditional fishing practices, and they have mobilized for the right to fish and to maintain their territory. Section 141.13 returns to the Cucapá in discussing water and resources.

The experiences of these peoples show that transboundary Indigenous communities came about through different processes: division of a homeland, migration, flight, forced displacement, and the transformation of a river. They also differ in whether the communities on each side have recognized governments, land, and resources.

The Cucapá example offers a contrasting form of territorial fragmentation. Their historical homeland includes the lower Colorado River and delta, where seasonal floods supported agriculture and fishing. The development of upstream storage and diversions transformed the ecology on which this economy depended. The INPI atlas records contemporary communities in both the river and delta region and describes the increasing importance of fishing in Mexico after the decline of floodplain agriculture. Students should not interpret that shift as the end of territorial continuity. It demonstrates that a community may maintain its homeland while the underlying resource system changes beyond its direct control. [26], [1], [2]

The relevant political geography includes two national governments, water-management institutions at multiple levels and distinct Indigenous authorities. A decision about upstream irrigation can have effects far from the location where it was made. A fishing regulation can be administered locally but reflect national environmental policy. The United States and Mexico have different legal arrangements for recognizing Indigenous governments and regulating fisheries. As a result, closely related communities may encounter the same environmental transformation through quite different administrative procedures. Their histories should be studied together without treating their political status as interchangeable. [26], [1], [2]

Apache histories similarly require distinction among peoples, bands and particular governments. The historical reach of Apache communities into northern Mexico should not be confused with a claim that a single contemporary Apache government administers that territory. The nineteenth-century treaties discussed in Section 141.4 reflect the states' objectives, but their language is not a neutral description of Indigenous communities. A comparison with Cucapá river history thus reveals two different processes: territorial disruption through warfare and removal, and disruption through the reallocation of ecological resources. Both could operate together, but neither is sufficient to explain every community's experience. [26], [1], [2]

Section 141.13

Indigenous Land Tenure, Water and Natural Resources

Land and water are the material basis of territorial continuity, and Indigenous communities on either side of the boundary hold them under different legal arrangements. Understanding those arrangements is essential to understanding the circumstances in which communities maintain their relationships to their homelands. It is also necessary to distinguish four things that are often conflated: legal ownership, territorial jurisdiction, customary use, and historical attachment. A community may hold legal title to land it does not control, may use land it does not own, may exercise jurisdiction over land held in trust by another government, or may remain attached to places from which it has been legally excluded.

Historical Vocabulary
Ejido

Definition. An ejido is a form of landholding with colonial antecedents and a modern legal structure developed after the Revolution, in which land is granted by the state to a community of rural people (ejidatarios), who hold rights of use and, historically, were restricted in selling or dividing the land. An ejido typically has an assembly of members and elected officers.

Historical context. The Constitution of 1917 provided the basis for agrarian reform, and during the 1920s and 1930s, and especially under the presidency of Lázaro Cárdenas, millions of acres of land were distributed through ejidos. Reforms in the early 1990s changed the legal framework, enabling ejidatarios in some circumstances to hold individual parcels and, under conditions, to rent or sell them. Under Articles 81–83 of the Ley Agraria, full private title is an optional procedure subject to statutory conditions; it is not automatic.

Why it matters. For some Indigenous communities, ejidos became the legal form through which land was held. In others, communities hold land as "comunidades agrarias" under distinct procedures, and some Indigenous territories are held as restituted communal lands.

Potential misunderstanding. An ejido is not necessarily an "Indigenous" land form. Many ejidos are composed of non-Indigenous peasants, and many Indigenous communities do not hold ejidos. The ejido is a state-created legal category that Indigenous communities have used, adapted, or been placed within, and it does not equal Indigenous territory in the community's own understanding.

Mexico and the United States Compared

In Mexico, Indigenous communities may hold land as ejidos, as agrarian communities with communal land titles, or as private property or other forms, and the legal arrangements affect how land can be used, divided, and defended. In the United States, Indigenous land is most commonly held in trust by the federal government for tribes or individuals, or held in fee simple by the tribe or by individuals, and tribal governments exercise jurisdiction over reservation lands in ways shaped by federal law. The two systems differ in the source of rights (a state grant of land in the Mexican ejido case, and in the U.S. case a mixture of treaty, statute, and executive action), in the role of the community's own government, and in the relationship between landholding and political authority. In the United States, reservation status is tied to tribal government and jurisdiction. In Mexico, communal land does not by itself confer a distinct Indigenous government with legal authority outside the municipal and state framework, though community assemblies and traditional authorities may exercise considerable informal and, in some cases, recognized authority.

Water

Water is the most contested resource in the region. The Yaqui River basin is a clear case. The river's waters feed irrigated agriculture in the valley and have been regulated by dams since the mid-twentieth century. The Yaqui have claimed rights to the waters and to the river as part of their territory, and conflicts over diversions for cities and commercial agriculture have been the subject of legal and political struggles, as noted in Section 141.5. Rarámuri communities face different pressures: forests, springs, and mining concessions, and the contest over the use of communal land and timber. On the lower Colorado River, the Cucapá have watched the river's flow toward the delta diminish because of upstream dams and diversions in the United States and Mexico, regulated by the international arrangements of the twentieth century, and their fishing and farming have been constrained by environmental protections and regulations. The O'odham are affected by groundwater pumping and the vulnerability of springs. Chapter 120 examines water rights in the United States; here the point is that Indigenous communities in northern Mexico confront similar structural problems under different legal systems.

Mining, forestry, tourism, and energy development add further pressures. Concessions granted by the state may allow extractive activity on land that communities regard as their territory, and the question of whether and how communities must be consulted has been the subject of legal and political debate in Mexico. Consultation and environmental authorization are distinct legal questions, as the Yaqui aqueduct litigation demonstrates.

The history of the ejido requires care with terminology. Earlier Iberian and colonial ejidos were associated with lands available for communal purposes; colonial Indigenous towns also held lands under other arrangements, including the fundo legal. The postrevolutionary agrarian system reworked this inheritance through state-administered redistribution. It is therefore not correct to identify every Indigenous communal territory as an ejido, nor to assume that the Revolution invented the term. Mexican agrarian law distinguishes ejidos from recognized agrarian communities. The modern ejido itself possesses legal personality and a patrimony, and its assembly makes decisions on matters identified in the agrarian law. Individual parcels, settlement land and common-use land have different functions and legal rules. A person can belong to an Indigenous community without holding rights as an ejidatario, and an ejidatario need not belong to the Indigenous people historically associated with the land. [27], [7], [8], [6], [10] [33]

The 1992 reform to Article 27 and the accompanying Ley Agraria modified the legal framework. The provisions on dominio pleno permit eligible parcel holders, following an authorized procedure, to acquire full individual title. They do not require all ejidos to dissolve or automatically convert all shared land to private property. This distinction matters for the economic history of the borderlands because the effect of reform depends on local decisions, land markets, access to credit and the value of the resources involved. A collective decision to protect common pasture, for instance, is not the same legal question as the disposition of an individually assigned agricultural parcel. An account of dispossession must identify the actual mechanism by which land was lost or control changed. [27], [7], [8], [6], [10]

The Yaqui River illustrates the difference between land rights and water administration. The 1940 resolution provided for irrigation using up to half of the water stored in the La Angostura reservoir in each agricultural year; that formulation is more specific than an unrestricted right to half of all flow in the river under every condition. In the dispute over the Independencia aqueduct, the Supreme Court's First Chamber in 2013 affirmed relief for members of the Yaqui community of Vícam. The judgment required the environmental authorities to provide the community a hearing through recognized representatives in connection with authorization. It should not be read as a final award of every disputed water claim or an unconditional permanent prohibition on the aqueduct. The case demonstrates the significance of a procedural right to consultation and the separate problem of securing effective material access to water. [27], [7], [8], [6], [10]

The 2021 decree establishing Irrigation District 018 created a new institutional framework associated with the eight Yaqui pueblos. Other decrees of 2021 and 2024 addressed restitution and compensation of land. Their historical significance is that the state responded to demands over territory and administration through legal instruments that could support greater community control. Their publication does not establish that every disputed claim was settled, or that all infrastructure and administrative arrangements were immediately implemented. Distinguishing an agreement, a legal decree, physical delivery of resources and practical operation is essential to evaluating a justice program historically. [27], [7], [8], [6], [10]

Section 141.14

Indigenous Government, Community Authority and Legal Recognition

Indigenous communities govern themselves through institutions that vary widely. In many communities of northern Mexico, authority rests with traditional authorities, such as the Yaqui pueblo governments, with their civil, military, and ceremonial officers, Rarámuri governors and community officers, and the cabildo-like structures of Mayo communities, who are chosen by community custom and are accountable to the community. Community assemblies, in which members discuss and decide matters of common concern, are central, and ceremonial leaders hold authority over ritual affairs. Some communities also have elected officials who interact with municipal and state governments. The relations among these institutions are not always harmonious, and communities sometimes dispute who speaks for them.

In the United States, tribal governments are typically organized under constitutions and ordinances and are recognized by the federal government in a government-to-government relationship, as discussed in Chapters 97, 114, and 118. The Tohono O'odham Nation, the Pascua Yaqui Tribe, the Kumeyaay bands, the Cocopah Tribe, and the Kickapoo tribal governments are examples. Federal recognition brings legal standing, access to certain federal programs, and the exercise of defined tribal jurisdiction, but it also brings federal oversight and legal limits.

In Mexico, the Constitution recognizes the composition of the nation as pluriethnic, based on its Indigenous peoples, and provides for Indigenous peoples' rights to self-determination and autonomy, including the right to apply their own normative systems in the resolution of internal conflicts and to choose their authorities according to their traditional practices, within the constitutional framework. These provisions are the product of reforms beginning in the 1990s and developed in 2001, and later reforms have added to them, as noted in Chapter 1 in relation to a major 2024 reform. The practical significance of constitutional recognition depends on secondary legislation, on the actions of state governments and courts, on resources, and on the capacity of communities to assert their rights. The 2001 reform delegated much to state legislatures and was criticized by many Indigenous organizations as insufficient, and implementation has been uneven. The September 2024 reform expressly recognizes Indigenous peoples and communities as public-law subjects with legal personality and their own patrimony.

The legal frameworks of both countries should not be treated as uniform or as automatically effective. Formal recognition does not automatically produce effective autonomy. A tribe with federal recognition may lack the economic resources to exercise its authority fully, and a Mexican community with constitutional recognition may lack the means or legal remedies to defend its territory. Conversely, communities without formal recognition may maintain strong, effective self-government in practice. Recognition is one dimension of political authority, and it is analytically separate from legitimacy within the community, from capacity, and from external respect.

The Mexican constitutional changes of 1992, 2001 and 2024 must be distinguished. The 1992 reform recognized the pluricultural composition of the nation in Article 4. The 2001 reform developed Indigenous rights through Article 2, recognizing autonomy and self-determination within the constitutional framework. The decree published on September 30, 2024, further changed that framework: Indigenous peoples and communities are recognized as subjects of public law, with legal personality and their own patrimony. Legal personality provides a basis for acting in law as a collective entity; patrimony refers to assets and rights belonging to that entity. Neither concept amounts to recognition as an independent state outside Mexico. Their practical effects depend on the implementation of the constitutional provisions and on specific legal arrangements. [28], [6], [7]

A comparison with the United States should begin with the nature of the institution rather than the number of services it provides. A federally recognized tribal government has a political relationship with the United States that differs from the constitutional status of a Mexican Indigenous community. Its powers are subject to the particular history of the tribe, federal law and relevant judicial decisions. Its land base may include trust land and other holdings under different rules. In Mexico, traditional authorities, Indigenous community institutions and agrarian assemblies can coexist with municipal and state institutions. The authority to decide a cultural or internal governance matter does not necessarily coincide with the authority to administer a water concession or a public road. The 2024 reform is important precisely because it alters the legal position of collective Indigenous institutions rather than simply recognizing individual cultural rights. [28], [6], [7] [37]

The eight Yaqui communities provide a concrete instance of this layered authority. A federal decree dealing with territory identifies the traditional communities and their governments, while a separate irrigation decree concerns a resource-management institution. The two documents address different responsibilities. Students should distinguish the recognized representatives of the pueblos from the officials and entities administering the irrigation system, even when their activities are closely connected. This distinction makes the difference between recognition and governing capacity intelligible: the former can help establish authority, but the latter also requires personnel, infrastructure, money and a workable relationship with other institutions. [28], [6], [7]

Section 141.15

Work, Migration and the Borderlands Economy

Indigenous people in the borderlands participate in many parts of the regional economy. They farm and raise livestock, work as laborers on commercial farms and ranches, fish, make and sell crafts, work in construction, manufacturing, and the maquiladora industries of border cities, take jobs in tourism, health care, education, and government, and run businesses. The tribal governments of the United States operate enterprises and employ community members, and Indigenous people in Mexico work in the public and private sectors. Employment is shaped by differences in infrastructure, schooling, language, discrimination, and access to credit and capital, which vary between the two countries and among communities.

Migration is a central feature of work. Indigenous people from the Sierra and from rural valleys move seasonally to commercial agricultural regions in Sonora, Sinaloa, and Baja California, and some move on to the United States for work. Rarámuri families migrate to cities such as Chihuahua City and Ciudad Juárez, sometimes working in informal commerce. Yaqui and Mayo people move for agricultural and urban work. Oaxacan Indigenous migrants, such as Mixtec and Triqui people, have for decades traveled to northern Mexican and U.S. farms. Remittances sent by relatives who work away are an important part of household economies, and money may flow in both directions as families support one another. Seasonal migration shapes family life: children may be left in the care of grandparents, and ceremonial and agricultural calendars are adapted to the schedules of work.

These patterns show that economic participation and Indigenous community membership are not opposed. A person who works in a factory or on a distant farm can remain an active member of a community, holding offices, contributing to ceremonies, and sending money home, and many people have done so across generations. The historical record also shows hardship: labor exploitation, dangerous working conditions, discrimination, and the difficulty of maintaining family and language across distance. Those who migrate may encounter hostility and may find that outsiders define them by stereotypes.

Commercial agricultural development in Sonora and Sinaloa provides a useful way to study the regional labor economy. Irrigation changed the scale and organization of production, making it possible for large farms to employ workers from nearby towns and more distant communities. Some workers maintained access to household plots while also earning wages; others depended more heavily on employment in the commercial sector. Ownership of irrigated land, ownership of agricultural equipment and employment on a farm are distinct economic positions. A person participating in the same regional industry as a large landowner may have little influence over the allocation of water or the terms of employment. Indigenous people have occupied varied positions within this economy, and historical analysis should not assume that they have all experienced it solely as laborers. [13], [15], [24], [12]

The distribution of earnings within a household is another important subject. Work away from a home community may make it possible to purchase food, pay for schooling or contribute to collective obligations. Yet the cost of transportation and housing can reduce the resources actually available to the household. Agricultural work may be seasonal, while family responsibilities continue through the year. The question is not whether wage labor is compatible with Indigenous identity—it plainly can be—but how the timing and conditions of employment affect opportunities to participate in community life. The experience of a Yoreme agricultural household may differ from that of a Rarámuri household combining mountain agriculture and urban employment, or of a Kickapoo family with relationships on both sides of the boundary. [13], [15], [24], [12]

Public institutions can also become important employers and economic participants. The Pascua Yaqui Tribe's development of a health program following federal recognition provides an example of service provision as institutional capacity. Such a program requires administration, trained staff and relationships with other providers. Its significance cannot be measured solely as commercial profit: it serves members and builds governmental capability. A community without similar access to funding or legal standing may sustain comparable responsibilities through other arrangements. These differences help explain why the chapter's framework treats economic capacity and political authority as related but distinct dimensions. [13], [15], [24], [12]

Section 141.16

Language, Religion and Ceremonial Continuity

Languages carry history, ceremony, and community knowledge. Indigenous languages of the borderlands include Yaqui and Mayo (Cahitan), Rarámuri, Guarijío, O'odham, Kumeyaay, Cucapá, Comcaac, Kickapoo, Apache, and many others. They are in differing conditions. Some, such as Rarámuri, are widely spoken in their communities, while others, such as Pima Bajo or some of the Baja California languages, have very few speakers, and the reasons include schooling in Spanish or English, migration, discrimination, and the pressure of economic life. Communities have responded with language programs, recording and documentation efforts, bilingual education, children's materials, and digital tools. Mexico has legislation recognizing Indigenous languages as national languages, and the United States has legislation supporting Native American language preservation, but the extent of practical support varies, and community-led efforts are often central.

Religion in the borderlands cannot be summarized as "Catholic" or "traditional." Many communities practice forms of Catholicism that incorporate Indigenous ceremonial structures, and Lenten and Holy Week observances among the Yaqui, Mayo, and Rarámuri, and fiestas honoring patron saints, are important community events. The relationship between Catholic forms and Indigenous ceremonial traditions has often been described as syncretism, a combination of two traditions. Many scholars and Indigenous people caution that this term can imply a simple blend, and that it can mislead by suggesting that the elements are separable or that the result is not authentically Indigenous. In many communities, ceremonies are understood as part of the people's own tradition, and the meanings of ritual are complex, plural, and sometimes debated within the community. Some communities also include Protestant and Evangelical members, and religious pluralism sometimes produces tension over community obligations.

Ceremonial cycles organize the year and structure obligations among members. Participating in them requires knowledge, training, and commitment, and they are often passed from elders to young people. Some ceremonial knowledge is public and some is restricted to specific people or contexts. This textbook does not disclose restricted ceremonial knowledge, and students who pursue further study should respect community protocols for access.

Migration and schooling place pressure on the transmission of language and ceremony. Children who grow up in cities or attend Spanish- or English-language schools may have limited exposure to their languages. Yet communities also use migration and technology to sustain culture: families return for ceremonies, and recordings, social media, and distance education connect people who are far from home. The next generation's relationship with language and ceremony may differ from that of their elders, and communities themselves debate how to adapt.

An important institutional development in Mexico was the General Law of Linguistic Rights of Indigenous Peoples, published in 2003. The law recognizes Indigenous languages and Spanish as national languages with equal legal validity in the relevant contexts. It also provided the basis for INALI, the National Institute of Indigenous Languages. This changed the legal framework for the use of Indigenous languages in public services and education. A declaration of rights, however, is not the same as the availability of teachers, interpreters or teaching materials. For dispersed communities the distance to a school, the language of its staff and the continuity of instruction can matter as much as the formal curriculum. [29], [18]

The national language atlas also helps distinguish a language's distribution from a simple political map. Kumiai or Tipai speakers are associated with several localities in northern Baja California, while Yoreme speakers live in numerous agricultural settlements. A language map therefore tells students where linguistic relationships have been recorded, not which government exercises territorial authority or who belongs to an Indigenous nation. It may omit speakers who have moved, who were not counted or who identify their language using another name. Language revitalization is consequently an institutional activity that requires collaboration with speakers and communities, not merely the reproduction of census labels. [29], [18]

Ceremonial and linguistic knowledge also involve questions about authority over information. Community members may choose to share language lessons, oral histories or public festival accounts while restricting other material. The adoption of recordings or digital communication does not change the right of the people concerned to make those decisions. A historian should treat public community-authored materials as an invitation to learn from the information made available, not as authorization to seek access to restricted practices. [29], [18]

Section 141.17

Indigenous Women, Families and Community Reconstruction

Communities in the borderlands have been sustained through the daily work of families and, within them, of women, elders, and young people. During the Yaqui deportations, families separated by force reconstituted ties in new places. During labor migration, grandparents raise grandchildren while parents work elsewhere. When ceremonial calendars require preparation, households contribute food, labor, and money. Language is transmitted in kitchens and fields as well as in schools. These activities are historical work, and they are often poorly documented because official records focus on male officeholders and public events.

It would nonetheless be a mistake to portray Indigenous women only as custodians of tradition. Women are farmers, wage workers, artisans, healers, teachers, health workers, entrepreneurs, tribal and municipal officials, lawyers, and community organizers. Women's roles vary by community and period. In some communities, women hold property and play defined roles in ceremonial life, in others their formal political participation has been limited, and in many, women have pressed for greater representation in community assemblies and governing bodies. Women have been prominent in movements to defend land and water and in organizing language programs, and they have faced particular risks in conflicts, including gender-based violence, a subject that earlier chapters treat for Indigenous communities generally.

Elders hold knowledge of language, ceremony, history, and land, and they play a central role in transmitting it. Young people often move between worlds, navigating schools, work, and community obligations, and they also lead new initiatives: digital media, music, activism, and language learning. Intergenerational relationships involve cooperation and tension, and community reconstruction depends on negotiation among generations about what to keep, adapt, or change.

The institutional history of Indigenous women's organizing in Mexico offers a specific example of this broader agency. The Casas de la Mujer Indígena y Afromexicana are supported through a federal program but are intended as spaces in which Indigenous and Afro-Mexican women organize services and defend rights in culturally appropriate ways. The program's areas include responses to violence, reproductive health and traditional midwifery. Its significance is not that the state created the role of Indigenous women in community welfare; rather, organized women have sought public recognition and resources for work that may otherwise be overlooked. The distinction between a community initiative and the administrative funding framework is important when assessing who makes decisions. [30], [31]

Education presents another aspect of intergenerational institutional reconstruction. In dispersed mountain regions, attendance at school may require travel or residence away from a family settlement. The INPI education-support program includes community student housing and meal arrangements, and its 2024 reporting identifies projects in the Sierra Tarahumara and the Yoreme region. Such facilities may reduce the costs of attendance while raising questions about the relationship between schools, language use and community participation. Families and young people are not merely recipients of educational policy: their decisions about attendance, work, return visits and the transmission of knowledge influence whether institutions meet their needs. [30], [31]

Historical evidence about families requires special care. Government records may count beneficiaries or record a household head but omit unpaid labor, language teaching and informal assistance among relatives. Community histories may describe such responsibilities more fully, while differing in what they choose to make public. Historians should not assume that women's roles are identical in every community, or that the expansion of women's public leadership necessarily entails abandonment of community traditions. The history of collective continuity includes changes in who participates in decision-making and how authority is exercised. It also includes the work of young people who combine local responsibilities with new educational and professional opportunities. [30], [31]

Section 141.18

Indigenous Mobilization and Political Advocacy in Mexico

Indigenous political mobilization in Mexico has deep roots, from colonial petitions and nineteenth-century revolts to participation in the Revolution. In the late twentieth century, Indigenous organizations grew stronger across the country. Mobilization in the south, especially the Zapatista uprising in Chiapas in 1994, drew national and international attention to Indigenous demands for autonomy and rights and influenced debates on constitutional reform. This chapter does not treat that movement in detail, since northern Mexico is its subject, but it is part of the national context in which northern Indigenous organizations operate.

In the north, mobilization has centered on territory, water, consultation about development projects, cultural recognition, language rights, and protection from violence. Yaqui authorities have taken legal action and carried out public protests concerning water, and Rarámuri organizations have pressed claims on forests and land and have sought to defend community members. Comcaac, Cucapá, Guarijío, and Mayo communities have also organized around resource rights and recognition, and Indigenous people have formed organizations in cities. Some communities have used the courts, including claims of constitutional and international rights, and have sought support from nongovernmental organizations, universities, and media. Their relationships with national political movements and parties vary, and Indigenous communities are not politically homogeneous.

Constitutional recognition has been an important framework. Article 2 of the Mexican Constitution defines the nation as pluriethnic and sets out principles regarding Indigenous peoples and communities. Its significance lies in its acknowledgment of Indigenous peoples as existing before the formation of the state, its recognition of rights of self-determination and autonomy exercised within the constitutional framework, and its provisions on territory, the choice of authorities, access to justice, and development. Critics argue that its mechanisms for enforcement are limited. Subsequent reforms, including one in 2024, have changed the legal framework. The primary source window below analyzes the September 2024 constitutional amendment.

Political mobilization can be studied through the institutional process of the justice plans. The federal government established a presidential commission for Yaqui justice in 2020 following discussions begun in 2019. The subsequent plans and decrees addressed land, water and community development. INPI also identifies justice and regional development plans involving the Yoreme, Comcaac, Guarijío, the Indigenous peoples of the Sierra Tarahumara and Yuman peoples in Baja California. These initiatives are not a single uniform settlement of Indigenous claims. Their subjects and agreements differ, reflecting the particular demands of communities. Government reports establish that programs were authorized or supported; they do not by themselves demonstrate that all community objectives were achieved. [9], [6], [8], [28]

This distinction between political mobilization and policy delivery is important. A community may press a claim through a public assembly, a negotiation with officials or litigation. An agreement may then require a decree, a budget appropriation, a transfer of land or the construction and operation of infrastructure. The process creates multiple opportunities for disagreement about representation, scope and implementation. For example, the Yaqui aqueduct litigation concerned the obligations of environmental authorities, while the restitution and irrigation decrees concerned distinct territorial and administrative arrangements. Treating all three as one event would obscure the mechanisms through which the community pursued its demands. [9], [6], [8], [28]

The 2024 constitutional amendment is best read within this history rather than as an isolated announcement. Its recognition of public-law status, legal personality and patrimony provides a more explicit constitutional basis for collective institutions. For students, the principal historical question is what changes when a community is legally acknowledged as an entity with its own assets and rights. The answer requires examining how communities use this status, how public authorities implement it and how conflicts are resolved over time. An amendment can be a significant political achievement without automatically resolving inequalities in land access, administrative resources or protection from violence. The historical record of earlier reforms gives reason to distinguish the significance of formal recognition from the future course of implementation. [9], [6], [8], [28]

Section 141.19

Reconstructing Indigenous Communities Across the Borderlands

Indigenous peoples maintain relationships across fragmented territories by many means. Kinship is the foundation: people marry across the line, send children to live with relatives, and attend funerals and festivals. Ceremonial participation draws people to home communities. Language programs link speakers. Community organizations, cultural centers, and political alliances operate across jurisdictions, and communications technology, including telephones, social media, and video, allows daily contact over distance. These practices are not new: they build on older patterns of movement and exchange.

Students must distinguish among four things that are often confused: historical migration, divided homelands, contemporary mobility, and formal political citizenship. A people may be divided by a boundary drawn through its homeland, as the Tohono O'odham and Kumeyaay were, without any member having migrated. A people may have arrived by migration, as the Kickapoo did. People may move for work in the present, as many Rarámuri, Yaqui, and Mayo do. And citizenship, the formal political membership of a state or tribal nation, may not match any of these. National citizenship and tribal citizenship are distinct legal statuses; their relationship depends on the particular law and the person’s circumstances. Chapter 144 addresses citizenship and enrollment, and Chapter 142 addresses border-crossing law.

Four regional cases help explain the different meanings of a transboundary community. The Yaqui case involves the growth of Arizona communities through work, flight and forced displacement from Sonora, followed by the reconstruction of governmental and ceremonial institutions. The Tohono O'odham case involves a boundary established across a longstanding desert homeland; the Nation's present reservation occupies only part of the wider historical region. The Kumeyaay case also involves a divided homeland, but its institutional geography includes numerous separately recognized governments in California and distinct communities in northern Baja California. The Kickapoo case involves a migration from the interior of the continent that established a durable homeland in northern Mexico and subsequently developed close relationships with Texas. All four histories have produced cross-border ties, but the processes are not interchangeable. [11], [23], [19], [24], [25]

There are also important differences among the institutions through which continuity is maintained. Yaqui pueblo governments and the Pascua Yaqui Tribe operate within different legal frameworks, even as family and ceremonial relationships connect their communities. Kumeyaay governments and communities may cooperate on matters of cultural heritage without being a single administrative organization. The Tohono O'odham Nation has publicly asserted the importance of its ancestral territory in discussions of the border, while O'odham communities in Sonora have their own institutional circumstances. The Kickapoo statutory arrangements address a particular Band, not every person who identifies as Kickapoo. These examples require students to keep cultural relationships, governing authority and state citizenship separate when making comparisons. [11], [23], [19], [24], [25]

Reconstruction also requires material resources. Community gatherings depend on access to meeting places, time and transportation. Language programs depend on speakers, learners and the means to support their work. Public advocacy depends on representatives, information and institutional capacity. Communication technology can reduce some costs of maintaining contact, but it cannot replace land, water or the ability to gather in person. Historical continuity is neither an entirely symbolic relationship nor the mere possession of a legal title. It is a continuing process through which people sustain relationships and make decisions under changing material and political conditions. [11], [23], [19], [24], [25]

Finally, these networks must be examined at an appropriate scale. Some relationships are maintained by individuals and families; some by community organizations; some by formally constituted governments. Evidence about one level cannot automatically establish activity at another. A student's comparison of two community websites, for example, may document institutional programs but say little about the experience of a household with members living in different jurisdictions. A fuller account combines the public evidence with community-authorized histories and recognizes the limits of the available sources. The next chapter will examine the laws and administrative practices that can facilitate or constrain such relationships. [11], [23], [19], [24], [25]

Section 141.20

Indigenous Mexico and the Continental Meaning of Sovereignty

The histories examined in this chapter point to a conclusion that is central to the textbook as a whole: Indigenous political and cultural continuity cannot be understood only through recognition by modern nation-states. The Yaqui pueblo governments have colonial-era roots, while the O’odham ceremonial geography and Kumeyaay kin ties draw on older histories; Rarámuri community assemblies and Kickapoo networks have evolved across colonial and modern periods, and in some cases they operate largely outside the formal categories of those states. At the same time, state recognition has real consequences for land, resources, legal standing, and protection, and Indigenous communities pursue it for good reasons.

Using the Six-Dimension Framework, the chapter can be summarized comparatively. Territorial autonomy ranges from the large land base of the Tohono O'odham Nation to the small, contested lands of other communities. Institutional capacity differs between tribal governments with federal recognition and communities relying on assemblies within municipal systems. Community authority is often strong in practice where legal recognition is weak. Economic participation involves wage labor and migration for most. Cultural reproduction depends on language and ceremonial transmission under pressure. Transboundary relationships are sustained by kinship and ceremony. These dimensions interact, and strength in one does not guarantee strength in others.

The circumstances under which Indigenous communities maintain these relationships are unequal. Communities differ in political recognition, economic resources, exposure to violence, and legal protection, and the differences correspond in part to the legal systems of the two states and in part to histories of dispossession. The chapter does not suggest that all communities enjoy equal sovereignty, nor that formal recognition is irrelevant. It suggests that sovereignty in the borderlands is exercised through many forms of authority, some recognized and some not.

This chapter prepares for those that follow. Chapter 142 turns to the international boundary as a political, legal, and administrative institution, including border-crossing rights, enforcement, and customs, and to the political consequences of imposed boundaries. Chapter 143 examines international Indigenous organization, and Chapter 148 considers evolving relationships between Indigenous nations and the governments of North America. The northern borderlands are an especially clear case of the continental argument made in Chapter 1: Indigenous nations are not remnants of an earlier North America but continuing political and cultural communities.

The region therefore requires a conception of historical continuity that allows institutions to change. The 1940 Yaqui restitution resolution, the 1978 recognition of the Pascua Yaqui Tribe, the 1983 Kickapoo statute and the 2024 Mexican constitutional reform were different kinds of political event. None created the Indigenous people to whom it applied. Each nonetheless altered the legal environment in which particular communities could pursue their objectives. By comparing them, students can distinguish recognition of existing political communities from the administrative creation or reorganization of particular institutions. The Six-Dimension Framework is useful when applied in this limited way: it encourages questions about territorial access, authority, livelihoods, language, kinship and transboundary relations without presuming that these dimensions have equal weight for every people. The answer to what continuity means belongs to the communities themselves. [6], [11], [25], [28]

Historical Debate

Can Indigenous Nationhood Be Adequately Understood Within the Territorial Framework of Modern States?

The question: Should historians and legal systems understand Indigenous nations primarily as entities within states, with their status defined by each state's law, or as political communities whose existence and authority are not exhausted by state recognition?

Interpretation A: Nationhood within states. Some scholars and advocates emphasize that Indigenous rights are most effective when embedded in state law, since states control land, courts, and resources. On this view, strategies of recognition, treaty enforcement, and constitutional reform offer the concrete means of protecting Indigenous communities, and a framework independent of states risks being unenforceable.
Interpretation B: Nationhood beyond state frameworks. Others emphasize that Indigenous nations predate states, were not parties to the boundaries that divide them, and maintain legitimacy through their own institutions and relationships. On this view, treating state recognition as the source of nationhood misdescribes history and can limit Indigenous political options.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on cases in this chapter: communities that gained protection through recognition, communities whose continuity has depended on their own institutions despite weak recognition, and communities whose relationships cross jurisdictions that neither state's framework fully captures.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and political strategy more than evidence. It also concerns terminology, since "sovereignty," "autonomy," and "recognition" are used differently in different legal traditions. Many Indigenous scholars and communities hold positions that combine elements of both views, and the two interpretations are not mutually exclusive.

Why it matters: How the question is answered affects whether histories are organized by state borders, how rights are claimed, and what counts as continuity.

Student task: Choose one people from this chapter and construct the strongest case for each interpretation using specific evidence, then identify what further evidence would help.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 141 has argued that northern Mexico and the southwestern United States form an interconnected Indigenous historical region whose peoples, homelands, institutions, and exchange relationships frequently predate the international boundary. The deserts, mountains, river valleys, and coasts of the region supported distinct economies, and those economies were linked by kinship, ceremony, and trade. The region is therefore best understood as an Indigenous historical space before it is understood as a borderland between two states.

Spanish colonial expansion transformed that world unevenly. Missions, mines, ranches, and presidios reached some communities directly and others only at a distance, and Indigenous peoples responded through accommodation, selective adoption of colonial institutions, withdrawal, and rebellion. Institutions such as pueblo offices and ceremonial calendars, colonial in origin, became community possessions, which shows that tradition and change are not opposites.

Independent Mexico replaced colonial categories with equal citizenship and liberal land policy, which weakened corporate Indigenous legal standing and exposed communal land to sale and seizure. The Yaqui resisted with particular persistence, and under the Porfirian state many Yaqui were deported or driven from their homeland. Historians describe these events as forced displacement, and debate how best to classify them.

The U.S.–Mexico War and the Gadsden Purchase established and adjusted the international boundary without Indigenous consent. The chapter has emphasized the distinction between a change in international sovereignty and a change in the relationship between a people and its homeland. The boundary reorganized jurisdictions slowly, and its practical force grew with later enforcement, a subject reserved for Chapter 142.

Indigenous peoples became transboundary through different historical processes. The Tohono O'odham, Kumeyaay, and Cucapá were divided in place; the Kickapoo created a community in Mexico through migration and negotiated settlement; the Yaqui in Arizona arose from flight and persecution; and Apache movement across the line was part of a military and political geography that closed with the campaigns of the late nineteenth century. Recognizing these differences prevents the error of treating all transboundary communities as identical.

The twentieth century brought different institutional environments. In Mexico, revolution and agrarian reform created the ejido and other communal forms, and later constitutional recognition acknowledged Indigenous peoples while leaving questions of enforcement and autonomy open. In the United States, federal recognition and tribal governments developed under federal law and oversight. Neither system automatically produces effective autonomy, and communities exercise authority through assemblies, traditional offices, and ceremonial leadership that formal law may or may not recognize.

Land and water remain central. Dams, diversions, concessions, forestry, mining, and protected-area regulations affect the Yaqui, Rarámuri, Cucapá, O'odham, and others, and the distinction among ownership, jurisdiction, customary use, and historical attachment helps to explain why legal title does not settle territorial continuity.

Work and migration have produced multilocal households in which members live in several places yet remain part of a community. Women, elders, and young people sustain language, ceremony, and kinship, and they are also workers, officials, and organizers. Language and ceremonial transmission are under pressure from schooling and migration, but communities use programs, technology, and return visits to maintain them, and respect for restricted knowledge shapes how historians may write about ceremony.

The chapter applied the Six-Dimension Borderlands Indigenous Continuity Framework, an MGU instructional tool, to compare territorial continuity, political and legal authority, economic capacity, cultural and linguistic continuity, kinship networks, and transboundary relationships. The framework is a set of questions and not a measure, and it assigns no scores.

The chapter's conclusion is that Indigenous nationhood in the northern borderlands cannot be understood solely through state recognition, though recognition matters. The peoples examined here have lost land, water, and population, and they have rebuilt institutions and maintained relationships across jurisdictions. Their histories are indispensable to the continental argument of this textbook and prepare the way for the examination of international borders in Chapter 142.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 141 matters because it corrects a habit of thought: the assumption that the international boundary is the natural frame for Indigenous history. The peoples of the northern borderlands show that rivers, mountains, kin ties, and ceremony were older organizing principles than the line drawn in the nineteenth century, and that Indigenous communities continued to act on those principles after the line existed.

The chapter also reveals how varied the histories behind apparently similar circumstances can be. Communities on both sides of the line may have been divided in place, may have migrated, may have fled, or may have been shaped by a transformed river. Treating these histories as one would hide the differing legal claims, institutions, and memories involved.

The chapter connects colonial missions, liberal state-building, revolution, agrarian reform, and recent constitutional change to Indigenous experience over several centuries. It shows that institutions of colonial origin can become community possessions, that legal recognition can coexist with weak capacity, and that communities build authority in many forms.

It also contributes to the textbook's continental argument. Indigenous nations are not remnants but continuing political and cultural communities, and the northern borderlands show this in a setting where two states claim jurisdiction over a single community's lands and members.

Chapter 142 will turn from peoples to the boundary itself, examining it as a legal and administrative institution. Students should carry forward the questions raised here about who defines belonging, what recognition accomplishes, and how Indigenous communities sustain themselves under more than one state.
