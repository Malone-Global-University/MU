Chapter Opening

For many Indigenous nations, a mountain, lake, island, or high prairie is not only a parcel with a legal description. It is a place of origin, a ceremonial site, a burial ground, and a record of collective memory. Law has often recognized only some of those meanings.

In 1970 Taos Pueblo regained Blue Lake and surrounding mountain lands after a struggle of more than six decades. In 1980 the Supreme Court upheld a large monetary award to the Sioux Nation for the taking of the Black Hills, yet many Lakota leaders have refused to accept the money. In 1988 the Court allowed the federal government to build a road through a sacred high country in California. In 2014 the Supreme Court of Canada declared Aboriginal title to a specific territory for the first time. These episodes, only a few years or decades apart, show how differently legal systems have answered the same question: what is owed when land is lost?

This chapter argues that Indigenous land claims are not solely disputes over ownership or money. They concern territorial authority, religious practice, community continuity, and responsibility for places that Indigenous nations understand as inseparable from their identities. Land recovery movements have therefore pursued several different objectives: restitution of title, recognition of historical wrongs, protection of sacred places, access for ceremony, and greater Indigenous authority over management.

The chapter follows a progression from dispossession to recovery. Division I explains Indigenous land relationships, the treaty and allotment history that transformed them, and the Indian Claims Commission. Division II examines restitution and settlement. Division III turns to sacred sites on public land. Division IV examines Canadian Aboriginal title and cooperative stewardship. Division V considers land return and the Land Back movement. The emphasis is on the United States, with substantial Canadian examples.

Evidence is varied: statutes, treaties, court opinions, congressional hearings, tribal testimony, archaeological and ethnographic records, and oral histories. Courts record Indigenous knowledge selectively and through legal categories that were not designed for it, so students should ask throughout whose terms are being used. Sacred knowledge is also sometimes confidential. This chapter does not disclose ceremonial information.

The chapter extends through November 2026. Several developments, including the 2026 Oak Flat transfer and the July 2026 Bears Ears proclamation, are recent and their consequences are unfolding. They are treated as historical events with dated outcomes, not as settled conclusions. Chapter 118 explained the judicial authority of Indigenous governments; this chapter asks what happens when those governments and communities seek the return, recognition, or protection of the territories on which their histories depend.

Historical Context

Indigenous territorial relationships varied widely before colonization: some peoples held farmland by clan or household, some managed fisheries and gathering grounds collectively, and some maintained seasonal rounds across large areas. Colonial governments imposed their own categories. Treaties ceded some lands, conquest and unilateral legislation took others, and allotment (Chapter 86) broke tribal holdings into individual parcels and opened the remainder to settlers. Much of what was removed from Indigenous control became federal, state, or provincial public land, national forests and parks among them, while some became private property.

Earlier chapters supply the background. Chapters 106, 111, and 113 treat treaty-rights activism, and Chapters 115 and 116 treat the Alaska and Nunavut settlements; this chapter does not retell them. Chapter 117 introduced Canadian constitutional law and Chapter 118 tribal courts and jurisdiction. Here the focus is land: who holds it, who governs it, who may use it, and who is responsible for it.

The early modern and nineteenth-century histories behind these claims cannot be understood by treating all Indigenous lands as if they passed through the same transaction. Some governments negotiated treaties; others took land by force or declared it public property without agreement. Legal documents often focused on boundaries and signatures, whereas Indigenous accounts also preserved diplomatic obligations, oral promises, and continuing responsibilities to particular places. The resulting documentary disagreements were not peripheral: they became the very questions litigated in later claims over title, treaty rights, and authority.

In the United States, the creation of federal parks and national forests sometimes placed important cultural landscapes under agencies whose mandates emphasized recreation, conservation, timber, grazing, or mineral access. In Canada, provincial Crown-land management and twentieth-century natural-resource development created their own disputes in areas of unceded Indigenous territory. These different systems imposed similar burdens on peoples whose historical relationships did not disappear with a change in legal ownership. The modern land-recovery movement therefore emerged from both the original taking and the subsequent administrative control of ancestral sites.

The modern case law must also be read against a period of Indigenous political mobilization. Tribal governments, traditional practitioners, intertribal coalitions, and community organizers used petitions, congressional advocacy, litigation, negotiation, public history, and direct land purchase. Their goals differed: restitution of a lake, protection of a mountain, recovery of local village land, recognition of territorial title, or authority to guide a federal monument. Understanding those differences provides the necessary framework for comparing the results described in the chapter.

Division I — Indigenous Land Relationships and the Historical Foundations of Land Claims

Section 119.1

Indigenous Homelands, Sacred Landscapes, and Territorial Relationships

Indigenous nations maintain different relationships with their territories, and landscapes may encompass places of origin, burial grounds, ceremonial locations, mountains, caves, migration routes, and sites of historical events. Individual use, collective tenure, custodial responsibility, and territorial governance are distinct. Diné tradition associates the homeland with four sacred mountains; the Lakota regard the Black Hills as central to their history and religion; Pueblo peoples maintain ceremonial relationships with ancestral landscapes. These traditions are not interchangeable, and this chapter describes them only at the level communities have made public. The central question is why land cannot always be adequately understood through market value or exclusive private ownership.

The history of a place cannot be reconstructed solely from its designation on a cadastral map. Among the Diné, the four sacred mountains mark relationships between land, origin narratives, and responsibilities within a homeland; the significance of these boundaries is not captured by the ownership of isolated parcels. For many Lakota people the Black Hills, or Paha Sapa, are not replaceable real estate but a landscape integral to collective history and ceremony. Taos Pueblo's relationship to Blue Lake likewise joins a particular body of water and surrounding mountain country to the continuance of religious obligations. These publicly described examples should not be treated as interchangeable teachings. Each belongs to a specific political community with its own authorities and rules for transmitting knowledge.

Indigenous land relationships also involved practical institutions. Households might cultivate particular fields, while councils or kin groups exercised responsibilities for fishing stations, forests, trails, hunting ranges, or seasonal gathering places. Access could depend on kinship, negotiation with neighboring peoples, or collectively recognized obligations. Territorial boundaries were meaningful, but were not always surveyed as exclusive property lines. Diplomatic agreements and shared-use arrangements could coexist with disputes over resources. These patterns changed over time: Indigenous nations adapted to new crops, livestock, trading networks, and environmental pressures without ceasing to understand land as part of their political existence.

Religious importance is not necessarily confined to one marked point on the landscape. A pilgrimage route, the approach to a mountain, the sound and appearance of a place, or the availability of plants and water may matter to ceremony. Consequently, a legal arrangement allowing a person to step onto a site may fail to protect the surrounding conditions on which its use depends. Conversely, a conservation designation can restrict mining or development while leaving Indigenous communities without a decision-making role. These differences help explain why demands for protection, physical return, and governing authority often appear together in campaigns that outside observers classify simply as land claims.

Historical evidence must be handled with particular care. Indigenous testimony, community histories, treaties, government land records, court exhibits, and ethnographic accounts illuminate different dimensions of the same landscape. Courtroom evidence may be translated into categories such as continuous occupation or exclusive use that do not fully express a nation's own account. Some ceremonial knowledge is not public, and scholarly responsibility does not require its disclosure. The appropriate historical task is to identify whose territorial relationship was disrupted, what institutions maintained it, and how affected communities chose to explain their claims to governments and courts.

Section 119.2

Treaties, Territorial Dispossession, and the Transformation of Indigenous Landownership

Indigenous territorial control was transformed through several distinct processes: treaty cessions, unilateral takings, disputed transfers, military conquest, forced relocation, allotment, and settler occupation. Some territories were never ceded by a land-cession treaty at all. Lands that left Indigenous control became reservations, national forests, parks, private parcels, or Crown lands, and many remained culturally Indigenous after they changed legal status. This section explains the origins of contemporary claims without reconstructing each treaty or removal.

Colonial dispossession did not occur through a single legal mechanism. In parts of eastern North America, treaties and purchases were the principal documentary instruments through which colonial and later state authorities claimed territorial rights. Elsewhere military conquest, punitive expeditions, removal policies, and unilateral legislation reduced Indigenous control. Agreements written by colonial officials sometimes represented land cessions differently from Indigenous oral understandings. Where treaties promised continued hunting, fishing, or residence, later land-management agencies could treat the same territory as ordinary public or private property. The result was an enduring divergence between maps of recognized legal ownership and maps of Indigenous homeland.

United States expansion accelerated that divergence. The Indian Removal era shifted entire communities westward; following the Civil War, warfare and reservation creation restricted other peoples to much smaller portions of their territories. The General Allotment Act of 1887 and related statutes divided reservation lands into individual allotments and opened many so-called surplus areas to non-Indigenous settlement. Land subsequently passed through taxes, sales, inheritance, and federal administrative decisions. In some places national forests and parks were created over landscapes whose Indigenous inhabitants had been excluded or whose traditional practices were newly regulated. Similar processes of Crown land administration, railway construction, settlement, and resource leasing reshaped territorial relationships in Canada, though the governing constitutional doctrines differed.

The language of a legal transfer could itself conceal a political conflict. A state might present a conveyance as a completed purchase; the Indigenous nation might describe it as unauthorized because the sellers lacked authority or the bargain violated federal restrictions. In the United States the Indian Trade and Intercourse Acts, beginning in 1790, limited purchases of tribal land without federal involvement. Those laws later became important to claims arising from state-negotiated transfers, especially in the Northeast. Thus, twentieth-century litigation frequently involved reconstruction of transactions that had occurred generations earlier, including the authority of signatories, the terms of treaties, and the role of governments that purported to acquire the land.

Property conversion did not erase territorial relationships. Families continued to visit burial places, gather plants where possible, teach place names, and object to developments that endangered ceremonial practice. Indigenous governments maintained treaty arguments, historical records, and oral accounts even when they lacked recognized title. The persistence of these relationships explains why modern claims may concern sites far beyond present reservation boundaries. It also explains the different remedies sought: a court declaration, money, renewed access, a land exchange, return of ownership, or a negotiated share in the exercise of authority.

Section 119.3

Ownership, Sacred Geography, and Competing Understandings of Land

Private property, collective Indigenous title, treaty rights, public-land management, and religious relationships to land are different things. Holding title, exercising governmental authority, possessing access rights, and participating in stewardship are legally distinct. Conflict arises when a government classifies a place as a recreational site, mineral resource, forest, or public asset while a community understands it as a living sacred landscape. This analytical distinction recurs throughout the chapter.

In American property law, fee-simple ownership ordinarily gives an owner broad rights to possess and transfer land, subject to law. Federal trust land is different: the United States holds legal title for the benefit of a tribe or individual, and federal statutes govern important aspects of its management. Yet neither category alone answers every jurisdictional question. A tribal government may own fee land outside its reservation without thereby acquiring the same authority it exercises on reservation land; land within a reservation can also be held by non-Indians while remaining Indian country for certain legal purposes. Chapter 118 examined that distinction in jurisdictional doctrine. Here it matters because recovering a parcel and recovering territorial governance are not synonymous.

Canadian Aboriginal title constitutes another legal category. Courts describe it as a collective, constitutionally protected interest derived from occupation before Crown assertion of sovereignty, with rights of use and decision-making subject to important qualifications. It is not a simple equivalent of American trust land, ordinary private ownership, or a right to receive compensation. The historical record also includes treaty-protected activities that may persist over lands no longer owned by the nation concerned. A right to hunt, fish, conduct ceremonies, or consult over a development can therefore exist even when the underlying registered title rests elsewhere.

Public agencies typically approach land through responsibilities established by legislation: managing a forest, administering a recreation site, protecting archaeological resources, or leasing minerals. Indigenous communities may ask a different question: what happens to the integrity of a sacred place if the government changes its use? A lease or permit can be valid under ordinary land rules while still threatening ceremonial continuity. Government consultation may require agencies to hear and consider objections, but does not always place final authority in Indigenous hands. In Canada, the Crown's constitutional duty to consult can be legally enforceable and depends on the circumstances; American preservation consultation follows a different statutory structure.

These categories should not be arranged as a ladder on which only one form is meaningful. Transfer of title may be the strongest response to one particular dispossession. Binding management authority may protect a site where transfer is unavailable. Court-ordered access may provide a narrower benefit. A settlement can combine all three, together with compensation or resource funding. The decisive historical questions are what the nation sought, what it received, what limits remained, and who retained the power to change the arrangement later. This chapter will repeatedly measure legal remedies against these different outcomes.

Historical Vocabulary

Title, Access, Consultation, and Stewardship

Definition. Title identifies a legally recognized interest in land; access is a right or permission to enter or use it. Consultation means a legally or administratively required process of engagement whose content varies by legal system: Canadian Crown consultation under Section 35 can be enforceable, while American historic-preservation consultation follows federal statutory rules. Stewardship concerns responsibility for care and management and may be shared without transfer of ownership.

Why it matters. A nation may hold one without the others; for example, access to a federal forest without title, or consultation without any power to say no.

Potential misunderstanding. "Protection" is often used for all four, but each gives different security.

Section 119.4

The Indian Claims Commission and the Problem of Compensation without Restitution

The Indian Claims Commission Act of 1946 created a forum for historical tribal claims arising from unlawful land takings, treaty violations, and inadequate payment. Nations used it to document losses. Its fundamental limitation was that it could award money but could not order land returned. Some nations accepted awards; others held that money could not resolve the loss of ancestral territory. This difference prepares students for the contrasting outcomes at Blue Lake and in the Black Hills.

Before 1946, many tribal claims against the federal government could proceed only after Congress enacted a special law allowing the particular suit. The Indian Claims Commission Act of August 13, 1946, created a more systematic forum for historical grievances. Its jurisdiction extended beyond straightforward contract disputes to claims based on fraud, unfair or dishonorable dealings, and inadequate payment under specified circumstances. Tribes and their counsel assembled treaty records, government correspondence, maps, valuations, and testimony to establish the nature of a taking or wrong. The process transformed many historical grievances into formal cases, but required communities to frame their accounts within the categories of federal claims law.

The commission's remedy was deliberately narrow. It could determine the value of land at the time of its loss and recommend monetary awards, but it lacked authority to order that an ancestral homeland be returned. An award did not automatically reconstruct tribal institutions, reopen a sacred landscape, or reverse the establishment of settlements and public lands. When historians describe the commission as recognizing an injustice, they must specify what the legal finding actually concerned: a particular claim, taking, or valuation rather than a general acknowledgment of every consequence of colonization. Awards were also subject to litigation, offsets, administrative processes, and disagreements over distribution.

The choice to accept or reject money was not uniform. Some communities regarded funds as resources for housing, schools, land purchases, and economic development, especially when physical restitution seemed politically impossible. Others argued that payment could imply a sale they had never agreed to make or extinguish an unresolved demand for territorial return. Disputes might divide leadership groups and generations within a nation. Those disagreements should not be reduced to a simple opposition between practical and spiritual interests: for many people economic security and the protection of sacred land were both essential obligations.

The commission ceased operating in September 1978, with remaining matters transferred to other federal claims institutions. Its archives now provide an unusually detailed documentary record of treaties, historical maps, expert testimony, and litigation strategies. Yet the archive also illustrates the limits of the institution that produced it. Evidence chosen to calculate a monetary loss is not necessarily sufficient to explain a people's continuing relationship to its land. The juxtaposition of Blue Lake, where Congress restored land, and the Black Hills, where the courts sustained money damages, demonstrates why the availability of a remedy shaped the meaning of a legal victory.

Historical Vocabulary

Restitution, Compensation, and Recognition

Restitution returns an item, interest, or territory that was taken. Compensation supplies money or another substitute for loss. Recognition formally acknowledges a right or claim, but may require separate steps before it can be exercised.

Historical context. The Indian Claims Commission was empowered chiefly to award damages, while the 1970 Blue Lake legislation restored land; the 1980 Black Hills judgment sustained compensation without ordering restitution. Canadian Aboriginal-title declarations involve a further legal approach.

Potential misunderstanding. A monetary award is not necessarily accepted by an Indigenous nation as a purchase, nor does formal recognition alone establish all practical means of access and government.

Division II — Land Recovery, Treaty Claims, and the Struggle for Restitution

Section 119.5

Taos Pueblo and the Return of Blue Lake, 1970

Blue Lake lies in the mountains above Taos Pueblo and is central to Pueblo religious life. In 1906 the area was incorporated into the national forest system, and for decades the Pueblo sought its return, resisting offers of money or limited use permits. Pueblo leaders, supporters, and congressional hearings eventually produced legislation, signed on December 15, 1970, that restored approximately 48,000 acres to the Pueblo. The case showed that land restoration could serve objectives financial compensation could not, and it became an important example of the emerging federal policy of self-determination (Chapter 114).

For Taos Pueblo, Blue Lake and its mountain watershed formed an inseparable ceremonial landscape. When President Theodore Roosevelt's administration incorporated surrounding lands into the Carson National Forest in 1906, federal forest management treated a place of longstanding Pueblo religious responsibility as government land administered for multiple public purposes. The change did not end Pueblo religious practice, but it subjected visits, access, and control of the surrounding area to federal decisions. Pueblo leaders sought the return of the lands rather than a permit to make occasional ceremonial use. The difference was fundamental: permits could be withdrawn, whereas restitution would recognize a continuing relationship of collective responsibility.

The campaign unfolded through petitions, testimony, lobbying, and alliances with non-Indigenous supporters over more than six decades. Taos Pueblo leaders argued that commercial and recreational uses of the lake and the surrounding mountains were incompatible with their obligations toward the place. The Indian Claims Commission had found grounds for compensation, but the Pueblo insisted that a monetary payment was not an adequate substitute for the physical territory. During the period of greater national attention to Indigenous self-determination, Pueblo representatives secured congressional consideration of legislation restoring the land. Their campaign was not simply the product of a sympathetic president; it rested on sustained Pueblo advocacy.

On December 15, 1970, President Richard Nixon signed Public Law 91-550, restoring approximately 48,000 acres including Blue Lake to Taos Pueblo in federal trust. The statute distinguished these lands from ordinary publicly accessible national-forest space and placed them under a protective arrangement reflecting Pueblo purposes. The Nixon administration publicly described the action as redress for an injustice. For the Pueblo, however, the restoration confirmed a relationship that predated federal ownership. This difference in perspective matters: the government spoke of a transfer under statutory authority, while Pueblo advocates spoke of regaining what should not have been taken.

The return also showed how legal and political mechanisms could intersect. Congress could change the status of federal land even though the Indian Claims Commission itself could not award restitution. At the same time, the case should not be mistaken for a general rule that every sacred-land claim thereafter would succeed. Success depended on the specific property history, congressional votes, executive support, and the capacity of Pueblo leaders to present a compelling, enduring claim. Blue Lake became a reference point for later Indigenous campaigns precisely because it demonstrated a possibility absent from many other available legal remedies.

Section 119.6

The Black Hills, the Fort Laramie Treaty, and the Sioux Nation's Land Claim

The Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868 recognized a Great Sioux Reservation that included the Black Hills. After gold was found and military pressure mounted, Congress in 1877 enacted legislation by which the United States took the Hills. A long legal campaign culminated in United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians (1980), in which the Supreme Court upheld a monetary award for the taking. Many Lakota political and traditional leaders have rejected the money, maintaining that the Hills are not for sale. The case makes the difference between compensation and restitution unmistakable: the law found a wrong and set a price, but did not return the land.

The 1868 Treaty of Fort Laramie recognized a Great Sioux Reservation that included the Black Hills and contained requirements governing further land cessions. After gold was discovered in the hills in the 1870s, miners entered the region in violation of the treaty arrangements, and the United States increased military pressure. Congressional legislation in 1877 took the Black Hills without obtaining the treaty-prescribed consent. The resulting dispossession became one of the clearest examples of the conflict between a formal treaty guarantee and the subsequent conduct of the state that had undertaken to honor it. For many Lakota people, the issue was inseparable from the sacred and historical importance of Paha Sapa.

Legal efforts to challenge the taking extended across generations and involved complex changes in jurisdiction and procedural rules. In United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians (1980), the Supreme Court sustained the conclusion that the United States had effected a taking requiring compensation under the Fifth Amendment. The dispute was thus not merely about whether the land was economically valuable; it concerned the federal government's breach of an obligation and the legal consequences of acquiring property from a treaty nation. The decision supported an award of monetary compensation, including interest, rather than an order restoring the territory. The distinction reflected the scope of the judicial remedy available in that litigation.

Many Lakota leaders and community members have opposed accepting the judgment funds, maintaining that the Black Hills are not for sale and that receiving payment risks recasting a coerced taking as a consensual transaction. Other views have existed about how financial resources might help communities facing deep economic need. Historians should represent these debates without suggesting that a single individual speaks for all Lakota, Dakota, and Nakota peoples or that every nation affected by the nineteenth-century treaty history has adopted an identical legal position. The publicly repeated demand for land return is a political claim that continued after the Court's financial determination.

Blue Lake and the Black Hills were both Indigenous territorial claims, but the outcomes were produced by different institutions. Congress changed ownership in the first case; litigation yielded a monetary remedy in the second. The significance of the Black Hills decision therefore cannot be measured simply by the amount awarded. It affirmed that the federal taking had legal consequences while leaving the central demand for restoration unresolved. The case remains an essential illustration of the limits of a compensation-centered legal system when a people understands the disputed territory as part of its nationhood and responsibilities to future generations.

Section 119.7

The Maine Indian Claims Settlement of 1980

The Passamaquoddy Tribe and Penobscot Nation argued that historic land transfers in Maine had violated federal law. Negotiations among the nations, the state, and the federal government produced the Maine Indian Claims Settlement Act of 1980, which provided money, funds to acquire land, and a distinctive jurisdictional framework. The settlement enabled territorial recovery but also created legal limitations and continuing controversy, illustrating the consequences of negotiating within an established state political system. Experiences differed among the affected nations.

The Maine claims arose from a particular history of treaties, state land transfers, and federal law. The Passamaquoddy and Penobscot peoples maintained that extensive nineteenth-century conveyances had occurred without the required federal approval under the Indian Trade and Intercourse Acts. In Joint Tribal Council of the Passamaquoddy Tribe v. Morton (1975), federal appellate litigation helped establish that the federal government owed trust responsibilities relevant to these claims. The possibility of challenges to title across large areas created legal uncertainty for private owners, municipalities, and the state, making a negotiated solution politically attractive to several parties. At the same time, the nations' claims expressed a history of dispossession not adequately described by the market value of land alone.

Negotiations produced interlocking state and federal arrangements in 1980. The Maine Indian Claims Settlement Act, Public Law 96-420, approved on October 10, addressed the Passamaquoddy Tribe, Penobscot Nation, and Houlton Band of Maliseet Indians. The related Maine Implementing Act formed part of the overall settlement framework. The Houlton Band's treatment differed from the arrangements for the Penobscot and Passamaquoddy governments; it should not disappear from an account simply because the litigation's better-known protagonists were the two larger claimant groups. Funds and mechanisms for acquiring land were central to the package, as were the rules assigning governmental authority.

The settlement illustrates the distinction between restoring a territorial base and agreeing to the legal order under which that land will be governed. Particular provisions structured relations with Maine in unusual ways, and they subsequently became subjects of debate over taxation, regulation, jurisdiction, and the interpretation of federal Indian laws enacted after 1980. Indigenous leaders differed about the advantages and restrictions of the negotiated bargain. The law offered resources and an end to substantial uncertainty; it did not recreate all historical territory or provide a completely unqualified sphere of sovereignty.

The Maine experience demonstrates the compromises inherent in negotiated restitution. State officials emphasized settled land titles and predictable administration. Tribal representatives pursued land, recognition, and resources while preserving as much self-government as the negotiations allowed. A historian must read the statutes together with Indigenous testimony and the history of claims that generated them. The settlement's success cannot be assessed solely by whether money was disbursed, because the continuing question was whether the arrangement secured meaningful territorial and governmental futures for the peoples involved.

Section 119.8

Oneida Land Claims and the Limits of Restoring Historical Territory

The Oneida Indian Nation challenged nineteenth-century land transfers in New York through litigation in the 1970s and 1980s. In City of Sherrill v. Oneida Indian Nation of New York (2005), the Supreme Court held that the Nation's reacquisition of former reservation land in ordinary fee ownership did not automatically restore immunity from local property taxation. The decision distinguishes recovering particular parcels from restoring governmental authority over a historical territory, a distinction that links to the mechanisms discussed in §119.18.

The Oneida people had occupied extensive territories in what became New York long before the creation of the United States. Following the American Revolution, state authorities concluded land transactions that the Oneida later challenged as inconsistent with federal restraints on purchases of tribal lands. Litigation revived during the twentieth century as the Oneida Indian Nation and related Oneida communities sought redress. In Oneida Indian Nation v. County of Oneida (1974), and a subsequent 1985 Supreme Court decision commonly described as Oneida II, the Court recognized an avenue for federal common-law claims concerning unlawful dispossession. The decisions showed that historic land transactions could remain legally significant many generations after they occurred.

Acquisition of land through market purchases created another question. If a tribal nation bought parcels inside an old reservation boundary, did those properties automatically regain the tax and regulatory status of reservation lands? In City of Sherrill v. Oneida Indian Nation of New York (2005), the Supreme Court concluded that the Nation could not unilaterally revive an exemption from local property taxation over reacquired fee land after a long period of non-Indian ownership and governmental administration. The opinion relied on equitable considerations, including the passage of time and expectations built up under the existing jurisdictional arrangements.

That judgment did not erase the historical record of dispossession or hold that a tribe loses its political identity by selling or reacquiring land. It addressed the governmental consequences sought for particular fee parcels. The Nation's ownership remained real, but the Court refused to treat the purchases alone as sufficient to transform taxation and jurisdiction. The decision prompted renewed attention to federal trust acquisition and negotiated arrangements as routes toward a more secure land base. The implications extended beyond the Oneida case because numerous tribal governments have purchased land that they later seek to place into trust.

The Oneida experience therefore belongs beside the Black Hills and Blue Lake, though it involves a different legal problem. One case concerns compensation for a federal taking; another concerns legislative return of national-forest land; this one concerns whether private acquisition restores public-law authority. For Indigenous governments, the distinction affects schools, economic enterprises, environmental regulation, taxation, and the capacity to plan for future generations. For state and local governments, it raises questions about existing public services and regulatory expectations. An adequate historical account must identify both sets of institutional interests without assuming that current land records fully settle the legitimacy of earlier transfers.

Division III — Sacred Sites, Religious Freedom, and Conflicts over Public Lands

Section 119.9

The American Indian Religious Freedom Act and Sacred-Site Protection

The American Indian Religious Freedom Act of 1978 declared federal policy to protect Indigenous religious practice, including access to sites, objects, and ceremonies. It was a statement of policy rather than a legally enforceable guarantee. Other tools followed: consultation under Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act, and Executive Order 13007 (1996) concerning access to and ceremonial use of sacred sites on federal land. Consultation, accommodation, and landownership are different forms of protection, while forms of authority and their durability vary; ownership is not the only source of meaningful control.

During the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, federal authorities suppressed or restricted many Indigenous ceremonial practices. These restrictions interacted with land loss, federal control of reservation administration, and the transformation of culturally important landscapes into parks and forests. The American Indian Religious Freedom Act of 1978 responded to demands for public recognition of religious practices, including access to sacred sites, possession of ceremonial objects, and freedom to conduct ceremonies. It signaled a change in federal policy. But its broad declaration of intent did not itself create a general cause of action permitting courts to invalidate every land-management decision harmful to Indigenous religion.

Other federal laws provided different procedural mechanisms. Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act requires federal agencies to take account of effects on historic properties and to consult with specified parties, including tribes under applicable rules. That process can identify culturally significant places and require agencies to consider alternatives or measures addressing adverse effects. It does not invariably prohibit the project or transfer ownership. The National Environmental Policy Act similarly requires environmental review in qualifying circumstances, but is primarily procedural. The Religious Freedom Restoration Act of 1993 later offered a different legal basis for some challenges to federal actions, though judicial interpretations of a substantial burden remained crucial.

Executive Order 13007, issued in 1996, directed federal land-management agencies, to the extent practicable and permitted by law, to accommodate access to and ceremonial use of sacred sites and avoid adversely affecting their physical integrity. Its implementation nonetheless operated within existing property and management authorities. The significance of each device lies in what it actually does: a consultation meeting, a protective management decision, and return of land to tribal control are not legally identical outcomes. Nor are all forms of consultation the same. The Crown's constitutionally grounded duty to consult in Canada, examined later, should not be casually merged with American historic-preservation procedures.

Indigenous nations have used these processes strategically, often building records that combine public testimony, environmental analysis, archaeological evidence, and statements of religious significance. The demands can range from advance notice and meaningful consultation to absolute protection or restitution. Agencies may weigh multiple-use mandates, public recreation, conservation, timber, minerals, and private permits. The continuing conflict is not simply between religion and development; it also concerns who defines public interest and whether a government can treat Indigenous religious harm as one interest among others without giving affected nations authority over the result.

Section 119.10

Lyng v. Northwest Indian Cemetery Protective Association, 1988

The Forest Service proposed a road and timber harvesting in the high country of northwestern California, which is central to the religious practices of Yurok, Karuk, and Tolowa peoples. In Lyng (1988), the Supreme Court rejected the constitutional challenge, reasoning that the government's use of its own land did not coerce anyone to act against their beliefs or penalize religious conduct, even if it could destroy the physical setting for practice. The decision draws a sharp distinction between restricting religious conduct and damaging the place where it occurs, and it remains the central example of the limits of U.S. religious-freedom law in sacred-landscape disputes.

The disputed high country lay within the Six Rivers National Forest of northwestern California. Federal planners proposed completing a section of the Gasquet–Orleans road, commonly called the G-O Road, and allowing related timber harvesting. Yurok, Karuk, and Tolowa practitioners maintained that the area was indispensable to religious practices that could not simply be moved elsewhere. Studies commissioned in the planning process documented the significance of the landscape, and litigation brought together Indigenous plaintiffs, other environmental advocates, and California. The case is important because the federal agency was aware of the threatened religious harm; the question was whether constitutional law compelled it to abandon a project on federal land.

Lower courts had restrained the project, but the Supreme Court's 1988 majority rejected the Free Exercise challenge. Justice Sandra Day O'Connor's opinion distinguished government coercion of religious conduct from a government decision about the use of its own property that would have severe adverse effects on religious practice. The decision acknowledged the gravity of the impact while concluding that the Constitution did not give the plaintiffs the requested power to control federal land-management choices. The dissent emphasized the practical destruction of the conditions necessary for religious exercise. These were disagreements over the meaning of religious freedom, not over whether the landscape mattered to the communities concerned.

The judgment must not be mistaken for proof that the proposed road was completed. Litigation history, congressional wilderness designations, agency decisions, and subsequent conservation measures influenced what happened to the physical landscape after the case. The Supreme Court resolved the constitutional question presented; it did not order construction of the road. That distinction between a permission under law and an implemented project is essential to historically accurate accounts of judicial decisions. A community may lose its constitutional claim yet continue to protect the place through political organizing, land-management decisions, or another statutory mechanism.

Lyng also illuminates the constraints of a property-centered legal framework. The government's argument rested partly on its authority to manage federal land; practitioners spoke of obligations that depended upon the site's integrity and could not be separated from it. The majority's test placed greater weight on coercion or penalties directed at believers than on the destruction of the setting of belief. Later sacred-site disputes, including litigation over the San Francisco Peaks, would confront related difficulties under a different statutory framework. For students, the enduring question is what kind of legal right is necessary when the exercise of religion depends on a place controlled by another government.

Section 119.11

The San Francisco Peaks and the Protection of Sacred Mountains

The San Francisco Peaks of northern Arizona are sacred to several peoples, including the Diné, Hopi, and other nations. Disputes arose over expansion of a ski area and use of reclaimed wastewater for artificial snowmaking. In Navajo Nation v. United States Forest Service (2008), the en banc Ninth Circuit ruled against the tribes' religious-freedom claims. The case shows that a public-land project may profoundly affect religious practice even when formal access is never prohibited.

The San Francisco Peaks rise above Flagstaff in northern Arizona and have distinct religious meanings for several Indigenous nations. They are identified in publicly available Diné teachings about the homeland and in Hopi and other traditions concerning ceremonial life. The Arizona Snowbowl ski area operates on federal forest land, where development was subject to Forest Service authorization. In the early 2000s a proposal to enlarge ski facilities and use treated municipal wastewater to make artificial snow prompted opposition from tribal governments, religious practitioners, and environmental organizations. Concerns included the relationship between water quality, the sacred character of the mountains, and the consequences of altering a place used in prayer and ceremony.

The dispute proceeded under several laws rather than one unified sacred-land guarantee. Plaintiffs invoked the Religious Freedom Restoration Act, which restricts government imposition of substantial burdens on religious exercise absent required justification, as well as environmental-review requirements. A Ninth Circuit panel initially ruled in the plaintiffs' favor on significant issues. In 2008, however, the court sitting en banc rejected the principal RFRA claim, reasoning that the challenged action did not impose the kind of substantial burden the statute prohibited under its interpretation. Dissenting judges and tribal plaintiffs argued that the threatened effects on religious exercise were much more serious than this legal test acknowledged.

The historical significance lies in the difference between recognizing spiritual injury and granting a judicial remedy. Plaintiffs did not need to prove that the peaks were important to them in order to make a moral or historical argument; the legal controversy turned on the requirements of a specific statute and the court's understanding of religious burden. Federal ownership allowed managers to authorize recreational uses while tribes lacked a general veto. Although environmental and religious claims followed different analytical routes, the resulting disputes demonstrated the vulnerability of important Indigenous places when their cultural significance was not reflected in the rules controlling land-use decisions.

The San Francisco Peaks case should not be presented as identical to Lyng. The former centrally involved RFRA, enacted after the 1988 decision, while the latter involved the First Amendment Free Exercise Clause. Yet the cases share an interpretive problem: formal access may remain even when the experience and integrity of a sacred place change substantially. Indigenous leaders continued to advocate for protection through consultation, public communication, and legal remedies. Their efforts place sacred-land protection within the ongoing work of governing a living landscape rather than a single moment of courtroom success or failure.

Oak Flat, Apache Religious Practice, and the Conflict over Land Exchange

Chi'chil Biłdagoteel, or Oak Flat, in Arizona's Tonto National Forest, is significant to Western Apache religious and cultural life, including ceremony and gathering of medicinal plants. Congress mandated a land exchange with Resolution Copper in 2014 legislation. Apache Stronghold's challenge failed in the courts, and the Supreme Court declined review in 2025. In March 2026, after the Ninth Circuit declined to block the exchange, title to roughly 2,400 acres, including Oak Flat, was transferred to Resolution Copper; the court acknowledged the transfer would alter the land and destroy sacred sites, but held that plaintiffs had raised no viable challenge to Congress's decision. Litigation and political opposition, including legislative proposals to reverse the exchange, continued; the Ninth Circuit declined rehearing in September 2026. The dispute is not resolved. The broader mining history belongs to Chapter 136.

Oak Flat, known to Western Apache people as Chi'chil Biłdagoteel, lies within the Tonto National Forest in Arizona. Apache communities have described the place as essential to prayer, ceremonies, and the gathering of traditional resources, including medicinal plants. The dispute emerged from the presence of valuable copper deposits beneath the area and proposals for a large underground mine. To the mining company and its supporters, the project represented an economic opportunity. To many Apache religious practitioners, its anticipated physical transformation represented a threat to a location that could not be substituted by a different parcel of federal land. The conflict thus joined two fundamentally different explanations of the value and permitted use of the territory.

Congress included the Southeast Arizona Land Exchange and Conservation Act in the National Defense Authorization Act enacted in December 2014. The legislation provided for transfer of specified federal lands, including Oak Flat, to Resolution Copper in exchange for privately held lands elsewhere, subject to statutory conditions. Opponents challenged both the substance and the process of the exchange through legal and political avenues. Apache Stronghold, an organization supporting protection of the site, pursued religious-freedom litigation; other plaintiffs challenged aspects of federal implementation. Courts addressed distinct legal questions, so an adverse result in one lawsuit should not be described as eliminating every avenue for contesting the project.

The litigation reached a critical stage in 2025–2026. The Supreme Court declined to review an important religious-freedom appeal in 2025. In March 2026 the Ninth Circuit declined to bar the congressionally directed exchange in another set of proceedings. Resolution Copper reported that the land exchange, involving approximately 2,422 acres of transferred federal land, was completed on March 16, 2026. In September the Ninth Circuit denied rehearing petitions in the challenge to the exchange. These are dated legal and administrative events, not a prediction that mine construction, all litigation, or the protection controversy had reached a final endpoint.

Oak Flat demonstrates why title matters without answering every question about the site's future. Before the exchange, the land was federally owned and subject to statutory management obligations; afterward, a private company held title under the exchange. Even when access arrangements exist, they cannot automatically assure that a place will remain physically intact through industrial development. Indigenous opponents continued to seek political intervention and legal protection, emphasizing that the harm they feared was not solely exclusion from property but permanent alteration of the sacred landscape. Division IV — Aboriginal Title, Territorial Recognition, and Indigenous Stewardship

Section 119.13

Aboriginal Title and the Canadian Duty to Consult

Canadian law recognizes Aboriginal title under Section 35 (Chapter 117). Delgamuukw v. British Columbia (1997) addressed Aboriginal title and held that Indigenous oral histories must be given appropriate weight as evidence. Haida Nation v. British Columbia (2004) held that the Crown may have a duty to consult and, where appropriate, accommodate Indigenous interests before title is proven. Consultation is not consent, treaty settlement, or a declaration of title.

Canada's legal framework developed through a distinct constitutional history. Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982 recognizes and affirms existing Aboriginal and treaty rights. Aboriginal title is an aspect of those rights, but its legal content was clarified gradually through litigation involving Indigenous nations, the Crown, and provincial authorities. The debate concerned whether Indigenous occupation before European settlement could establish an enduring collective territorial interest even where no treaty had surrendered the land. British Columbia provided a major arena because extensive areas had never been covered by historic land-cession treaties, while forestry, mining, and settlement continued under asserted Crown authority.

In Delgamuukw v. British Columbia (1997), the Supreme Court of Canada addressed the claims of Gitxsan and Wet'suwet'en hereditary chiefs and set out important principles about Aboriginal title and the treatment of oral histories as evidence. The judgment required that oral traditions be approached with sensitivity to the difficulties of translating Indigenous systems of knowledge into conventional court procedures. It was not itself a final declaration of title over the entire territory claimed. The case nevertheless helped change the terms of debate by clarifying that title was more than a right to undertake particular customary activities: it could encompass a broad territorial interest.

The duty to consult was developed further in Haida Nation v. British Columbia (Minister of Forests) (2004). The Court held that the Crown may owe consultation and, where appropriate, accommodation where it contemplates conduct that may adversely affect asserted rights or title, even before final proof of those claims. The duty follows from the honor of the Crown, and its content depends on the strength of the claim and seriousness of the possible impact. It is an enforceable legal obligation, but it is not automatically the same as an Indigenous power to veto every project. Nor does consultation itself declare Aboriginal title or settle ownership.

This framework created both opportunities and tensions. Indigenous nations could seek to influence forestry and other resource decisions before exhausting litigation over title; governments were required to take claims seriously rather than treating them as legally irrelevant until final judgment. Yet governments retained important decision-making powers, and disputes persisted about whether consultation was timely, sufficiently informed, or meaningful. The historian must distinguish the developing Canadian doctrine from the U.S. regime of federal trust lands, historic claims, and public-land consultation. Both systems arose from colonial territorial dispossession, but they did not provide the same institutions or remedies.

Section 119.14

Tsilhqot'in Nation v. British Columbia and the Recognition of Aboriginal Title, 2014

The Tsilhqot'in Nation sought to stop unwanted logging in its territory in central British Columbia. In 2014 the Supreme Court of Canada issued the first judicial declaration of Aboriginal title to a specific area. The decision treated title as collective, grounded in occupation, and constraining the Crown's powers, which may override title only if justified. This differs fundamentally from the monetary-claims framework discussed earlier.

The Tsilhqot'in struggle emerged from sustained resistance to resource development in central British Columbia. Members of the nation sought to protect a large part of their traditional territory against unwanted logging, and the dispute eventually became a test case over the meaning of Aboriginal title. The historical question was not simply whether individual sites had been permanently occupied as villages or cultivated fields. It was whether the evidence of territorial use, governance, and occupation could establish title over a wider landscape in which Indigenous people hunted, traveled, and maintained social and political relations. The form of the evidence was therefore crucial to the outcome.

After lengthy trial proceedings and appeals, the Supreme Court of Canada on June 26, 2014, issued a declaration of Aboriginal title to a specific area. It was the first such judicial declaration over a particular territory outside the reserve framework. The Court rejected an excessively narrow understanding of occupation that would confine title only to small intensively used sites. Its approach considered sufficiency, continuity where relevant, and exclusivity of occupation in the historical context of the nation concerned. That reasoning mattered for peoples whose territorial practices could not be mapped as a patchwork of European-style farms or fenced properties.

Title was described as a collective right that includes the authority to determine uses of the land and to enjoy its benefits, subject to the collective nature of the interest and its preservation for future generations. The Crown generally must seek consent to uses that would infringe established title, although a justified infringement remains legally possible under the constitutional framework. Therefore it would be inaccurate to say that the decision either eliminated every governmental role or converted Aboriginal title into ordinary private fee simple. It recognized a distinct form of territorial right and set demanding legal conditions for outside interference.

The judgment changed the practical terms of negotiation over lands not covered by historical treaties. Indigenous nations could point to a concrete example of judicially recognized territory, while governments and resource users faced renewed incentives to negotiate. At the same time, each future claim still required attention to its own evidence, and the declaration did not automatically resolve other nations' overlapping territories or private-property questions elsewhere in Canada. The Tsilhqot'in Nation's own explanations of the litigation emphasize generations of leadership and territorial defense; a court judgment is the formal culmination of that history, not its beginning.

Section 119.15

Haida Gwaii and the Negotiated Recognition of Indigenous Title

The Haida Nation's campaign to protect and govern Haida Gwaii includes forestry disputes, assertions of sovereignty, and conservation initiatives. The 2024 Rising Tide Haida Title Lands Agreement and related provincial legislation recognized Haida Aboriginal title. On September 5, 2025, the British Columbia Supreme Court, by consent of the Haida Nation, British Columbia, and Canada, declared Aboriginal title to the terrestrial lands of Haida Gwaii. The arrangements address private property and existing interests through negotiated terms and transitional governance. The outcome was criticized by some opposing political figures, and broader questions about Aboriginal title and private land continue in other cases, so it should not be treated as settling those questions.

Haida Gwaii's political history includes longstanding Haida assertions of nationhood and responsibility for the islands, as well as conflicts over industrial logging and the decision-making powers exercised by British Columbia and Canada. The Haida Nation organized to defend forests and cultural landscapes and developed institutions capable of negotiating with governments. Litigation helped clarify the Crown's duty to consult, but a lasting arrangement required addressing ordinary residents' property rights, local government services, federal interests, and questions about how authority would change over time. The eventual title agreements arose from this combination of political organizing, legal leverage, and negotiation rather than from one isolated courtroom contest.

The Haida Nation and British Columbia reached the Gaayhllxid • Gíihlagalgang Rising Tide Haida Title Lands Agreement in 2024. Provincial legislation recognized the agreement, and its implementation came into effect in July 2024. Canada later entered the separate Chii x uujin • Chaaw K aawgaa Big Tide (Low Water) arrangement. These documents distinguished recognition of Haida Aboriginal title from immediate assumption of every responsibility formerly exercised by provincial or federal governments. They also set terms for a staged governmental transition and protection of existing interests.

On September 5, 2025, the Supreme Court of British Columbia issued a consent declaration recognizing Haida Aboriginal title to the terrestrial lands of Haida Gwaii under the agreed framework. The court order was supported by the Haida Nation, Canada, and British Columbia, and expressly linked implementation to the negotiated agreements. It did not simply cancel private owners' fee-simple titles. The provincial arrangements preserve existing private-property interests, local government jurisdiction, and government services under specified terms. This is a vital distinction: the declaration recognized Indigenous territorial title, but the practical legal relationship among titleholders, residents, and governments was mediated by negotiated protections and future institutional arrangements.

Haida Gwaii therefore offers a different route from both the American claims-commission system and the earlier Tsilhqot'in litigation. Negotiated recognition, provincial legislation, an agreement with Canada, and a judicial declaration operated together. Supporters emphasized a new foundation for governing the islands and rebuilding relationships; critics raised questions about certainty and implementation. The historical outcome through November 2026 was recognition accompanied by an evolving governance transition, not an instantaneous transfer of every local power. The distinction is especially important when students compare Canadian title recognition with the restoration of specific U.S. trust lands.

Section 119.16

Bears Ears and Indigenous Participation in the Stewardship of Federal Lands

The Bears Ears Inter-Tribal Coalition (Hopi, Navajo, Ute Mountain Ute, Ute Indian, and Zuni) formed in 2015 to seek protection of the landscape in southeastern Utah. Bears Ears National Monument was established in 2016 with a Commission giving the tribes a formal role in management. The monument was reduced in 2017 and restored in 2021. On July 13, 2026, a presidential proclamation reduced it from roughly 1.36 million acres to approximately 121,100 acres and terminated the Bears Ears Commission, with excluded lands to be reopened to mineral leasing after 60 days subject to law. The Commission's nations said their commitment to the landscape continues, and litigation challenging the reductions has been sought. The episode shows both the possibilities and vulnerabilities of stewardship dependent on executive policy.

Bears Ears, named for prominent paired buttes in southeastern Utah, lies within a landscape containing many archaeological, historical, and culturally important places. In 2015 five Indigenous nations—the Hopi Tribe, Navajo Nation, Ute Mountain Ute Tribe, Ute Indian Tribe, and Zuni Tribe—formed the Bears Ears Inter-Tribal Coalition to advocate protection guided by Indigenous knowledge and relationships to the region. The coalition's proposal differed from conventional preservation campaigns in an important respect: its members sought a continuing institutional voice in the management of a landscape encompassing ancestral connections across present-day reservation and state boundaries.

President Barack Obama established Bears Ears National Monument in December 2016 under the Antiquities Act. The proclamation created the Bears Ears Commission to provide tribal guidance and recommendations regarding planning and management. Federal agencies retained formal land-management responsibilities; the commission was not a transfer of title to the five tribes and did not confer an unrestricted veto. Nevertheless it created an unusually visible mechanism for formally incorporating tribal knowledge into decisions about protected objects, recreation, conservation, and land use. The 2022 intergovernmental cooperative arrangement reinforced this role and became part of the monument's governance history.

Presidential action repeatedly changed the protected area. In 2017 President Donald Trump substantially reduced the monument, and in 2021 President Joe Biden restored a larger boundary. On July 13, 2026, President Trump issued Proclamation 11043 reducing the monument to approximately 121,096 acres in two units, Shash Jáa and Indian Creek. The proclamation expressly disbanded the Bears Ears Commission and ended the consultation arrangements tied to its creation, while leaving other applicable duties to consult intact. Federal documents establish what the executive declared; they do not, by themselves, finally determine the outcome of challenges concerning presidential authority or the future treatment of every excluded acre.

The historical significance is institutional instability. A cooperative framework based on presidential designations may offer meaningful participation, yet remain vulnerable to subsequent executive decisions. Coalition nations continued to articulate obligations to the landscape despite changes in monument boundaries, and advocates challenged reductions through legal and political avenues. It would be inaccurate to describe the original commission as full Indigenous co-government, but equally inaccurate to dismiss it as a purely symbolic institution. Its recommendations, agency relationships, and Indigenous expertise affected how stewardship was organized. The repeated changes also raise a broader question: how can territorial responsibilities spanning generations be protected when legal arrangements can change with an administration?

Division V — Land Back, Territorial Restoration, and the Historical Future of Indigenous Lands

Section 119.17

Tuluwat Island and the Wiyot Land-Return Movement

Wiyot people were subjected to destruction of communities and exclusion from Humboldt Bay territory, including a massacre at Tuluwat on Indian Island in 1860. The Tribe purchased land there in 2000, the City of Eureka returned land in 2004, and the remaining city-owned portion was transferred in October 2019. Restoration became linked to ceremony, healing, and control of a historically important place, an example of community-driven recovery outside a major federal court judgment.

The Wiyot homeland centers on Humboldt Bay and neighboring waterways and lands in northern California. Tuluwat on Indian Island was an important village and ceremonial place before the violent dispossession of the nineteenth century. During the 1860 attacks on Wiyot communities, including the massacre on the island, families and ceremonial traditions suffered catastrophic disruption. Subsequent exclusion from the island compounded that violence by severing the community's ability to care for an important ancestral place. The history of Tuluwat is therefore not simply a dispute over a municipal parcel; it concerns the survival and restoration of Wiyot community life after an episode of colonial violence.

Beginning in the late twentieth century, the Wiyot Tribe and its supporters undertook a sustained campaign to regain control of the site. The Tribe purchased a small parcel in 2000, creating an initial foothold. In 2004 the City of Eureka transferred nearby city-owned land, and in October 2019 the city transferred the remaining city-owned portion of the island. The steps were incremental, involving fund-raising, public advocacy, municipal decisions, and patient negotiation. A local government's voluntary return of land differed institutionally from a federal court award or congressional restoration statute, yet the result was tangible recovery of ownership.

Land return enabled restoration work that extended beyond title records. Wiyot efforts have included protecting the village area, addressing the consequences of industrial uses and contamination, and renewing cultural responsibilities and ceremonial presence. The Tribe's own published accounts describe the project as part of a longer story of healing and the restoration of relations with place. Historians should be careful not to imply that acquiring a title deed instantly repaired the violence of the 1860s or resolved every environmental problem. Restitution can create conditions for renewed community control while leaving substantial work for successive generations.

Tuluwat provides an important counterpoint to large federal claims cases. A city government, rather than Congress or the Supreme Court, made a decisive transfer. The campaign's effectiveness depended on local Indigenous political capacity, relationships with municipal officials, and the ability to make a historical injustice publicly visible. It also shows that the scale of acreage is not a reliable measure of cultural significance. A relatively small ceremonial homeland can matter profoundly to a nation's continuity, and restoration can be historic without involving millions of dollars or an expansive judicial doctrine.

Land owned directly by a nation, land held in trust by the United States, and land managed collaboratively without transfer of title carry different governmental authority and legal security. These arrangements are not equivalent achievements of territorial sovereignty.

Land recovery operates through several legal pathways whose practical effects vary. Congress can place federal land into trust for a nation, as in the Blue Lake restoration. A tribe can buy land from a willing seller, but purchased fee land may remain subject to local taxes or regulation unless another legal step changes its status. A municipality can convey land directly, as Eureka did for the Wiyot. A negotiated settlement can combine financing, acquisition authority, jurisdictional provisions, and waivers of historical claims. In Canada, declarations or negotiated recognition of Aboriginal title arise under a distinct constitutional regime. These mechanisms should be compared by their actual terms, not grouped together as equivalent expressions of sovereignty.

Federal trust acquisition is especially important in the United States. When the Department of the Interior takes qualifying land into trust under applicable law, important changes in tax and jurisdictional status may follow. Such acquisitions nevertheless involve statutory conditions, administrative proceedings, and at times litigation. The existence of trust land does not mean that all state, federal, and tribal powers disappear or merge. The Oneida experience illustrates why ordinary fee acquisition alone is not necessarily enough to restore territorial authority. The legal status of a recovered parcel influences economic planning, housing, natural-resource protection, and the ability to maintain institutions.

Cooperative stewardship provides another set of possibilities. Tribes may participate in management planning or enter agreements with agencies without acquiring formal title. Such arrangements can improve protection of cultural resources, access, and the incorporation of Indigenous knowledge. Their durability depends on the legal authority for the agreement, available funding, agency obligations, and whether the parties share final decisions or only consult. Bears Ears illustrates the consequences of executive-dependent arrangements. An agreement with a stronger statutory or contractual foundation may provide different protections, but no general label guarantees a particular outcome.

Evaluating a land-return program requires attention to the entire sequence from claim to implementation. Who initiated the proposal? Which community representatives had decision-making authority? What property interests were affected? Was the land conveyed free of restrictions or subject to continuing obligations? Can the nation exclude harmful uses, regulate development, or provide access for ceremony? What would happen if political leadership changed? These are not merely technical questions. They determine whether the outcome supports intergenerational obligations or offers only a temporary accommodation vulnerable to reversal.

Section 119.19

The Land Back Movement and Contemporary Debates over Ownership and Stewardship

Land Back has been used in contemporary Indigenous political movements to mean restoring ancestral land, securing jurisdiction, protecting sacred landscapes, obtaining ceremonial access, and shifting decision-making authority to Indigenous communities. Tribal governments, grassroots organizers, environmental organizations, private landowners, and federal and provincial authorities differ on aims and methods, and Land Back campaigns also raise questions of long-term stewardship. Symbolic recognition has limits when ownership and governing authority remain unchanged.

The phrase Land Back became widely visible in twenty-first-century Indigenous organizing, but the aspirations it gathers have a much longer history. Indigenous nations have sought the return of specific territories, defended treaty relationships, purchased land, and challenged imposed jurisdiction since the colonial era. Contemporary organizers use the expression in several senses. For some campaigns it means actual transfer of ownership; for others it means restoring tribal jurisdiction, protecting a sacred area, securing ceremonial access, or ensuring Indigenous authority in conservation decisions. Treating the phrase as a single policy program obscures these differences.

Historical examples in this chapter reveal the range of possibilities. Blue Lake was returned through federal legislation; Tuluwat through purchases and municipal transfers; Haida title was recognized through agreements, legislation, and a court order; Bears Ears involved Indigenous participation in federal stewardship without transfer of ownership. The Black Hills demand continues to emphasize restitution despite the availability of court-ordered money. These examples share a rejection of the premise that the existing pattern of title and control necessarily settles questions of historical justice, yet their desired remedies and institutional methods differ significantly.

Contemporary debates involve more than a simple division between Indigenous communities and non-Indigenous landowners. Tribal governments must weigh housing, public access, conservation, development, economic security, and the responsibilities owed to citizens. Grassroots organizers may demand changes that governments pursue more gradually or through negotiation. Conservation organizations can support return or cooperative management while maintaining their own mandates. Private owners, local governments, and public agencies may have legal interests that need to be addressed. Within Indigenous nations, people may also disagree about acceptable compromises; such debate is a normal part of political life and should not be portrayed as evidence that a nation lacks legitimate claims.

The importance of the movement is historical as well as institutional. It reframes dispossession not as an event permanently completed in the past but as an ongoing pattern of authority and exclusion that present governments can choose to alter. Restoring a tract of land can permit families to visit ancestral places, governments to protect cultural resources, and communities to plan for future generations. Yet symbolic acknowledgments do not by themselves transfer title, and a consultative role does not always assure final control. The relevant standard of success is therefore the specific change in rights, access, governance, and responsibility achieved by each campaign.

Section 119.20

The Historical Legacy of Indigenous Land Claims and Sacred Landscapes

Blue Lake restitution, Black Hills compensation, negotiated settlements, limits on religious-freedom claims, Canadian recognition of title, and contemporary land-return movements show Indigenous land recovery taking many institutional forms. Legal systems that emphasize ownership, compensation, regulation, and jurisdiction have translated Indigenous territorial relationships imperfectly. The central question remains the same: who has the authority to determine the future of ancestral lands, and what responsibilities accompany that authority?

The cases considered here do not form a straight line from dispossession to justice. The Indian Claims Commission offered a federal forum but limited remedies to money. Taos Pueblo persuaded Congress to return a sacred landscape, while the Sioux Nation's successful taking claim produced compensation without restoration. Northeastern land-claims litigation confronted the limitations of recovering land in ordinary fee ownership, and negotiated settlements provided resources while imposing new legal arrangements. These outcomes were shaped not merely by the strength of a historical claim, but by differences in institutional power, the available remedy, political organizing, and the distribution of ownership at the time of decision.

Sacred-site controversies reveal another set of legal limits. The American Indian Religious Freedom Act announced a protective policy, but did not create a universal means to prevent development. Lyng and the San Francisco Peaks litigation showed how courts could acknowledge deep religious significance yet deny a requested remedy under the legal standards presented. Oak Flat demonstrated that congressional control of federal property could place a sacred landscape in private ownership amid continuing opposition. The problem was not a lack of Indigenous arguments about the importance of place; it was the difficulty of translating those relationships into enforceable authority within the governing law.

Canadian developments illustrate a different path, though not an uncomplicated one. Delgamuukw and Haida Nation helped shape the legal treatment of Aboriginal title and consultation. The Tsilhqot'in title declaration recognized a specific territory as subject to a collective constitutional right, and the negotiated Haida agreements created an institutional framework for recognition and transition while protecting existing interests. These histories underscore that title recognition must be distinguished from the practical process of allocating and exercising governmental responsibilities. None of the cases makes the demands of other Indigenous nations automatically identical.

Community-driven recovery and cooperative stewardship add further possibilities. Tuluwat showed that land return can grow through local action, while Bears Ears demonstrated both the significance of Indigenous participation in the management of federal land and the vulnerability of arrangements tied to executive policy. Across these cases, land is simultaneously an object of law, a location of historical memory, a foundation for collective life, and a subject of political responsibility. The lasting question is not only who is registered as an owner, but whose decisions will guide the future of ancestral places and how those decisions will protect the relationship between communities and their homelands.

Chapter Summary

Indigenous claims to ancestral lands cannot be understood exclusively as disputes over ownership. Across North America, nations maintained relationships to places through government, ceremonial obligations, collective memory, resource use, and intergenerational responsibilities. Colonial law often recognized only some of those relationships. The resulting mismatch shaped the demands for restitution, access, protection, and territorial authority explored throughout the chapter.

Treaties, conquest, allotment, and the creation of federal or provincial public lands transformed the formal status of territories without necessarily severing Indigenous ties to them. The same place might be classified in public records as a forest, mine, park, or private parcel while remaining a homeland and sacred landscape. Historical analysis must therefore distinguish the authority asserted by the state from the relationships Indigenous nations continued to defend.

The Indian Claims Commission opened a federal forum for historical grievances in 1946, but its principal remedy was money rather than physical return. Its cases generated valuable archives while demonstrating the constraints of a compensation-centered system. Claimants differed over whether financial awards could be used for community development or would inadequately convert an unresolved territorial demand into a monetary settlement.

Taos Pueblo secured restoration of approximately 48,000 acres around Blue Lake in 1970 through congressional action following decades of Pueblo advocacy. The Black Hills case reached a different outcome: the Supreme Court in 1980 upheld compensation for a taking, while many Lakota continued to reject money as a substitute for the sacred homeland. These cases illustrate why the institutional power to order restitution matters as much as the recognition of historical wrongdoing.

Maine and Oneida demonstrate further limits. The 1980 Maine settlement included the Passamaquoddy Tribe, Penobscot Nation, and Houlton Band of Maliseet Indians under distinct arrangements, providing resources while establishing a contested legal framework. Oneida litigation preserved the relevance of historical land claims, but the 2005 Sherrill judgment clarified that purchasing former reservation parcels did not automatically restore tax immunity or full jurisdictional authority.

Sacred-site disputes exposed the weakness of legal approaches focused primarily on coercion against religious believers or procedures for consultation. The 1978 American Indian Religious Freedom Act declared policy but did not create universal enforceable protection. In Lyng the Supreme Court rejected a Free Exercise challenge even while recognizing the importance of the threatened landscape. Later San Francisco Peaks litigation showed the interpretive limits of RFRA in another setting.

Oak Flat shows how federal land transactions can shift a sacred site into private ownership despite longstanding Indigenous objection. Congress legislated the exchange in 2014, the transfer to Resolution Copper was completed in March 2026, and opposition continued afterward. The chronology illustrates why a court decision, conveyance of legal title, and implementation of an industrial project must be carefully distinguished as separate historical events.

Canadian Aboriginal title developed through a different constitutional framework. Delgamuukw changed the treatment of title and oral histories; Haida Nation elaborated the Crown duty to consult; and the 2014 Tsilhqot'in decision declared title over a specific area. Haida Gwaii followed a negotiated and judicial path, with agreements in 2024 and a 2025 consent declaration that recognized title while expressly protecting existing private-property arrangements and providing for staged implementation.

Bears Ears and Tuluwat illustrate two further forms of territorial politics. The Wiyot Tribe recovered portions of a culturally vital island through purchase and municipal transfers culminating in 2019. At Bears Ears, an intertribal coalition obtained a formal role in federal monument planning without a transfer of ownership, but the 2026 proclamation reduced the monument and ended the Bears Ears Commission. These cases highlight both community agency and the different durability of legal arrangements.

The Land Back movement gathers these experiences without reducing them to a single remedy. Land can be returned, purchased, held in trust, recognized as Aboriginal title, or managed through agreement; these mechanisms produce different combinations of control, access, and responsibility. The enduring historical question is who has the recognized authority to determine the future of ancestral territories, and how that authority can support both present communities and generations not yet born.

Chapter Reflection

The legal history of land claims reveals a recurring difficulty: governments may treat land primarily as an asset to convey or regulate, while Indigenous nations understand it through inherited political and ceremonial responsibilities. Neither statutes nor court decisions can be interpreted responsibly without considering both the legal question presented and the broader territorial relationship at issue.

The cases also reveal different forms of historical agency. Pueblo leaders secured a congressional return, Lakota communities continued to press a territorial demand despite an award, Wiyot citizens pursued local restoration, and Canadian nations combined litigation with negotiation. These actions were not merely reactions to outside policy; they were sustained efforts to define and pursue Indigenous political futures.

Land restitution and stewardship should be assessed by concrete outcomes. Who controls access, can prevent harmful use, funds management, and retains the authority to alter arrangements? The 2025–2026 examples show that recognition and transfer may occur alongside renewed loss or legal uncertainty. A single label such as protection cannot substitute for that analysis.

The next chapters turn to water, cultural repatriation, environmental knowledge, extraction, and contemporary governance. Each returns to the same institutional question posed here: whether Indigenous nations can carry responsibilities to ancestral places forward through arrangements that are durable, legitimate, and meaningful to the communities themselves.
