Indigenous Environmental Knowledge

Chapter Opening

A hunter reading the color of sea ice, a clam harvester tending a stone-walled terrace on a Pacific shore, a Hidatsa gardener choosing which seed corn to save, and a Haudenosaunee speaker opening a council by giving thanks to the waters, the plants, and the winds are doing different things. Each is also drawing on a body of knowledge about the living world that was built, tested, and corrected over many generations.

This chapter opens Part XXIII, which examines Indigenous peoples and the environment from the late twentieth century to the present. Before the chapters that follow take up fire, fisheries, mining, pipelines, and climate change, students need to understand what Indigenous environmental knowledge is, how it has been produced and carried across generations, and why it became a subject of law, science, and politics. The chapter therefore treats knowledge itself as a historical subject. It asks how Indigenous nations came to know their homelands in detail, how colonial institutions ignored, restricted, or borrowed that knowledge, and how Indigenous governments and scholars have worked since the 1970s to assert authority over it.

The subject is easily misread in two opposite ways. One reading treats Indigenous peoples as timeless "natural conservationists" whose harmony with nature requires no historical explanation. The other treats their knowledge as folklore with no bearing on measurement, management, or policy. Both readings deny Indigenous peoples their history. The evidence points elsewhere: Indigenous communities across the continent developed systematic, place-specific, and continually revised knowledge of animals, plants, waters, weather, ice, soils, and seasons. They also made choices about harvest, territory, and use that sometimes protected resources and sometimes did not. Those choices were shaped by population, technology, ethics, law, and, after contact, by markets and colonial pressure.

The chapter's geographic frame is the continent as defined in the textbook's Editorial Scope. Inuit sea-ice knowledge in Nunavut, Greenland, and Alaska, Coast Salish and Kwakwaka'wakw shellfish management, Anishinaabe wild-rice harvesting, Hopi dry farming, Hidatsa river-bottom gardening, and community forest management in Oaxaca belong in the same analysis, even though they sit in different modern countries. The chapter does not repeat the deep history of agriculture assigned to Chapter 13, the Haudenosaunee political history of Chapter 26, or the detailed treatments of fire (Chapter 134), fisheries and wildlife stewardship (Chapter 135), extractive industry (Chapter 136), pipelines (Chapter 137), and climate (Chapter 138). It supplies the conceptual and historical foundation those chapters build upon.

The evidence is varied and unevenly distributed. Historians and ecologists draw on oral histories and teachings, Indigenous place names, community-held maps, and the field notes of ethnographers and botanists who relied heavily on Indigenous experts, often without naming them. They also use archaeological features such as fish weirs, shellfish terraces, and culturally modified trees, along with the records of fur traders, land-claims research, and the written work of Indigenous scholars. Each source type has limits. Oral teachings are shaped by the communities that hold them and by protocols governing who may speak. Ethnographic records reflect the questions and assumptions of outsiders. Archaeological features can show what people did but not always why.

Environmental knowledge also became a field of political struggle. Treaty negotiations, land-claims research, wildlife boards, impact assessments, and international agreements all raised the same questions. Who counts as an expert on a river, a caribou herd, or a coastline? Can knowledge held by Elders and harvesters stand beside the measurements of government scientists? And who decides how that knowledge is recorded, shared, and used? Sections 133.12 and 133.19 show that answers developed unevenly across the United States, Canada, Mexico, and Greenland, and that they remain contested.

The chapter proceeds through five divisions and twenty sections. The first establishes the foundations of Indigenous environmental knowledge; the second examines living environments; the third turns to teaching, classification, and governance; the fourth follows colonial disruption and the changing relationship with science; and the fifth examines contemporary stewardship, regional comparisons, consent, and sovereignty. The chapters that follow investigate cultural burning, fisheries, extraction, pipelines, and climate change in greater depth.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define Indigenous environmental knowledge and distinguish it from related terms such as traditional ecological knowledge, Indigenous science, and traditional knowledge.
  2. Explain how knowledge about animals, plants, waters, ice, and weather was produced through observation, practice, and testing across generations.
  3. Describe the roles of language, place names, story, ceremony, and kinship in storing and transmitting environmental knowledge.
  4. Analyze relational ethics and Indigenous law as frameworks governing harvest and use, using examples such as the Haudenosaunee Thanksgiving Address and the Mi'kmaw concept of netukulimk.
  5. Compare knowledge systems in at least four regions of the continent, including the Arctic, the Northwest Coast, the Great Lakes, and the Southwest.
  6. Evaluate evidence that many North American landscapes were cultivated and managed, and explain why historians criticize the "pristine wilderness" idea.
  7. Assess how dispossession, game and park laws, residential and boarding schools, and dam construction disrupted the practice and transmission of knowledge.
  8. Interpret the "ecological Indian" debate and explain why both romantic and dismissive images mislead.
  9. Trace the movement of Indigenous knowledge into land claims, co-management, and environmental review from the 1970s to the 2020s.
  10. Explain the concept of knowledge sovereignty and the principles Indigenous nations have adopted to govern research and data.
  11. Analyze a primary source of disputed authorship and explain why verifying the authorship of environmental statements attributed to Indigenous leaders matters.
  12. Connect this chapter to the later treatments of fire, fisheries, extraction, pipelines, and climate in Chapters 134 through 138.

Historical Context

Students arriving at this chapter have already met the long history of Indigenous environmental adaptation. Volume I traced how the first peoples entered a continent of changing glacial and postglacial environments and developed regional economies (Chapters 2 through 12). Volume II examined the spread of agriculture and the societies that depended on it (Chapters 13 through 24), and Volume III surveyed the peoples and homelands of the fifteenth century (Chapters 25 through 36). Throughout those volumes, Indigenous peoples appear as observers and managers of particular environments: coastal fishers, desert farmers, forest gardeners, river-valley townspeople, and Arctic hunters.

Later volumes explained how that relationship was disrupted. Epidemics (Chapter 40), the fur trade (Chapter 46), the destruction of the buffalo economy (Chapter 81), and the reservation and reserve order (Chapter 84) all reduced access to territories where knowledge was lived. The residential and boarding schools (Chapters 87 and 88) interrupted transmission between generations. In the twentieth century, Indigenous nations moved from defending land and resources to asserting authority over them, through treaty-rights campaigns (Chapters 106 and 113), land-claims settlements (Chapters 115 through 117), and water and land struggles (Chapters 119 and 120). Language revitalization and tribal colleges (Chapters 123 and 124) rebuilt the institutions through which knowledge could be taught.

Three developments of the 1970s through the 1990s shaped how environmental knowledge entered public life. Indigenous nations negotiating land claims discovered that their legal arguments often depended on documenting land use, place names, and harvesting histories. Biologists, anthropologists, and geographers who worked with hunters and fishers began to report that local observations sometimes detected changes before formal surveys did. And a growing international debate about biodiversity and development produced documents that referred to the knowledge of Indigenous and local communities. These developments are examined here in the order they unfolded.

A caution about vocabulary is needed at the outset. No single Indigenous term or institution unites the varied knowledge traditions of the continent. Terms such as "traditional ecological knowledge" are largely scholarly and administrative conventions, developed from the 1980s onward, and many Indigenous communities use their own words and concepts. This textbook uses the general term "Indigenous environmental knowledge" and points to nation-specific terms wherever the evidence allows.

Division I — Foundations of Indigenous Environmental Knowledge

Section 133.1

Indigenous Environmental Knowledge as a Historical System

Indigenous environmental knowledge is best understood as an accumulation of observations, practices, and ethical commitments tied to specific places. A hunter on the Beaufort Sea needs to judge whether ice will bear a sled. A Coast Salish harvester needs to know when a shellfish bed is safe to dig and when it should rest. A Pueblo farmer must decide where in a wash to plant and which seed will germinate with little rain. Such decisions depended on information about weather, plants, animals, soils, tides, and water that could be wrong in ways with immediate consequences: hunger, drowning, poisoning, or a failed crop. Where mistakes were costly and conditions varied from year to year, communities had strong incentives to observe closely, share what they learned, and correct inherited assumptions.

The scale of the knowledge was also immense. Ethnobotanical studies across the continent have recorded Indigenous uses for thousands of plant species as foods, medicines, fibers, dyes, tools, and building materials. Hunters and fishers distinguished animal populations, migration timing, and seasonal condition in detail. Navigators learned to read swells, snowdrift patterns, star positions, and river currents. Treating such knowledge as an undifferentiated "folk wisdom" misses its specificity. Knowledge among the Inuit of the central Arctic differs from that of the Dene of the Subarctic forest, and both differ from that of the O'odham of the Sonoran Desert, because each was built in a different environment through different practices.

This chapter treats that knowledge as historical in two senses. First, it changed: communities adopted new crops, adapted to new species and technologies, and revised understandings as conditions shifted. Second, it was caught up in the history of colonization, which both drew on Indigenous expertise and weakened the conditions in which it was lived. Understanding both dimensions prevents the error of treating Indigenous environmental knowledge as either an unchanging relic or a recently discovered resource.

The vocabulary for this subject is itself a product of recent history. Anthropologists in the mid-twentieth century studied "ethnobotany" and "ethnozoology," terms that framed Indigenous knowledge as a topic for outside study of "folk" classification. From the 1980s, researchers in ecology, anthropology, and resource management began using "traditional ecological knowledge," often abbreviated TEK, to describe knowledge held by Indigenous and other long-resident communities about their environments. Scholars such as Fikret Berkes emphasized that such knowledge combined observation, practice, and belief, and that it evolved through adaptation rather than remaining fixed. The term spread through government and academic usage, and it was incorporated into laws, agreements, and research programs.

Many Indigenous scholars and communities have criticized the term. "Traditional" can suggest a static past, "ecological" can separate environmental knowledge from law, spirituality, and governance, and "knowledge" can imply information that can be extracted and stored in a database. Alternatives include "Indigenous knowledge," "Indigenous science," "Native science" (a phrase associated with Gregory Cajete of Santa Clara Pueblo), and nation-specific terms. In Nunavut, Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit, usually translated as that which Inuit have long known, guides government policy and includes principles about decision-making through discussion, skill development, working together, respect for the environment, and resourcefulness. In Mi'kmaw communities, netukulimk refers to the use of natural resources in ways that sustain community well-being without damaging the long-term integrity of the environment. In each case the concept links knowledge, ethics, and governance.

An environmental knowledge system is not a list of isolated useful plants or techniques. It joins observations about the distribution of living things to explanations of why they occur, practices for working with them, rules about appropriate conduct, and institutions that preserve and revise what is learned. An ice traveler assesses weather, ocean current, and the structure of a floe within the same decision. A woodland harvester considers the age of a plant stand, the route to reach it, the claims of other families, and the consequences of taking too much. Such decisions make ecological understanding an element of social history as well as natural history.

The word Indigenous indicates a relationship between peoples, histories, and territories; it does not authorize a single definition imposed on all North American nations. The knowledge of Tlingit fishers, Diné pastoralists, Inuit hunters, and Maya milpa farmers differs not only in subject matter but in the authorities who validate instruction and the circumstances in which knowledge may be shared. Some knowledge may be publicly taught; other material may be linked to a particular clan, lineage, ceremony, or practitioner. Scholarly comparison becomes useful when it specifies those differences instead of erasing them under a convenient continental label.

Evidence for these systems appears unevenly across time. Archaeologists can identify fish traps, constructed beaches, canals, or modified vegetation but often cannot reconstruct every rule governing them. Oral histories preserve relationships and interpretations that a structure alone cannot disclose, but they must be approached with attention to language, historical context, and the purposes of their tellers. Ethnographies record detailed practices, sometimes through anonymous Indigenous informants whose contributions were appropriated by a named researcher. More recent collaborative projects attempt to correct that imbalance through attribution, consent, and shared control over results.

The chronology also requires care. Many practices discussed here existed before European contact, yet their contemporary form may include metal tools, motors, digital maps, new crops, and observations about rapid environmental changes. Change does not automatically disqualify knowledge as Indigenous. Conversely, identifying an ancient antecedent does not prove that every contemporary activity is a direct and uninterrupted survival. This chapter treats continuity, innovation, borrowing, suppression, and renewal as processes to be explained with historical evidence rather than as competing tests of authenticity.

Historical Vocabulary
Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK) and Indigenous Knowledge

Definition. Traditional ecological knowledge refers, in the scholarly and administrative usage that developed from the 1980s, to knowledge, practices, and beliefs about the relationships among living beings and their environment, developed through long experience in a place and transmitted across generations. "Indigenous knowledge" is a broader and now more common term that is not limited to ecology.

Historical context. The term TEK entered wide use as researchers worked with Indigenous hunters, fishers, and elders in resource-management and land-claims settings. It was later adopted in international and national policy documents.

Why it matters. The labels used determine whether knowledge is treated as supplementary "data" or as part of a living system of law, ethics, and governance. Chapters 145 and 146 return to these questions.

Potential misunderstanding. "Traditional" does not mean unchanging or ancient in every detail. Much Indigenous environmental knowledge is continually revised, and it includes observations about recent changes such as altered ice conditions or newly arrived species.

Section 133.2

Land, Place, and the Formation of Ecological Knowledge

That knowledge was not abstract. It was carried by people with particular roles, learned through participation, and attached to named places. In many nations, responsibility for stewarding certain places or resources was assigned to families, clans, or house groups. The Gitxsan and Wet'suwet'en house territories documented in the Delgamuukw litigation (Chapter 117) are one example. In Cree communities of eastern James Bay (Eeyou Istchee), family hunting territories are overseen by stewards, often called tallymen in English. Such arrangements tied knowledge to responsibility: the person who knew a territory best was expected to guide its use.

Place-based expertise develops from repeated encounters. A coastal family might learn which headlands shelter a canoe in a particular wind, where currents reverse on a spring tide, and which shellfish beds become accessible at a low tide. Far inland, knowledge may turn on the relation between spring runoff and valley-bottom planting, or on the places where animals cross a watershed during different seasons. Such information is practical, but its geography is also political: knowing an area does not necessarily grant permission to enter it, and the authority to authorize use may be exercised by families, houses, councils, or nations.

Indigenous maps did not always take the form of paper documents. Routes could be learned through stories attached to landmarks, journeys with experienced travelers, place names recording resource conditions, and accounts of former settlements. These systems could support travel over very large distances without organizing the land into the surveyed grids later used by settler governments. When colonial officials translated territory into surveyed parcels, reserves, or public lands, they often reduced such complex patterns of access and responsibility to simplified boundaries. Land-use and occupancy maps created during twentieth-century claims negotiations translated some Indigenous spatial knowledge into forms legible to courts, while inevitably leaving other relationships undocumented.

Environmental variation mattered within regions as much as between them. Not every Arctic shore presents the same ice conditions; a lake connected to a river system changes differently from an isolated lake; and a north-facing mountain slope may hold moisture and plant communities absent from an adjacent sunny slope. Skilled environmental observers therefore attend to microhabitats rather than assuming a whole cultural region behaves uniformly. The distinction helps explain why a standardized government survey may miss locally important differences even when its measurements are technically accurate at the scale for which it was designed.

Long occupation also creates knowledge of unusual events. Recollections of exceptionally late freezing, failed salmon runs, insect outbreaks, or destructive storms can provide historical baselines unavailable in a brief instrumental record. But memories are not automatically annual measurements: chronology may be relative, and particular episodes may be remembered because they were extraordinary. Strong historical analysis asks what the account can show, how it can be dated, and what independent records or environmental proxies might corroborate it. This is especially important when knowledge is used in contemporary management or legal proceedings.

The relationship between a homeland and a knowledge tradition is not one-way. People transformed places through harvesting, cultivation, movement, burning, and construction, while those transformations changed the conditions observed by later generations. A clam garden created a more productive beach and a new setting for learning how clams responded to sediment and tidal exposure. An irrigation system altered water distribution and the obligations of those maintaining it. A place thus became both the source and the product of environmental knowledge, which is why territorial dispossession could destroy infrastructure for learning as well as deprive people of material resources.

Section 133.3

Observation, Experimentation, and Environmental Understanding

How did communities with oral traditions build reliable environmental knowledge? The answer lies in repeated observation, shared testing, and institutions for passing information on. Much knowledge was produced through everyday practice: setting and checking fish weirs, planting seed and noting which varieties survived drought, tracking animals through changing seasons, or recording which snow conditions preceded dangerous ice. Observations accumulated over lifetimes and were compared across households and generations. When many observers watched the same river, ice edge, or berry patch year after year, patterns that single-season scientific surveys could miss became apparent, including long cycles, rare events, and gradual shifts.

Communities also conducted deliberate trials. Farmers saved and exchanged seed, selecting for traits suited to local soils and moisture, a process that produced the many maize landraces of the Southwest, the Plains river valleys, and the Northeast. Harvesters of plants such as camas, basket materials, and berries observed the effects of digging, pruning, and rotation on later yields. Such practice resembled experiment in its logic of trial and adjustment, although it was not described in the vocabulary of controlled variables and hypotheses. Scholars therefore caution against two simple claims: that Indigenous knowledge is "just like" laboratory science, and that it lacks empirical rigor. Its methods and standards of evidence differed, and in many cases it addressed different questions, including ethical and relational ones.

Observation was often organized as comparison rather than as the recording of one remarkable sign. An experienced hunter may combine wind direction, the texture of snow, tracks, animal behavior, and memories of previous journeys, rejecting a route when several indicators point to danger. A farmer might compare seeds planted on different slopes or in differently prepared soil, while recalling years when a particular planting date failed. The information becomes reliable through repeated successful use, correction by other practitioners, and awareness of exceptional circumstances. Those processes can be systematic even when they do not produce a written laboratory notebook.

Environmental knowledge can also be wrong, partial, or in conflict. The unusually warm year may not resemble the years from which a rule was learned. An elder's recollection of a migration corridor may no longer predict present animal movements after road construction or habitat change. Different families may disagree about the condition of a resource because they visit different parts of its range. Historical scholarship gains credibility by recording uncertainties and disagreements rather than treating Indigenous expertise as universally accurate. The same standard should apply to reports by professional scientists and administrators, whose surveys also contain measurement errors, assumptions, and changing categories.

The archaeological record sometimes permits a test of practical hypotheses. Researchers studying ancient shellfish terraces can compare walled and unwalled beaches and ask how sediment accumulation, tidal elevation, and clam growth respond to construction. Archaeobotanical remains can reveal which seeds or crop varieties were present at a site, but linking them to a specific harvesting rule requires additional evidence. Documentary and oral evidence may explain why people selected a location or restricted access; an excavation alone generally cannot reconstruct an entire customary legal order. The strongest conclusions emerge when independent forms of evidence support one another without being treated as interchangeable.

European naturalists regularly depended on Indigenous people for field identification, routes, medicines, and descriptions of seasonal behavior. Yet official publications commonly credited the visiting collector rather than the guides and knowledge holders whose expertise made the work possible. The nineteenth-century collection of plants and animals thus sometimes preserved valuable Indigenous observations while also severing them from their social context. The historian's responsibility is not merely to praise Indigenous knowledge in retrospect but to recover the labor and authority through which that knowledge entered archives and scientific collections.

The modern expression Two-Eyed Seeing, discussed in Section 133.16, may encourage constructive collaboration, but it should not be read backward as a single ancient research method practiced throughout North America. Indigenous communities developed many different ways of evaluating observations. Likewise, scientific disciplines use multiple methods rather than one universal procedure. The meaningful comparison concerns what question is being asked, who is entitled to make a judgment, how an observation may be checked, and who bears the consequences when a decision proves wrong.

Section 133.4

Ecological Relationships, Reciprocity, and Environmental Responsibilities

Indigenous environmental knowledge is frequently inseparable from ethical and legal frameworks. Many nations understand other living beings and features of the land as relatives or as persons with whom humans share obligations. This does not mean that all Indigenous peoples hold identical beliefs; the nations discussed in this textbook have distinct cosmologies, languages, and legal orders. It does mean that in many of them, knowing a place includes knowing how to behave in it.

The Haudenosaunee Thanksgiving Address, recited at the opening of councils and many other gatherings, offers a clear example. Spoken in the Haudenosaunee languages, it proceeds in a set order, greeting and giving thanks to the people, the earth, the waters, the fish, the plants, the animals, the trees, the birds, the winds, the sun, the moon, and the stars, among other beings. The address does not simply praise nature. It establishes an inventory of relationships and reminds speakers and listeners of their obligations to them. It also functions as a mnemonic structure, a spoken map of the living world.

Other nations articulate similar principles in their own terms. Mi'kmaw netukulimk emphasizes using resources to meet needs without undermining the capacity of the environment to support future generations. Anishinaabe teachings about manoomin (wild rice) include rules about timing and methods of harvest that leave seed to fall and reseed the beds, and the White Earth Band of Ojibwe adopted a tribal law in 2018 recognizing legal rights of manoomin. Botanist and writer Robin Wall Kimmerer, a citizen of the Citizen Potawatomi Nation, has described a set of principles of reciprocity and restraint she calls the Honorable Harvest, drawn from Potawatomi and other Indigenous teachings, such as taking only what is needed, giving thanks, and sustaining the one who sustains you. These examples show that harvest rules were embedded in ethics, ceremony, and community law rather than imposed only by outside regulators.

These frameworks also help explain a recurring point of conflict with state regulators. When an agency treats a fish or a caribou herd as a stock to be allocated, and a nation treats it as part of a relationship that carries duties, the two parties may agree on a harvest figure while disagreeing about what the figure means. Chapter 135 examines how that tension has shaped treaty-based fisheries and wildlife management.

Reciprocity can carry practical consequences. Rules may limit the size or age of animals taken, require respect for an opening harvest, reserve portions for elders, or assign obligations to restore a gathering place. In some settings ceremonies reinforce conduct that also affects ecological sustainability; in others their primary meaning cannot be reduced to resource management. Historians should resist translating every spiritual duty into a modern conservation principle. A community may regard the proper treatment of a harvested animal as important even when that act produces no measurable change in a stock assessment.

Not all communities organized resource use in the same way. Northwest Coast houses could hold defined rights and duties in relation to fishing sites and harvesting places, while other communities emphasized more flexible seasonal access negotiated among kin. In the northern Plains, mobility and political alliances helped govern access to shifting animal herds. Agricultural villages required labor and rules to maintain canals, fields, and stored seed. These institutions were modified through warfare, trade, diplomacy, population change, and changes in technology; portraying them as universally egalitarian or purely communal would be an additional distortion.

A historical account must also distinguish ideals from behavior. Communities faced hunger, competition, environmental shocks, and internal disputes. Resource depletion and habitat alteration occurred in Indigenous as well as non-Indigenous histories, although their scale and causes varied widely. The fact that a particular people possessed teachings of restraint does not prove every harvest met those teachings. Conversely, a documented episode of depletion does not invalidate an entire system of accumulated ecological observation. Separating norms, institutions, and outcomes makes comparison more accurate and avoids the romantic stereotype examined later in the chapter.

These ethical and legal frameworks became politically consequential when conservation officers, courts, or researchers required Indigenous communities to translate their responsibilities into governmental categories. A rule embedded in kinship and ceremony might be described in an agency document merely as a voluntary tradition. A place where obligations are held by a house or clan might be labeled unowned public land. Such translations are not neutral: they can change who is consulted, which practices are enforceable, and whether environmental knowledge is treated as a source of law or as information supplied to another authority.

Historical Vocabulary
Kincentric Ecology

Definition. A term used by ethnobotanist Enrique Salmón, of Rarámuri descent, to describe Indigenous perceptions in which humans and other living beings form an extended family or community with mutual responsibilities.

Historical context. The concept was proposed in scholarly literature around 2000 to explain how many Indigenous communities speak of animals, plants, and places as relatives. It builds on earlier work by Native scholars and elders about relational understandings of the world.

Why it matters. It helps explain why harvesting practices in many communities are governed by ritual, gratitude, and restraint, and why disputes over resource use can be disputes about relationship and law as well as economics.

Potential misunderstanding. The term is an analytical generalization. It does not mean all Indigenous nations hold the same beliefs, nor that members of these nations always acted in line with ideals. Chapter 133's debate feature returns to the gap between ethics and practice.

Historical Debate

The "Ecological Indian": Image, Evidence, and Responsibility

The question: Is the idea that Indigenous peoples were conservationists an accurate description of historical practice, a useful ethical principle, a stereotype, or some combination of these?

Interpretation A — The image is a stereotype that obscures history: In The Ecological Indian: Myth and History (1999), historian Shepard Krech III argued that Indigenous peoples were human societies that, like others, sometimes caused environmental change, and that the image of an inherently conservationist "ecological Indian" was largely a modern construct shaped by non-Indigenous environmentalism. He argued that evidence for some cases of intensive hunting or resource depletion complicates the portrait. Earlier, historian Calvin Martin's Keepers of the Game (1978) argued that Indigenous hunters, shaken by epidemics, abandoned their ethical relations with animals and over-hunted for the fur trade. That argument drew sharp criticism from historians for its treatment of Indigenous belief and its reliance on thin evidence, including a critical collection edited by Krech himself (1981).
Interpretation B — Many Indigenous systems show long-term, effective stewardship: Critics of Krech and of Martin, including some Indigenous scholars and historians, argue that the evidence for sustained management systems is strong, that depletion cases are often tied to colonial markets and disruption rather than to Indigenous ethics, and that stressing exceptions can feed the argument that Indigenous peoples have no special claim to environmental authority. A volume edited by Michael Harkin and David Rich Lewis, Native Americans and the Environment (2007), collects essays examining the debate from several angles.
Interpretation C — Ethics and practice should be separated, and context specified: A third approach holds that the question is badly posed when asked about all Indigenous peoples at once. It asks instead what ethical principles specific nations articulated, what practices they followed, what institutions enforced restraint, how outcomes varied over time, and how colonial conditions altered incentives. On this view, recognizing that ideals and conduct can diverge makes neither group less human nor knowledge less valuable.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on archaeological evidence of managed landscapes and of local depletion, ethnographic accounts of harvest rules, fur-trade records, oral teachings, and studies of present-day management. The disagreement concerns scale, causation, and the interpretation of evidence rather than whether Indigenous peoples had knowledge of their environments.

Why it matters: Depending on how the question is answered, Indigenous environmental authority may be argued to rest on a claim about character, on demonstrated results, or on legal and political rights as nations. This chapter holds that the last of these does not depend on resolving the first.

Division II — Understanding North America’s Living Environments

Section 133.5

Plants, Vegetation, and Indigenous Botanical Knowledge

Botanical knowledge involves more than knowing whether a plant can be eaten. It includes the identification of similar species, recognition of toxic look-alikes, understanding of habitat requirements, timing of harvest, and the effects of different methods of gathering. Indigenous harvesters learned to distinguish root patches ready for digging from those needing another season, and to recognize where baskets, medicines, dyes, and construction materials could be gathered without exhausting the source. In coastal British Columbia, research associated with Nancy Turner and Indigenous collaborators has documented generations of knowledge of edible roots, berries, cedar, and other plant resources, alongside social institutions that regulate access.

The camas plant offers a concrete illustration. Its bulbs were an important food in parts of the Pacific Northwest, where knowledgeable harvesters distinguished edible camas from poisonous death camas. Digging could loosen soil, separate bulbs, and alter the composition of productive meadows, but practices differed by community and locale. Camas management was embedded in travel, exchange, family labor, and ceremonial life; it cannot be reduced to a single recipe for increasing yield. In a historical reconstruction, evidence from plant distributions and modified soils must be read alongside accounts by the people who worked these grounds.

In the Great Lakes, the collection of manoomin, or wild rice, required knowledge of water depth, seasonal maturation, wind, and the fragility of stems. Harvesters traditionally worked from canoes, using poles and short sticks to bend and strike plants without uprooting them. Grain that fell into the water could contribute to future growth, although success depended on broader habitat conditions and water management. In the Southeast, river cane provided basketry material but required understanding of cane brakes as living stands rather than simply cutting the nearest culms. Across regions, harvesting and habitat stewardship were intertwined but not identical practices.

The role of plants in medicine presents different evidentiary questions. Indigenous healers developed extensive knowledge of preparation, dosage, season, and appropriate use, but a recorded historical remedy is not in itself proof of modern clinical safety or efficacy. Commercial appropriation and scientific study sometimes removed medicines from the cultural and intellectual traditions in which they were held. Historians should explain both the original expertise and the limits of available documentation without reproducing restricted teachings or recommending unverified medical treatments.

Agricultural selection also produced botanical knowledge. Hopi dry-farming traditions, Hidatsa seed-keeping, and diverse Mexican milpa practices illustrate how farmers monitored germination, moisture, soil, and long-term fertility. Seeds passed through households and exchange networks; varieties could be valued for taste, storage, resistance to drought, or use in ceremonial life. Colonial records often described these systems as primitive because they differed from European monoculture or plow agriculture, yet those differences were frequently informed adaptations to particular ecologies and labor arrangements.

The specialized history of cultivation appears in earlier chapters of the textbook, and Chapter 134 addresses cultural burning in detail. Here the historical point is that plants were objects of careful attention within institutions of teaching, tenure, work, and exchange. An inventory of useful species captures only a fraction of the knowledge. The decisions about when to harvest, what to leave, who may enter a patch, and how to recognize decline connect botanical observation to environmental governance.

Section 133.6

Animal Behavior, Migration, and Ecological Observation

Knowledge of animals begins with differences among individuals, age classes, seasons, and habitats. Hunters may attend to tracks, calls, feeding grounds, breeding periods, fat reserves, or movements preceding storms. Such observations require repeated encounters and are often shaped by the techniques through which a community hunts or fishes. In the Arctic, knowledge of seal breathing holes and marine mammal travel relates to the changing structure of sea ice. In the Subarctic, the movements of caribou and other ungulates may be understood in relation to snow crust, insects, and seasonal forage. On the Plains, the movements of bison historically connected environmental observation with the labor and organization of mobile communities.

A migration is not simply a line drawn on a map. Animals can shift routes with climatic variation, human disturbance, predator pressure, and the condition of feeding grounds. Families who travel repeatedly through an area may recognize local deviations from the pattern recorded in a regional wildlife survey. Yet an account from one community may cover only part of an animal's range. Wildlife management therefore benefits from asking how the scales of local observation and formal population estimates relate, and from acknowledging why communities situated in different parts of a migration corridor may reach different judgments.

The late-1970s bowhead whale dispute shows these questions with unusual clarity. An International Whaling Commission assessment then placed the Bering-Chukchi-Beaufort population at approximately 600 to 2,000 animals and prompted restrictions on Alaska Native subsistence whaling. Iñupiat hunters challenged the assessment from direct experience of migration, particularly whales passing beneath ice and beyond the view of shore-based observers. The crisis helped bring the Alaska Eskimo Whaling Commission into being in 1977; it was incorporated in 1981, when a cooperative agreement with U.S. authorities established a distinctive management framework. Improved visual and acoustic counting subsequently supported much higher population estimates. The historic estimate is not a present population count.

This example should not be recast as Indigenous insight defeating science. Hunters also required reliable assessments, while researchers could improve their methods by recognizing what they had failed to observe. The dispute concerned survey design, uncertainty, subsistence livelihoods, international regulation, and the authority to determine acceptable risk. Joint management did not eliminate these tensions; it created procedures through which some of them could be negotiated. Chapter 135 will address the broader legal and treaty dimensions of fisheries and wildlife stewardship.

Animal knowledge also shapes food security and social relations. The distribution of a large catch, preparation of hides, maintenance of fishing gear, and observance of seasonal restrictions require skills held by people who may not participate in the capture itself. Children learn signs and proper conduct through work in households and on trips. Records written only from the viewpoint of a licensed hunter or federal biologist can overlook this social infrastructure. Environmental knowledge is therefore not confined to a single occupation or gender, even when certain tasks are conventionally associated with particular roles.

Cause and Effect

Bowhead Whales, Iñupiat Knowledge, and the Alaska Eskimo Whaling Commission

Long-term causes

Centuries of Iñupiat whaling tied to communities such as Utqiaġvik (Barrow) and Point Hope; commercial whaling in the nineteenth century that depleted bowhead populations; and the absence of Indigenous voices in the international bodies that regulated whaling.

Short-term causes

Scientific estimates in the 1970s, reported as a few hundred to roughly two thousand animals, suggested the population was dangerously low, and the International Whaling Commission moved toward restricting or banning the Alaska Native hunt.

Immediate trigger and context

In 1977 the Commission acted against bowhead hunting. Iñupiat whalers, who observed whales passing beneath and along ice leads in numbers not captured by aerial and ship-based surveys, disputed the estimates and mobilized politically. Federal authorities also faced legal obligations to Alaska Native communities.

Event or transformation

The North Slope Borough and federal scientists organized ice-based counts and acoustic monitoring from the late 1970s. Alaska Native whaling captains formed the Alaska Eskimo Whaling Commission in 1981, which entered a cooperative arrangement with federal authorities to manage the hunt under quotas.

Immediate effects

The hunt continued under limits. Subsequent estimates were substantially higher than the earlier figures, and Iñupiat observations about whale movement were credited with improving survey design.

Long-term consequences

The case became a widely cited example of how community knowledge can improve scientific assessment and of how co-management bodies can be built around Indigenous institutions. Subsequent assessments have described the population as growing, though management disputes and the pressures of climate change and industrial activity continue.

Historical complexity: The outcome was not produced by Iñupiat knowledge alone. It involved legal rights, political organizing, funding for new surveys, scientific collaboration, and negotiation among governments. Some scientists and Iñupiat participants continue to disagree about the relative weight of different forms of evidence. The case should be read as an example of mutual correction, not a simple vindication of one side.

Section 133.7

Water, Weather, Snow, Ice, and Seasonal Change

Water is both a physical process and the setting for routes, food production, ceremonies, and political relations. River communities learn how a channel changes after floods, where eddies form, and how seasonal low water affects travel and fishing. Lake communities observe water levels, temperature, vegetation, and the timing of spring breakup. Coastal communities combine knowledge of tides, wind, surf, current, and navigation. These distinctions are not merely geographical vocabulary: they govern everyday decisions about where to cross, when to launch, and which places are temporarily dangerous.

Arctic sea ice makes the interplay of knowledge and risk especially visible. Inuit and Iñupiat travelers distinguish new, multiyear, landfast, and drifting ice, as well as cracks, pressure ridges, and changing leads. Wind patterns and currents alter ice strength and movement, so yesterday's safe route may become hazardous. Knowledge is learned through guided travel, specialized terminology, and attention to animal behavior, and it is updated by experience. Scientific instruments can provide satellite imagery or regional temperature measurements, but they do not automatically substitute for on-the-ground assessments of the route a family will take.

Weather knowledge may draw on cloud formation, wind direction, animal behavior, snowdrift shape, seasonal plant development, and remembered cycles. The evidentiary status of individual indicators varies; some correspond closely to physical conditions, while others may be reliable only in particular localities or seasons. A historical account should not turn every traditional weather sign into a universal predictive law. It should instead examine why a sign mattered to particular travelers, how the prediction was tested in repeated use, and what happened when changing conditions made inherited relationships less dependable.

Knowledge of water also developed through engineered environments. Irrigation canals in arid agricultural regions demanded observations of gradient, runoff, sediment, maintenance needs, and social obligations for distributing water. Wetland harvesting required recognition of how flooding and drainage influence useful plants. Along the Northwest Coast, people working clam gardens attended to tidal elevation and sediment, and fishing communities managed weirs and seasonal access. The specialized histories of fisheries and engineered agricultural landscapes appear elsewhere; the common subject here is how hydrological knowledge becomes socially organized practice.

As instrumental records, aerial photography, and satellite data expanded in the twentieth century, comparisons between local accounts and agency measurements became increasingly common. Disagreements can arise because the two observers ask different questions: a satellite may map regional ice extent, while a hunter must decide whether a particular floe can safely support a vehicle today. Neither scale is automatically superior. A productive inquiry makes the question, time span, spatial scale, and uncertainty explicit before declaring that the observations agree or conflict.

Section 133.8

Soils, Wetlands, Forests, Grasslands, and Ecological Relationships

For much of the twentieth century, popular and some scholarly accounts described the Americas in 1492 as a "wilderness" in which sparse human populations had little effect on forests, grasslands, and waters. Geographer William Denevan's 1992 essay "The Pristine Myth" challenged this view, arguing that landscapes had been extensively modified by human activity, from forest clearing and farming to burning and the construction of earthworks and fish weirs. Subsequent research has supported the argument that many environments were products of long human management, although estimates of the scale of Indigenous populations, and of the effects of epidemics on landscapes after contact, remain debated (Chapter 40).

The evidence is cumulative. Clam gardens, root gardens, fish weirs, canals, raised fields, managed plant patches, and culturally modified trees show deliberate modification of environments. Pollen and charcoal records, soil studies, and archaeobotanical remains document changes in vegetation associated with cultivation and burning. Oral histories describe tending practices that archaeology can sometimes confirm. In the Eastern Woodlands, the earthworks and fields of Adena, Hopewell, and Mississippian peoples (Chapters 19 through 23) are evidence that environments were shaped to support towns and ceremonial centers. The argument is not that Indigenous peoples controlled every landscape, since climate, fire, and animals also shaped ecosystems, but that human management was one of the forces producing the environments Europeans later described.

This conclusion carries two implications that students should hold together. First, it undercuts the claim that Indigenous peoples had no meaningful relationship to land as owners or governors, an argument used to justify dispossession. Second, it discourages the opposite error of imagining that management always meant ecological harmony. Management involved choices, and the ecological effects of those choices varied. Where evidence suggests that Indigenous practices maintained biodiversity or productivity, this chapter says so, and where evidence is thin or contested, it says that too. Fire, the most widespread management tool, is the subject of Chapter 134, and the deep-time debate over megafaunal extinction belongs to Chapter 6.

A landscape's visible appearance does not reveal all the human labor that produced it. Managed root grounds may resemble natural meadows; regularly harvested berry patches may be mistaken for untended shrubs; and maintained wetlands may appear to be unmodified habitat. The older assumption that absence of European fences or plowed fields signified absence of management erased important forms of Indigenous land use. Archaeology, palynology, oral histories, and early written observations now demonstrate extensive transformation in many regions, while also revealing places where human effects were comparatively small.

Soils are central to the problem. Farmers learned the differences between well-drained and waterlogged ground, between soils capable of holding moisture and those likely to lose seed to erosion, and between freshly exposed sediment and depleted fields. In river valleys, seasonal flooding could replenish soils while also threatening crops or settlements. In northern settings, peatland, permafrost, and wetland conditions constrained where people could travel or build. Such distinctions do not depend on a single standardized classification but demonstrate an empirical attention to variation in the land.

The coastal clam garden offers a particularly tangible example of engineered habitat. Low rock walls built in the intertidal zone may retain sediment and create a broader, more favorable bench for clams. A 2019 archaeological study of nine gardens on northern Quadra Island in British Columbia used radiocarbon-dated deposits, local sea-level history, and taphonomic analysis to conclude that some studied gardens were more than 3,500 years old. The finding does not date every garden on the Northwest Coast, and interpretation requires distinguishing when a wall was built from the ages of surrounding shells. It nonetheless establishes an extraordinarily long history of deliberate marine habitat modification.

Forest and grassland relationships were likewise complex. Selective harvesting could favor particular tree or shrub communities, while grazing by wild animals, storms, insects, and fire also altered vegetation. Indigenous people sometimes used burning to change habitats, but the techniques, political controls, and ecological results varied by place and are developed in Chapter 134. Understanding those histories requires resisting both the fantasy of a continent without human impacts and the opposite claim that every ecosystem was continuously and deliberately engineered.

The idea of a cultivated landscape thus changes the interpretation of sovereignty and conservation. If a protected park is portrayed as untouched nature, the history of people who planted, harvested, built, and governed it disappears from view. Yet recognizing prior management does not prove that every contemporary practice is sustainable or that current ecological restoration has an easy historical template. Different species, climates, and communities now operate in transformed conditions. History supplies evidence about earlier relationships, not a command to reproduce them without adaptation.

Division III — Transmission, Classification, and Environmental Governance

Section 133.9

Families, Elders, Apprenticeship, and the Transmission of Ecological Knowledge

Transmission relied on layered institutions. Stories encoded warnings and instructions: where thin ice forms, how a species behaves, what a plant is for. Songs, ceremonies, and seasonal feasts marked times for harvest or restraint. Apprenticeship, especially between grandparents and grandchildren or between experienced hunters and youth, ensured skills were learned by doing. Specialized knowledge could be held by particular people, such as healers, navigators, or river and fishery stewards. Winter counts, wampum, carved and painted house posts, and other mnemonic systems introduced in Chapter 1 supported memory for events, including famines, floods, and migrations. Knowledge was not equally distributed, and protocols could restrict who might learn or repeat it.

Such systems proved sturdy but not indestructible. They depended on continued access to land, on living speakers, and on intergenerational contact, all of which colonial policy undermined, as Section 133.13 explains. Their durability, where it survived, owed much to the work of communities to maintain, teach, and revise them under pressure.

The practical setting of instruction varied by livelihood, age, and place. A child might learn to notice a wind shift while traveling with relatives, recognize the condition of a berry patch while gathering food, or understand the responsibility to share a harvest during a communal event. None of this requires the romantic claim that every child was taught identically or that all elders possessed the same expertise. Households could specialize in particular plants, fishing sites, navigation routes, or ceremonial knowledge, and apprenticeship was shaped by trust and demonstrated competence. A person might remain a learner over many decades because the environment itself never stopped presenting new cases.

Gender has been important but not uniform. Hidatsa gardens along the Missouri River were substantially managed by women, as Maxi'diwiac's account makes clear, while many accounts of Arctic hunting emphasize the expertise of male hunters but document women's extensive knowledge of animal processing, weather, travel preparation, and household provisioning. Colonial ethnographers often concentrated on activities that resembled men's economic work in European categories and overlooked knowledge transmitted in kitchens, camps, or gatherings. A fuller social history reconstructs the interdependence of tasks and recognizes differences among generations and communities.

Storytelling can function as teaching without being reducible to a literal field manual. A narrative about improper behavior at a dangerous crossing may preserve a practical warning while also expressing social norms. Seasonal ceremonies can reinforce respect for a resource without serving as a biologically quantified harvest schedule. Students should distinguish the purposes of knowledge from the methods used to remember it. Written documentation introduced by schools, ethnographers, and tribal programs may expand some forms of preservation but may also expose restricted material if community permissions are ignored.

Educational disruption operated through multiple institutions. Boarding and residential schools separated many children from languages and land-based instruction; wage labor, relocation, and restricted access to traditional harvesting grounds changed how families spent their time. These changes were uneven: some students returned with skills they used for political advocacy or professional conservation, and some families taught practices during vacations, in secret, or in new places. The record therefore supports both profound loss and active continuity, not a simple moment at which knowledge vanished.

Modern land-based programs often combine language instruction, mentorship, field observation, and practical work. Their historical significance lies partly in restoring the setting in which knowledge can be assessed through use rather than merely learned from a printed description. Such programs must still address the ordinary barriers of transport, school calendars, equipment, land access, funding, and the availability of experienced instructors. Rebuilding knowledge transmission is an institutional project as much as a cultural statement.

Everyday Life and Social History

Learning the Land: Seasonal Work, Households, and Teaching

Environmental knowledge was embedded in the ordinary calendar of work. In many communities the year was organized around a seasonal round: spring runs of fish or waterfowl, summer gathering of roots and berries, fall harvest of seeds, rice, or nuts, and winter hunting or ice fishing. The Hidatsa gardener Maxi'diwiac, whose account of the farming year was recorded in the early twentieth century, described planting, tending, and harvesting as work organized by women across families and linked to the selection and storage of seed. Her testimony also shows how knowledge was held in households and passed between generations.

Division of labor varied by nation, era, and region, and generalizations should be made carefully. In many communities women held major responsibility for gathering, processing, and gardening and for the plant knowledge those tasks required, while men commonly held knowledge connected to hunting, trapping, or certain kinds of fishing. In other settings, such as among Inuit families, both women and men held detailed knowledge of animals, weather, and travel, and responsibilities shifted with age and circumstance. Children learned by watching, assisting, and being corrected. Elders often held the memory of rare events, including years of famine, flood, or unusual animal movements, which served as the longest "datasets" a community possessed.

The disruption of such routines had human consequences. When families were forced onto reserves or reservations, when children were placed in distant schools, or when land base shrank, the daily practices through which knowledge was learned became harder or impossible to perform. Historians therefore read dispossession not only as the loss of territory but also as the loss of classrooms.

Section 133.10

Indigenous Languages, Place Names, and Ecological Classification

Language is one of the principal storehouses of environmental knowledge. Indigenous vocabularies often make distinctions that other languages do not mark, such as multiple terms for kinds of snow, ice, tides, or stages of a plant's growth. Inuit travelers distinguish sea-ice conditions by terms tied to thickness, age, and movement, and they use snowdrifts formed by prevailing winds as directional guides, a practice documented in research with hunters from Igloolik and other communities. Such vocabularies are not curiosities. They encode working knowledge that can be life-saving. When a language declines, as discussed in Chapter 123, communities risk losing a finely tuned way of describing their environment.

Place names perform similar work. A name may describe what can be gathered or hunted at a site, recall an event, mark a boundary, or attach a teaching to a landscape. The anthropologist Keith Basso, working with Western Apache communities at Cibecue, Arizona, showed in Wisdom Sits in Places (1996) how place names and the stories linked to them are used to remind people of moral lessons and proper conduct, so that traveling across the land recalls both its history and its ethics. Similar systems exist in many nations. In Nunavut and the Northwest Territories, collective projects have recorded thousands of Inuit and Dene place names, creating records useful for education and, in some cases, for claims.

Stories carry information about environmental events. Some accounts of floods, volcanic activity, earthquakes, or sea-level change have been compared by scholars with geological records, as Chapter 1 noted. Such comparisons require care. Stories compress time, express meaning, and are told for purposes beyond reporting. Where independent evidence supports a link, it strengthens the case for the story's historical memory, but a lack of corroboration does not make an account worthless. Historians treat oral knowledge as one line of evidence to be interpreted with community guidance and attention to cultural protocols.

In land-claims litigation and negotiation, place names, harvesting histories, and oral accounts moved from teaching tools into documentary evidence. The Inuit Land Use and Occupancy Project, reported in 1976 under the leadership of Milton Freeman and Inuit researchers, interviewed hunters across the Canadian Arctic and mapped travel routes, camps, and harvest areas. Its findings supported Inuit claims that eventually led to the Nunavut Agreement of 1993 (Chapter 116). In Delgamuukw v. British Columbia (1997), the Supreme Court of Canada held that courts must give oral histories appropriate weight alongside documents, a ruling that followed a trial in which the Gitxsan and Wet'suwet'en presented ceremonial and oral records of their territories. These cases established that knowledge long dismissed as legend could be admitted as evidence, though the challenges of translating it into legal form remain.

An ecological vocabulary is more than a dictionary of equivalents. Terms may classify a place by its use, a fish by its condition or behavior, or an ice surface by the dangers it presents. A translation that substitutes a general English word can lose distinctions that make a term valuable in practice. Conversely, academic writing sometimes exaggerates the number or uniqueness of Indigenous terms without studying their grammar, geography, or actual use. The defensible approach is to examine a named language community and document which distinctions its speakers regard as meaningful.

Western Apache place names described in Keith Basso's work illustrate another dimension: landscapes can become repositories of moral history. A placename may prompt a narrative that reminds a listener of earlier conduct or an ancestral obligation. Such knowledge is not always ecological in a narrow sense; it helps define the social relationships through which land use becomes legitimate. Inuit and Dene place-name projects have likewise recorded routes, hunting places, and historical events, sometimes combining community expertise with contemporary mapping. The translation of that knowledge into a digital gazetteer can assist legal claims or schools while raising questions about public disclosure.

Environmental classification also changes historically. Animals and plants may acquire new names when species arrive or when the vocabulary of commercial exchange spreads. Older terms can fall out of common use when an activity becomes impossible, yet survive in songs, ceremonial instructions, or the recollections of elders. Language revitalization therefore has an environmental dimension: restoring words may renew access to distinctions among local habitats and practices. Nevertheless, a person can possess strong practical expertise while speaking primarily English, French, or Spanish, and linguistic fluency should not be used as a crude test of who counts as a knowledge holder.

Documentation projects face choices about orthography, permission, and intended audience. A published atlas may include terms appropriate for general education but exclude locations of vulnerable cultural sites or resources. A bilingual school lesson may use a simplified classification while an expert's field vocabulary is far more elaborate. Community protocols can specify which records remain local, who may correct spellings or interpretations, and how knowledge is attributed. These decisions demonstrate that language preservation and knowledge sovereignty are related but distinct responsibilities.

The chapter's evidence can be read comparatively: a colonial map showing a newly assigned name, an Indigenous name recorded decades later, and a contemporary community account may present different versions of the same landscape. The historian asks not only which name is oldest but also what each source was designed to do. A survey could establish governmental ownership claims; a community name might direct travelers to a gathering place; a school map may seek standardization. The documents are not equivalent, and their differences reveal changing authority over environmental information.

Context: Si'ahl was a leader of Puget Sound peoples during the arrival of American settlers and negotiations with territorial officials in the 1850s. The widely reproduced "environmental" speech descends from a version published in the Seattle Sunday Star on October 29, 1887, by Henry A. Smith, who said he had taken notes at an address decades earlier, in a setting where more than one language may have been used; no surviving transcript establishes the exact original wording or translation chain. In the early 1970s, screenwriter Ted Perry wrote a script for a film about ecology that adapted Smith's text, adding sentences about the web of life and humanity's place within it. Subsequent printings frequently circulated Perry's wording as though it were Si'ahl's.

Brief paraphrase: Smith's 1887 version presents a speaker who addresses the territorial governor with dignified, ornate language about the permanence of Indigenous presence in the land, the sacredness of ancestors' burial places, and the unequal relationship between the Suquamish and the incoming settlers. The more famous ecological claims, such as humans being strands in a web, are generally traced to Perry's film script rather than to any surviving record from the 1850s. No quotation is reproduced here because the wording differs among versions and cannot be reliably attributed to Si'ahl.

Analysis: The case shows how environmental statements can become attached to Indigenous leaders through a chain of translation, memory, editing, and creative adaptation. It also shows why many scholars distrust popular quotations: even Smith's account came after decades of recollection, translation across several languages, and the literary conventions of the late nineteenth century.

Limitations: No contemporary transcript is known to survive that would allow verification of the 1850s speech. The existence of a spurious text does not mean that Si'ahl, or Puget Sound peoples, lacked relationships and responsibilities toward land and water; it means that this particular text cannot serve as evidence of them.

Perspective: The surviving versions are products of non-Indigenous authors: a nineteenth-century settler physician and writer, and a twentieth-century screenwriter. Perspectives of Suquamish and Duwamish communities about how Si'ahl's words and legacy should be remembered are largely absent from the published versions, though those communities continue to speak for themselves today.

Section 133.11

Customary Environmental Rules and Resource Stewardship

Customary environmental rules cannot be reconstructed solely from statements that people respected nature. Rules govern actions: they may specify who may harvest at a site, when fishing begins, how an animal is distributed, what equipment is permitted, or which areas are left alone for particular reasons. Some are enforced through the authority of families, hereditary leaders, or community councils; others through reputation, customary dispute resolution, spiritual obligation, or the reciprocal expectations of neighboring groups. Historical accounts should identify the institution, the behavior regulated, and the consequences of violation rather than assuming that every ethical ideal functioned as an enforceable law.

Northwest Coast fishing places furnish examples of geographically specific rights. In some nations, hereditary houses or kin groups possessed recognized authority to oversee important fishing locations, organize labor, and govern distribution. Agreements, marriages, and diplomacy could alter access. The legal vocabulary of modern property law does not always describe these arrangements well: rights could include responsibilities and relations with other houses, not simply an individual's power to exclude. Where reliable evidence is available, historians should reconstruct the form of authority at the named river or site instead of referring vaguely to an undifferentiated tribal custom.

In agricultural communities, rules were often tied to communal infrastructure. Irrigation canals require maintenance, allocations of water, agreement about timing, and mechanisms for resolving conflicts among users. Where communal labor underlay the food supply, a governance failure could have direct environmental consequences. In mobile societies, negotiations over hunting grounds and changing migration corridors could be more flexible than fixed parcel boundaries suggest. The variety of institutions cautions against describing Indigenous stewardship as one uniform system of collective ownership.

Modern environmental regulation can collide with these rules. A provincial fishing season may open according to a regional stock assessment while community knowledge identifies local spawning differences. A national park prohibition on harvesting may conflict with a nation's established use of plants for food, ceremony, or teaching. Agencies may recognize a customary practice as a cultural interest without recognizing it as an exercise of political authority. These conflicts cannot be resolved solely by comparing ecological data; they involve laws, jurisdictions, treaty provisions, and negotiated responsibilities.

The distinction between customary rules and measurable environmental outcomes remains essential. Some norms produced conservation benefits under particular ecological conditions; others primarily organized labor, authority, and social relations. An effective law in one place may be unsuited to another, and new markets or gear can change the effects of longstanding practices. Historical evaluation therefore asks what the rule was meant to accomplish, what evidence indicates about its operation, and how its consequences changed across time.

Section 133.12

Territory, Collective Rights, and Indigenous Environmental Governance

From the 1970s, Indigenous knowledge moved from the margins into negotiated institutions. Four routes can be distinguished: land-claims research, co-management of wildlife, jointly governed protected areas, and environmental review. Each emerged from Indigenous political action rather than from state initiative alone, and each produced different distributions of authority.

Land-claims research, as Section 133.10 showed, converted knowledge into evidence of use and occupancy. The resulting agreements, including the James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement (1975) and the Nunavut Agreement (1993), created boards and procedures through which Indigenous participants hold formal roles. The Nunavut Wildlife Management Board, established under the 1993 agreement, shares responsibility for wildlife management and incorporates Inuit knowledge alongside scientific information, though how that balance operates in practice has been debated by participants. In Alaska, the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act (1980) set a priority for subsistence uses by rural residents on federal lands, a framework tied to settlement politics examined in Chapter 115.

Protected areas offer a second model. After Haida people blockaded logging roads on Lyell Island in 1985, a process followed that led to the Gwaii Haanas Agreement of 1993 between the Council of the Haida Nation and the Government of Canada. It created an Archipelago Management Board with equal representation, which makes decisions by consensus. In the United States, tribal coalitions have pursued co-stewardship of federal lands. The Bears Ears Inter-Tribal Coalition, formed in 2015 by Hopi, Navajo, Ute Mountain Ute, Ute Indian Tribe, and Zuni nations, helped bring about a national monument in 2016 and a collaborative management agreement in 2022, though the monument's boundaries and management have shifted with changes in federal policy. In Canada, the Indigenous Circle of Experts' 2018 report We Rise Together proposed recognizing Indigenous Protected and Conserved Areas in which Indigenous governments hold authority.

Territorial knowledge has long supported political claims. A people who can identify named fishing stations, travel corridors, burial places, resource areas, and the laws governing them can articulate a history of occupancy extending beyond the categories contained in colonial land records. In the Canadian North, land-use and occupancy research collected hunters' accounts and maps during major claims negotiations. Its significance was not merely that Indigenous people possessed local knowledge; it was that they could demonstrate systems of sustained activity and authority which government maps had frequently ignored.

Co-management is an institutional arrangement, not a single level of sovereignty. A board may give Indigenous appointees important influence over decisions while leaving final authority with a federal minister. A cooperative agreement may provide a process for consultation and funding but remain vulnerable to unilateral legal change. The Nunavut Wildlife Management Board, created through the 1993 Nunavut Agreement, exists within a different legal order from a national park advisory committee or a tribal government exercising its own regulatory powers. Those distinctions determine who can set quotas, issue permits, collect evidence, or resolve disputes.

The Gwaii Haanas agreement of 1993 followed years of Haida organizing against logging and a wider effort to protect and govern the archipelago. Its Archipelago Management Board is often cited as an important example of shared management, but the parties did not settle every underlying question of title or sovereignty through the agreement. This is the institutional value of such arrangements: they can establish workable procedures under conditions of continued disagreement. Their limitation is that operational cooperation is not the same as agreement about the source of ultimate legal authority.

Bears Ears in Utah shows that institutional settlements can reverse. Following organizing by five tribal nations, the monument was established in 2016; an intergovernmental cooperative agreement with the Bears Ears Commission followed in June 2022. Presidential Proclamation 11043 of July 13, 2026 reduced the reserved area from approximately 1.36 million acres to about 121,096 acres and purported to terminate the commission, while leaving other consultation duties in place. Five tribal nations filed suit on November 18, 2026. As of this chapter's November 2026 editorial review, the legality and practical consequences of the new proclamation remain contested. The episode reveals how difficult it can be to convert recognized expertise into secure authority when federal executive decisions can change the institutional framework.

A rigorous institutional comparison asks who selected representatives, whether decisions are binding, what money and staff support the process, whose knowledge is recorded, and whether a community may withhold culturally restricted information. It also asks whether the government has a duty to consider recommendations or a duty to obtain consent. An arrangement may be celebrated as co-management while containing substantial inequalities in those areas. Environmental governance is therefore best studied through specific agreements and the conduct of the institutions they create.

Division IV — Colonial Disruption, Adaptation, and the Reassessment of Knowledge

Section 133.13

Colonization, Dispossession, and the Disruption of Environmental Knowledge

European and settler colonial states depended on Indigenous environmental knowledge while weakening the systems that sustained it. Explorers, surveyors, traders, and naturalists relied on Indigenous guides for routes, food sources, and animal and plant identification, and they incorporated that information into maps, reports, and scientific descriptions, frequently without attributing it. The fur trade operated through Indigenous trapping expertise (Chapter 46). Missionaries and agents documented plant uses for medicine and agriculture. The knowledge was valued as a resource even as the people who held it were displaced.

Dispossession severed communities from the places where knowledge was practiced. When territories were reduced to small reserves and reservations (Chapter 84), families lost access to gathering grounds, hunting territories, fisheries, and sacred sites. The destruction of bison herds (Chapter 81) undermined Plains economies and the ceremonial and ecological knowledge tied to them. Large infrastructure projects removed entire landscapes: the Garrison Dam, completed in the 1950s on the Missouri River, flooded bottomlands at Fort Berthold where Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara families had farmed, and the construction of The Dalles Dam in 1957 submerged Celilo Falls, a major Columbia River fishery and trading place (Chapter 120). Such losses were not only economic; they eliminated places in which environmental knowledge was taught.

The destruction of environmental knowledge was rarely a single deliberate act. It developed through mutually reinforcing disruptions: removal from territory, restrictions on travel and harvesting, schooling policies that separated children from their teachers, the introduction of unfamiliar legal categories, changes in markets, and ecological transformations associated with disease, livestock, mining, or dams. A knowledge holder might still know where a plant grew while no longer having permission to gather it. After several generations, a young person might know the name of a fishing site but lack the opportunity to learn its seasonal conditions in practice. The distinction between possessing information and being able to exercise it is central to the chapter.

A fishing community provides a useful causal sequence. Rivers blocked by dams or diverted for irrigation change salmon migration and access to fishing places. Government restrictions can criminalize customary harvesting or require licenses not organized around Indigenous rights. Families consequently spend less time at places where children formerly learned skills from experienced relatives. The resulting decline in everyday transmission may later be mistaken for evidence that a traditional practice lacked historical significance. It is instead partly a record of institutions that prevented it from continuing.

The impact was uneven and often resisted. Some communities negotiated access through treaties or later litigation; others continued activities quietly in areas where enforcement was inconsistent. In certain regions, wage labor and commercially available food changed how much time was available for land-based work, while the knowledge itself survived in language or ritual. Historians must avoid assuming that adoption of new technology means surrender of older environmental expertise. Motorboats and modern navigation tools, for example, can coexist with close observation of currents and ice, though they also alter risk and mobility.

Colonial science participated in contradictory processes. Surveyors and naturalists depended on Indigenous guides, while public institutions often represented the resulting information as discoveries by outsiders. Administrators sometimes treated Indigenous observations as anecdotal even when they relied on them for safety or practical logistics. Later archival collections preserved information that descendant communities could use to renew teaching, but collecting without consent sometimes revealed confidential details. These contradictions are part of the historical record and should not be flattened into a story of either total appropriation or uniformly beneficial cooperation.

Restoring access and rebuilding knowledge transmission may require multiple reforms at once: recognition of rights to gather and travel, appropriate management arrangements, community-controlled education, restoration of damaged habitats, and resources for the teachers who hold expertise. One successful court case or public acknowledgment does not automatically supply those conditions. The chapter's political history therefore begins with specific constraints on practice and ends with questions about the durability of institutional change.

Cause and Effect

Dispossession, Restricted Access, and the Interruption of Knowledge Transmission

Long-term causes

Land seizure, political dispossession, legal systems that displaced Indigenous tenure, and policies that privileged settler property and conservation authority.

Short-term causes

Expansion of reserves and reservations, park restrictions, boarding and residential schooling, and state regulation of hunting, fishing, and gathering.

Immediate trigger or context

A family loses access to a named harvesting site, a school separates a child from instructors, or an agency prohibits a familiar seasonal practice.

Event or transformation

Less time is spent working and learning in that place, and knowledge formerly acquired through repeated observation becomes harder to practice and test.

Immediate effects

Interrupted apprenticeship, reduced access to food and materials, penalties for traditional activities, and pressure to rely on outside agencies or markets.

Long-term consequences

Uneven loss of ecological vocabulary and experience, continuing resistance, later rights campaigns, and the development of language, land-based education, and Guardian initiatives.

Historical complexity: Removals and regulations varied greatly. Many knowledge holders continued teaching, and some scientific or written records later assisted renewal. Restrictions on practice, rather than a simple disappearance of information, are the essential causal mechanism.

Section 133.14

Colonial Conservation, Protected Areas, and Indigenous Exclusion

Law and policy restricted practice. National parks, including Yosemite, Yellowstone, and Glacier, were established on Indigenous homelands and typically excluded or limited Indigenous residents and harvesters. Game, fishing, and migratory-bird laws imposed seasons and licensing requirements that conflicted with community practice and treaty rights (Chapters 106 and 113). Fire suppression policies discouraged burning that communities had long used (Chapter 134). Schools, discussed in Chapters 87 and 88, removed children from families and prohibited languages, interrupting the transmission of knowledge in the settings where it was most effectively taught. Missionary suppression of ceremonies also affected the practices through which knowledge and ethics were reinforced.

The rise of conservation in North America produced protected spaces but also new forms of exclusion. National parks and forest reserves were frequently created within territories where Indigenous peoples had long lived, harvested, traded, and maintained cultural responsibilities. Federal administrators sometimes imagined preservation as the removal of people from an allegedly untouched natural landscape. The result could be regulation of hunting, prohibition of plant gathering, relocation of residents, or restrictions on ceremonies and travel. These outcomes varied by jurisdiction and period, but the underlying problem was that public conservation authority displaced existing Indigenous forms of environmental governance.

The creation of Yosemite and Yellowstone is often narrated as a pioneering conservation achievement, yet the earlier Indigenous histories of those lands are indispensable to explaining the sites themselves. In Yosemite, Native people experienced dispossession and restrictive federal park management even as tourism promoted a wilderness ideal. Yellowstone's history similarly involves long-standing Indigenous use as well as the later expansion of federal control. These examples should not be transformed into a claim that parks provided no ecological benefits: they protected habitats from some forms of development. The historical question is who paid the social and political costs of that protection and whose expertise informed the resulting rules.

The Canadian record offers related but distinct histories. Parks and game regulations developed in a constitutional and administrative framework where federal and provincial authority, treaty rights, and Indigenous land claims interacted. Some authorities later revised policies to recognize harvesting and cooperative management, while other disputes continued. Indigenous communities often criticized rules that prohibited subsistence or ceremonial use while accommodating tourism, commercial recreation, or the scientific collection of specimens. Such cases reveal that conservation is never only the enforcement of a scientific conclusion; it involves choices about access, values, and legal standing.

During the twentieth century conservation agencies became more attentive to traditional ecological knowledge, sometimes because field staff recognized that Indigenous observations were essential for management. Yet a request to provide information is not equivalent to control of the protected area. Agencies may invite Indigenous participants to describe a site while retaining authority over designation, budgets, enforcement, and interpretation. Co-management agreements can modify this imbalance, but their strength depends on the underlying law. This distinction is examined through the Nunavut, Gwaii Haanas, and Bears Ears cases in Sections 133.12 and 133.17.

A further challenge is environmental change. The plants, animals, and waterways managed under a historical park regime may shift as climate, fire, invasive species, and neighboring land use change. Indigenous expertise is not merely a record of an earlier ecosystem; many knowledge holders observe present change and revise their practices accordingly. Effective governance therefore needs procedures for continuing dialogue, not just a one-time collection of traditional stories at the beginning of a management plan.

Section 133.15

Indigenous Continuity, Adaptation, and Environmental Knowledge Renewal

Indigenous communities nonetheless maintained and adapted knowledge. Families continued teaching despite restrictions, ceremonies survived in altered forms, and individuals recorded and shared knowledge selectively with outsiders. Resistance sometimes took the form of continued harvesting in defiance of regulation, and sometimes of legal action. The recovery of knowledge in the late twentieth century drew on those continuities, on surviving Elders, and on records, including some compiled by outsiders, that communities could reclaim or reinterpret.

Knowledge renewal is often described through public ceremonies or the opening of a cultural center, but its practical dimensions are more demanding. A community may need access to a harvesting place, reliable transportation, equipment, time away from wage work, speakers who can teach specialized vocabulary, and younger people able to practice safely. Without those conditions, a published guide or recorded interview may preserve information but fail to restore the experience through which its reliability was learned. Renewal therefore depends on institutions that connect knowledge holders and learners in the environments where the knowledge has practical meaning.

The Menominee forest illustrates a particularly long institutional history. Menominee stewardship developed within the tribe's relationship to its Wisconsin homeland and later through a sustained tribal forestry enterprise. Congress enacted the Menominee Termination Act in 1954; termination took effect April 30, 1961; and federal recognition was restored by legislation signed December 22, 1973. These events affected political control, finances, and the forest's institutional setting. The Menominee case should not be reduced to a simple fable of uninterrupted sustainable harvesting. Its significance lies in the persistence and reorganization of resource governance across major changes in legal status.

Contemporary restoration sometimes draws on accounts from elders, historical photographs, early botanical surveys, and archaeological evidence to reconstruct earlier conditions. Such sources can assist but rarely provide a single definitive baseline. Landscapes may have changed through invasive species, new hydrology, urban expansion, or altered fire regimes, making literal restoration impossible. Practitioners must decide which historical processes remain useful, which goals are practical now, and how scientific and community expertise will inform those choices. These are modern decisions grounded in history, not merely acts of preserving the past.

Indigenous colleges, community research centers, language programs, and environmental offices can connect education to territorial responsibilities. Training in geographic information systems or wildlife biology may expand the tools available to a tribal agency while traditional mentoring preserves knowledge of local places and ethics. Such collaboration works best when the community establishes the goals of the project and controls how sensitive information is used. Outside institutions that celebrate traditional knowledge while monopolizing funding or authorship risk reproducing the older pattern of extraction in a more inclusive vocabulary.

Continuity and innovation also vary by generation. Elders who learned without formal credentials may collaborate with graduates who hold technical degrees; young harvesters may use satellite imagery but still rely on learned observations of weather and animals. Differences between generations can produce debate about acceptable techniques or what counts as authentic practice. Historical writing should treat these disagreements as evidence of an active knowledge tradition rather than proof that such traditions have lost coherence.

Section 133.16

Indigenous Knowledge, Modern Science, and Environmental Research

One influential response to the problem of relating Indigenous and Western scientific approaches came from Mi'kmaw Elder Albert Marshall of Eskasoni, working with Murdena Marshall, Cheryl Bartlett, and colleagues at Cape Breton University. They described Etuaptmumk, usually translated as Two-Eyed Seeing, as a way of drawing on the strengths of Indigenous ways of knowing with one eye and the strengths of Western ways of knowing with the other, and using both together. The framework has been taken up in health research, education, and environmental management in Canada and beyond.

Frameworks of this kind matter because they shape institutions. A government that regards Indigenous knowledge as anecdote will gather it differently from one that regards it as a body of expertise and law held by nations. Section 133.12 shows how that difference appears in actual agreements and statutes.

From the late nineteenth century, anthropologists, botanists, and folklorists recorded Indigenous plant and animal knowledge, often under the assumption that Indigenous cultures were disappearing. Melvin Gilmore's study of plant uses among peoples of the Missouri River region (1914) and Gilbert Wilson's recording of Maxi'diwiac's account of Hidatsa agriculture (1917) are examples of work that preserved valuable detail. They also reveal the pattern: Indigenous experts supplied the substance, outsiders received the authorship and institutional credit, and the resulting publications were housed in libraries and museums far from the communities. Some Indigenous communities now use such records to support language and cultural revival, while recognizing that the circumstances of their creation raise ethical questions.

Outsiders also produced images of Indigenous peoples and nature. Romantic portrayals cast Indigenous people as inherently harmonious with the natural world, while others depicted them as wasteful or as obstacles to progress. Both served political purposes: the first could be used to praise a vanishing past while overlooking living nations, and the second to justify removing Indigenous people from land. Environmental movements of the twentieth century often invoked Indigenous voices, sometimes accurately and sometimes through texts whose origins were not Indigenous. The following Primary Source Window examines the best-known case.

The popular image raises a scholarly question: did Indigenous peoples in fact manage resources sustainably? The answer varies by place, period, and practice. Communities developed effective restraint systems, and many managed resources over centuries. They also hunted, burned, farmed, and traded in ways that altered ecosystems, and in some cases they depleted local resources. Pre-contact examples include localized overharvesting, and post-contact examples include expanded hunting for markets. Explanation requires attention to population, technology, ownership systems, and the pressures of colonial economies. The following Historical Debate sets out the main lines of disagreement.

Environmental assessment is a third. Canada's Impact Assessment Act (2019) requires that Indigenous knowledge be considered in federal assessments, and U.S. agencies in 2022 received federal guidance on respectfully including Indigenous knowledge in research and decision-making, although the status and application of such guidance have shifted with changes in administration, and students should consult current sources. At the international level, Article 8(j) of the Convention on Biological Diversity (1992) calls on states to respect and maintain the knowledge and practices of Indigenous and local communities, with the approval and involvement of knowledge holders, and Article 31 of the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (2007) affirms rights over traditional knowledge (Chapter 143). The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change's 2022 assessment of impacts and adaptation also recognized Indigenous knowledge as a source of evidence.

Interactions between Indigenous knowledge and academic environmental science have never followed a single path. Early naturalists frequently depended on Indigenous experts while publishing under their own names; later ethnographers sometimes preserved detailed accounts but framed them as remnants of a supposedly vanishing culture. From the late twentieth century, collaborations increasingly discussed traditional ecological knowledge as a potential source of observations about animal movements, plant change, and ecosystem dynamics. The language of inclusion was an advance over simple dismissal, but it introduced a new question: who decides which information is useful, and whose criteria determine whether the contribution counts as valid evidence?

Scientific studies and community knowledge may operate on different time and spatial scales. A research project may estimate a regional population with a formal sampling frame and confidence interval; a harvesting community may distinguish local movements and seasonal changes from many years of direct experience. The Iñupiat bowhead dispute, examined in Section 133.6, demonstrates how these approaches can reveal different parts of the same process. Neither method eliminates the need for checking its assumptions. A reliable joint inquiry states the decision problem, records uncertainty, identifies the source of each observation, and makes clear how disagreement will be handled.

The Mi'kmaw framework Etuaptmumk, commonly translated as Two-Eyed Seeing, provides a practical language for collaboration. Associated with Albert Marshall, Murdena Marshall, Cheryl Bartlett, and colleagues, it encourages researchers to use the strengths of Indigenous and Western ways of knowing together without treating them as identical. Yet adopting the phrase cannot solve a conflict over ownership of samples, access to sacred knowledge, or the authority to set research priorities. A project may quote Indigenous participants respectfully while still denying them a role in interpreting the results; its governance would then fall short of the partnership implied by its language.

The ecological Indian controversy requires similar care. Shepard Krech III challenged the romantic portrayal of Indigenous peoples as consistently conservationist, while Indigenous scholars and environmental historians emphasized the risks of ignoring customary institutions and the colonial conditions in which certain practices were documented. Evidence of an unsustainable harvest in one region cannot establish a general characterization of Indigenous peoples. Nor can a statement of reciprocal obligation show that every community produced stable ecosystems. The strongest historical explanation distinguishes a community's ethical ideals, the institutions governing behavior, and the outcomes that can actually be established through records or environmental evidence.

As public agencies incorporated Indigenous knowledge into assessment and policy, questions about consent and control became unavoidable. The U.S. Office of Science and Technology Policy and Council on Environmental Quality issued federal guidance on November 30, 2022, and NOAA issued more specific guidance in 2024. These are important historical documents, but guidance is not the same thing as an enforceable statute; application differs by agency and administration. Claims about a uniform federal policy in 2026 would exceed the evidence without a current agency-by-agency review. The distinctions among government rhetoric, procedures, and actual authority must remain visible.

The Chief Seattle speech provides a warning about seemingly convenient evidence. Henry A. Smith published a reconstructed account in 1887 of a speech attributed to Si'ahl several decades earlier; the original wording cannot be recovered from a verbatim transcript. The famous later ecological version was substantially composed by screenwriter Ted Perry around 1970. Because the speech circulated as if it expressed a timeless Indigenous environmental worldview, it shows how a sympathetic-sounding text can erase the circumstances and voice of the person to whom it is attributed. Responsible scholarship identifies each version and its author rather than quoting later inventions as authentic nineteenth-century testimony.

Historical Vocabulary
Two-Eyed Seeing (Etuaptmumk)

Definition. A guiding principle associated with Albert Marshall and colleagues at Cape Breton University's Institute for Integrative Science and Health, encouraging researchers and communities to combine Indigenous and Western knowledge without subordinating either to the other.

Historical context. The framework emerged in the early 2000s from collaboration between Mi'kmaw Elders and university researchers working on health and environmental questions in Nova Scotia.

Why it matters. It offers an alternative to two common positions: demanding that Indigenous knowledge be "validated" by Western science, and rejecting any engagement with scientific methods.

Potential misunderstanding. Two-Eyed Seeing is not a claim that the two kinds of knowledge are identical or that they never conflict. It is a stance toward working across differences, and its practice depends on who holds authority in a given project.

Multiple Perspectives

Indigenous Knowledge in Environmental Review: Four Positions

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing positions documented in policy and scholarly discussions. They are not quotations from any named individual or institution.

A knowledge holder and community harvester: Knowledge is a living relationship with a territory, held with responsibilities and rules about sharing. Being asked to supply "data" for a project timeline risks stripping it of context, exposing sensitive sites, and turning a community's expertise into an input for decisions it did not control.
An Indigenous government: The question is jurisdiction, not information. A nation with inherent authority over its territory expects to decide whether a project proceeds and under what conditions, and treats consultation that only gathers knowledge as inadequate.
A government scientist or regulator: Agencies work under statutes that require documented, reviewable evidence. Many want to incorporate Indigenous knowledge more fully, but may lack the institutions, trained staff, or legal mechanisms to do so, and they worry about handling confidential information appropriately.
A project proponent: Companies seek predictable processes and clear requirements, and some fund community-led studies. Their interests in project approval may conflict with community priorities, and critics argue that funding relationships can shape what knowledge is shared and how it is used.

The four groups did not have equal power. Indigenous governments and communities typically had less control over the timing, funding, and legal outcome than regulators or proponents, a point that drives much of the push for consent-based and Indigenous-led processes.

Historical Debate

How Should Indigenous Knowledge Inform Environmental Decisions?

The question: Can a government treat Indigenous observations as evidence within an assessment without subordinating Indigenous authority to the assessment agency?

Technical validation: Some agency processes favor standardized sampling, reproducible measurements, and explicit error estimates. These can help comparisons, but may undervalue long observation periods or locally specific experience.
Community authority: Indigenous governments and knowledge holders emphasize appropriate consent, attribution, place-specific expertise, and their own standards for deciding who is qualified to share information. This approach can resist extraction but also requires procedures for addressing disagreements.
Collaborative practice: A third approach designs research jointly: questions, methods, data access, interpretation, and decision rules are negotiated in advance. Collaboration is possible without pretending that scientific and Indigenous frameworks always coincide.

Evidence: The bowhead whale census, land-use and occupancy maps, environmental-assessment requirements, and written research agreements permit comparisons of actual practice.

Points of disagreement: Evidence quality, scale, epistemology, rights, and institutional authority are intertwined but distinct issues.

Why it matters: Decisions about conservation and development can change if knowledge holders are given a genuine role in determining questions and remedies rather than merely supplying information.

Division V — Indigenous Stewardship, Contemporary Institutions, and Environmental Sovereignty

Section 133.17

Indigenous Guardians and Community-Based Environmental Stewardship

Indigenous Guardian programs provide one contemporary institutional response to the older separation of environmental expertise from authority. Guardians may monitor wildlife, observe environmental changes, record cultural sites, support community access, assist with restoration, and teach younger people in the field. The specific roles vary among First Nations, Inuit, and other Indigenous communities and among the governments financing programs. These initiatives should not be treated as a single uniform occupation: some participants have enforcement functions, others are monitors or educators, and some programs emphasize community responsibilities more than the categories used by outside conservation agencies.

The Canadian Indigenous Circle of Experts' 2018 report We Rise Together offered a framework for Indigenous Protected and Conserved Areas. It emphasized the importance of Indigenous leadership and governance in conservation rather than simply inviting Indigenous people to assist in parks designed elsewhere. The institutional question is consequential: who establishes the area's conservation goals, how are local laws reflected, and what authority survives changes in provincial or federal funding? Where Indigenous governments are accountable for monitoring but not for decisions, recognition of expertise may coexist with dependence on another institution.

Guardians can also change the relationship between written records and land-based learning. Field notes, wildlife observations, digital maps, and scientific training may be combined with conversations with elders and with long-term experience at a site. A young worker recording water conditions can learn how a local place is named, who may use it, and why a sudden change is significant. Such knowledge integration does not happen automatically through the purchase of devices; it requires mentoring, community direction, and protocols that prevent sensitive information from being circulated without permission.

The institutional record contains both achievements and limitations. A program may create paid employment in a remote community while depending on short-term grants, inadequate boats or vehicles, or limited access to technical laboratories. Partnership agreements can improve training and recognition but leave unresolved disputes over jurisdiction. Scholars should evaluate outcomes, staffing, durability, and decision-making rather than assuming that adopting the language of Indigenous stewardship produces the same institutional powers in every region.

Environmental monitoring also creates evidence for later political decisions. Records of altered salmon runs, changing ice safety, invasive species, or damage to cultural sites may support restoration plans or legal claims. Yet the same data might be sought by commercial developers, tourism interests, or governments for purposes the community does not endorse. The rights to authorize collection, interpret results, restrict access, and demand correction are therefore central to the long-term usefulness of Guardian programs.

Section 133.18

Regional Indigenous Environmental Knowledge in Comparative Perspective

This section and the next survey regional examples. They are illustrations, not a complete catalog, and they follow the cultural regions used in Volume III while recognizing that those regions are analytical conveniences (Chapter 1).

Across the Arctic, Inuit, Yupik, Iñupiat, and Unangan communities (Chapter 35) built knowledge adapted to sea ice, tundra, and marine mammals. Hunters interpret ice by color, texture, sound, and the direction of winds and currents, and they track animal behavior across seasons. Alongside it ran a body of ethical guidance about sharing meat, treating animals respectfully, and avoiding waste. The Inuit Circumpolar Council, founded in 1977, represents Inuit in Alaska, Canada, Greenland, and Chukotka, with participation from related communities there; Unangan/Aleut peoples have separate representation, including the Aleut International Association. Arctic knowledge has become especially important as ice conditions change, a subject for Chapter 138.

In the Subarctic (Chapter 34), Cree, Dene, Innu, and neighboring peoples developed knowledge of boreal forests, lakes, and migratory animals, including caribou, geese, fish, and fur-bearers. Trappers' observations of population cycles in hares, lynx, and other animals are part of the history of ecology itself: the Hudson's Bay Company's long run of pelt-return records, which biologists in the early twentieth century used to study population cycles, rested on the labor and knowledge of Indigenous and other trappers. The Migratory Birds Convention between Canada and the United States (1916), implemented in Canada by statute in 1917, introduced seasonal limits on waterfowl hunting that conflicted with spring hunts important to northern communities, an early example of conservation law colliding with Indigenous harvest practice.

Northwest Coast peoples (Chapter 33) managed marine and forest resources through house-based stewardship. Along the coast of British Columbia, Washington, and Alaska, archaeologists and Indigenous communities have documented clam gardens: rock-walled terraces built in the intertidal zone that create a gentler slope and an environment favorable to clams. Research with Heiltsuk, Kwakwaka'wakw, Tsleil-Waututh, and Coast Salish communities, among others, found that such terraces were associated with higher clam densities and growth than unmodified beaches, and archaeological dating indicates that some are thousands of years old, with chronology still being refined. In estuaries, Kwakwaka'wakw and neighboring communities maintained root gardens of plants such as springbank clover and Pacific silverweed, as documented in scholarship edited by Douglas Deur and Nancy Turner (2005). Culturally modified trees, in which bark or planks were removed from living cedar without killing it, remain visible in forests and are protected as archaeological evidence in some jurisdictions. Researchers have also identified distinctive plant communities around former village sites, suggesting long-term cultivation.

Salmon were central to coastal and Plateau economies, and many nations held first-salmon ceremonies that welcomed the returning fish, shared the first catch, and returned remains to the water. Such practices embodied rules about respect and restraint and are discussed further in Chapter 135. On the Columbia Plateau, the Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation use a "First Foods" framework, which orders the foods central to their culture, including water, salmon, game, roots, and berries, as a basis for river and land management. Root-digging grounds for camas and bitterroot were stewarded through seasonal timing and harvest practices. In California (Chapter 32), basket-makers tended plants such as sedge and willow to produce straight, flexible shoots, using pruning and the careful timing of harvest, practices that interact with Indigenous burning examined in Chapter 134.

In the Great Lakes region (Chapter 27), Anishinaabe communities harvested manoomin from shallow lakes and rivers by canoe, using poles and knocking sticks that left seed to fall into the water. They also tapped maples for sap and processed it into sugar, and fished and hunted in seasonal rounds. The Menominee of Wisconsin are associated with a forest managed for continuous yield: after their reservation was established in the nineteenth century, logging proceeded under selective cutting rules, and the forest has been widely studied as an example of long-term management. Their federal recognition was terminated in 1961 and restored in 1973 (Chapter 104). In the Northeast (Chapter 25), Haudenosaunee, Wabanaki, and other peoples combined farming, fishing, and hunting. The Boylston Street fish weir in Boston, built thousands of years ago, is an archaeological reminder of early engineered fishing in the region. Mi'kmaw knowledge of eels, salmon, and moose is tied to netukulimk and has figured in Mi'kmaw treaty and fisheries claims.

Southeastern nations such as the Cherokee, Muscogee, Choctaw, and Chickasaw (Chapter 28) developed knowledge of river cane, a plant used for baskets, mats, and construction, and of medicinal and ceremonial plants. Analysis of residues from vessels at Cahokia has suggested that people there consumed a caffeinated drink made from yaupon holly, a species of the southern coast, pointing to the exchange of plants and the knowledge to prepare them (Chapters 22 and 23). On the Plains (Chapter 29), village peoples such as the Mandan, Hidatsa, Arikara, and Pawnee farmed river bottomlands and hunted bison. Maxi'diwiac's account of Hidatsa agriculture, published in 1917, documents seed selection, field layout, and storage in considerable detail. Pawnee star knowledge, recorded by anthropologists in the early twentieth century, linked the sky to planting and ceremony. Bison and the economy that depended on them are treated in Chapters 49 and 81.

In the arid Southwest (Chapter 30), Hopi, Zuni, O'odham, and other communities developed agriculture suited to scarce and unpredictable rainfall. Hopi dry-farmers plant at the mouths of washes and use seed varieties adapted to germinate with deep soil moisture, relying on timing, field placement, and careful observation of weather. Zuni farmers have used small sunken gardens bordered by ridges to conserve water. O'odham communities used floodwater farming and canal irrigation, building on the earlier Hohokam traditions discussed in Chapter 17. Calendars based on observation of the sun's position along the horizon were used by Hopi to time ceremonies and planting, and some communities still keep such records. This knowledge was embedded in clan responsibilities, ceremony, and long-term seed stewardship.

In Mexico, Indigenous farmers have long worked in milpa systems, in which maize is grown together with beans, squash, and other plants, often rotated with fallow periods and tended alongside managed trees and wild plants. Chinampa agriculture, the raised-bed farming of the lakes of the Basin of Mexico, continues in places such as Xochimilco (Chapter 14). Many Indigenous and agrarian communities hold forests in common, and community forestry enterprises in the Sierra Norte of Oaxaca and other regions have been studied as examples of locally governed forest management. Rarámuri, Yoeme, Kumeyaay, and Tohono O'odham communities, whose homelands cross the U.S.–Mexico border, hold knowledge of desert and mountain environments that is complicated by border enforcement (Chapters 141 and 142).

Regional comparison is most useful when organized around questions rather than stereotypes. Who observes the environment and in what activities? Which institutions transmit expertise? How are access and harvesting governed? What kinds of ecological change have required revision? An Inuit account of travel across sea ice and a Hopi account of dry farming may both involve close attention to weather, but they differ in the physical phenomena monitored, the consequences of error, and the social settings in which observations are shared. Comparison should preserve those distinctions.

Across the Inuit Arctic, navigation and marine mammal observation reflect relationships among ice, ocean, wind, and animal movement. In the Subarctic, people have developed knowledge of forest conditions, inland waters, caribou movements, and networks of family hunting territories. Along the Northwest Coast, shellfish terraces, salmon streams, cedar, and hereditary responsibilities demonstrate environmental knowledge that includes both practical intervention and social authority. An archaeological clam garden wall can be placed beside oral histories of harvesting, but neither source substitutes for the other.

The Great Lakes offer different evidence. Manoomin harvests depend on shallow-water habitats, seasonal timing, and techniques suited to the fragility of ripening grain. The Menominee forest demonstrates how a resource enterprise and political institutions have evolved through termination and restoration. Eastern woodland plant management also intersects with historically changing agricultural and village systems. These histories illustrate why knowledge cannot be classified only by the useful species involved; it also concerns changing forms of governance and adaptation to colonial economies.

In the arid Southwest and northern Mexico, farming communities developed techniques for coping with variable rainfall, runoff, and soil moisture. Hopi dry farming, O'odham floodwater cultivation, and diverse milpa systems in Mexico reveal distinctive combinations of crop selection, planting location, and labor. Community forest enterprises in parts of Oaxaca provide another institutional setting where collective decision-making and modern forestry practice interact. No single case is representative of all the Indigenous peoples within Mexico or the borderlands, and the effects of land reforms, markets, and contemporary environmental regulation differ widely among communities.

The cases also make visible methodological limits. Some systems are known through archaeological remains and historic documents; others through contemporary interviews or the publications of Indigenous scholars. A millennia-old engineering feature does not automatically reveal its full social governance, while a recent account cannot simply be projected unchanged into the distant past. Good comparative history places evidence beside its date, location, and purpose. The aim is to understand how different peoples developed working relationships with their environments without erasing political boundaries, historical disruption, or differences in authority.

Section 133.19

Environmental Knowledge, Consent, and Indigenous Intellectual Authority

As outside interest in Indigenous knowledge grew, Indigenous nations and organizations asserted that knowledge, like land, was subject to their jurisdiction. They pointed to historical extraction: plants, seeds, stories, and images collected without consent and sometimes used commercially. They also raised practical concerns, such as the risk that publishing the location of a gathering site or sacred place would invite harm. The result was a series of protocols, policies, and principles governing research.

In Canada, the First Nations Information Governance Centre promoted the OCAP principles, which stand for ownership, control, access, and possession, as a framework for First Nations data, a concept formalized in the late 1990s. Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami released the National Inuit Strategy on Research in 2018, calling for Inuit direction of research in Inuit Nunangat. Many tribal nations in the United States maintain tribal research review boards or historic preservation offices that approve and oversee research, and many require agreements before outside researchers work with communities. Indigenous scholars such as Shawn Wilson, an Opaskwayak Cree researcher, have written about research as a relational and ceremonial practice in which accountability to community is central. International principles, including the CARE principles for Indigenous data governance proposed in 2019, add emphasis on collective benefit, authority to control, responsibility, and ethics. Chapter 145 examines data sovereignty in greater detail.

Seed and plant knowledge also raised ownership disputes. International instruments such as the Convention on Biological Diversity call for benefit-sharing and consent when genetic resources and associated knowledge are used, but enforcement is uneven, and Indigenous critics argue that intellectual-property regimes fit poorly with knowledge held collectively. Communities therefore create their own safeguards: restricted access to some information, community archives, and digital platforms that let communities set access conditions. The underlying principle is that holding knowledge in a community does not make it free for anyone to collect.

These protocols also shape how scholars and students use the sources discussed in this chapter. Older ethnographic collections remain valuable, and descendants often wish to consult them, yet using them responsibly means recognizing the circumstances of collection and seeking guidance from communities where possible. This textbook follows that approach by identifying where knowledge came from, avoiding publication of sensitive details, and noting where communities, not outsiders, are the authorities.

The collection of environmental knowledge can create value for those who do not hold it. A research team may learn the location of a rare plant, a company may seek information about resources near a project, or a government may ask about routes and culturally important sites. Sharing can support conservation, legal claims, and public education, but it can also expose sensitive places, encourage commercial exploitation, or detach information from the people entitled to interpret it. Indigenous knowledge sovereignty addresses the authority to decide whether collection occurs, what is recorded, who may view it, and what benefits and obligations follow.

The First Nations principles of ownership, control, access, and possession—OCAP—were developed to address data governance in the Canadian First Nations context. They should not be generalized as a universally adopted Indigenous code; Inuit organizations have developed different research priorities, including Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami's 2018 National Inuit Strategy on Research. In the United States, tribes and community institutions have established research-review procedures that differ in legal basis and practice. A researcher who secures university ethical approval has not necessarily obtained the consent or authorization required by an Indigenous nation.

The United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples provides an international statement of relevant principles. Article 29 concerns rights to the conservation and protection of the environment and the productive capacity of Indigenous lands or territories and resources. Article 31 concerns rights to maintain, control, protect, and develop cultural heritage, traditional knowledge, traditional cultural expressions, and associated intellectual property. The declaration was adopted in 2007; its precise legal effect depends on the jurisdiction and subsequent domestic law. It is neither a universal substitute for treaties nor proof that every government has implemented the rights in identical ways.

A distinction is also necessary between acknowledgment, consultation, participation, consent, and government-to-government authority. A museum or agency can acknowledge a community in a report while retaining exclusive power over a collection. Consultation gives an opportunity to be heard but not necessarily to decide. A research agreement may confer review rights over particular publications, while a tribal environmental law may exercise direct governmental authority over certain activities. These arrangements are not interchangeable. Students should ask what practical powers, enforcement mechanisms, funding, and legal remedies accompany a stated commitment to respect Indigenous knowledge.

Knowledge governance is not identical to secrecy. Communities may wish to publish language materials, teach the public about conservation, share findings with other nations, or collaborate with researchers to improve environmental decisions. The right to make those choices is what matters. A source can be ethically usable in one educational context and inappropriate for a public database that allows unrestricted reproduction. Historical scholarship should explain why a record exists, who created it, whether its intended audience was limited, and how contemporary authorities understand the conditions of its use.

Section 133.20

Indigenous Environmental Knowledge and the Future of Stewardship

Indigenous environmental knowledge today is neither a vanished heritage nor an unbroken inheritance. It is a living, contested, and increasingly institutionalized field. Elders, harvesters, and scholars teach on the land, tribal colleges and language programs support curricula, and community monitoring programs track changes in wildlife, water, and ice. Indigenous researchers and organizations are building archives and digital tools under community control. At the same time, many communities face the loss of Elders, the decline of languages, restricted access to land, and the effects of pollution and climate change, which can make long-standing observations less reliable as conditions shift.

The chapters that follow take up specific subjects. Chapter 134 examines Indigenous fire management and the revival of cultural burning. Chapter 135 considers salmon, bison, caribou, and treaty stewardship. Chapters 136 and 137 examine conflicts over mining, energy, and pipelines, in which the questions of consent and authority raised here appear in concrete form. Chapter 138 turns to climate change, in which Indigenous observations of altered ice, permafrost, and wildlife are central evidence, and Indigenous adaptation raises the question of what it means to keep knowledge alive in a changing environment. Together they show that the history of Indigenous environmental knowledge is inseparable from the history of Indigenous nationhood.

The long history of Indigenous environmental knowledge is neither a tale of harmonious life before colonization nor a modern story in which governments finally discovered Indigenous expertise. It is the history of people observing particular environments, refining practices, assigning responsibilities, and adapting to change. The same historical record contains sophisticated ecological engineering, contested harvesting decisions, institutional experimentation, and periods of damaging disruption. Treating all of these as parts of one history gives Indigenous peoples the historical agency denied both by romantic idealization and by colonial dismissal.

The chapter has shown that the act of knowing depends on social conditions. Observations of sea ice or a river's flow may be made individually, but the expertise needed to interpret them is transmitted within families and communities. Local languages and place names record distinctions; councils, houses, and other institutions regulate conduct; and opportunities for practice reinforce or weaken what is passed to the next generation. That system can be injured when colonial governments remove people from their homelands even if archived descriptions of a resource remain available.

Environmental sovereignty also requires institutional distinctions. Government scientists may invite Indigenous people to contribute observations without ceding the authority to decide how a project proceeds. An advisory board can improve policy without holding the legal power of an Indigenous government. Agreements may create meaningful collaboration while remaining vulnerable to changes in law or executive decisions, as the Bears Ears developments of 2016–2026 illustrate. The history of recognition is therefore not equivalent to a history of completed self-government.

Knowledge traditions are dynamic precisely because environments and communities are dynamic. Land-based instruction can combine older seasonal calendars with contemporary weather instruments; Guardians can pair community knowledge with formal field methods; farmers can preserve heirloom varieties while adapting to changing rainfall. These developments call for historical analysis of evidence, authority, and outcomes rather than a search for an imagined pure tradition. There can be genuine disagreements within and among Indigenous communities about appropriate stewardship, and scholarship should not erase them in the name of presenting a unified Indigenous perspective.

The next chapters build on this foundation. Chapter 134 examines cultural burning and forest management, Chapter 135 investigates fisheries and wildlife stewardship, and Chapters 136–138 address extractive industry, pipelines, and climate change. Those subjects involve specialized technologies, law, economies, and environmental impacts. Their shared starting point is the recognition that Indigenous environmental knowledge is not detached information: it is historically embedded in the peoples, territories, institutions, and responsibilities through which it becomes meaningful and consequential.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Indigenous Environmental Knowledge from Antiquity to the Present

What Changed

  • Access to territory contracted sharply from the sixteenth century through the twentieth, removing many places where knowledge was practiced
  • Species, tools, markets, and regulations changed what was harvested and how, including firearms, nets, commercial fisheries, and seasonal limits
  • Transmission shifted from daily family practice toward a mix of family teaching, schools, programs, and recorded media
  • Recognition moved from dismissal to selective inclusion in law and policy, beginning mainly in the 1970s

What Stayed the Same

  • Place-based observation and the responsibility to pass knowledge to the next generation
  • Ethical frameworks tying harvest to relationship, gratitude, and restraint in many nations
  • Assertions of authority over territories and resources, expressed through ceremony, law, and diplomacy
  • Adaptation to changing conditions, which was a feature of the knowledge before and after contact

Pattern: Change was uneven and often abrupt where law, dispossession, or schooling intervened, and gradual where communities adapted techniques and species. Those who lived on the land, especially hunters, harvesters, farmers, and Elders, experienced the greatest effects of disruption and continue to carry much of the knowledge. The pattern matters because it shows that loss and persistence coexist and that recovery requires attention to both.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined Indigenous environmental knowledge as a historical subject. It defined the field as an accumulation of observation, practice, ethics, and law tied to particular places, and it argued that such knowledge was neither timeless nor merely folkloric. Communities facing real consequences for error built detailed understandings of ice, tides, plants, animals, soils, and weather, and they revised those understandings as conditions changed.

The chapter reviewed the vocabulary through which the subject is discussed. Traditional ecological knowledge emerged as a scholarly and administrative term in the 1980s, and many Indigenous scholars and communities have preferred terms such as Indigenous knowledge, Native science, Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit, or netukulimk, which keep knowledge connected to ethics and governance. Two-Eyed Seeing, developed by Mi'kmaw Elder Albert Marshall and colleagues, offered a model for working across Indigenous and Western knowledge without ranking them.

Knowledge was produced through repeated observation, seed selection, trials of harvesting methods, and comparison across households and generations. It was carried by stories, songs, ceremonies, apprenticeship, place names, and mnemonic records, and held by people with recognized roles. Protocols governed who could learn and share it. Language and place names stored information, as the work of Keith Basso with Western Apache communities and Inuit wayfinding research illustrate.

Many nations framed environmental practice in relational and legal terms. The Haudenosaunee Thanksgiving Address, Mi'kmaw netukulimk, Anishinaabe teachings about manoomin, and the principles described by Robin Wall Kimmerer illustrate ethics that tie harvest to gratitude, restraint, and reciprocity. The concept of kincentric ecology offers an analytical generalization, with the caution that ideals and conduct can diverge.

Regional surveys showed the range of knowledge systems: Inuit sea-ice and marine knowledge, Subarctic trapping and hunting expertise, Northwest Coast clam gardens, root gardens, and house stewardship, Plateau First Foods, Great Lakes wild rice and forest management, Plains river-valley gardening, Southwestern dry farming, and Mexican milpa, chinampa, and community forestry. These examples were not a complete catalog, and they demonstrated how each knowledge system was built in a particular environment.

Archaeological and ecological evidence indicated that many landscapes were cultivated and managed, undermining the "pristine" idea. The chapter stressed that this conclusion supported Indigenous claims to governance of land while not requiring the opposite romantic claim that management was always harmonious.

Colonial states relied on Indigenous knowledge while disrupting its conditions. Dispossession, bison destruction, dams such as Garrison and The Dalles, park creation, game laws, fire suppression, residential and boarding schools, and missionary suppression of ceremonies reduced access to places and interrupted transmission. Communities nonetheless preserved and adapted knowledge through family teaching, selective sharing, continued harvest, and legal action.

Collectors, ethnographers, and environmental movements produced images of Indigenous peoples that served non-Indigenous purposes. The "Chief Seattle" speech case showed how a widely quoted environmental text came from layers of settler and twentieth-century authorship. The "ecological Indian" debate showed disagreement about the scale and meaning of sustainable practice, with scholars such as Calvin Martin, Shepard Krech III, and their critics offering different readings, and with a third approach calling for specificity and attention to context.

From the 1970s, Indigenous political action brought knowledge into land claims, co-management, protected areas, and environmental review. The bowhead whale case, the Nunavut Agreement, Gwaii Haanas, and later developments such as the Canadian Impact Assessment Act and international instruments showed both achievements and limits. Indigenous nations also asserted knowledge sovereignty through principles such as OCAP and research strategies such as the National Inuit Strategy on Research, laying the groundwork for the fire, fisheries, extraction, pipeline, and climate chapters that follow.

The 2026 controversy over Bears Ears illustrates how quickly federally recognized participation can be challenged by changes in executive policy and by litigation. It demonstrates the difference between having expertise acknowledged in a management plan and possessing secure authority that cannot be altered without contest. A historian must distinguish the proclamation’s stated effects from the outcome of the legal challenge, which remained unresolved at the chapter’s November 2026 cutoff.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it reframes a familiar subject. Environmental history is often told as a story of what happened to land, with Indigenous peoples as either its original inhabitants or its victims. Treating knowledge as a historical subject shows Indigenous nations as thinkers, observers, and governors of their territories, whose understanding was neither static nor secondary to anyone else's science. It also shows why dispossession was an intellectual as well as a territorial loss.

The chapter reveals a recurring pattern: states and institutions that relied on Indigenous knowledge while restricting its practice, followed by Indigenous political action that compelled recognition. That pattern appeared in the bowhead dispute, the Haida defense of Gwaii Haanas, land-claims research, and the development of data principles. It also shows that recognition is neither automatic nor permanent, and that it can be narrowed or expanded as governments change.

The chapter also demonstrates the value of careful evidence. The "Chief Seattle" speech, the "ecological Indian" debate, and the cautions about oral and ethnographic sources show that claims about Indigenous peoples and nature must be tested like any others. Doing so does not weaken Indigenous claims to authority; it grounds them in documented practice, law, and political standing rather than in stereotype.

The next chapters apply these themes in particular fields: fire and forests, fisheries and wildlife, extraction and pipelines, and a changing climate. In each, the questions raised here recur: who knows a place, who decides, and how knowledge can be kept alive. Readers who carry these questions forward will see that Indigenous environmental knowledge is not only a record of the past but part of the continuing history of Indigenous nationhood, the argument with which this textbook concludes.
