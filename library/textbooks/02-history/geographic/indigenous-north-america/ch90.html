Wounded Knee, 1890

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

On the morning of December 29, 1890, several hundred soldiers of the United States Army surrounded a camp of Miniconjou and Hunkpapa Lakota people on a creek in the southwestern corner of present-day South Dakota. Within a short time, the camp had become a field of killing. This chapter reconstructs how that happened, who was there, what survivors endured, and how the event has been remembered, contested, and carried forward by Lakota families ever since.

The community that the Seventh Cavalry escorted to Wounded Knee Creek on December 28 was not an army. It was a traveling community of roughly three hundred and fifty people led by a Miniconjou headman whom the sources name Spotted Elk and whom the Army and the press called Big Foot. The group included men, women, children, and elders, among them a number of Hunkpapa Lakota who had fled the Standing Rock Reservation after the killing of Sitting Bull two weeks earlier. Its leader was gravely ill. By most accounts the community was traveling toward the Pine Ridge Reservation, where Oglala leaders had invited its headman to help preserve peace. By the evening of December 28 it was encamped under military guard, surrounded by troops and artillery. Fewer than twenty-four hours later, a large share of its members were dead or dying.

This chapter is about that day and its consequences. It is not primarily a chapter about the Ghost Dance, which Chapter 89 has already examined, including the teachings of Wovoka, the movement's spread among western Indigenous communities, and the federal anxieties that brought thousands of troops to the Lakota reservations in the autumn of 1890. Here the religious movement appears as background: one element in a crisis that also included the loss of land, reduced rations, drought, epidemic illness, coercive agency administration, and the long aftermath of the wars examined in Chapters 75 through 77. The central argument of the chapter is that Wounded Knee was not the inevitable result of Indigenous religious revitalization. It resulted from a succession of federal, administrative, and military decisions, made within a system of territorial conquest and coercive control, each of which narrowed the choices available to the people confined at the creek.

The chapter is organized in five divisions of four sections each. The first traces the road to Wounded Knee: the structural crisis on the Lakota reservations, the history and composition of Spotted Elk's community, the movements that followed the death of Sitting Bull, and the interception of the community on December 28. The second reconstructs the morning of December 29, including the terrain, the military positions, the order to disarm, the accounts of Lakota participants, and the disputed moment of the first gunshot. The third examines the killing itself, the weapons and terrain that shaped it, the experiences of women, children, and elders, and the problem of counting the dead. The fourth follows the aftermath: the wounded, the blizzard, the burial of the dead in a mass grave, the military inquiries, and the construction of an official narrative in newspapers and photographs. The fifth considers historical judgment, the long Lakota effort to secure recognition and justice, and the place of Wounded Knee in Indigenous and American memory through the autumn of 2026.

Reconstructing Wounded Knee requires unusual care with evidence. The principal sources include the reports and testimony of army officers, the statements of Lakota participants and witnesses recorded in 1891 and in later decades, the recollections of interpreters, missionaries, physicians, and newspaper correspondents, contemporary photographs, military maps, and the work of modern archaeologists and historians. These sources differ in perspective, in proximity to the events, and in the interests that shaped them. Army reports were written by officers whose conduct was under review. Lakota statements were taken through interpreters, in English, often in settings controlled by agency or military authorities. Later recollections, including those of descendants, are oral histories transmitted across generations and are not firsthand 1890 eyewitness testimony. This chapter distinguishes among these kinds of evidence throughout and identifies what remains uncertain, including the exact circumstances of the first gunshot and the precise number of dead.

Two kinds of uncertainty must not be confused. Historians cannot establish with confidence who fired the first shot, or precisely how it was fired. That uncertainty is real. But it does not extend to what followed. The evidence is overwhelming that soldiers killed large numbers of Lakota women, children, and unarmed men, that artillery and rifle fire struck a camp and the people fleeing it, and that most of the dead were not combatants in any ordinary sense. The disputed opening moment cannot be used to blur the established killing of noncombatants, and this chapter does not allow it to do so.

The chapter also resists a final misreading. Wounded Knee is sometimes presented as the symbolic close of the Indian Wars and the end of Indigenous resistance. That framing, which appears in nineteenth-century newspapers, military memoirs, and many later textbooks, serves an interpretive purpose: it makes conquest look complete. The historical record shows otherwise. The Lakota nations survived, reorganized, petitioned, testified, remembered, and continued to govern their communities. Survivors such as Dewey Beard and members of the Horn Cloud family built a documentary and commemorative record that outlasted the official account. A monument was raised at the mass grave in 1903. In the twentieth century descendants and activists invoked the memory of 1890 in new political struggles, including the occupation of 1973 examined in Chapter 112. In 1990 Congress expressed deep regret, and in 2024 through 2026 the question of the Medals of Honor awarded to soldiers at Wounded Knee returned to national attention. Wounded Knee is part of a history that continues.

Students should be prepared to read some of this history with difficulty. It involves the deaths of children and the suffering of families in winter. This textbook neither sensationalizes that suffering nor softens it. The purpose of historical reconstruction here is accuracy, and accuracy requires attention to the victims as persons with names, families, and futures, not only as casualties in an army's report.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the structural conditions of 1889 and 1890, including land loss, reduced rations, drought, disease, and coercive administration, that formed the context of the massacre.
  2. Describe Spotted Elk's role as a Miniconjou leader and the composition and intentions of the community that traveled toward Pine Ridge in December 1890.
  3. Trace the sequence of decisions, from the death of Sitting Bull through the interception of December 28, that placed Spotted Elk's community under military control at Wounded Knee Creek.
  4. Reconstruct the physical geography of the encampment and the spatial relationship between soldiers, artillery, and the Lakota camp.
  5. Analyze the plan to disarm the Lakota men and identify which choices were discretionary rather than militarily unavoidable.
  6. Evaluate conflicting evidence about the first gunshot while distinguishing that uncertainty from the established killing of noncombatants.
  7. Explain how weapons, terrain, and command decisions shaped the pattern of killing, including the role of Hotchkiss guns and ravines.
  8. Assess the experiences of women, children, and elders using documented testimony rather than generalization.
  9. Compare casualty estimates from military reports, survivor accounts, and later research, and explain why they differ.
  10. Analyze the military investigations and the competing assessments of Colonel James W. Forsyth and Major General Nelson A. Miles.
  11. Interpret contemporary newspapers and photographs as evidence, identifying both what they document and what they cannot prove.
  12. Evaluate the terminology of "battle" and "massacre," and trace Lakota survivance, commemoration, and political action from 1891 through 2026.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why is it historically misleading to explain Wounded Knee as the direct and inevitable result of the Ghost Dance?
  2. How did the loss of land, reduced rations, and coercive agency administration shape the choices available to Lakota communities in 1890?
  3. What do the sources reveal about Spotted Elk's aims in December 1890, and how did military assumptions differ from Lakota explanations?
  4. Which decisions made between December 15 and December 29 were avoidable, and who made them?
  5. How do historians reconstruct an event for which the principal participants gave accounts through interpreters, under official pressure, and with different degrees of firsthand knowledge?
  6. Why can uncertainty about the first gunshot coexist with certainty that noncombatants were killed?
  7. How did terrain, weapons, and the arrangement of troops shape who survived and who did not?
  8. Why do estimates of the dead vary, and what does the range of estimates reveal about the evidence?
  9. Why did official investigations largely protect the officers involved, and what interests shaped those conclusions?
  10. How have Lakota survivors and descendants preserved and used the memory of Wounded Knee, and how does that memory bear on questions of recognition and justice today?

Historical Context

The Lakota, the western division of the Oceti Sakowin (Seven Council Fires), had been a dominant Plains power in the nineteenth century. Their political life was organized around bands (tiyospaye) and larger divisions, including the Oglala, Sicangu (Brule), Hunkpapa, Miniconjou, Sihasapa, Itazipco (Sans Arc), and Oohenunpa (Two Kettle). Chapters 49, 50, and 81 have traced the transformation of Plains societies by horses, commerce, and the destruction of the buffalo economy. Chapters 75 through 77 have traced the wars and treaties of the 1860s and 1870s: Red Cloud's War, the Treaty of Fort Laramie of 1868 and the Great Sioux Reservation, the conflict over the Black Hills, and the Great Sioux War culminating at the Little Bighorn. By the early 1880s most Lakota communities had been confined to reservation agencies, dependent on federal rations, and subject to the administrative systems examined in Chapter 84.

The year 1889 brought a new rupture. Congress passed legislation dividing the Great Sioux Reservation into several smaller reservations, including Pine Ridge, Rosebud, Cheyenne River, Standing Rock, and Lower Brule, and opening a large portion of the former reservation to non-Indian settlement. Commissioners obtained Lakota signatures under conditions that many Lakota people contested, and the arrangement, set within the allotment philosophy discussed in Chapter 86, cut deeply into the land base that the 1868 treaty had guaranteed. At the same time, ration allotments were reduced, crops failed in a drought year, and epidemic illnesses moved through the reservations. Agents, missionaries, and teachers pressed the schools and policies examined in Chapters 87 and 88 on Lakota families. These pressures formed the setting in which Lakota communities received the news of Wovoka's teachings, examined in Chapter 89, and in which army officers and agency officials interpreted Lakota gatherings as signs of insurrection.

Historians must avoid two opposing simplifications. One treats the Lakota of 1890 as fanatical converts to a violent movement. The other treats them as passive victims with no strategy of their own. The evidence shows Lakota leaders making hard decisions under pressure: some moving toward the Badlands, some staying near agencies, some serving in the agency police or as army scouts, and some, like Spotted Elk, negotiating between military demands and the safety of their families. The Army, too, was not a monolith. Officers differed about policy, about the interpretation of the Ghost Dance, and about how far to press for arrests and disarmament. The path to Wounded Knee passed through many such decisions.

The Road to Wounded Knee

Historical context, Lakota decisions, and military intervention

Section 90.1

The Lakota Crisis of 1890: Land, Hunger, Sovereignty, and Federal Power

To understand Wounded Knee it is necessary to begin with the conditions on the Lakota reservations in 1890, not with the massacre and not with the Ghost Dance. The Lakota had agreed in the Treaty of Fort Laramie of 1868 to a reservation that, in the treaty's own terms, was to be set apart for their use, with the Black Hills at its center. Lakota leaders understood the treaty as an agreement between nations and as a guarantee against further loss. The United States, within a decade, seized the Black Hills after the Great Sioux War, as Chapter 77 describes, and in 1889 Congress divided what remained of the Great Sioux Reservation into smaller reservations and opened millions of acres to settlement. Lakota people experienced these measures as breaches of treaty obligation. Many leaders had opposed the 1889 arrangement, and the manner in which signatures were gathered remained a matter of bitter dispute.

Land loss was tied to hunger. The buffalo economy had collapsed in the early 1880s, a transformation traced in Chapter 81, and Lakota communities depended on rations issued under federal treaty and statute. Those rations were reduced in 1889 and 1890 as appropriations and contracting decisions tightened. In the same period drought damaged the gardens and small farms that agents urged Lakota families to cultivate, and epidemic illnesses, including measles, whooping cough, and influenza, were reported on several reservations. Contemporary agents and missionaries described hunger and sickness in their own correspondence, even as they argued about their causes. Historians do not claim that ration cuts alone produced the crisis. They do show that insecurity about food, health, and land formed the everyday background against which the Lakota heard about Wovoka's teachings and against which agents and officers read Lakota gatherings.

The reservations were also governed by coercive administrative systems. Agents controlled rations, regulated movement, supervised schools, and could request troops. Agency police, who were Lakota men serving under federal officers, enforced agency rules. Children were pressed into boarding and day schools (Chapters 87 and 88), ceremonies including the Sun Dance had been prohibited, and the allotment program (Chapter 86) was reorganizing the relationship between families and land. Authority within Lakota communities was divided and contested. Some leaders sought accommodation, some resisted openly, and many tried to do both at different times. The attempt in December 1890 to arrest Sitting Bull, carried out by agency police, drew on this divided and strained arrangement and exposed its fault lines.

The Army's presence in the autumn of 1890 followed requests from nervous agents. By late November thousands of soldiers had been deployed to or near Pine Ridge and Rosebud, a concentration so large that, by some estimates, it represented a substantial fraction of the Army's strength. Army commanders and agency officials framed the Ghost Dance as a threat. Some officers privately expressed doubt that the dancing itself was a military danger. Others pressed for arrests of leaders they regarded as fomenters of disturbance. This difference between ceremonial practice and military threat is central: soldiers were mobilized in response to religious gatherings and the fear of what they might become, and the people at Wounded Knee were treated as potential combatants because of how they were categorized.

The violence at Wounded Knee therefore emerged from a broader colonial crisis rather than from an isolated misunderstanding. A misunderstanding explanation suggests that more careful communication would have prevented the tragedy, and some officers and later writers advanced that explanation. It captures part of the story: interpreters, panic, and confusion mattered. But it leaves out the structure within which the misunderstandings took place. Soldiers held overwhelming force, Lakota people had been stripped of most of their land and subjected to administrative control, and the Army's plan, as it developed, treated disarmament of a captive community as the next step. The question of why these particular decisions were made, and by whom, is the subject of the sections that follow.

Cause and Effect

From Structural Pressure to Killing: Why Wounded Knee Occurred

Long-term causes

Treaty violations and land loss from 1868 through 1889; the collapse of the buffalo economy; dependence on rations; agency control of daily life; the legacy of the Great Sioux War.

Short-term causes

Ration reductions, drought, and illness in 1889–1890; spread of the Ghost Dance on Lakota reservations; agents' requests for troops; the mobilization of thousands of soldiers.

Immediate trigger and context

The killing of Sitting Bull on December 15; army orders to arrest or watch leaders such as Spotted Elk; the flight of Hunkpapa people; interception of Spotted Elk's community on December 28.

The event

The attempted disarmament of December 29, a disputed first gunshot, and the army's use of rifles and Hotchkiss guns on a camp full of women, children, and men.

Immediate effects

Deaths of well over one hundred Lakota people, probably substantially more; about fifty wounded survivors taken to Pine Ridge; dispersal of families; hardening of army narrative as a "battle."

Long-term consequences

A mass grave and a living commemorative tradition; contested investigations; medals awarded to soldiers; survivors' compensation claims; political memory invoked in 1973, 1990, and 2024–2026.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the massacre. The religious movement mattered because officials interpreted it through fear, but the decisions that placed armed troops around a captive community were made by identifiable commanders working within a coercive system. Contingency also mattered: a leader's illness, a missed rendezvous, a morning's weather, the arrangement of troops, and a struggle over one rifle.

Section 90.2

Spotted Elk and the Miniconjou Lakota Community

Historians and descendants often use two names for the leader at the center of this chapter. Army officers, newspapers, and much later writing call him Big Foot, a rendering of a Lakota name. Lakota accounts and many modern scholars also use Spotted Elk, a name by which he was known in his own community. This chapter uses Spotted Elk as the principal name, in keeping with the preference of many descendants, and notes Big Foot where sources use it. Treating him as a historical person rather than as an exotic figure in an army report matters, because the army's dossier on him, which portrayed him as a fomenter of disturbance, helped shape the decisions that put his community at risk.

Spotted Elk was a Miniconjou Lakota headman whose community was based on the Cheyenne River Reservation. The Miniconjou were one of the seven divisions of the Lakota, with relatives among the Oglala, Hunkpapa, and other divisions through marriage and alliance. Kinship, more than any formal tribal structure, organized political life: bands were extended families whose headmen held influence through generosity, reputation, and the consent of those who followed them. Spotted Elk was an experienced leader. By the accounts of Lakota people and of some army and agency officers, he had a reputation as a negotiator and mediator who advocated peaceful accommodation with the United States while trying to protect his people's interests. His community had joined in Ghost Dance ceremonies, as had many other Lakota communities, but contemporary evidence does not show him as the planner of an uprising. The distinction between a leader who permitted a ceremony and a leader who organized a war is the heart of the matter, and army reports did not always maintain it.

The traveling community that left the Cheyenne River in late December was a cross-section of an ordinary Lakota band under stress. Commonly cited counts, drawn from army reports made at Wounded Knee, put the party at roughly 350 people, including about 120 men and older boys and about 230 women and children. Such figures are estimates and should not be read as precise. The group included elders, mothers with infants, and families who had sought safety with a respected leader as soldiers and agency police moved through the region. It also included people from outside Spotted Elk's immediate band. After the killing of Sitting Bull on December 15, a number of Hunkpapa people from the Standing Rock area, commonly estimated at about forty, left their homes and made their way to the Cheyenne River, where some joined Spotted Elk's community. They had seen what happened when the army and agency police tried to arrest a leader, and they sought a place of safety.

Distinguishing among the Lakota groups involved clarifies the political relationships. The Hunkpapa of Standing Rock had been Sitting Bull's people, and his death left them frightened and angry. The Miniconjou of the Cheyenne River were a separate division with their own leaders and their own agency, though many were related to Hunkpapa families. The Oglala of Pine Ridge were led by prominent headmen who had themselves taken different positions: some, like Red Cloud, were older leaders who had negotiated with the United States for decades, while younger men had joined the dancers or were scouting for the army. Spotted Elk's decision to travel toward Pine Ridge reflected these networks. Oglala leaders were reported to have urged him to come, and his ability to speak across divisions was part of why army commanders regarded him with suspicion and Lakota people regarded him with respect.

Reconstructing the community also reveals the limits of the evidence. We know a great deal about army counts and officers' impressions, and relatively little about individual members of the party before December 28 beyond what survivors later said. Names appear in later testimony, and several survivors, including Dewey Beard and members of the Horn Cloud family, described their families' participation. But the full roster of the community remains incomplete, and the identification of the dead, as §90.12 explains, depended on records that were compiled under difficult conditions and, in some cases, much later. The Lakota people who followed Spotted Elk should be understood as families with histories and relationships, each of whom made decisions about whether to stay, go, or seek another refuge.

Section 90.3

December 1890: Fear, Flight, and the Search for Safety

The immediate context of Spotted Elk's movements was the death of Sitting Bull on December 15, 1890. Army and agency officials, anxious about the Ghost Dance among the Hunkpapa at Standing Rock, arranged for Lakota agency police to arrest the Hunkpapa leader at his home on the Grand River. In the confrontation that followed, Sitting Bull and several of his followers were killed, and several agency police officers also died. This chapter treats those events only as context. What matters for Wounded Knee is the effect: the arrest attempt demonstrated that armed intervention against a Lakota leader could produce immediate violence, and it caused groups of Hunkpapa to flee, some toward the Cheyenne River and Spotted Elk.

Army commanders meanwhile regarded Spotted Elk as a leader to be watched or arrested. Officers stationed near the Cheyenne River, including units of the Eighth Cavalry, had been in contact with his community, and some of the exchanges were reasonably cordial. Reports from the period indicate that Spotted Elk repeatedly expressed his intention to avoid trouble, and that on at least one occasion he agreed to move his community near army forces. But senior commanders were also issuing orders to arrest leaders considered dangerous, and Spotted Elk appeared on such a list. The combination of a cooperative local relationship and a more aggressive arrest policy meant that his community lived with a growing sense of insecurity in the week after Sitting Bull's death.

Lakota accounts and army reports both indicate that Spotted Elk's community left its camp on the Cheyenne River in the last days before Christmas, after learning that army forces intended to take him into custody or disarm his people. Accounts differ about precisely what triggered the departure and about the date, but the best-supported reconstruction places it around December 23. The community then traveled south and southwest across difficult country, through the Badlands region, toward the Pine Ridge Reservation. By Lakota accounts, the purpose was to reach Pine Ridge, where leaders had urged Spotted Elk to come and help broker peace and where relatives lived. Army officers, by contrast, often interpreted the movement as flight or as a move to join Lakota people thought to be gathering in the Badlands. These contrasting explanations are not equally supported: the direction of travel, toward the agency and away from the area of reported dancer concentration, favors the Lakota account, though the evidence is not complete and some army officers believed the party might turn toward the dancers.

Spotted Elk himself was gravely ill. Accounts describe a serious respiratory illness, commonly called pneumonia, and report that he traveled in a wagon and was often unable to speak above a whisper or to ride. His condition shaped the journey: it slowed the party, made negotiations harder, and left him dependent on the care of his family. It also bears on the army's interpretation of the party as a threat. A leader in his condition was not obviously commanding a military force. Yet officers who had sought him for days and who thought of him as a source of danger did not apparently treat his illness as evidence about his aims.

Students should note that "competing intentions" attributed to the community reflected the interests of those making the claims. Army officers described a group that might be hostile or might be fleeing; some feared it might join dancers in the Badlands. Agency officials saw a disturbance. Lakota survivors recalled a community seeking safety and a leader trying to prevent bloodshed. The surviving evidence about the group's actual intentions consists of what its members later said, what army scouts and officers reported about the party's route, and the fact that when soldiers appeared on December 28 the party raised a flag of truce and, by most accounts, accompanied the troops without resistance. That behavior is more consistent with the Lakota explanation than with a plan for battle.

Section 90.4

December 28: Interception by the Seventh Cavalry

On December 28, a battalion of the Seventh Cavalry commanded by Major Samuel M. Whitside intercepted Spotted Elk's community near Porcupine Butte, in the country east of Pine Ridge Agency. The scouts who located the party included Lakota men serving the army. Accounts agree that the Lakota displayed a white flag and that Spotted Elk, who was ill, met Whitside and arranged for the party to travel with the soldiers. Whitside, according to his own report and other testimony, took the ailing leader into an army ambulance. Spotted Elk was reported to have said that he had been headed for Pine Ridge. The soldiers then directed the community southwest to Wounded Knee Creek, a trip of several miles, where the Army planned to hold it for the night.

The decision to move the community to Wounded Knee Creek, rather than to Pine Ridge Agency a short distance farther on, was a command decision. It reflected the Army's wish to keep the party under control, to guard against the possibility of flight, and to prepare the disarmament that senior commanders regarded as necessary. It also placed the community in a location that the Army could surround and in which artillery could be positioned. The Lakota people did not resist the move. By most accounts they set up their tipis, with soldiers' help in some cases, on a flat west of the creek. The camp was subjected to a count, and troops were posted around it. Army surgeons and others provided some assistance, and the ailing Spotted Elk was placed in a heated tent, where a regimental surgeon treated him.

Later on the evening of December 28, Colonel James W. Forsyth arrived with the rest of the Seventh Cavalry and took command. The regiment was now present in strength: with Whitside's battalion and Forsyth's arrival, there were several hundred soldiers at the site. A battery of Hotchkiss rapid-fire guns, commanded by Captain Allyn Capron and drawn from the First Artillery, had also arrived, and four guns were positioned on a rise overlooking the camp. Army scouts, including Lakota scouts from Pine Ridge, were present, as were interpreters, among them Philip Wells and John Shangreau, whose translations later became central evidence. A Catholic priest, Father Francis Craft, was also with the troops. The soldiers were aware that the Seventh Cavalry had been Custer's regiment, a fact that some contemporaries and later writers have emphasized, though the evidence about its influence on decisions at Wounded Knee is disputed (see the Historical Debate in §90.17).

The orders carried by Forsyth are known primarily through army correspondence and later testimony. The commanders, including Brigadier General John R. Brooke and Major General Nelson A. Miles, had directed that Spotted Elk's community be captured, disarmed, and removed from the area; reports indicate that the intention was to take the people to the railroad and ship them away, with Omaha mentioned as a destination. The plan treated the Lakota as prisoners rather than as a community on its way to the agency. Whether commanders anticipated that disarming a captive community of families in winter might provoke violence is a question the surviving records answer imperfectly. What the records show is that the plan placed armed soldiers, artillery, and several hundred Lakota people, most of them women and children, in close proximity, and left to the morning the delicate and dangerous task of taking weapons from the men.

On the night of December 28, the Lakota families were therefore under military control. Their leader lay ill in a tent. Soldiers surrounded the camp, with guns trained on it, as Lakota people lay down for the night. Sources do not allow a reliable reconstruction of what individual family members felt or said that evening, and this chapter does not fabricate it. What can be said is that, on the army's own account, the community had arrived peacefully, accepted escort, and made camp where it was told. The next morning's events would take place within a situation that the army had created.

Section 90.5

Wounded Knee Creek: Terrain, Encampment, and Military Positions

Students cannot follow the events of December 29 without a picture of the ground. Wounded Knee Creek is a small, winding stream in the rolling prairie of the Pine Ridge Reservation, in the southwestern part of present-day South Dakota. The land around it is not flat. The creek bottom is bordered by slopes and low ridges, and the terrain is cut by dry ravines that drain toward the creek. The camp on which the army's attention was fixed lay on a flat area west of the creek. A rise to the north of the camp, later the site of the mass grave and, eventually, of a church and cemetery, overlooked the flat and commanded the approaches. A ravine, often called the dry ravine or the main ravine, ran south and southwest of the camp, and smaller draws opened toward the creek. The terrain gave soldiers elevated positions from which to observe and fire into the camp and gave fleeing people limited and dangerous routes of escape.

The army's deployment on the evening of December 28 and the morning of December 29 is reconstructed from reports, maps, testimony, and archaeological work. The Lakota tipis stood in an area that the troops surrounded on several sides. Soldiers of the Seventh Cavalry, dismounted for much of the morning's operation, were arranged in lines and groups around the camp and the council area where the men would be assembled. Four Hotchkiss guns on the rise to the north looked down onto the tipis. Mounted troops were positioned to prevent escape. Lakota scouts and interpreters moved among the soldiers. The geometry matters because it created a situation in which soldiers stood on opposite sides of a compact area. If shooting began, soldiers fired toward and sometimes across the camp, and the Lakota people in the middle had no safe direction.

National Park Service documentation of the Wounded Knee site, along with historic military maps and later archaeological surveys, provides the spatial evidence on which modern reconstructions depend. Students should use these with care. Military maps of 1891 reflect the army's view of the field, drawn after the event and shaped by the need to illustrate reports. Later surveys have located artifacts, including cartridge cases, that indicate where soldiers fired from and, with more uncertainty, where fighting occurred. The site was altered by erosion, burial, building, and, in 1973, by a second occupation and armed standoff. The terrain on which the massacre took place is therefore both a primary source and a landscape that has changed.

A useful reconstruction begins by separating three distinct scales of space. At the regional scale, Spotted Elk's party was moving between agency jurisdictions and across a landscape that offered few winter routes suitable for a large group with families and an ill leader. At the encampment scale, the soldiers controlled the approaches to the creek bottom; they could move between their bivouacs and command positions while the Lakota were expected to remain in place. At the scale of the disarmament circle, men, guards, and interpreters occupied a much smaller area in which weapons could be seized only at close quarters. Statements about the army's numerical advantage are incomplete unless students also understand this spatial asymmetry: a large force held the perimeter, and the smaller community faced an organized command that could redeploy men and guns.

The landscape must not be mistaken for a diagram of exact ballistic trajectories. Section 90.6

Colonel Forsyth's Orders and the Attempted Disarmament

The morning of December 29 began with a military operation. Colonel Forsyth, according to the officers' accounts, directed that the Lakota men be assembled, separated from the women and children, and made to surrender their weapons. The regiment's command structure placed Forsyth in overall charge, with Whitside and other officers commanding battalions and troops. The orders were given to the Lakota through interpreters, and the men were called to a council. Reports describe the men being asked to come forward from the camp, seated in a group in front of the soldiers, and told to bring their weapons. The women and children remained in or near the tipis, in the area south and west of the men.

The surrender of arms did not go as the army expected. Officers reported that the men produced few weapons, and soldiers were therefore sent to search the tipis. This search, conducted by soldiers who moved through the camp, entering and overturning family dwellings in the presence of women and children, produced more rifles but no complete surrender. Surviving accounts differ on how many weapons were turned over or found, and on whether the soldiers considered the number inadequate. The Lakota men, for their part, held rifles that were valuable, in many cases purchased at significant cost, and that served hunting and, in the perception of some owners, protection. Whether men understood that the surrender would be final, or what would happen to them afterward, is uncertain. Some Lakota accounts suggest suspicion that the soldiers meant to harm them.

The communication problems were substantial. Orders were conveyed through interpreters including Philip Wells and John Shangreau. Translation across Lakota and English, under tension, produced uncertainty about what had been asked and what had been agreed to. Many of the men did not speak English. Some of the exchanges between officers and the men were witnessed by Father Craft and others. The interpreters later gave testimony about what they heard. As §90.8 explains, their accounts differ from one another and from Lakota recollections, and their positions at the time of the shooting affected what they could see.

Historians evaluating the operation ask which of the army's choices were discretionary rather than militarily unavoidable. The plan to disarm the men was a policy decision, not a necessity imposed by Lakota actions on the morning of December 29. The community had come peacefully, and its members were under guard. Several choices made the operation hazardous. Forsyth chose to proceed with disarmament that morning rather than, for example, escorting the community to Pine Ridge Agency and conducting any disarmament there, or delaying it to allow the ailing leader to recover. He chose to carry it out with the Hotchkiss guns trained on the camp. He chose to search tipis while women and children were present. He chose to position soldiers close to the men being disarmed, and in lines that placed some troops opposite others. And he chose to carry it out with an interpreter's help under conditions that left little room for clarification. These were decisions of command, and later investigators asked whether they were prudent. Miles, in particular, criticized the disposition of the troops (see §90.15).

None of this means that a different outcome was certain. Contingency shaped the morning: the number of weapons found, the behavior of individuals, the state of tension among soldiers who had been under strain for weeks, and the ceremonial actions of some Lakota men. But recognizing contingency is different from treating the result as unavoidable. The plan itself created the conditions under which a struggle over one weapon could become a massacre.

Section 90.7

Lakota Perspectives on the Disarmament

Reconstructing the morning from Lakota accounts requires attention to who spoke, when, and how. Surviving Lakota testimony falls into several categories. Some of it was given within weeks of the event, in official settings and through interpreters. Some was given years later, in interviews such as those that Judge Eli Ricker conducted in the first decade of the twentieth century with survivors including Dewey Beard and Joseph Horn Cloud. Some is the oral history of descendants, transmitted across generations. These categories differ in their closeness to the event and in the circumstances of recording, and they should not be merged into a single "Lakota account."

The people in the camp were not of one mind. Men in the council area included older leaders, younger men, and ceremonial participants. Some may have been more suspicious of the soldiers than others. Women and children, the majority of the camp's residents, were in different places, and their experience of the morning differed from that of the men. Lakota accounts speak of fear and uncertainty as soldiers searched tipis and took belongings, of the men's reluctance to give up their rifles, and of a sense that something was wrong. They also describe the presence of the medicine man Yellow Bird, who addressed the men and, in several accounts, performed ritual actions, including blowing an eagle-bone whistle and speaking about protection. Interpreters later reported that he told the men the soldiers' bullets would not hurt them. The surviving accounts differ about what exactly Yellow Bird said and about how many people heard him.

The spiritual practices bear on the interpretation of the morning but must be handled carefully. Some Lakota people wore Ghost Dance shirts, which were understood within the movement to confer spiritual protection. Army and press accounts afterward treated the shirts as evidence of fanaticism. Lakota accounts treat them as part of a religious practice, and Chapter 89 has examined their meaning. For Wounded Knee, the question is how much such beliefs shaped the men's behavior. Historians should avoid the assumption that religious belief meant a willingness to fight soldiers on that morning. The evidence does not show a plan for battle; it shows men facing soldiers with rifles in a situation of acute danger, some of whom held to a religious conviction.

The conflicting expectations about the disarmament also matter. Soldiers expected a swift surrender. Lakota men expected, perhaps, to be treated as the escort had implied, as a community on its way to an agency. Some of them regarded the surrender of weapons as dangerous, since weapons were the only means of defense. The women and children, who had no say in the matter, were close to the soldiers, who were searching their tipis. The result was a tense gathering of armed soldiers and unarmed or partly armed people in a small space.

The reliability of witnesses varies with position and proximity. Survivors who were in the council area and who lived to testify had direct knowledge of the moments before the shooting. Others, who were in the camp, saw different things. Lakota leaders who spoke to officials in the weeks after the massacre, including those named in the February 11, 1891 statements discussed below, were in many cases not present at the shooting and relied on what survivors told them. This does not make their statements unreliable, but it determines how they should be used: as evidence of what Lakota communities learned and how they interpreted it, as well as of what happened. Students should identify, for each witness, whether the knowledge claimed is firsthand or secondhand.

Section 90.8

The First Gunshot: Conflicting Accounts and Historical Uncertainty

The circumstances of the first shot are among the most disputed details of Wounded Knee, and the chapter's treatment of this question must be disciplined. The widely cited account holds that, as soldiers were searching the men or attempting to take a rifle from one of them, a gun went off, and soldiers opened fire. Several witnesses, including some soldiers and interpreters, identified a young Lakota man named Black Coyote as the person who was struggling with soldiers over his rifle when it discharged. Some accounts say he was reluctant to give up the weapon, having paid a good deal for it, and that he held it up and said it was his. Other accounts, including later ones that historians treat cautiously, say he did not hear or understand the orders, and some describe him as deaf. The evidence does not allow a definitive statement of his state of mind or of whether the discharge was deliberate.

Competing accounts place the opening shot in different ways. Some army witnesses described a Lakota man firing at soldiers. Some described a gun discharging during a struggle. Some Lakota accounts maintain that soldiers began the shooting, and some describe an accidental discharge during a struggle with soldiers. Several witnesses described Lakota men drawing weapons from beneath their blankets, and some described soldiers firing a volley into the men gathered in the council area at close range. A historian should compare these accounts by asking the following questions. Where was the witness standing? What could he or she see? When was the statement given, and to whom? What did the witness have at stake? Soldiers testifying before inquiries had reason to emphasize Lakota aggression, because their conduct was under review. Lakota witnesses had reason to emphasize the army's responsibility, because they sought redress. Interpreters, whose positions made them important witnesses, were also employed by the army.

The timing of statements matters as well. The earliest accounts, in army reports and in testimony before the court of inquiry in January 1891, were shaped by the institutional context. Lakota testimony taken in February 1891 was recorded through interpreters, and later interviews, such as those in 1906 and afterward, were given after years of reflection and conversation within families. Scholars such as Jerome A. Greene, in American Carnage, and David W. Grua, in Surviving Wounded Knee, have examined these accounts and the politics of their production in detail. Their work indicates that the sources do not converge on a single version of the opening moment, and that careful historians present the evidence and its limits rather than choosing an unverified account as established fact.

What the uncertainty does not do is call into question what followed. A disputed discharge in a struggle over one rifle cannot account for the volume and character of the killing that followed. Whatever happened to Black Coyote's weapon, soldiers fired into a crowded camp and at fleeing women and children, and army artillery fired on tipis. The disputed first shot is a legitimate subject for historical analysis because it bears on questions of intention and on how the army and the Lakota accounted for the event. But it must not become a device for obscuring the established record of the killing of noncombatants. The distinction is essential for any honest account.

The Wounded Knee Massacre

The violence itself, the victims, and the survivors

Section 90.9

The First Minutes: Cavalry Fire and the Collapse of the Encampment

Within moments of the discharge, soldiers opened fire at close range on the men in the council area. Accounts from soldiers, Lakota survivors, and other witnesses agree that the first volleys were devastating and that a large number of Lakota men fell in the first minutes. Some Lakota men drew concealed rifles or revolvers and returned fire, and there was brief, confused, close-quarters fighting in the council area, including hand-to-hand struggles with knives, clubs, and rifles used as clubs. Father Craft, who was standing near the men, was reported to have been struck or stabbed in the struggle. Army casualties in these first minutes included officers and enlisted men, among them Captain George D. Wallace of Troop K, who was killed.

The scene then widened. Soldiers in the surrounding lines fired not only at the men but into the camp, where women, children, and elders stood or crouched near the tipis. The Hotchkiss guns on the rise opened fire. Tipis were struck and some were torn apart or set afire. People ran. The accounts describe confusion on both sides: soldiers firing in different directions, officers shouting orders, Lakota people scattering toward the creek and the ravines. Smoke and noise made it difficult for witnesses to see or to understand what was happening. The experience of the first minutes thus varied sharply with location. A man in the council area saw soldiers at close range; a woman near a tipi saw people falling; an artilleryman on the rise saw people running across the flat below.

Historians analyzing this stage consult three types of evidence. Military reports and court testimony describe the soldiers' actions and often emphasize Lakota resistance. Lakota accounts describe the soldiers' fire on the camp and on fleeing people. Physical evidence, including the distribution of cartridge cases and the location of bodies recorded in army descriptions, indicates where soldiers fired and where people died. These sources do not agree on every detail, but they converge on key points: that soldiers fired into the camp, that women and children were among the dead, that some Lakota men resisted with the weapons they had, and that the fire from soldiers produced most of the casualties.

The distinction between limited armed resistance and a battle waged by the whole community is central. Evidence shows that some Lakota men fought back, and that some soldiers were killed or wounded by Lakota fire. The Army's casualty figures, roughly two dozen soldiers killed and many more wounded, reflect this. But the Lakota community in the camp consisted largely of women, children, and elders, and many of the men had been disarmed or were in the process of being disarmed when shooting began. Some of the army's own casualties are believed to have resulted from fire by other soldiers, given the arrangement of the troops. Treating the entire camp as a combatant force misdescribes the evidence. As §90.17 explains, the army's account of a battle depended on collapsing this distinction.

The opening minutes were also a breakdown of communication and control within the attacking force. A regimental line designed to constrain movement did not automatically provide a way to identify individual targets when people fell, ran, or tried to take cover. Smoke, shouting, overlapping fields of fire, and soldiers moving between units could make an officer's shouted instruction ineffective even when he intended to stop or redirect shooting. That tactical confusion does not erase responsibility for the deployment: commanders had chosen the placement of armed troops and artillery around an encampment that contained families. The relevant distinction is between uncertainty about each man's target and the foreseeable danger of allowing multiple bodies of troops to fire into or across a confined civilian space. The evidentiary test is whether commanders recognized the presence of noncombatants and whether their decisions made their exposure worse.

Individual soldiers and Lakota participants did not have a uniform experience of those minutes. A soldier wounded almost immediately may have known little of what happened after he fell. A Lakota man in the disarmament area might have seen armed resistance at arm's length, while someone who escaped from a tipi could report fire directed at people attempting to flee. Later reports may combine these different moments into one continuous 'fight.' Historians should therefore ask when a witness could still see the scene, whether the witness changed position, and whether a statement describes an observation or a story heard afterward. This procedure produces a firmer account than a dramatic minute-by-minute narrative assembled from incompatible vantage points.

Section 90.10

Hotchkiss Guns, Ravines, and the Geography of the Killing

The weapons deployed at Wounded Knee shaped the scale and pattern of the killing. Soldiers carried breech-loading rifles and carbines, which they fired at close range into the council area and, within minutes, at people moving across the flat. The four Hotchkiss guns on the rise, rapid-fire light artillery that fired explosive shells, were positioned to sweep the camp. Their use against people in a camp, rather than against a fortified position or a military formation, is one of the features that has led historians and descendants to describe the event as a massacre. Accounts describe shells striking tipis, killing and wounding people inside, and igniting some dwellings. The guns, soldiers' testimony states, were also turned on people running toward the ravines.

Historical Vocabulary
Hotchkiss Gun

Definition: A light, rapid-fire breech-loading artillery piece, developed in the later nineteenth century, that fired explosive shells and could be moved by a small crew. The army's version at Wounded Knee was a small-caliber mountain gun.

Historical context: The Army had adopted this class of weapon for use in frontier campaigns. At Wounded Knee four such guns were manned by artillerymen of the First Artillery who accompanied the Seventh Cavalry. They were placed on a height overlooking the camp.

Why it matters: Artillery gave the army the capacity to inflict casualties quickly and across an area. Its use in a camp of families means that the killing cannot be explained only as a close-quarters struggle among armed men.

Potential misunderstanding: The Hotchkiss gun is sometimes called a machine gun in popular writing. It was an artillery piece firing explosive shells, not a machine gun in the modern sense, and its effects came from explosive shells as well as the volume of fire.

Terrain governed who could escape and who could not. People running from the camp had few options. To the south and southwest the main dry ravine offered cover, but soldiers moved to positions from which they could fire along it, and some soldiers pursued people into it. Smaller draws to the creek provided other routes. Women with children, elders, and the wounded could not move quickly. Accounts by soldiers and Lakota survivors indicate that people were shot as they fled, and that bodies were found along the ravine at distances of up to two or three miles from the camp, according to some reports. Those distances are reported rather than precisely measured, but they indicate that the killing extended across the countryside and not merely within the camp.

The possibility of friendly fire is part of the evidence. With soldiers on opposite sides of the camp and the council area, shots intended for Lakota men or for people in the camp could strike other soldiers. Historians, including Greene, have argued that some of the army's casualties resulted from this crossfire. The exact number cannot be determined from available evidence, and it is sometimes difficult to distinguish between deaths caused by Lakota fire and those caused by army fire. Nonetheless, the pattern bears on the army's claim that its losses showed the ferocity of Lakota resistance: some losses may have reflected the arrangement of its own troops.

Students analyzing the killing should use the National Park Service documentation and the army's 1891 maps as spatial evidence while recognizing their limits. Maps show the positions of troops and, in some cases, where bodies were found, but they were produced by the army, partly to support its account. Archaeological work and the study of cartridge distributions supplement them. Together they suggest a killing that moved across the landscape in stages: close-range fire in the council area, fire into the camp, artillery fire, and pursuit of fleeing people into the ravines and across the open ground.

The Hotchkiss guns should be understood as artillery pieces operated by trained crews, rather than as automatic weapons that fired independently. Their explosive rounds and the elevated position from which they were employed gave the army a form of firepower sharply different from the rifles available to the few Lakota men who retained weapons. This disparity was not merely numerical: artillery could reach places from which an individual rifleman was not visibly firing and could injure people who were sheltering together. The historical issue is therefore not whether every shell can now be assigned to a known casualty. It is the documented choice to employ artillery in terrain where fleeing civilians and possible armed resisters were intermingled. Artillery testimony needs to be read alongside survivor accounts and landscape evidence, not treated as a self-contained technical record.

The ravines also create a problem of scale and language. In some accounts 'the ravine' refers to the prominent dry gulch west of the campsite; other reports describe branches, depressions, or approaches to the creek. A report that troops pursued Lakota people 'up the ravine' does not, without an accompanying map, identify a single point or a fixed distance from the camp. Descriptions of bodies recovered in a gulch may document where people finally fell or were found, not the exact location where they were first wounded. Historians must resist drawing an apparently exact pursuit route from fragmentary testimony. Even with those limits, the combined reports identify the ravines as places of refuge, flight, pursuit, and killing, which is sufficient to explain why the topography matters to the massacre.

Section 90.11

Women, Children, Elders, and the Flight from Wounded Knee

Any account of Wounded Knee that treats the army's operations as the principal subject and the Lakota as casualties misrepresents the event. Most of the people in the camp were women, children, and elders. They had not been summoned to the council. They were in or near the tipis when the shooting began, and their experiences were distinct from those of the men. Survivor accounts describe mothers running with infants, people falling as they ran, families becoming separated, and wounded people hiding in the ravines and brush. Some survivors reached cover and were able to remain hidden. Others were found by soldiers. Some were found days later.

The record of civilian experience is uneven. Soldiers' reports mention women and children among the dead, sometimes in explanatory language intended to account for the presence of civilian casualties in a "battle." Lakota testimony gives more detailed accounts. The statement by American Horse, among those recorded on February 11, 1891, is frequently cited for its description of women and children killed as they fled, including a mother who was shot while carrying an infant; students should consult the published text and the source note on its speaker's knowledge. Survivors' later interviews describe the search for relatives, the discovery of dead family members, and the experience of waiting through a night and a blizzard for help. These accounts are valuable but must be treated as testimony recorded under particular conditions and, in some cases, years later.

Elders and infants were especially vulnerable. People who could not run were killed in or near the camp, and infants were wounded or killed in their mothers' arms. Some children were found alive after the event, including a baby girl who was discovered days later beside her mother's body and was taken from the field. She became known as Zintkala Nuni, or Lost Bird, and her later life, which included adoption by a white family and an early death, is documented in the historical record; her remains were returned to the Lakota for reburial in the twentieth century. Her story is one of many that show how the massacre scattered families and how survivors' lives continued in altered circumstances.

Everyday Life and Human Experience

Dewey Beard and the Horn Cloud Family

Among the survivors whose testimony is documented is Dewey Beard (Wasumaza, also known as Iron Hail), a Miniconjou man who was in Spotted Elk's community and was wounded at Wounded Knee. Beard and his brothers, including Joseph Horn Cloud, gave accounts of the massacre in interviews with Judge Eli Ricker in the early twentieth century, and their family became central to the survivors' effort to preserve an accurate record. According to the accounts of Beard and his family, he had been a young man at the Little Bighorn in 1876, and in 1890 he traveled with his relatives toward Pine Ridge. Accounts by Beard and his brothers report that they lost close relatives at Wounded Knee, including, in Beard's case, members of his immediate family.

These accounts describe the aftermath through the eyes of people who survived and then had to find, mourn, and bury their kin. They describe wounds, flight, hiding, the discovery of the dead, and the effort to obtain recognition for what had been lost. Beard lived until the middle of the twentieth century and, according to commonly cited reports, was among the last adult survivors. Over decades, he and the Horn Cloud family petitioned for compensation, helped organize survivors, and participated in the creation of the 1903 monument at the mass grave.

What this case shows: A single family's documented experience reveals what casualty counts and military reports cannot: the specific names, relationships, and losses behind the numbers; the long work of memory; and the political agency of survivors. It also shows the limits of the record. Interviews recorded years later reflect memory, translation, and the interviewer's purposes, and should be used with the care that applies to any oral testimony. Students should not extrapolate from one family's account to claim that it represents all survivors' experiences.

  1. What kinds of knowledge does a family's testimony provide that an army report cannot?
  2. What limits does the passage of time and the interview process place on such testimony?
  3. How did Beard and the Horn Cloud family act as historical agents after the massacre?
  4. Why should historians avoid presenting one family's experience as representative of all survivors?

A documented family history gives substance to the otherwise anonymous phrase 'the women and children.' Joseph Horn Cloud, who survived as an adolescent, later spoke to Eli S. Ricker about the circumstances of his family and people taking refuge in a pocket of the ravine. Ricker's notes identify Joseph's brothers Dewey Beard and Daniel White Lance among the survivors; History Nebraska's archival description records that Joseph lost his parents, two brothers, and a sister in the massacre. A family relationship is not a military unit, and its losses cannot be understood merely by counting deaths on a report sheet. The brothers' later recollections preserve details of attempted escape, wounds, and the search for safety; as testimony recorded in 1906–1907, they must be distinguished from statements taken in the immediate aftermath. The family history also demonstrates how a record of survival can be simultaneously a record of irreparable loss.

The February 1891 statement attributed to American Horse has frequently shaped narratives of women and children shot while fleeing. It must not be called a firsthand view of those shootings: American Horse stated that he arrived after the firing and saw the scene before burial. His account remains valuable as evidence of what an influential Lakota leader learned from participants and observed in the aftermath, and of the argument he presented to federal officials. For direct experiences of violence and escape, the historian must consult people who identified themselves as present, later interviews whose speakers described their own participation, and other independent evidence. Treating the sources honestly does not diminish the civilian deaths; it protects the people whose histories are being told from claims falsely placed in their mouths.

Section 90.12

Death, Injury, Survival, and the Problem of Casualty Counts

Among the dead was Spotted Elk himself, who was ill and was reported to have been shot while trying to rise from his tent. Photographs taken in the following days show his body frozen on the field. The dead also included Lakota leaders and members of the community, from elders to infants. The soldiers who died included Captain Wallace, and army records list about two dozen killed, with about three dozen or more wounded, some of whom died later. These army figures, like all casualty data, depend on reports compiled in the aftermath.

The number of Lakota dead cannot be fixed precisely, and the sources illustrate why. The army burial party that worked in the first days of January 1891 counted roughly 146 bodies on the field, a figure often cited as the minimum. But that count did not include people who died of their wounds in the days that followed, people whose bodies were removed by relatives, or those who may have died beyond the reach of the party. Survivors and some other observers gave higher estimates. Modern scholarship generally treats the number of Lakota killed as somewhere from about 150 to 300 or more, with wide uncertainty, depending on how deaths from wounds, bodies removed, and people who disappeared are counted. The range reflects incompleteness, not dispute about whether a massacre occurred.

Counts of the dead must also be distinguished from counts of the wounded. After the massacre about fifty wounded Lakota people, according to military reports, were transported to Pine Ridge, the great majority of them women and children. Some of the wounded died, and those who survived often carried injuries for life. Combining these figures or treating wounded people as part of the death toll introduces error. Careful accounts therefore state separately the number counted dead on the field, the number of subsequent deaths from wounds, and the total estimated killed, and identify the sources for each.

The composition of the dead is as important as the total. Burial counts and survivor accounts both indicate that women and children made up a substantial share of those killed. The army's own count of bodies, as later reconstructed, included dozens of women and children among the dead. This pattern is consistent with the evidence about the artillery fire on the camp and the pursuit of fleeing people. It undermines any claim that the dead were primarily armed combatants. Even the lowest plausible estimate involves the killing of many noncombatants.

Why do the numbers matter? They matter because the question of scale is entangled with the question of responsibility and of memory. Army officers had an interest in emphasizing Lakota aggression and in downplaying civilian deaths. Lakota survivors had an interest in documenting the full extent of the loss. Later memorials and legislation, including the 1990 congressional resolution, have used figures that reflect these debates. The responsible historian therefore reports ranges, identifies their sources, and refuses to treat the lowest figure as the "official" truth or the highest as unquestionable. The people behind the numbers, who had names, families, and stories, are the proper subject of the record.

One carefully bounded figure gives readers an example of what an actual count can and cannot prove. The National Park Service's landmark documentation records 146 people buried in the mass grave during the recovery operations of January 3–4, 1891. A frequently cited breakdown of those 146 bodies lists 84 men, 44 women, and 18 children. That accounting, even if the categories are accepted exactly as recorded, establishes only the composition of those interred there; it does not count everyone who died in the field, everyone later buried elsewhere, or those who died subsequently of their wounds. The categories themselves may depend on the observers' judgments about age and sex. Students should not use the subtotal of 62 women and children as the final count of civilian deaths, or use 146 as if it closed the question of total casualties.

Section 90.13

The Wounded, the Winter, and the Immediate Humanitarian Crisis

After the shooting subsided on December 29, the army gathered its own dead and wounded and began moving the wounded Lakota survivors it had found toward Pine Ridge Agency. Army wagons carried the wounded, mostly women and children, across the winter landscape. The journey was cold, and the wounded had already spent hours exposed on the field. At the agency, the survivors were placed in the Episcopal mission chapel, which had been decorated for the Christmas season. A photograph of the survivors lying on the chapel floor beneath a Christmas banner has become one of the best-known images of the aftermath and is discussed in §90.16.

Events elsewhere complicated the situation. Following the massacre, armed Lakota men from Pine Ridge, some of them relatives of the dead, moved to confront soldiers, and fighting occurred on December 30 near the Holy Rosary (Drexel) Mission. The Seventh Cavalry was caught in a difficult position there and was assisted by units including the Ninth Cavalry, whose Black soldiers rode to its relief. These engagements are part of the aftermath but should not be confused with the massacre itself. They show the extent to which the killing had shaken the region, and they heightened the army's perception of continuing danger, which affected the pace of relief and recovery.

Weather transformed the humanitarian crisis. A blizzard moved in during the night of December 29 and continued into the following days, covering the field with snow and making it impossible for the army or anyone else to return promptly. The dead lay where they had fallen. Wounded people who had not been found remained exposed. Because the army's priority was to secure its position and its wounded, and because the region was in turmoil, there was a delay before a party returned to the field. On January 1, 1891, a group that included Dr. Charles Eastman, a Santee Dakota physician at Pine Ridge, together with others, went to Wounded Knee to search for survivors. They found the bodies of the dead, frozen, and located several people who were still alive, including a few who had survived for days. Their account is one of the principal sources for the condition of the field.

Medical personnel and others at Pine Ridge did what they could for the wounded. Eastman and the agency's doctors treated injuries in the cold and crowded chapel. Missionaries and teachers, including Elaine Goodale, who worked in education and later married Eastman, assisted. The Army surgeons and the agency physicians faced severe shortages. Records of the numbers who died in the days after arrival are incomplete, and the experiences of individual patients are known primarily through the notes and recollections of those who cared for them and, in some cases, through the later accounts of survivors.

The documentation of individual experiences at this stage is limited, and it is important to avoid generalization. Some people survived because relatives found them; others because they crawled to shelter; others because soldiers or Lakota scouts picked them up. Some of the wounded were separated from their families for weeks. The records of the Pine Ridge Agency, the memoirs of Eastman and others, and Lakota recollections offer glimpses rather than a complete picture. What they establish is that the suffering did not end with the shooting.

The fighting near the Catholic mission on White Clay Creek, often called the Drexel Mission or Holy Rosary Mission engagement, occurred on December 30, the day after the massacre. Military and archival accounts distinguish it from other clashes and alarms near Pine Ridge on December 29. Keeping the dates separate is more than a minor chronological correction: a casualty, an award, or a troop movement associated with the December 30 engagement should not automatically be entered into the record for the December 29 massacre. In a crisis where units operated over several days, combining all fighting into one 'Wounded Knee battle' blurs the specific responsibility for the killing at the creek.

Section 90.14

Recovery of the Dead, Burial, and the Destruction of Families

After the blizzard, the army sent a burial detail to Wounded Knee, which began its work in the first days of January 1891. The party included soldiers and civilians hired for the task. They found the bodies frozen in the positions in which people had died, some near the camp, some in the ravines, some far from it. A trench was dug on the rise where the Hotchkiss guns had stood, and the bodies were placed in it. The military count of roughly 146 bodies derives from this operation. The burial was conducted under army direction and was not a Lakota funeral. The dead were interred together in a single grave, without the rites that families would have performed.

Photographs taken at the site show the burial operation and the frozen bodies. They are discussed in §90.16 as evidence and as a means by which the event was represented. Soldiers and civilians also removed items from the dead, including clothing and Ghost Dance shirts. Such objects entered private collections and museums and became the subject of later efforts at repatriation, a subject examined in Chapter 125. A Ghost Dance shirt held in a Scottish museum, for example, was returned to Lakota representatives in 1999 after a long campaign by Lakota people. The treatment of the dead and their belongings was an additional harm to families who could not recover them.

Identification of victims was difficult. The army's count was a count of bodies, and the dead were not systematically identified by name at the time of burial. Families sought to learn who had died, and the knowledge accumulated through the testimony of survivors and relatives rather than through a single official record. Later efforts by descendants and researchers to compile lists of the dead have continued, and they remain incomplete. The challenge of identification means that for many victims, the historical record preserves little more than their presence in a count. Treating them only as numbers repeats the original erasure.

The consequences for families were profound. Mothers, fathers, and children were killed, and in some households entire lines were lost. Children were orphaned or separated from relatives. Wounded survivors faced long recoveries, and some died. Surviving relatives had to mourn without access to the bodies, in winter, on reservations already burdened by hunger. Kinship systems, which organized Lakota social life, absorbed orphaned children into extended families, and in some cases survivors cared for the children of dead relatives. Documented accounts show these adaptations, but they also show the grief that endured. Historians should distinguish documented individual experiences from generalizations about all survivors: families differed in what they lost, how they responded, and what they later said.

The mass grave, the unnamed dead, and the interrupted ceremonies shaped later memory. The site, on the rise above the camp, became a place that survivors and descendants returned to and sought to mark. The effort to name, commemorate, and protect the grave begins in this period and continues through the 1903 monument and the commemorations of the late twentieth century, which later sections describe.

The chronology of recovery itself shaped the historical record. The National Park Service identifies an Indigenous rescue party that reached the field on January 1 and located eleven wounded survivors. The principal burial detail assembled on January 3 and finished its work the following day. Those dates make clear that the intervening days were not an orderly military inventory carried out immediately after the shooting. Exposure to winter weather, movement of survivors, and the delay before burial all affected what could be learned about individual deaths. The mass grave became a place of mourning and later memorialization, but a burial count compiled under these circumstances cannot be an exhaustive register of the people who had been present in the camp.

Section 90.15

Military Investigations, Command Responsibility, and Official Findings

The army investigated Wounded Knee in the weeks after the massacre, and the investigations reveal the institutional interests that shaped official conclusions. Major General Nelson A. Miles, who commanded the campaign and had arrived at Pine Ridge, was dissatisfied with the conduct of the operation. He relieved Forsyth of command and ordered a court of inquiry to examine Forsyth's actions. The court, composed of army officers, reviewed testimony from officers, interpreters, and other witnesses, and it concluded that Forsyth had acted properly, without finding fault in his disposition of the troops or the conduct of the disarmament. Secretary of War Redfield Proctor endorsed this outcome, and Forsyth was restored to command. He was later promoted to brigadier general.

Miles continued to criticize. In his report and in later statements, he argued that Forsyth had not followed his instructions and had positioned troops in a way that exposed the camp, and he stressed the killing of women and children. The disagreement between Miles and Forsyth is sometimes presented as a simple contest between a critic and a defender, but it was also shaped by institutional politics: Miles's relationship with the War Department, the Army's wish to defend the Seventh Cavalry, and a public that was inclined to celebrate the campaign's end. Historians have debated Miles's motives. Some see a genuine concern about the conduct of the operation; others emphasize his own interests and reputation. The documents do not allow a definitive resolution, though they establish that senior army officers disagreed about the propriety of Forsyth's decisions.

The 1891 compilation of Miles's and Forsyth's reports printed in the Congressional Record is a significant documentary starting point for students. It reproduces the competing assessments and shows how each officer described the disarmament, the first shots, the fire on the camp, and the responsibilities of subordinate officers. The documents show that Forsyth defended his plan, emphasized Lakota concealment of weapons and resistance, and pointed to the difficulties of the situation. Miles emphasized the army's failure to separate the women and children and the dangers of the troop dispositions. Students should read both in full and note the evidence each cites.

Command responsibility at Wounded Knee extended beyond Forsyth. Brooke and Miles had formulated the orders for the capture and disarmament of Spotted Elk's community, and the senior commanders had shaped the framework in which Forsyth operated. The court of inquiry, composed of officers, examined Forsyth's field decisions without critically examining the broader policy. Its procedures, which took testimony largely from army witnesses and which did not give Lakota survivors a significant role, limited what it could find. The result was an institutional finding that protected the Seventh Cavalry. In 1891 and afterward, the army also awarded Medals of Honor to soldiers for actions at Wounded Knee, which signaled an institutional endorsement of the troops' conduct.

Two different kinds of responsibility must be analyzed separately. Command responsibility concerns the decisions to hold the group under guard, concentrate the men for disarmament, place troops on multiple sides of the camp, and employ artillery. Individual responsibility asks what identifiable soldiers did when shots began and whether they fired at noncombatants, continued firing after danger had passed, or attempted to protect the wounded. An inquiry focused narrowly on whether a colonel obeyed his superior's orders can miss the effects of the orders themselves. Likewise, the conclusion that one soldier displayed courage while under fire does not answer whether a military operation as a whole was justified. The documentary record allows historians to evaluate command choices at a broader scale than an inquiry restricted to the conduct of particular officers.

Nor should the disagreements among officers be recast as a simple struggle between the truthful commander and a dishonest subordinate. Miles had institutional reasons to distance his command from the consequences of the operation; Forsyth had equally obvious reasons to defend the orders and dispositions for which he was directly responsible. Their reports are especially useful when read in conjunction with testimony, military correspondence written before the shooting, and the evidence of the field. An early report can establish what a commander said before the political costs of the event became clear; a later defensive statement can reveal which criticisms the officer considered dangerous. Neither kind of document stands outside the institution that created it. The court of inquiry's treatment of Forsyth is an indispensable fact, but a finding about disciplinary responsibility is not a final historical verdict on the massacre.

Section 90.16

Newspapers, Photographs, and the Construction of the Official Story

The public learned about Wounded Knee through newspapers and, increasingly, photographs. Correspondents from regional and national papers had gathered in the region during the winter, and their reports shaped public understanding. Many newspapers described the event as a battle, adopting the army's vocabulary, and some emphasized the death of soldiers and the danger of the Ghost Dance. Some correspondents and others, including a few who visited the field, described the killing of women and children, and a number of papers expressed unease or criticism. The press was divided and often inconsistent, but the dominant framing of the first reports was military.

The journalism of the period included openly hostile voices. In Aberdeen, South Dakota, the editor of the Saturday Pioneer, L. Frank Baum, who would later write The Wonderful Wizard of Oz, published editorials in December 1890 and January 1891 that called for the destruction or extermination of the Lakota. Baum's writings are documented, and they show that extreme anti-Indigenous opinion circulated in print. They should not be treated as representative of all newspapers, but they illustrate a climate in which the killing could be described as a necessary conclusion to a conflict.

The description of the event as a battle had consequences. It framed the army as having fought an enemy rather than as having killed a camp of families. It supported the awarding of medals and the defense of the Seventh Cavalry. It also fit a public narrative of the 1890s, in which the frontier was said to be closing and the Indian Wars ending; the Census Bureau's remarks about the end of a frontier line appeared in 1890. Wounded Knee was thus absorbed into a story of completed conquest, in which Indigenous resistance had ended. The next section explains why that framing was persuasive, and the rest of the chapter shows why it was incomplete.

Photographs became important evidence. Commercial photographers working from towns near the reservation traveled to the site and recorded the field, the frozen dead, and the burial. Their images were sold and circulated widely. Photographs of Spotted Elk's frozen body, of the burial trench, and of the wounded in the chapel carried a visual force that written reports lacked. They document consequences: that people were dead, in numbers, in winter. But photographs have limits. They do not show the sequence of events or who fired first. Captions, many supplied by photographers or publishers, were sometimes inaccurate or sensational. Photographs were composed and sometimes staged, and their circulation was shaped by commercial motives. Students should therefore ask who took an image, when, for whom, and what the caption claims.

The Library of Congress holds several photographs from the period, including images of the burial of the dead at Wounded Knee, which students can examine with their catalog records. The records identify photographers and dates where known; when attribution is uncertain, the records say so. Used with attention to these limits, the photographs complement the testimony and reports, giving visual confirmation of the aftermath while leaving the question of sequence to other evidence.

Section 90.17

Battle or Massacre? Evidence, Terminology, and Historical Responsibility

Nineteenth-century military and press accounts called the event the Battle of Wounded Knee, and the name persisted in official usage, on some maps, and in the language of military recognition. Lakota survivors and descendants, and the great majority of modern historians, call it a massacre. The difference is not a quarrel over style. It reflects different judgments about what the evidence shows, and it carries consequences for how the event is taught, commemorated, and judged.

Historical Vocabulary
Massacre

Definition: In historical usage, the indiscriminate or disproportionate killing of people who are largely defenseless or who are not engaged in combat, usually by a force with overwhelming power. The word describes the character of the violence, not the identity of the victims or the presence of any armed resistance.

Historical context: Nineteenth-century Americans used "massacre" most often for attacks by Indigenous people on settlers, and "battle" for army operations. The terminology reflected a political perspective as well as description. Chapters 73, 74, and 70 discuss other events, including the Dakota War, Sand Creek, and California killings, that have been similarly contested.

Why it matters: The term shapes whether the killing is understood as warfare or as an atrocity. It bears on responsibility, remembrance, and, in the case of Wounded Knee, on the continuing dispute over Medals of Honor.

Potential misunderstanding: Calling an event a massacre does not mean that no one on the targeted side was armed or resisted. It means that the evidence of killing noncombatants and of disproportionate force defines the event.

The evidence that supports the term is extensive. Soldiers disarmed and surrounded a community that had come peacefully under escort. They fired at close range into men gathered at the council, then fired into the camp where women and children stood, and army artillery struck the tipis. Soldiers pursued fleeing people into the ravines. Burial counts and survivor accounts show that women and children made up a substantial share of the dead. Wounded survivors included many women and children. The weight of this evidence, from both army and Lakota sources, supports the conclusion that a massacre occurred. The army's own officers disputed the propriety of the operation, as Miles's criticism shows.

Genuine disputes remain about specific matters. Historians disagree about the first shot, the number of Lakota men who resisted with weapons, whether particular soldiers deliberately fired on noncombatants, how many army casualties resulted from friendly fire, and the degree to which individual officers shared responsibility. These disputes concern details. They do not equal the broad evidence for the character of the event, and presenting them as though they do would amount to false balance. A careful account identifies unresolved questions and states what is established. It uses the terms "massacre" and, where relevant, "army's account of a battle," and explains why.

Responsibility must be assigned with precision. Responsibility attaches to commanders who planned and directed the operation, to officers who ordered or permitted fire on noncombatants, and to individual soldiers whose actions were documented. It does not attach to every soldier present in the same way, and it does not extend to the American public as a collective. Nor does it attach to the Lakota victims. The Army's own decisions, as the preceding sections have shown, created the conditions in which the killing took place.

Historical Debate

Accident, Escalation, or Design? How Did the Violence Begin and Who Bore Responsibility?

The question: Historians agree that a massacre occurred but have debated how it began. Was it the accidental result of a struggle over one rifle in a tense situation? Was it the foreseeable result of a flawed plan of disarmament? Or did some commanders and soldiers intend violence, perhaps influenced by the Seventh Cavalry's history at the Little Bighorn?

Interpretation A — A tragic accident or misunderstanding: Some earlier military accounts and popular histories emphasized a chance discharge and confusion, with the violence escalating faster than officers could control. This view stresses contingency and the breakdown of communication.
Interpretation B — A foreseeable result of command decisions: Many modern historians, including Jerome A. Greene, emphasize that the plan to disarm a captive community with troops positioned in close proximity and artillery overlooking the camp created a high risk of violence. On this view, officers' decisions, not simply chance, explain the scale of the killing.
Interpretation C — Intent or revenge: Some writers, and many Lakota accounts, have argued that soldiers acted at least in part from vengeance or hostility, pointing to the regiment's connection to Custer. Other scholars find that evidence of premeditated revenge is not conclusive and caution that it cannot be demonstrated from the surviving record.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on army reports, the court of inquiry, Miles's criticism, Lakota testimony, interpreters' statements, maps of troop positions, and the pattern of casualties. None of the sources provides direct proof of a decision to kill noncombatants.

Points of disagreement: The debate concerns causation and intention, the weight of different witnesses, and how to interpret evidence of soldiers' mindset. It does not concern whether noncombatants were killed.

Why it matters: If the violence was an accident, responsibility appears diffuse. If it followed from command decisions, accountability attaches to identifiable officers and to the system that authorized the plan. If it was intended, the event takes on a different moral and legal character. This chapter's argument rests on Interpretation B: decisions within a system of coercive control, without requiring proof of premeditation.

Student task: Identify the evidence that best supports each interpretation and explain what additional evidence would be needed to demonstrate intent. Consider why the persistence of Interpretation A served the army's institutional interests.

Cause and Effect

Why the "Battle" Narrative Persisted

Long-term causes

A long tradition of describing army operations against Indigenous people as battles and Indigenous attacks as massacres; the habit of relying on military sources.

Short-term causes

The army's reports and court of inquiry; the need to defend the Seventh Cavalry; the press's dependence on military sources; public wish to see the "Indian wars" concluded.

Immediate context

Awards of medals in 1891 and Forsyth's restoration to command reinforced the official account.

Effect

The "battle" label shaped textbooks, maps, and public memory for generations, while Lakota testimony was marginalized.

Historical complexity: The narrative persisted not simply because of one institution's choices but because several institutions, the army, the press, and national narratives of frontier closure, reinforced one another. It was challenged from the beginning by Lakota survivors and some officers.

The vocabulary of 'battle' carried practical consequences. It helped organize army reports around opposing sides, acts of gallantry, casualties suffered by soldiers, and presumed hostile intentions of the other party. Those categories could accommodate the fact that some Lakota men returned fire, but they could also make the killing of women and children appear incidental to an ordinary military engagement. 'Massacre' is not a claim that no soldier was attacked or that no Lakota man resisted. It is a judgment about the larger action and its victims: armed troops had surrounded a community that included many noncombatants, and soldiers' fire killed large numbers of those people. An accurate historian can accept testimony about isolated armed resistance and still identify the event as a massacre, without treating the two conclusions as contradictory.

The terminology also changed over time and across institutions. Military records, monuments, newspapers, congressional debate, and Lakota family testimony did not adopt one neutral label. A student looking only at a newspaper headline from 1891 may find a 'battle'; a later National Historic Landmark document may record both terms while describing the event's contested meaning; contemporary descendants may emphasize the sacred site and the people buried there. The words are evidence about the authors as well as labels for the event. To judge between them, students should return to what happened at the disarmament circle, what happened in the camp, where civilians were killed, and what happened to people who tried to escape. Historical analysis must make its terms accountable to the record.

Section 90.18

Lakota Survivors, Testimony, Compensation Claims, and the 1903 Memorial

Survivors and descendants did not accept the official account, and their efforts to counter it began immediately. The statements of February 1891 placed Lakota perspectives in the official record. Survivors continued to speak, to petition, and to organize. David W. Grua's Surviving Wounded Knee: The Lakotas and the Politics of Memory traces this long effort in detail, showing how survivors, descendants, and their allies used testimony, petitions, and public ceremony to contest the army's narrative and to seek recognition. The title's emphasis on survival is deliberate: the Lakota who endured the massacre did not disappear from history.

Compensation claims were a central form of action. Survivors and relatives sought payment for losses of life, injury, and property, directing petitions to federal officials and, in later decades, to Congress. They gathered documentation of the victims and of the losses suffered. Congress did not provide compensation for the massacre, and the claims, which continued in various forms into the twentieth century, met repeated rejection. The Horn Cloud family and Dewey Beard were prominent in this work, and in the twentieth century survivors organized for claims and recognition; a Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian account dates the founding of a Wounded Knee survivors’ association to 1901, although the later organizational history and naming require care. The failure to secure compensation does not mean the effort was ineffective: it created records, kept the memory active, and established a tradition of petition that later generations drew on.

In 1903, survivors and relatives raised a monument at the mass grave. The monument was erected by Lakota people, including members of the Horn Cloud family, and its inscription, according to descriptions of the monument, memorialized Spotted Elk and the people killed, and stated that many innocent women and children died there. Students should consult the monument's inscription directly through documented photographs and National Park Service records for the exact wording. The monument is significant because it was a Lakota-created statement of historical judgment, placed at the site and expressed in terms that contradicted the army's account. It remains at the mass grave today.

Students should consult the National Park Service's published documentation for the interview records and the monument's text.

Paraphrase of content (not a quotation): The monument memorializes Spotted Elk and the dead and affirms that innocent women and children were killed. The 1990 interviews present descendants' recollections of family stories, the passage of memory across generations, and the continuing significance of the site.

Analysis: The monument shows survivors publicly asserting their interpretation of the event within thirteen years. The interviews show how memory is transmitted within families and how descendants understand its meaning.

Limitations: The 1990 interviews are later oral histories. They are not firsthand 1890 eyewitness testimony, and they should be read as accounts of what descendants learned and believe, shaped by the passage of a century and by the setting of the interview. Questions for students:

  1. What does the monument record or assert?
  2. Who created it and under what circumstances?
  3. What purposes might have shaped its form and wording?
  4. Why must descendant interviews be distinguished from eyewitness testimony?
  5. What can the interviews teach historians about memory that other sources cannot?

Joseph Horn Cloud and his relatives were not simply commemorated by outsiders after the fact. The record of their interviews, the public memorial, and repeated demands for recognition show survivors working to determine how the event would be remembered. History Nebraska identifies an interview with Joseph in Ricker's notebooks and a photograph taken in 1907 of Joseph with his brothers White Lance and Dewey Beard. Such materials are not a complete family archive, and Ricker selected what to record; they nonetheless document a history of speaking, remembering, and transmitting evidence in the decades after 1890. To treat the survivors only as passive recipients of later sympathy would repeat the erasure that the memorial and interviews contested.

The National Park Service records a dedication on May 28, 1903, at the mass grave, with Joseph Horn Cloud and Fire Lighting among those who spoke. The dated dedication is stronger evidence than an undifferentiated reference to a monument 'built around the turn of the century,' although publications differ about the monument's completion or dedication date. A Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian account places the founding of a Wounded Knee survivors' association in 1901. That report should be used with the qualification that organizational naming and membership evolved; the earlier survivors' campaign should not be conflated automatically with every later group using a similar title. Together these records document practical organization, not merely retrospective sentiment: identifying the dead, maintaining the grave, demanding recognition, and resisting narratives that justified the killing.

Section 90.19

Wounded Knee in Indigenous and American Historical Memory

Wounded Knee acquired different meanings for different audiences. For Lakota families it is a site of mourning and a record of loss within a continuing history. For many Indigenous people across North America it became a symbol of conquest and of the survival of Indigenous nations. For many Americans in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries it marked the end of the frontier. In the later twentieth century it became, for a growing number of scholars and citizens, evidence of the violence of U.S. expansion. These meanings overlap and conflict, and the history of the memory is itself part of the history of the event.

Lakota memory has been preserved in ceremony, family narrative, and historical writing. Descendants have maintained stories of the dead and the survivors, and communities have held ceremonies at the site and elsewhere. Historical writing has expanded the record. Dee Brown's Bury My Heart at Wounded Knee, published in 1970, brought the massacre to a very wide readership, though its treatment of the event relied on the sources then available and later scholarship has refined and in places corrected it. Jerome A. Greene's American Carnage, published in 2014, draws on a broad range of military and Lakota sources, and Grua's study examines how memory has been shaped and contested.

In 1973 the memory of 1890 was invoked by participants in the occupation of Wounded Knee, a seventy-one-day standoff on the Pine Ridge Reservation involving the American Indian Movement and Oglala Lakota residents, examined in Chapter 112. The participants chose the site deliberately for its symbolic power and framed their action in relation to the massacre and to treaty rights. The occupation is a distinct historical event, with its own causes, conflicts, and consequences, and it should not be read simply as a repetition of 1890. But the participants' deliberate use of the earlier event shows how the memory of the massacre had become a language of Indigenous political claims. Chapters 110 and 111 place the occupation within the larger movement.

The centennial period brought new commemorative practices. The Big Foot Memorial Ride, which began in 1986, retraces the route of Spotted Elk's community each December, ending at Wounded Knee on the anniversary; organizers describe a concluding ceremony of mourning and healing often called the wiping of the tears. Riders include descendants and young people, and the ride has become a form of transmission across generations. The site, a National Historic Landmark on the Pine Ridge Reservation, has been the subject of discussion about how to protect, interpret, and steward it, with Lakota communities, the Oglala Sioux Tribe, and federal agencies among the parties concerned. Museums holding materials taken from the dead and from the site have also been drawn into repatriation efforts (Chapter 125), and Chapter 132 examines the broader struggle over how colonial violence is remembered.

Students should recognize that memory is not a single thing. Lakota memory is diverse, with different families preserving different accounts and emphasizing different aspects. American memory has moved from celebration of a victory toward, in many quarters, acknowledgment of a massacre, though the change is incomplete and contested, as the dispute over the medals shows. The historian's task is to study both the event and the memory, to distinguish them, and to note where each has shaped the other.

Section 90.20

The Legacy of Wounded Knee: Justice, Recognition, and Indigenous Continuity

In October 1990, in the centennial year, the United States Congress passed a concurrent resolution that expressed deep regret for the massacre and acknowledged the wrong done to Lakota people. It was a significant statement, the result of long advocacy by Lakota leaders and their supporters. It did not provide compensation, did not revoke the medals awarded to soldiers, and, as a concurrent resolution, did not carry the force of law.

Historical Vocabulary
Concurrent Resolution

Definition: A form of legislative measure passed by both houses of the U.S. Congress that expresses the sentiment of Congress and does not require the president's signature or have the force of law.

Historical context: The 1990 resolution on Wounded Knee was of this type. It expressed regret and recognized the wrong but created no legal obligation to compensate or to revoke honors.

Why it matters: The form shows what the resolution could and could not do. It was an official expression of regret, which is different from a legal remedy or a binding apology.

Potential misunderstanding: The resolution is sometimes described as an apology that settled the matter. It did not provide compensation or change the status of the medals.

The question of the Medals of Honor has kept Wounded Knee in national debate. Historical accounts sometimes cite twenty awards, but the 2024–2026 Defense Department review considered nineteen Wounded Knee-specific medals. Its panel excluded two names initially listed because their honors concerned White Clay Creek on December 30, 1890, and added another recipient identified by the Army. The distinction between the December 29 massacre and the separate December 30 engagement explains why the counts differ; the review figure should not be silently generalized to every historical award list. Lakota leaders, descendants, and the National Congress of American Indians have called for revocation, and bills known as the Remove the Stain Act have been introduced in Congress without passing. In July 2024 Secretary of Defense Lloyd Austin ordered a review of the awards, and a panel, which included Indigenous members, examined the record. On September 25, 2025, Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth announced in a video that the soldiers would keep the medals, describing the event as a battle and calling the decision final. A report on the panel's work, obtained by a South Dakota news outlet in May 2026, indicated that three military members of the panel found no evidence of disqualifying conduct by the honorees, while two civilian panel members rejected that assessment. The three-to-two split reflected differing judgments about individual action and the context of the massacre; it was not a unanimous historical finding.

In mid-2026 a Senate Armed Services Committee report accompanying the defense authorization bill for fiscal year 2027 directed the Secretary of Defense to provide the full review report, underlying records, and a briefing by February 1, 2027, and descendants said they would continue to press for revocation. The issue also entered a South Dakota Senate campaign in the autumn. As of November 2026, the medals remain in place and the question remains unresolved. Students should distinguish the contemporary decision, which concerns military recognition and the standards for revoking a medal, from the historical assessment of the massacre, which rests on the evidence reviewed in this chapter. A decision about an award does not settle what happened, and a historical judgment does not by itself determine how a government honors or revokes an honor.

Multiple Perspectives

The Medals of Honor: Differing Positions, 2024–2026

Note: the viewpoints below are analytical summaries of positions reported in public coverage of the dispute. They are not quotations, and they do not imply equal evidence for each position.

From the perspective of descendants and Lakota advocates: The medals honor participation in the killing of noncombatants and should be revoked. They point to the scale of civilian deaths and to scholarly work on the massacre, and they continue to press Congress and the Defense Department.
From the perspective of the Defense Department's decision: The review, as characterized by the department and by the Secretary, found no basis to rescind the awards, and the decision was announced as final. Reports on the panel's work indicate that three military panelists concluded there was no evidence of disqualifying conduct by individual recipients.
From the perspective of members of Congress: Positions vary. Some have introduced legislation to rescind the medals, while others have sought more information. A Senate committee report in 2026 called for the full report and unredacted materials, and candidates in a South Dakota Senate race took differing positions.
From the perspective of historians: Scholars of Wounded Knee, including Greene, have documented the conduct of soldiers and the killing of noncombatants. Some have argued that individual awards require review of the specific conduct of each recipient, which differs from either blanket revocation or blanket retention.

Wounded Knee did not extinguish Lakota sovereignty, and it did not end Indigenous political and cultural resistance. Lakota nations, including the Oglala Sioux Tribe, the Cheyenne River Sioux Tribe, and the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe, endure as political communities with governments, languages, ceremonies, and claims. Their efforts include the long legal struggle over the Black Hills, in which the Supreme Court in 1980 held that the taking had violated the Constitution's requirement of just compensation; the Lakota nations have refused payment, insisting on land. Later chapters, including Chapters 112, 118, and 119, return to this history. The narrative of Wounded Knee as the end of resistance served those who wished to see conquest as complete, but it was contradicted by the survivors, descendants, and communities who persisted.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Lakota Nationhood from 1890 to 2026

What Changed

  • The land base, reduced by 1889 legislation and later allotment, and the loss of the Black Hills
  • Political institutions, from band councils and agency-era arrangements to constitutional tribal governments after the Indian Reorganization Act (Chapter 97)
  • Public recognition, from the army's "battle" to the 1990 expression of regret and the continuing dispute over medals
  • Forms of resistance and advocacy, from petitions and testimony to the 1973 occupation, legal claims, and memorial rides

What Stayed the Same

  • Kinship ties and the transmission of memory in families
  • The claim to treaty rights, land, and self-government
  • Ceremonial and linguistic life, preserved despite suppression (see Chapters 88 and 123)
  • The practice of testifying, petitioning, and commemorating in order to correct the official record

Why the pattern matters: The pattern shows a nation that was devastated but not ended. The change was uneven and contested, and it was felt most directly by survivors and their families; the continuity was carried by those same families and communities. Together they explain why Wounded Knee belongs to Part XV's history of assimilation pressure and to Part XVI's history of Indigenous modernity (Chapters 91 through 96), in which Lakota and other Indigenous people appear as political actors in a changing world.

A separate and consequential development concerned control of the ground itself. In 2022 the Oglala Sioux Tribe and the Cheyenne River Sioux Tribe jointly acquired approximately forty acres encompassing part of the Wounded Knee Massacre site and agreed to a covenant governing its preservation. On December 19, 2025, the Wounded Knee Massacre Memorial and Sacred Site Act became Public Law 119-61. It directed the Secretary of the Interior to complete the steps necessary for those jointly owned acres to be held in restricted fee status, with limits on transfer, protection from state and local taxation, and recognition that the land remained within the Pine Ridge Reservation under the Oglala Sioux Tribe's specified jurisdiction. The statute also tied permitted uses to the tribes' 2022 covenant and forbade gaming. The act set a 365-day deadline for federal implementation; enactment should not be confused with proof that every administrative step had already been completed by November 2026.

This measure illuminates a distinction that nineteenth-century military histories rarely consider: who may decide how a site of violence is protected, visited, and interpreted. For descendants, stewardship is not exhausted by placing a sign beside a mass grave. Access, ceremonial use, the treatment of burials, development restrictions, and the authority to speak about the site all shape commemoration. The statute deals with a specific parcel, not with the entire National Historic Landmark, the Black Hills treaty dispute, or every question of compensation. Its importance lies in recognizing a continuing tribal role in controlling land associated with the massacre. The disagreement over military medals and the legal protection of memorial land were thus different political processes unfolding in the same period—one about federal honors, the other about the status and stewardship of a sacred place.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Wounded Knee Massacre of December 29, 1890, was not the inevitable consequence of Indigenous religious revitalization. It resulted from a series of federal, administrative, and military decisions made within a system of territorial conquest and coercive control. The Ghost Dance, examined in Chapter 89, formed part of the background, but the operative causes of the massacre lay in the conditions of the Lakota reservations and in the decisions of army commanders.

Those conditions included the breakup of the Great Sioux Reservation in 1889, the reduction of rations, drought and illness, and administrative control over daily life. Lakota leaders responded in different ways, and the Army, alarmed by the dancing, concentrated thousands of troops in the region. The killing of Sitting Bull on December 15 demonstrated the danger of armed arrests and caused Hunkpapa people to seek refuge among relatives and with leaders such as Spotted Elk.

Spotted Elk was a Miniconjou headman with a reputation as a negotiator, and his community of roughly 350 people, most of them women and children, left the Cheyenne River in late December and traveled toward Pine Ridge. His illness, the party's direction of travel, and its peaceful submission to Whitside's battalion on December 28 are more consistent with the Lakota explanation of a search for safety than with the army's suspicion of hostile intent. The decision to escort the community to Wounded Knee Creek, surround it, and position Hotchkiss guns overlooking the camp was a command decision.

On the morning of December 29, Colonel Forsyth's plan to disarm the Lakota men placed armed soldiers in close proximity to a captive community. The operation involved separating the men, searching the tipis, and relying on interpreters under tension. Many of the choices that made the operation dangerous, including the timing, the troop dispositions, and the decision to proceed under the guns, were discretionary. The Lakota accounts, army reports, and later testimony differ about the first gunshot, and a struggle over a rifle, often associated with Black Coyote, is central to many accounts. This chapter has insisted that this uncertainty be reported as uncertainty and that it not be allowed to obscure what followed.

What followed was the killing of a large number of Lakota people, among them many women, children, and elders, by rifle and artillery fire in the council area, in the camp, and in the ravines. Some Lakota men resisted with the weapons they had, and soldiers were killed and wounded, some probably by fellow soldiers' fire. The estimate of Lakota dead ranges from about 150 to 300 or more, and the counts of the dead, the wounded, and the later deaths from wounds must be distinguished. Survivors fled, hid, and were separated from their families, and the documented experience of figures such as Dewey Beard and the Horn Cloud family reveals what the numbers conceal.

The aftermath compounded the harm. A blizzard delayed recovery of the dead and the wounded, and the burial in a mass grave, conducted by the army and hired workers, denied families the rites they would have performed. Investigations were limited. Miles criticized Forsyth, but a court of inquiry found in his favor, and the army's institutional interests, together with a press that largely adopted the vocabulary of battle, produced an official narrative that obscured the killing of noncombatants. Photographs documented consequences but could not by themselves establish the sequence of events.

The evidence supports the designation of the event as a massacre. Genuine disputes remain about the first shot, the numbers of armed men, friendly fire, and the intentions of individual officers, and historians have debated whether the violence was accident, escalation, or design. The disputes concern details and do not equal the broad evidence for the killing of noncombatants. Responsibility belongs with the commanders and soldiers whose decisions and actions are documented, not with the Lakota victims and not with a collective American public.

Survivors and descendants refused to accept the official account. They gave testimony in 1891, petitioned for compensation, organized, and in 1903 raised a monument at the mass grave. They preserved memory in families and ceremony, and in the later twentieth century the Big Foot Memorial Ride and the occupation of 1973 gave the memory new forms. In 1990 Congress expressed deep regret, a concurrent resolution that provided no compensation and left the medals in place.

The medals remain a point of contention. A 2024 Defense Department review, the September 2025 announcement that the awards would be retained, the 2026 report of the Senate Armed Services Committee seeking the full review, and continued advocacy by descendants show that the question was unresolved as of November 2026. The contemporary dispute concerns military recognition and should be distinguished from the historical judgment about what happened.

Wounded Knee did not end Lakota sovereignty or Indigenous resistance. The Lakota nations survived and reorganized, and the history of survivors and descendants working to preserve historical truth and pursue recognition and justice is part of the event's meaning. This understanding prepares the transition to Part XVI, in which Indigenous economies, intellectuals, and political leaders appear in a modern world shaped by, but not defined by, conquest.

Building a Source Dossier on Wounded Knee

This project asks you to assemble and analyze a dossier of primary and secondary sources on one aspect of Wounded Knee, using library and archival resources to compare perspectives and evaluate evidence.

  3. Evaluate each source. For each, identify the creator, date, circumstances, audience, purpose, and degree of firsthand knowledge, and note the limits of its evidence.

Chapter Reflection

Wounded Knee matters because it shows how a system of conquest produced violence through decisions that could have been made differently. The people who were killed had come peacefully under escort, and the plan that surrounded them and attempted to disarm them was a choice. A history that begins with the Ghost Dance and ends with a battle misses this. A history that begins with a camp of families and the commanders who decided their fate comes closer to the evidence.

The chapter also reveals how historical evidence is made. Army reports, interpreters' translations, Lakota statements, photographs, newspapers, and monuments each represent a perspective and a purpose. The uncertainty about the first shot is real and must be stated honestly, but the established evidence of killing noncombatants is equally real, and an honest history holds both without letting either dissolve the other.

Wounded Knee shaped later developments. It informed the assimilation policies of Part XV, in which boarding schools, allotment, and administrative control continued to press on Lakota and other Indigenous communities. It became a language of political claims in 1973 and in the movements that Volume X traces. It continues to shape disputes over honors, memorials, and the stewardship of sacred sites, including the 2025 federal legislation governing a jointly held Oglala–Cheyenne River memorial parcel. Its legacy lies partly in the killing and partly in the generations of survivors and descendants who insisted on the truth.

For the larger textbook, the chapter closes a section of conquest and opens a section about Indigenous modernity. The Lakota nations did not disappear. They continued to govern, remember, and organize, as did other Indigenous nations across the continent. That continuity, not the end of resistance, is the story to which the next chapters turn, beginning with Indigenous economies after conquest in Chapter 91.
