Indigenous Peoples and the Criminal Justice System

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

Who may define a crime, who may investigate it, who may judge it, and who may impose a penalty? Every society answers those questions, and the answers reveal where political authority lies. For Indigenous nations, the transfer of those answers to outside governments was one of the central events of colonization, and the effort to recover them is one of the central themes of the present.

In 1881, on the Rosebud Reservation in present-day South Dakota, the Sičháŋǧu Lakota leader Spotted Tail (Sinte Gleska) was shot and killed by another Lakota man, Crow Dog (Kangi Sunka). According to the account preserved in the case record and later scholarship, Lakota leaders and relatives moved to settle the matter through their own means, which emphasized reconciliation between families and the payment of compensation, rather than through execution. A territorial court of the United States nevertheless tried Crow Dog and sentenced him to death. In 1883 the Supreme Court held in Ex parte Crow Dog that federal courts had no jurisdiction over a killing of one Native person by another within Indian country. Congress answered in 1885 with the Major Crimes Act, which extended federal criminal jurisdiction over a list of serious offenses. The episode contains the chapter's governing problem in miniature: a dispute about one killing became a dispute about whose law applied on Indigenous land.

Criminal justice is easily imagined as a technical administrative field, a matter of statutes, police patrols, courtrooms, and prisons. This chapter treats it as a field of political power. The authority to make criminal law, to arrest, to prosecute, and to punish is among the most visible attributes of government, and in Indigenous territories that authority was repeatedly reassigned by colonial, federal, provincial, and state governments. At the same time, many Indigenous nations had long maintained their own methods of preventing harm, resolving disputes, compensating victims, and sanctioning wrongdoing. Those methods varied enormously and were not uniformly gentle; some included coercive enforcement, banishment, and severe penalties. What they shared was that they belonged to Indigenous communities and were accountable to them.

The chapter concentrates on the United States and Canada, where distinct legal frameworks produced different relationships among Indigenous nations, police, courts, and prisons, and it gives Mexico a substantial comparative section in Division V. In the United States, criminal jurisdiction in Indian country is divided among tribal, federal, and state governments according to a body of statutes and judicial decisions that depend on the location of the offense, the identity of the accused and the victim, the type of offense, and the state in which the land lies. In Canada, criminal law is a federal responsibility administered largely by provinces and territories, so the history unfolds through the Indian Act, national and provincial police services, sentencing law under the Criminal Code, and federal and provincial corrections. In Mexico, a constitutional framework recognizes Indigenous normative systems within limits set by the Constitution and has been revised several times, most recently in 2024.

The chapter argues that criminal justice became an instrument of colonial authority, and that Indigenous peoples have challenged discriminatory institutions while rebuilding their own systems of law, public safety, and accountability. It also insists on two points that are sometimes treated as opposed. Indigenous sovereignty and effective public safety are not competing goals; Indigenous governments and families have often sought stronger protection for victims and more authority to provide it. And criticism of discriminatory policing and imprisonment does not diminish the rights of Indigenous victims to protection and investigation, just as concern about violence does not remove the requirement of fair trials and lawful sentencing. Victims and defendants both remain visible throughout.

The evidence for this history is varied. It includes statutes and treaties; appellate decisions that fix the boundaries of jurisdiction; commission and inquiry reports, such as those on the prosecution of Donald Marshall Jr. and the killing of Dudley George; government statistics from agencies such as the Bureau of Justice Statistics, Statistics Canada, and the Correctional Service of Canada; oversight reports; Indigenous community records and legal teachings; and the testimony of survivors and families. Each has limitations. Statistics depend on definitions and on who is counted; court decisions answer only the question presented; inquiry reports record findings that governments may or may not implement; and policy announcements are not evidence of results. The chapter therefore asks of every development both what a law or program promised and what independent evidence shows about its operation.

The chapter also has boundaries. Chapter 129 examined missing and murdered Indigenous women, girls, and Two-Spirit people, including the data, investigations, and national action plans; this chapter does not repeat that material but explains the wider policing and jurisdictional structures within which those responses occur. Chapter 118 treats federal Indian law and tribal sovereignty broadly, and this chapter concentrates on criminal jurisdiction. Chapters 127 and 128 address residential and boarding schools, Chapter 131 addresses child welfare, and Chapter 125 addresses repatriation; each is referred to only where it bears directly on criminal justice.

Students should read the chapter as a progression. Division I establishes Indigenous legal traditions and the colonial expansion of criminal law and policing. Division II follows the changing allocation of criminal authority in the United States. Division III turns to Canadian policing, sentencing, and wrongful convictions. Division IV examines incarceration and the alternatives communities have built. Division V compares Mexico, Canada, and the United States and asks whether modern reforms modify colonial arrangements or enable durable Indigenous control over legal institutions.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why criminal justice functioned as an instrument of colonial governance and how it shifted authority from Indigenous governments to outside states.
  2. Describe the diversity of Indigenous legal traditions and distinguish them from contemporary restorative justice programs, avoiding the assumption that all were nonpunitive.
  3. Trace the development of agency police, Courts of Indian Offenses, the North-West Mounted Police, and later Indigenous-controlled police services.
  4. Apply the variables that determine criminal jurisdiction in United States Indian country: location, status of defendant and victim, type of offense, and applicable statute.
  5. Analyze how the Major Crimes Act, Public Law 280, the Indian Civil Rights Act, Oliphant, Duro, the Tribal Law and Order Act, and VAWA changed tribal criminal authority.
  6. Distinguish McGirt v. Oklahoma from Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta and explain what each decided.
  7. Evaluate the findings of the Marshall Commission and the Ipperwash Inquiry and the difference between exposing injustice and preventing its recurrence.
  8. Explain section 718.2(e) of the Criminal Code, Gladue, and Ipeelee, including why Gladue principles are not an automatic sentencing discount.
  9. Interpret correctional statistics responsibly by identifying the population, year, jurisdiction, and measure involved.
  10. Compare Indigenous community justice initiatives, healing lodges, and tribal courts in terms of governing authority and victim and defendant protections.
  11. Assess Mexico's Article 2 framework on its own constitutional terms, rather than as an equivalent of U.S. or Canadian arrangements.
  12. Distinguish reform of justice systems administered over Indigenous peoples from the restoration of Indigenous authority to govern justice.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why is authority over criminal law among the most consequential powers transferred in colonization?
  2. How did Indigenous legal orders differ from one another, and why is "restorative justice" an incomplete description of them?
  3. How did police institutions serve both colonial administration and public safety, and why does the distinction between outside-controlled and Indigenous-controlled policing matter?
  4. How do the identity of the accused, the identity of the victim, the type of offense, and the location of the crime together determine jurisdiction in United States Indian country?
  5. Why did Congress, rather than the Supreme Court alone, repeatedly reshape tribal criminal authority, and what did each intervention restore or restrict?
  6. What can public inquiries establish about police and prosecutorial misconduct, and what can they not guarantee?
  7. What problems arise when statistics on Indigenous incarceration are compared across jurisdictions with different definitions?
  8. Under what conditions do alternative justice programs reflect genuine Indigenous authority rather than culturally adapted state programs?
  9. What would it mean for a modern state to share criminal justice authority with Indigenous governments while protecting individual rights and victims?

Historical Context

Before sustained colonization, Indigenous nations across the continent regulated conduct through institutions suited to their size, economy, and values: clan and family obligations, councils, ceremonial and police societies, mediators, elders, and, in larger polities, formal officeholders. Treaties and diplomatic practice sometimes recognized this authority. Among the Haudenosaunee, condolence and the Great Law shaped responses to killing (Chapter 26); the Cherokee Nation, in the early nineteenth century, replaced clan revenge with national law and a mounted police force (Chapter 64); and treaties with the United States and Britain generally presupposed that Indigenous governments would continue to manage internal order.

That presupposition eroded over the nineteenth century as settler states expanded. In the United States, military commissions, territorial courts, and later federal statutes asserted jurisdiction over Indigenous people. In California, the 1850 state statute governing Indians and rules barring Indigenous testimony against white persons shaped a legal order in which Indigenous people were more easily punished than protected (Chapter 70). In Minnesota, a military commission condemned more than 300 Dakota men after the 1862 war, and thirty-eight were hanged at Mankato (Chapter 73). In Canada, after the 1885 North-West Resistance, eight Indigenous men were hanged at Battleford and Cree leaders Poundmaker and Big Bear were convicted of treason-felony (Chapter 83). These were exceptional episodes, but ordinary administration mattered as much: reservation and reserve systems (Chapters 84–86) placed agents, farm instructors, police, and, in Canada, Indian agents acting as justices of the peace between Indigenous communities and the outside world.

Policing and punishment also enforced assimilation. Rules against ceremonies, dances, gatherings, and movement without permission criminalized practices central to religious and political life, while boarding and residential schools (Chapters 87–88, 127–128) were supported by compulsory attendance and the authority of police to return students. Where these topics bear on criminal justice, they are discussed in this chapter; where they concern the schools themselves, they belong to the chapters named.

By the twentieth century, a distinct pattern had emerged. Indigenous people were often subject to more police attention and harsher consequences than other residents, while Indigenous communities were frequently underprotected: federal prosecutors declined cases, police response in remote places was slow, and local authorities lacked resources. Later chapters on termination (104), self-determination (114), and tribal sovereignty (118) explain the political background. The present chapter takes those structures as its subject and follows them to 2026.

Indigenous Legal Traditions and the Expansion of Colonial Criminal Justice

How Indigenous systems of law and accountability encountered colonial courts, police forces, and criminal legislation.

Section 130.1

Indigenous Legal Traditions, Community Authority, and the Meaning of Justice

Indigenous North America contained hundreds of legal orders, each grounded in a people's language, kinship system, environment, spiritual teachings, and political structure. Scholars of Indigenous law emphasize that these were not merely "customs" but systems of obligation with recognized sources, procedures, and authorities. Law might be taught through stories and ceremonies, recorded in wampum or other mnemonic forms, applied by clan mothers, councils, elders, hereditary leaders, or specialized societies, and enforced through relationships of kin, reputation, and reciprocal duty. The evidence for these systems comes from oral tradition and community knowledge, treaty records, missionary and agent reports, ethnographic accounts, and the legal practice of Indigenous governments today. Because outsiders wrote many early descriptions, historians must read them critically and give weight to Indigenous knowledge keepers.

Across this diversity, certain patterns recur. Many societies treated harm as an injury to relationships among families or clans, not only to an individual, and so emphasized restitution, compensation, and the restoration of balance. Responsibility was often collective in the sense that a lineage or clan might be answerable for a member's act and entitled to redress for a member's injury. Among the Haudenosaunee, the practice of condolence addressed the grief of the bereaved and the repair of relations after a killing. The Lakota killing in 1881 described in the chapter opening was addressed through a process of reconciliation and gifts. In many Northwest Coast societies, wealth transfers publicly acknowledged and settled wrongs. Inuit communities relied heavily on consensus, family authority, and the moral teaching of elders, and for serious or repeated harm could resort to ridicule, ostracism, or, in extreme circumstances, lethal community decisions, as described in ethnographic sources and Inuit testimony.

It would be a serious error to conclude that Indigenous law was uniformly gentle. Plains societies maintained military or police societies, such as the akíčhita among the Lakota and warrior societies among the Cheyenne, that enforced rules during communal bison hunts and on the march; penalties included destruction of property, public punishment, and confiscation. Clan revenge was a recognized obligation among the Cherokee before 1810, when the national council forbade it and shifted responsibility to the nation (Chapter 64). Some societies practiced banishment, execution for specified crimes, or retaliation. Others stressed mediation but also maintained sanctions for those who refused to reconcile. The point is not to rank these systems but to recognize that each was a functioning legal order with its own definitions of wrongdoing and its own methods of enforcement.

These traditions are different from modern "restorative justice," a term developed in the late twentieth century in Canada, the United States, and elsewhere to describe programs that bring offenders, victims, and communities together to address harm outside or alongside conventional court processes. Restorative programs borrow from Indigenous practices and have often been advocated by Indigenous leaders, but they are typically created by governments or non-Indigenous organizations and operate under statutory limits. An Indigenous legal order, by contrast, derives its authority from the people who hold it. A circle that a provincial program convenes to divert a case from court is not the same as a nation's own law-making and adjudication, even if it uses similar ceremonies. The difference between culturally informed practice and Indigenous governance runs through the rest of the chapter.

Finally, Indigenous legal traditions have changed over time. Nations adopted written codes, constitutions, and courts as they confronted colonial pressure and new circumstances; the Cherokee adopted written laws and a constitution in the early nineteenth century, and Pueblo governments blended traditional offices with imposed Spanish and American institutions. Contemporary Indigenous law is both continuous with earlier teachings and the product of adaptation. A Diné peacemaker today draws on teachings about relationships and harmony, but works within a court system governed by Navajo Nation statutes and constitutional rules of the United States. Treating Indigenous legal traditions as static survivals would repeat the colonial error of denying that Indigenous peoples have a legal history.

Section 130.2

Colonial Criminal Law and the Displacement of Indigenous Authority

Colonial criminal jurisdiction expanded in stages. Early treaties and trade-and-intercourse laws frequently recognized that disputes among Indigenous people were their own affair, while claiming authority over offenses between Indigenous people and settlers. The United States' General Crimes Act of 1817 applied federal criminal law in Indian country to offenses involving non-Indians, but excepted crimes by one Indian against another, and the exception carried real force. As reservations replaced treaty-recognized territories and Indigenous economies collapsed (Chapters 81 and 84), federal officials began to treat Indigenous self-government as an obstacle to "civilization," and criminal law became a means of managing and transforming communities.

The Courts of Indian Offenses, established administratively in 1883 by the Secretary of the Interior, illustrate the method. The Department of the Interior promulgated a code for agencies that prohibited dances, ceremonies, and the work of medicine people; restricted plural marriage and certain mourning practices; and policed behavior such as drinking. The courts were staffed by Indigenous judges appointed and paid by the federal agent, and often drawn from agency police. They offered a forum that was nearer and faster than a distant federal court, and some Indigenous judges used the position to protect community interests. But the courts' legal basis was the agent's authority, not tribal sovereignty, and the code made practicing religion a crime. Prohibitions on religious ceremonies were further reinforced by Interior circulars in the 1920s and by federal prosecutions, and they continued until Commissioner John Collier's reforms of the 1930s (Chapter 97) and, much later, the American Indian Religious Freedom Act of 1978.

The Major Crimes Act of 1885 changed the legal map more fundamentally. After Crow Dog, Congress extended federal jurisdiction over specified offenses by Indians against Indians in Indian country. The original statute listed seven crimes; later amendments expanded the list, and by 2023 the Bureau of Justice Statistics counted fifteen categories, including murder, manslaughter, kidnapping, sexual abuse, assault with a dangerous weapon, felony child abuse and neglect, arson, burglary, robbery, and felony larceny. In United States v. Kagama (1886) the Supreme Court upheld the act, grounding congressional power in the government's guardianship of Indian tribes rather than in an enumerated constitutional power. The reasoning treated Indigenous nations as dependent communities subject to federal authority, a premise later challenged but never wholly abandoned. The Major Crimes Act did not abolish tribal jurisdiction over the listed offenses; tribes retained concurrent authority, but their sentencing power was later limited by statute, so that the federal system, with its distant courthouses and prosecutorial discretion, became the principal forum for serious crime.

Canada followed a parallel course through the Indian Act of 1876 and its amendments (Chapter 85). The Act placed Indian agents in the role of justices of the peace for many offenses, regulated intoxicants, and in 1884 amended the law to prohibit the potlatch and, in 1895, certain dances. Enforcement was selective but real. After the 1921 potlatch at Village Island, British Columbia, dozens of Kwakwaka'wakw participants were charged under the ban; several were imprisoned, and others avoided sentences by surrendering ceremonial regalia (the later return of these items belongs to Chapter 125). The pass system, an administrative practice of the 1880s requiring permission to leave reserves, had no clear statutory basis but was backed by police. The North-West Resistance of 1885 led to treason and felony trials, and eight Indigenous men were hanged at Battleford. In every case, the criminal process was a means of political control as well as punishment.

The result was a double displacement. First, outside governments defined crimes affecting Indigenous communities, and the definitions often reflected settler priorities, not Indigenous ones. Second, the institutions that administered the definitions were outside Indigenous control: federal marshals, agency police, federal and provincial courts, and prisons. Indigenous governments did not disappear, and many continued to resolve disputes informally, but formal law, with its power of arrest and imprisonment, increasingly belonged to others. This displacement is the legal background for every later reform in this chapter.

Section 130.3

Reservation and Reserve Policing: Institutions of Control and Public Safety

Policing in Indigenous territories developed through institutions created by outside governments to meet administrative goals. In the United States, Congress began funding "Indian police" at agencies in the late 1870s. These forces were composed mostly of Indigenous men who were paid modestly, wore uniforms, and answered to the agent. Their duties combined public order with enforcement of federal policy: arresting people for liquor offenses or violations of the Indian Offenses code, retrieving students for schools, discouraging ceremonies, and supporting ration distribution. Some Indigenous officers took the job for pay or out of a sense of duty to their communities; others were drawn from factions favored by agents. The death of Sitting Bull (Tatanka Iyotake) in December 1890, when agency police sent to arrest him at Standing Rock were drawn into a fatal confrontation with his supporters, shows how the arrangement could place Indigenous officers and communities in violent conflict (Chapter 90).

Canada's analogue was the North-West Mounted Police, established in 1873 to extend Canadian authority across the Prairies. The force's early work included suppressing the whiskey trade, monitoring Indigenous movement, supporting treaty negotiation and implementation (Chapter 82), and enforcing the pass system. The Mounted Police could also act as intermediaries and, at times, as protectors against settler violence, and many Indigenous leaders negotiated with them as representatives of the Crown. But the force's presence served the national project of settlement. In the North, the RCMP became the visible agent of the Canadian state, and Inuit accounts, as documented by the Qikiqtani Truth Commission, describe police roles in enforcing government policies in the mid-twentieth century. The specific allegation that police carried out a campaign to kill sled dogs in Inuit communities remains disputed: the commission documented many killings, while an RCMP review concluded there was no policy of eradication.

Over time, public-safety functions grew. Police responded to assaults, thefts, domestic violence, and deaths, and communities depended on them. In the United States, the Indian Reorganization Act and later federal law encouraged tribes to adopt law-and-order codes and to operate their own police and courts (Chapter 97). The Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975 allowed tribes to contract with the Bureau of Indian Affairs to run law-enforcement programs previously operated by the bureau (Chapter 114). Today the Bureau of Indian Affairs' Office of Justice Services, tribal police departments, state and county agencies (especially in Public Law 280 states), and, in Alaska, the Village Public Safety Officer program, all provide services in different combinations. In Canada, a significant share of First Nations communities rely on RCMP detachments or provincial police services, with distinct Indigenous-administered services in some regions; Section 130.9 examines these arrangements.

The central analytical distinction is between policing of Indigenous communities by outside governments and policing by Indigenous governments or communities. A police force may employ many Indigenous officers and still be an arm of an outside government, accountable to its leadership and shaped by its priorities. Conversely, a tribal police department accountable to a tribal council and enforcing tribal law is an exercise of Indigenous self-government, even if it is funded in part by federal grants and constrained by federal limits on jurisdiction. Between the two lie hybrid arrangements: cross-deputization agreements, tripartite policing agreements in Canada, and community safety officers working alongside outside police.

Students should also avoid assuming that police presence equaled effective public safety. In many places, the practical problem was absence: few officers across vast territories, long response times, limited detention capacity, and jurisdictional uncertainty about whether tribal, federal, or state officers could act. The paradox of Indigenous policing history is that communities experienced both too much and too little police: intensive control of conduct disfavored by outsiders, and inadequate protection against violence suffered by community members.

Section 130.4

Indigenous Peoples and the Unequal Administration of Criminal Justice

By the late twentieth century, a series of public inquiries had documented patterns of unequal treatment. In Canada, the Royal Commission on the Donald Marshall, Jr., Prosecution (1989), the Aboriginal Justice Inquiry of Manitoba (1991), and the Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples (1996) each concluded that the justice system treated Indigenous people unfairly and recommended greater Indigenous involvement. The Manitoba inquiry was prompted by the 1971 killing of Helen Betty Osborne, whose murder was not prosecuted for years, and the 1988 shooting of J. J. Harper by a Winnipeg police officer. In the United States, the Indian Law and Order Commission (2013) and congressional hearings associated with the Tribal Law and Order Act documented a jurisdictional maze, inconsistent federal prosecution, and poorly funded tribal systems. Different institutions, same conclusion: unequal administration was not an aberration but a structural feature.

Unequal administration can take several forms, and it is important to distinguish them. Over-enforcement refers to disproportionate stops, arrests, or charges for comparable conduct; under-enforcement refers to inadequate investigation or protection when Indigenous people are victims. Differential processing includes bail and pretrial detention decisions, access to counsel, plea bargaining, and sentencing. Institutional effects include classification and conditions in custody, access to programs, and parole decisions. Evidence for each form comes from different sources, and a statistic describing one stage does not automatically explain another.

Measuring Indigenous representation requires care because the stages of the system count different things. A crime may be committed but not reported; reported but not recorded; recorded but not cleared; cleared by arrest or by other means; charged, prosecuted, and convicted; sentenced to custody or to community supervision; and, if sentenced to custody, held in a federal, provincial or state, local, or tribal facility. Offending (what people actually do) is not the same as reporting (what is brought to police attention), which is not the same as arrests, convictions, admissions to custody (the number of times people enter custody in a year), or the population incarcerated on a given day, which is affected by sentence length and time held on remand. Using one measure to describe another invites error.

Comparison across countries is especially hazardous. Canada's federal corrections system holds people sentenced to two years or more, while provincial and territorial systems hold shorter sentences and remand; the United States has federal prisons, state prisons, and local jails, with Indian country offenses dominating the federal caseload because of the Major Crimes Act. The Bureau of Justice Statistics' 2023 federal report, for example, explicitly notes that most violent cases involving Indigenous people on tribal lands are counted in federal statistics while most violent cases involving non-Indigenous people are counted in state or local systems. Racial and ethnic categories also differ: the United States uses self-identification into "American Indian or Alaska Native," while Canadian agencies distinguish First Nations, Inuit, and Métis and use different sources, including administrative and census data.

Causes of disproportion are debated, and serious scholarship treats them as interacting rather than singular. Researchers and commissions point to socioeconomic marginalization, intergenerational effects of dispossession and residential and boarding schools, substance use and mental health conditions that go untreated, geographic isolation, differences in police presence, bail and pretrial practices, mandatory penalties, risk assessment tools, and discrimination by officials. Some factors affect the likelihood of coming into contact with the system, others affect what happens afterward. The chapter returns to these questions in Division IV.

Division II

The United States: Tribal Sovereignty, Criminal Jurisdiction, and the Courts

How federal statutes and judicial decisions restricted, redistributed, and partially restored Indigenous criminal jurisdiction.

Section 130.5

Federal, State, and Tribal Jurisdiction in Indian Country

Criminal jurisdiction in United States Indian country is often described as a "maze," but it can be understood by asking four questions. Where did the offense occur? The central geographic category is "Indian country," defined in federal law (18 U.S.C. §1151) to include reservations, dependent Indian communities, and Indian allotments. Who is the defendant and who is the victim? The key distinction is whether each is an "Indian," a legal status generally determined by Indian ancestry and recognition by a tribe or the federal government, not simply by race. What is the offense? Serious offenses listed in the Major Crimes Act are treated differently from ordinary crimes. Which statute applies? The General Crimes Act, the Major Crimes Act, the Assimilative Crimes Act, Public Law 280, and other provisions each assign authority.

In outline, the baseline rules are as follows. Tribes retain inherent authority to prosecute Indians who commit offenses within their territory, subject to federal statutory limits on sentencing. For offenses by Indians in the Major Crimes Act, federal courts have concurrent jurisdiction with tribes. For other offenses by Indians against non-Indians, the General Crimes Act authorizes federal prosecution, concurrent with tribal authority. Where both defendant and victim are non-Indians, the state generally has jurisdiction (United States v. McBratney, 1881). Where a non-Indian commits a crime against an Indian, federal jurisdiction has long existed, and since Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta (2022) states also have concurrent jurisdiction, as Section 130.8 explains. Tribes lack inherent authority over non-Indian offenders except as Congress has restored it, notably through VAWA. These rules are subject to exceptions, and the Bureau of Justice Statistics' 2023 report, which sets out a similar framework, cautions that tribal authority generally runs concurrent with overlapping federal and state layers.

Public Law 280, enacted in 1953 during the termination era (Chapter 104), changed the baseline in specified states. Congress required six states, Alaska, California, Minnesota, Nebraska, Oregon, and Wisconsin, to assume criminal jurisdiction over most offenses in Indian country within their borders, with some reservation-specific exceptions, and it permitted other states to assume jurisdiction. Over time, some states assumed jurisdiction in whole or part, and tribes in other states were never subject to it. The 1968 Indian Civil Rights Act required tribal consent for any further assumption and allowed states to retrocede jurisdiction, and some did, including Nebraska for the Omaha Tribe in 1970. The BJS report notes that, of the ten optional states, six (Arizona, Iowa, Nevada, North Dakota, South Dakota, and Utah) did not assume jurisdiction because tribes did not consent, and four (Florida, Idaho, Montana, and Washington) have one or more tribes under optional assumption.

Public Law 280 produced uneven consequences. In mandatory states, state and local police were given responsibility for crimes that federal agents elsewhere would investigate, but Congress provided little funding to support the added burden, and tribal governments that had previously handled such matters found their authority overlapped or displaced. The Indian Law and Order Commission found that many tribes in these states lacked meaningful law-enforcement presence and recommended a mechanism that would allow tribes to opt out of state jurisdiction. Others, especially in Alaska, face distinctive conditions because of the limited amount of land classified as Indian country after the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act (Chapter 115).

Beyond jurisdiction lies enforcement. Tribal police need to know what they can do when they encounter a non-Indian suspect. In United States v. Cooley (2021), the Supreme Court held that a tribal officer may temporarily detain and search a non-Indian on a public right-of-way crossing a reservation when the officer has reason to believe a state or federal law has been violated, pending transfer to the appropriate authority. The decision clarified a narrow but practical power. It left intact the broader limit on tribal prosecution of non-Indians, so the officer's authority to stop and hold is not equivalent to authority to try.

The practical effect of the system is that each case can require multiple determinations before officers know who should respond. A reservation may host a tribal police department, county deputies with cross-deputization agreements, federal agents, and state troopers, and the identity of the actors, not the severity of the harm, can determine whether a case proceeds. The system does not mean "no one has jurisdiction"; in many cases several governments do. But overlapping authority combined with limited capacity can produce gaps, and the history of reform in this division is largely a history of efforts to narrow them.

Historical Vocabulary
Criminal Jurisdiction, Sovereignty, and Concurrent Authority

Definition. Jurisdiction is a government's legal authority to make, apply, or enforce law. Criminal jurisdiction involves several distinct questions: territorial jurisdiction (does the offense occur in a place the government may regulate?), personal jurisdiction (does the government have authority over this defendant, given status such as membership in a tribe?), subject-matter jurisdiction (does a statute authorize the court to hear this offense?), and sentencing authority (what punishment may be imposed?). Concurrent jurisdiction means that two or more governments may each prosecute the same conduct; exclusive jurisdiction means only one may.

Historical context. Tribes possess inherent sovereignty that predates the United States, but Congress and the courts have limited its exercise. A tribal prosecution and a federal prosecution for the same conduct are not "double jeopardy" because they are by separate sovereigns, a principle affirmed in United States v. Wheeler (1978) and United States v. Lara (2004).

Potential misunderstanding. To say a tribe has "sovereignty" does not settle whether it can prosecute a particular person for a particular act, and to say a tribe lacks jurisdiction over a particular defendant does not mean it lacks sovereignty. The terms describe different things.

Section 130.6

The Limits and Recovery of Tribal Criminal Authority

Tribal courts expanded after the Indian Reorganization Act and again with the self-determination policy of the 1970s (Chapter 114), but their criminal power was narrowed by Congress and the Supreme Court. The Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968 applied to tribal governments many of the protections of the Bill of Rights, including free exercise and speech, protection against unreasonable searches, the right to a speedy and public trial, and equal protection and due process, while limiting punishment to six months' imprisonment and a $500 fine; Congress raised the limit to one year and $5,000 in 1986. Congress did not apply every constitutional guarantee. It did not, for example, require tribes to appoint counsel for indigent defendants in ordinary cases; that requirement came in 2010 for longer sentences. The act made habeas corpus the federal remedy for detention, and in Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez (1978) the Court held that other civil enforcement of the act lies in tribal forums, reinforcing tribal self-governance while restricting federal review.

In the same year, Oliphant v. Suquamish Indian Tribe (1978) held that tribes lack inherent criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians. Mark Oliphant, a non-Indian, had been arrested on the Port Madison Reservation in Washington during a tribal celebration and charged with assaulting an officer and resisting arrest. The Court's majority reasoned that tribes, by virtue of their dependent status, had implicitly given up the power to try non-Indians unless Congress said otherwise. Critics have noted that the opinion relied on assumptions about the "overriding sovereignty" of the United States and on selective historical sources, and that treaties had often recognized tribal authority to deal with intruders. Whatever its merit as history, the decision defined the field for decades: reservations contained residents, workers, and visitors over whom tribal police and courts could not prosecute.

Twelve years later, in Duro v. Reina (1990), the Court extended the logic of Oliphant to nonmember Indians, holding that a tribe could not prosecute an Indian who was not a member of that tribe. Duro, a member of a California tribe, was charged with shooting a minor on the Salt River reservation in Arizona. The ruling created a particular problem because the Major Crimes Act covered only serious crimes and federal prosecutors often declined lesser offenses, leaving misdemeanor violence by nonmember Indians unprosecuted. Congress responded in 1990 with a temporary measure and in 1991 with a permanent amendment to the Indian Civil Rights Act recognizing the inherent power of tribes to exercise criminal jurisdiction over all Indians. In United States v. Lara (2004), the Court upheld that statute, reasoning that Congress may relax restrictions on tribal authority and that a tribal prosecution under that power is a prosecution by a separate sovereign, so a later federal prosecution does not violate double jeopardy.

The Tribal Law and Order Act of 2010 addressed other limits. Responding to complaints that federal authorities declined many reservation cases, the act required the Department of Justice to track and report declinations, encouraged cross-designation of tribal prosecutors as special assistant U.S. attorneys, and improved tribal access to federal criminal databases. It also created "enhanced sentencing" authority: tribes that provide specified safeguards may impose up to three years' imprisonment per offense and up to nine years in total, with fines up to $15,000 per offense. Those safeguards include effective assistance of licensed counsel for indigent defendants, law-trained judges licensed to practice, publicly available criminal laws and rules, and a recorded record of proceedings. The statute thus tied expanded power to procedural requirements. Many tribes lacked the funds to meet them, and implementation has varied.

These developments show that tribal criminal authority has been neither fixed nor uniformly shrinking. It was restricted by Oliphant and Duro, partly restored by Congress after Duro, and expanded in procedure and sentencing by the Tribal Law and Order Act. It was also shaped by practical matters such as funding, detention capacity, and the availability of trained judges and public defenders. The Bureau of Justice Statistics notes that most violent crime involving Indigenous people on tribal lands appears in federal rather than tribal or state statistics, which means that statistics on tribal courts alone understate how disputes are handled.

Cause and Effect

How Statutes and Decisions Changed Tribal Courts' Ability to Prosecute

Long-term causes

The nineteenth-century premise that Indigenous nations were "domestic dependent" communities under federal guardianship; the Major Crimes Act (1885); the reservation system; and the assimilation-era courts, which created institutions that were administratively, not tribally, controlled.

Short-term causes

Postwar termination and Public Law 280; the civil-rights movement's insistence on individual rights, reflected in the Indian Civil Rights Act; and cases in which non-Indian and nonmember defendants challenged tribal jurisdiction.

Immediate triggers

The arrest of Mark Oliphant (1973) and the shooting charged against Albert Duro, each of which produced a Supreme Court test of whether tribal authority reached people outside the tribe or outside Indian identity.

Immediate effects

Oliphant (1978) and Duro (1990) removed tribal authority over non-Indians and nonmember Indians; Congress restored authority over all Indians in 1991; sentencing limits stayed at one year until 2010.

Long-term consequences

A durable enforcement gap concerning non-Indian offenders on reservations; reliance on federal prosecutors who often declined cases; the Tribal Law and Order Act (2010) and VAWA (2013, 2022) as incremental restorations tied to procedural safeguards.

Historical complexity

The outcomes did not follow from any single cause. Courts and Congress acted in different directions; tribal governments built capacity unevenly; and the same decisions that restricted authority prompted political mobilization that produced legislative restoration.
Section 130.7

The Violence Against Women Act and Special Tribal Criminal Jurisdiction

The Violence Against Women Act reauthorization of 2013 marked the first statutory recognition since Oliphant of tribal criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians. The law recognized "special domestic violence criminal jurisdiction" for participating tribes over non-Indian defendants who committed domestic violence, dating violence, or violations of protection orders against Indian victims in the tribe's Indian country. The jurisdiction was limited to defendants with specified ties to the tribe, such as residence or employment in the tribe's Indian country or an intimate relationship with a tribal member or Indian resident. It took effect generally in March 2015, after a pilot project in which three tribes, Pascua Yaqui, Tulalip, and Umatilla, began exercising it in 2014. Tribes that wished to participate had to provide protections equivalent to those in the Tribal Law and Order Act and, in addition, a jury drawn from a fair cross-section of the community that did not systematically exclude non-Indians.

Congress expanded this authority in the 2022 reauthorization, which renamed it "special Tribal criminal jurisdiction" (STCJ) and became effective on October 1, 2022. The Department of Justice lists the covered crimes as assault of tribal justice personnel, child violence, dating violence, domestic violence, obstruction of justice, sexual violence, sex trafficking, stalking, and violation of a protection order. The 2022 law removed the earlier requirement that the defendant have ties to the tribe. In cases of obstruction of justice and assault of tribal justice personnel, the victim need not be Indian. A tribe may not exercise STCJ over an offense, other than those two, if neither the defendant nor the victim is an Indian, and the statute does not authorize tribal prosecution of drug offenses or other crimes not listed. The elements of the covered offenses are defined by tribal law. The act also created a reimbursement program for tribes' costs and a pilot program for a limited number of Alaska tribes, in recognition that limited Indian country in Alaska had made the 2013 provision largely unavailable there.

STCJ is conditioned on procedural protections. Participating tribes must provide the rights required for enhanced sentencing under the Tribal Law and Order Act, including licensed counsel for indigent defendants and law-trained judges, together with the fair-cross-section jury requirement, notice of rights, and the opportunity to seek federal habeas review. These requirements respond to the concern, voiced in debates over the law, that non-Indian defendants might not receive fair treatment in tribal courts. Tribal governments responded that they already had strong incentives to provide fair trials and that the safeguards made the exercise of jurisdiction costly. The Intertribal Technical-Assistance Working Group, composed of tribal officials, helped tribes prepare codes, train staff, and share lessons.

Two points should be kept clear. First, STCJ is a recognition by Congress of inherent tribal authority within limits, not a general restoration of tribal jurisdiction over non-Indians. Tribes still cannot prosecute a non-Indian for most crimes, and the Department of Justice's guidance stresses that crimes between two non-Indians remain outside STCJ except in the two special categories. Second, recognition is not the same as capacity. Exercising STCJ requires trained prosecutors, judges, defense counsel, detention or alternatives, victim services, and probation, which many tribes had to build. The reimbursement program and grants under the act respond to this need, but funding and staffing remain central questions in the evaluation of the law. This section concerns institutional jurisdiction; for the broader history of violence against Indigenous women and girls and the response to missing and murdered Indigenous people, see Chapter 129.

Section 130.8

McGirt, Castro-Huerta, and the Modern Boundaries of Tribal Jurisdiction

McGirt v. Oklahoma (decided July 9, 2020) arose from the prosecution by the State of Oklahoma of Jimcy McGirt, a citizen of the Seminole Nation, for sexual offenses against a child, committed within the historic boundaries of the Muscogee (Creek) Nation. McGirt argued that because the offenses occurred in "Indian country" and he was an Indian, the Major Crimes Act gave the federal government, not the state, jurisdiction. The Supreme Court, in a 5–4 decision written by Justice Neil Gorsuch, agreed. It held that Congress had reserved land to the Creek Nation by treaty in the 1830s after the removal of the Muscogee from the Southeast (Chapters 65–66), and that although Congress had greatly reduced Creek governmental authority around the time of Oklahoma statehood, it had never disestablished the reservation. Only Congress, the Court said, can diminish a reservation, and courts may not find disestablishment from historical practice, demographic change, or practical consequences alone. For purposes of the Major Crimes Act, the land remained a reservation.

The ruling had substantial effects, but it is important to describe them accurately. McGirt itself decided whether the Creek Reservation continued to exist for Major Crimes Act purposes; it did not hold that tribes may prosecute everyone or that all state authority had disappeared. Its immediate effect was that Oklahoma could not prosecute Indian defendants for Major Crimes Act offenses within the reservation, so those cases moved to federal or tribal courts. The Oklahoma Court of Criminal Appeals extended the logic to the Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw, and Seminole reservations in 2021, and in State ex rel. Matloff v. Wallace (2021) held that McGirt did not apply retroactively to void convictions that were already final. Federal and tribal courts experienced rising caseloads, tribes and prosecutors negotiated cross-deputization and cooperative agreements, and political leaders debated the consequences. The BJS 2023 report describes the decision as having caused dismissals of state cases and a shift of major crimes from state to federal courts, with lesser offenses by Indians prosecuted in tribal court.

Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta (decided June 29, 2022) concerned a different question. Victor Manuel Castro-Huerta, a non-Indian, was convicted by Oklahoma of child neglect of his stepdaughter, who was a citizen of the Cherokee Nation, in Indian country. After McGirt, the state court vacated the conviction on the ground that only the federal government could prosecute a non-Indian for a crime against an Indian in Indian country. In a 5–4 opinion by Justice Brett Kavanaugh, the Supreme Court reversed, holding that the federal government and the state have concurrent jurisdiction to prosecute crimes by non-Indians against Indians in Indian country, unless federal law preempts. The majority reasoned that Indian country is part of the state, that the General Crimes Act did not make federal jurisdiction exclusive, and that the case did not require the balancing test used in some civil preemption cases. Justice Gorsuch, dissenting, argued that the decision departed from long-standing understandings and treaty promises.

The two decisions must not be conflated. McGirt concerned the continued existence of a reservation and the consequent federal role over Indian defendants; Castro-Huerta concerned concurrent state authority over non-Indian defendants who victimize Indians. Castro-Huerta did not overrule McGirt, and it did not address a state's authority over Indian defendants. Neither decision itself created tribal criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians or established tribal authority over every offender; tribal jurisdiction over non-Indians continues to depend on statutes such as VAWA. The decisions together illustrate how a judicial ruling can affirm a reservation's continued legal existence while also adjusting the distribution of prosecutorial power among governments.

Their significance extends beyond Oklahoma. McGirt reaffirmed that treaty promises and congressional intent, not the passage of time, determine the legal status of reservations. Castro-Huerta treated the sovereignty of states as extending into Indian country absent preemption, and tribal advocates argued that it weakened the principle that tribal governments should be the principal authorities within their territories. Both decisions affect the practical work of police: whom to call, who may arrest, and which prosecutor will accept the case. Tribal governments responded by expanding courts and prosecutors and negotiating agreements, state officials responded in different ways, and the decisions remain contested in Congress, in tribal councils, and in the courts.

Major Case Study 2

McGirt and Castro-Huerta, 2020–2022

Question in McGirt: Does the Creek Reservation still exist for purposes of the Major Crimes Act? Answer: Yes; Congress did not disestablish it. Question in Castro-Huerta: May a state prosecute a non-Indian for a crime against an Indian in Indian country? Answer: Yes, concurrently with the federal government, unless preempted.

What changed. In the Oklahoma context, state prosecution of Indian defendants for Major Crimes Act offenses ended; federal and tribal workloads rose; states regained concurrent authority over non-Indian offenders against Indian victims.

What did not change. Tribal jurisdiction over non-Indians, which remains limited to VAWA categories and other statutory grants; the rule that tribes may prosecute Indian defendants; and the requirement that federal law, not state preference, determines the reach of state power in Indian country.

Lesson. Distribution of prosecutorial power can shift without any change in the legal existence of the reservation, and vice versa.

Canada: Policing, Sentencing, and Judicial Accountability

The relationship among Indigenous communities, Canadian police services, sentencing law, wrongful convictions, and judicial reform.

Section 130.9

Policing First Nations, Inuit, and Métis Communities in Canada

In Canada, criminal law is made by Parliament, but the administration of justice, including policing, is mainly a provincial and territorial responsibility. Provinces may establish their own police services, contract with the RCMP, or authorize municipalities to do so. Ontario and Quebec maintain provincial police (the Ontario Provincial Police and the Sûreté du Québec); most other provinces and all three territories contract with the RCMP. First Nations, Inuit, and Métis communities are therefore served by whichever service holds the contract for the region, except where Indigenous-administered police exist. Because reserves fall under federal jurisdiction through the Indian Act while policing is provincial, responsibility has often been unclear, and communities have experienced gaps in funding and service.

The federal government's principal response was the First Nations Policing Policy, introduced in 1991, which supports policing agreements negotiated among the federal government, a province or territory, and a First Nation or group of First Nations. Funding is shared between Canada and the province or territory. Agreements take two main forms: self-administered police services governed by a First Nations police board or commission, and community tripartite agreements under which a provincial police service or the RCMP provides dedicated officers to a community. Examples of self-administered services include the Nishnawbe-Aski Police Service in northern Ontario and the Kativik Regional Police Force in Nunavik, Quebec. The program was designed as a funding program for agreements rather than as a statutory guarantee, and communities and chiefs have criticized the resulting dependence on periodic renewals and negotiations.

Persistent problems have been documented by inquiries, evaluations, and Indigenous organizations. Many fly-in northern communities have few officers, long response times, inadequate detachment and cell facilities, and high staff turnover. Self-administered services have struggled with underfunding, inadequate equipment, and housing for officers. Because First Nations policing is not classified in law as an essential service in the way that municipal policing is in many provinces, funding disputes can affect basic operations. First Nations leaders and the federal government have therefore pursued legislation to treat First Nations, Inuit, and Métis policing as an essential service; students should consult current federal sources for the status of that work. Provincial law has also changed: Ontario's Community Safety and Policing Act, 2019, which replaced the Police Services Act in 2024, includes provisions on First Nations policing.

Accountability has been a second concern. Public inquiries have criticized police investigations involving Indigenous people. The Wright Inquiry (2004) in Saskatchewan examined the death of Neil Stonechild, a seventeen-year-old Indigenous youth who froze to death in 1990, and concluded that he had last been in the custody of two Saskatoon police officers. In Ontario, a 2018 review by the Office of the Independent Police Review Director into Thunder Bay Police Service investigations of Indigenous deaths found systemic racism in those investigations, and a coroner's inquest examined the deaths of seven First Nations students who had left home to attend high school. In Quebec, the Viens Commission (2019) concluded that Indigenous people experience systemic discrimination in public services, including policing. Independent civilian oversight bodies have been created in several provinces, though their authority and Indigenous participation vary.

Policing of Inuit and Métis communities presents distinct features. Nunavut and the Northwest Territories are policed by the RCMP, and Inuit in Nunavik are served by the Kativik force. Many Métis people live in towns and cities and are served by municipal and provincial services, and Métis governments have emphasized concerns about data, victim services, and access to justice rather than separate police forces. Indigenous and non-Indigenous commentators generally agree that the problem is not simply the number of officers but the relationship: officers unfamiliar with the community's language, culture, and history may misinterpret behavior, and communities may distrust police because of historical enforcement of the Indian Act, residential school attendance, and more recent controversies. Reforms that emphasize hiring Indigenous officers, community policing, and Indigenous governance of police boards address that relationship, but as Section 130.19 explains, their results require independent evidence.

Section 130.10

Oka, Ipperwash, and the Problem of Police Accountability

The summer of 1990 brought the police and army to Kanehsatà:ke, near Oka, Quebec, where Mohawk residents opposed the expansion of a golf course onto land they regarded as theirs and as a burial and sacred site. On July 11, 1990, the Sûreté du Québec attempted to dismantle a barricade; in the exchange of gunfire, Corporal Marcel Lemay was killed. A seventy-eight-day standoff followed, with solidarity actions by Kahnawà:ke, the intervention of the Canadian Armed Forces, and extensive national attention. The crisis, which belongs to a broader history of land disputes, became a reference point for later debates about how police should respond to Indigenous land protests and whether government should negotiate rather than rely on force.

Five years later, at Ipperwash, Ontario, a related question was tested again. In 1942, under the War Measures Act, the federal government had appropriated land at Stoney Point (Aazhoodena), the home of members of the Kettle and Stony Point First Nation, for a military training camp, with a promise to return it after the war. The land was not returned. In 1993, community members began occupying parts of Camp Ipperwash; in September 1995 some moved into adjacent Ipperwash Provincial Park, claiming a burial ground. The government of Premier Mike Harris, elected that summer, was concerned about the occupation and sought an injunction. On the night of September 6, 1995, the Ontario Provincial Police deployed a tactical team and crowd-management unit. In a confrontation near the park, OPP Sergeant Kenneth Deane shot Dudley George, who died the following morning. Deane was convicted in 1997 of criminal negligence causing death.

The public inquiry, established in 2003 and led by Commissioner Sidney B. Linden, heard extensive evidence and issued its four-volume report on May 31, 2007. The Commissioner concluded that the OPP, the Government of Ontario under Premier Harris, and the federal government each bore responsibility for the events that led to the death. The report criticized the premier for impatience and for racially offensive remarks at a meeting of officials, and for misleading the legislature, but found that he did not direct or interfere with the OPP's operations. The report documented police failures in planning and command, deficient communication with the First Nation, and the persistence of the unresolved land grievance. It made roughly one hundred recommendations (accounts differ on whether the count is 98 or 100) concerning policing of Indigenous protests and occupations, the independence of police from political direction, clarity in the roles of the premier and officials, negotiation of land claims, and the return of Camp Ipperwash to the Kettle and Stony Point First Nation. It also urged a public apology and recognition of the harm to the George family and community.

Two themes of the Ipperwash Inquiry bear on the chapter's central question. The first is police independence. In the Canadian constitutional tradition, police exercise their investigative and enforcement discretion free from political direction in individual cases; this protects against partisan use of coercive power. Linden's report did not find a political order to the OPP, but it emphasized that the line between government policy-setting and operational decision-making needs clear protocols, and that expressions of political impatience can create pressure. The second is the relationship between land and policing. The confrontation grew from an unresolved land dispute, and the inquiry stressed that police should not be left to resolve disputes that require political and legal solutions. Police may maintain public safety during conflict, but they cannot remedy the underlying grievance.

The inquiry's legacy is mixed. The report prompted changes in police practices and provincial policy and strengthened recognition of Indigenous land claims, and the Stoney Point community eventually regained parts of the former military land. Yet a public inquiry cannot compel governments to adopt its recommendations, and subsequent incidents have kept the question of police accountability before the public. Section 130.19 returns to this issue and asks what evidence would show that an inquiry's recommendations were implemented.

Section 130.11

Gladue, Ipeelee, and Indigenous Sentencing in Canadian Courts

In 1996 Parliament enacted a major reform of sentencing law that included section 718.2(e) of the Criminal Code. The provision instructs courts that all available sanctions other than imprisonment that are reasonable in the circumstances should be considered for all offenders, with particular attention to the circumstances of Aboriginal offenders. The provision was part of a legislative effort to reduce overreliance on imprisonment and to respond to the documented overrepresentation of Indigenous people in custody, which the inquiries of the previous decade, including the Manitoba Inquiry and the Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples, had emphasized.

The Supreme Court of Canada first interpreted the provision in R. v. Gladue (1999). Jamie Tanis Gladue, a young Cree woman, had pleaded guilty to manslaughter for killing her common-law partner in British Columbia in 1995 and received a three-year sentence. The Court held that the provision is remedial, that it requires judges to consider the unique systemic or background factors that may have contributed to bringing the Indigenous offender before the court, and to consider the types of sentencing procedures and sanctions that may be appropriate because of the offender's Indigenous heritage or connection. It also said that, for serious offences, the sentences for Indigenous and non-Indigenous offenders may often be similar. Applying those principles to Gladue's case, the Court upheld her three-year sentence. The decision's significance was thus doctrinal, not the creation of a result for one offender.

In practice, Gladue had an uneven reception. Some courts treated it as limited to less serious offenses or required Indigenous offenders to prove a causal link between background factors and the offence, and some judges lacked information about the individual. Practitioners developed "Gladue reports," prepared by specially trained writers who interview the offender, family, and community members and describe life history, community context, and available alternatives to custody. Programs for such reports, such as those of Aboriginal Legal Services in Toronto, have been funded in different ways in different provinces, and access is uneven, particularly in remote communities.

In R. v. Ipeelee (2012), the Supreme Court revisited the issue in two companion cases involving breaches of long-term supervision orders: Manasie Ipeelee, an Inuk man, and Frank Ladue, a Yukon man. The Court, in reasons by Justice LeBel, said that more than a decade after Gladue the situation of Indigenous overrepresentation had not improved and attributed part of the failure to misunderstanding and misapplication of the provision and of the earlier decision. It stated that Gladue applies to all offences, including serious ones; that no causal link between background factors and the offence need be shown; that judges must take judicial notice of the history of colonialism, displacement, and residential schools and their continuing effects; and that those factors bear on the offender's moral blameworthiness and on the appropriateness of different sanctions. The Court reduced Ipeelee's sentence to one year and left Ladue's one-year sentence in place. Justice Rothstein dissented in part, disagreeing with the disposition of Ipeelee’s appeal while agreeing with the result in Ladue’s case.

It is equally important to describe what these decisions do not do. They do not create an automatic discount, an exemption from criminal responsibility, or a rule that judges must impose community sentences in every case involving an Indigenous defendant. Sentencing remains governed by the fundamental principle of proportionality, which requires that a sentence be proportionate to the gravity of the offence and the offender's degree of responsibility, and by objectives such as denunciation, deterrence, public protection, and rehabilitation. The Gladue framework requires meaningful consideration of systemic factors and of culturally appropriate alternatives, and it may reduce the sentence or change its form; but it does so within these principles. Later cases, including the Supreme Court's 2022 decision in R. v. Sharma, in which a divided Court rejected a constitutional challenge to limits on conditional sentences, show that legislative restrictions can narrow the options available even when Gladue principles are applied.

The deeper limitation is structural. Sentencing occurs after arrest, charge, and often a period of pretrial detention, and the Court in Ipeelee itself acknowledged that the provision had not produced a discernible reduction in overrepresentation. Judges can respond only to the cases brought before them, and may lack community-based programs to which to sentence an offender. Sentencing reform alone therefore cannot address policing patterns, bail decisions, charging practices, or the social conditions that bring people into contact with the system. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission and later strategies, discussed in Section 130.18, treat sentencing as one part of a wider program.

Historical Vocabulary
Gladue Principles, Proportionality, and Sentencing Restraint

Definition. "Gladue principles" are the sentencing approach that the Supreme Court of Canada derived from section 718.2(e) of the Criminal Code. They require courts to consider systemic and background factors affecting an Indigenous offender and to consider restorative and culturally appropriate sanctions. Proportionality is the fundamental principle that a sentence must fit the seriousness of the offence and the offender's responsibility. Sentencing restraint refers to the legislative instruction to consider non-custodial options.

Historical context. The principles developed in response to evidence of overrepresentation of Indigenous people in prisons and to criticism that sentencing ignored the effects of colonization and residential schooling.

Why it matters. The principles require judges to inform themselves and to give reasons; they do not dictate a particular outcome.

Potential misunderstanding. The principles are sometimes described as a "discount" for Indigenous offenders. The Court has described them instead as a method of arriving at a fit sentence by taking account of relevant circumstances that would otherwise be overlooked.

Section 130.12

Wrongful Convictions, Indigenous Defendants, and Barriers to Justice

In May 1971, in Sydney, Nova Scotia, a young Black man, Sandy Seale, was stabbed to death in a park. Donald Marshall Jr., a seventeen-year-old Mi'kmaw man from the Membertou reserve who had been in the park, was charged, convicted of murder in November 1971, and sentenced to life imprisonment. He spent roughly eleven years in custody before new evidence led to a reference to the Nova Scotia Court of Appeal, which acquitted him in 1983. The Court of Appeal also suggested that his own conduct had contributed to the miscarriage of justice, a comment widely criticized. Roy Ebsary, the man who had actually stabbed Seale, was later convicted of manslaughter.

The provincial government appointed the Royal Commission on the Donald Marshall, Jr., Prosecution, which reported in 1989. The Commission found that the criminal justice system had failed Marshall at multiple stages: police had pursued him despite contrary evidence and had pressured witnesses, two of whom later said their testimony at trial was untrue; the Crown had failed to disclose relevant material; his defense counsel did not adequately investigate; the courts had not corrected the errors; and later review processes were slow and defensive. The Commission concluded that racial prejudice had played a role in the way Marshall was treated and recommended compensation, changes in the prosecution service, and measures to improve Indigenous access to legal services and the justice system. Nova Scotia later created an independent public prosecution service, and Mi'kmaw organizations developed legal support programs, though advocates have argued that some Indigenous-focused recommendations were implemented only partially.

Marshall's story did not end with his exoneration. In 1993 he was charged with fishing for eels without a license and selling them. In R. v. Marshall (1999), the Supreme Court of Canada acquitted him and held that eighteenth-century treaties with the Mi'kmaq protected a right to hunt, fish, and gather and trade for a moderate livelihood, a landmark in treaty rights (Chapter 106 discusses treaty-rights struggles in the United States; Canadian constitutional implications are treated in Chapter 117). The sequence underscores that the same community faced both a criminal justice system that wrongly convicted a young man and an enforcement system that criminalized the exercise of treaty rights.

Wrongful convictions are not unique to Indigenous defendants, and the number of such cases cannot be reliably counted. But the Marshall Commission identified factors that remain relevant: police assumptions, unequal access to counsel, language and cultural barriers, discretionary decisions by prosecutors, and institutional reluctance to admit error. Indigenous people have fewer resources to challenge convictions, and defendants in remote communities may meet counsel only on the day of court. In the North, the Nunavut Court of Justice and the territorial courts operate on circuit, with judges, prosecutors, defense counsel, and interpreters flying to communities for short sittings; defendants and witnesses may speak Inuktitut or Inuinnaqtun, and interpretation is essential and not always readily available. Inuit also observe that legal concepts, rights warnings, and the adversarial process can be unfamiliar, and that the preference of many communities for consensus-based resolution is not easily accommodated.

A comparative note concerns Mexico, where language access is an acknowledged problem in Indigenous defendants' cases, as Section 130.17 will explain. Across jurisdictions, public inquiries can expose these failures, as the Marshall report did. But the reports' recommendations are not self-executing. The lesson of the Marshall case is that legal acknowledgment of discrimination differs from institutional transformation: acknowledgement is a precondition, and what changes depends on resources, institutional incentives, and Indigenous participation in design and oversight.

Section 130.13

Indigenous Overrepresentation in Prisons and Correctional Systems

Indigenous overrepresentation in custody is documented in both Canada and the United States, but the measures differ and must be read with care. In Canada, the most frequently cited indicator is the share of Indigenous people under federal correctional jurisdiction. Indigenous people made up about 5 percent of the Canadian population but, at the end of fiscal year 2025–2026, approximately 28.6 percent of persons under federal correctional jurisdiction, a figure that includes people serving federal sentences of two years or more in custody and those supervised in the community. This is not the same as the share of people physically incarcerated. The Correctional Service of Canada's 2023–2024 accountability report, for example, put Indigenous people at 29 percent of the population under CSC jurisdiction and 33 percent of the in-custody population, and the rise of overrepresentation in custody is a recurring finding of the Office of the Correctional Investigator, whose 2025–26 annual report states that overrepresentation has continued to rise and that risk assessment and security classification processes produce inequitable outcomes. Students should always ask whether a percentage refers to custody, community supervision, admissions, or the combined population.

Federal institutions hold only part of the Canadian correctional population. Provincial and territorial systems hold people on remand (awaiting trial or sentencing) and those sentenced to less than two years, and in many provinces Indigenous overrepresentation in these systems is also severe. Statistics Canada's annual reports on adult correctional services, most recently published in January 2026, provide data by jurisdiction, though not all provinces and territories report identically. The complexity is greater because Indigenous identity is recorded differently across agencies, and because Statistics Canada's measures of admissions (each time someone begins a custody or supervision period) can exceed the number of individual people, since many people are admitted more than once.

In the United States, the data are organized differently. Indian country offenses that fall under the Major Crimes Act are mainly prosecuted federally, so American Indian and Alaska Native (AIAN) people appear in federal statistics for violent offenses at higher rates than in other federal categories. The Bureau of Justice Statistics' March 2026 report on fiscal year 2023 found 2,908 federal arrests of AIAN persons, about 3 percent of all federal arrests (94,411), an arrest rate of 65 per 100,000 AIAN persons; AIAN suspects accounted for 44 percent of those federally arrested for murder and 34 percent of those arrested for assault, reflecting federal jurisdiction. Of AIAN defendants charged in U.S. district court that year, 60 percent were ordered detained before trial; 88 percent of those sentenced received imprisonment only, and the median prison term was 37 months. At the end of fiscal year 2023, 3,698 federally sentenced AIAN persons were in Federal Bureau of Prisons custody, a rate of 82 per 100,000. About 31 percent of AIAN persons released from federal prison in fiscal years 2018–2020 returned within three years, and 91 percent of returns were for supervision violations rather than new offences. Rates varied widely by state; the 2021–2023 average federal incarceration rate was highest in South Dakota (747 per 100,000), Wyoming, North Dakota, and Montana.

These numbers cannot be compared directly with Canadian ones. The BJS report counts federally sentenced persons in the federal system, which is a caseload shaped by the Major Crimes Act, and does not include persons in state prisons, local jails, or tribal facilities. State prison data come from separate BJS series: for example, Prisoners in 2021 reported an imprisonment rate of 1,004 per 100,000 AIAN adults in state and federal prisons, second only to Black adults and well above the rate for white adults (222 per 100,000), while Prisoners in 2022 reported that American Indians and Alaska Natives were 2 percent of sentenced state and federal prisoners. The U.S. Sentencing Commission's fiscal year 2025 data show 1,514 Native American sentenced individuals, with an average sentence of 73 months and 94 percent sentenced to prison; this differs from the BJS median of 37 months for fiscal year 2023 because it uses a different year and a mean rather than a median. A responsible comparison identifies the population (federal, state, provincial, or local), the year, the measure (stock, admissions, or rates), and the denominator.

Explanations for the disparities draw on several interacting factors. Historical dispossession, forced assimilation, and the intergenerational effects of residential and boarding schools (Chapters 127–128) contributed to poverty, unemployment, housing instability, and untreated trauma. Policing patterns and discretionary decisions affect who is stopped, charged, and detained; bail systems may penalize those without stable addresses or without sureties; mandatory minimum penalties and limits on conditional sentences reduce options; and correctional risk-assessment tools have been criticized for failing to account for Indigenous experience, as in the Supreme Court of Canada's 2018 decision in Ewert v. Canada, which held that CSC had failed to ensure the accuracy of its assessment tools as applied to Indigenous offenders. Geographic isolation can prevent access to counsel, to community programs, or to release plans. None of these factors on its own explains the disparity, and rigorous analysis tests each with evidence.

Section 130.14

Indigenous Women, Young People, and Unequal Correctional Outcomes

Indigenous women and youth experience criminal justice institutions in ways shaped by gender, age, and colonial history. In Canada, the federally sentenced women's population has grown, and the Office of the Correctional Investigator's 2025–26 report notes that the growth has been disproportionately among Indigenous women. A 2026 CSC research brief reported that Indigenous women made up 42 percent of women serving life or indeterminate sentences in federal institutions as of October 2025. Many Indigenous women in custody have histories of abuse, poverty, addiction, and involvement with child-welfare systems (Chapter 131), and many are mothers. These histories do not erase accountability for offences, but they bear on risk, on programming, and on the likelihood that standard classification tools will misjudge them. The Supreme Court's reasoning in Ewert concerned precisely this risk.

Women's imprisonment also produces distinctive harms. Because there are fewer women's institutions, women are often incarcerated far from home, making visits difficult. Pretrial detention and the loss of housing or employment can set off a chain of consequences for children and extended family. Access to legal assistance may be limited, particularly in remote communities. In the United States, BJS data show that AIAN women in the federal system are far more likely than men to be incarcerated for drug offenses (43 percent of AIAN women in federal prison, compared with 13 percent of men) and that AIAN women in fiscal year 2023 were more likely than men to receive probation with community confinement, a difference worth noting alongside the overall pattern of imprisonment. These comparisons describe the federal caseload only.

Young people also face particular risks. In Canada, the Youth Criminal Justice Act directs police and courts toward extrajudicial measures and community sanctions, but Indigenous youth are overrepresented in youth custody, and the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (Call to Action 38) called for eliminating that overrepresentation within a decade. Among the factors identified are police surveillance, instability in housing and family, child-welfare involvement, and limited local programs. In the United States, youth in Indian country may be prosecuted in tribal court, in federal court for certain offences, or, in Public Law 280 states, in state juvenile systems, with varying services. This chapter does not attempt a comprehensive account of youth justice, but it emphasizes that the age and the circumstances of a defendant require attention.

Differences among Indigenous people also matter. First Nations, Inuit, and Métis communities, on-reserve and urban populations, northern and southern residents, and women and men have distinct experiences, and this chapter avoids assuming that all Indigenous defendants experience the system identically. A First Nations man in a southern city may encounter urban police and provincial remand centres; an Inuk woman from Nunavut may be sentenced in the territory but serve a federal sentence in a southern institution because Nunavut has no federal prison; a Pueblo defendant in New Mexico may face tribal, federal, or state processes depending on the offence and defendant. Intersectional analysis does not predict outcomes; it directs the historian to look for how combinations of status and place shape treatment.

Everyday Life and Social History

Arrest, Detention, Distance, and Return

Policies are experienced in daily detail. A person arrested in a remote community may be flown or driven long distances to a courthouse or remand centre, away from family and language. Pretrial detention can mean loss of a job or housing, interrupted care of children or elders, and difficulty meeting counsel; people in fly-in communities may see a lawyer for the first time on the morning of a short court sitting. For defendants who plead guilty to end detention, the choice may be driven by the conditions of confinement rather than the merits.

After conviction, family separation continues. Visits require travel costs, telephone fees, and time; for Inuit sentenced to federal terms and for women in few institutions, the distance may be vast. Families may lose income, and relatives may take on child care (a topic for Chapter 131). Legal costs, fines, restitution, and supervision conditions such as abstaining from alcohol or reporting to a distant office can result in breaches that return people to custody. The BJS finding that most returns to federal prison of AIAN releasees were for supervision violations, and the Ipeelee case on breach of supervision conditions, illustrate this dynamic.

Reintegration depends on housing, employment, treatment, and community acceptance. Communities may welcome members home or may be divided; where a person has harmed a family member, safety planning is needed. Community-based release planning, Elders, and ceremony can help, but only where programs and funding exist. Everyday experience also includes the experience of victims, who may have to travel for court, wait for decisions, and rely on services that are limited in remote places.

Section 130.15

Community Justice, Peacemaking, Diversion, and Indigenous Courts

Indigenous communities and governments have developed a range of alternatives to conventional prosecution and imprisonment. Some are the direct continuation of a nation's legal tradition, others are programs created with government funding, and others are courts within or adjacent to state systems that incorporate Indigenous participation. Their differences matter because the source of authority differs. The Navajo Nation's peacemaking process, hózhóójí naat'aanii, draws on Diné teachings about relationships and harmony and, since the early 1980s, has been integrated into the Navajo Nation's judicial branch, where peacemakers, who are respected community members, facilitate talking-it-out sessions for family, property, and some criminal disputes. The Navajo court system exercises authority derived from Diné government, and the legal status of peacemaking is thus a matter of Navajo law.

In Canada, innovations took several forms. Circle sentencing, associated with the 1992 Yukon decision in R. v. Moses involving a member of the Teslin Tlingit community, brought community members, elders, victims, and offenders together with a judge to discuss an appropriate sentence. Community holistic circle healing in Hollow Water, Manitoba, which began in the late 1980s, addressed sexual abuse through a community process combined with court involvement; it drew both admiration and criticism, with advocates emphasizing community healing and critics asking whether victims' safety and rights were adequately protected. Justice Canada's Aboriginal Justice Strategy, begun in 1991 and later called the Indigenous Justice Program, funds community-based programs, including diversion and mediation. Specialized courts include the Tsuut'ina Court in Alberta (established in 2000), the Toronto Aboriginal Peoples Court (2001), the Cree Court in Saskatchewan (2001), and the First Nations Court in British Columbia (2006), as well as Gladue courts and Elder-assisted sentencing in other places. Such courts typically operate under provincial or territorial authority, use provincial judges, and incorporate Elders, community members, and Indigenous court workers.

In the United States, many tribal courts have adopted peacemaking, restorative or "healing to wellness" drug courts, and community panels. Tribal governments also operate their own juvenile and family courts. Because these initiatives occur within tribal justice systems, they are exercises of tribal authority subject to federal limits on jurisdiction and sentencing, and to the Indian Civil Rights Act's requirements.

Assessment of these programs requires attention to three questions. First, victims' rights and safety: restorative processes must not pressure victims to participate, minimize violence, or place victims in the presence of offenders without safeguards. Concerns have been raised in particular about mediation in domestic and sexual violence cases, and Indigenous women's organizations have urged that programs be designed with victims' input and with support services. Second, defendants' rights: voluntariness, access to counsel, clear rules on what happens if the process fails, and protection against coerced admissions. Third, authority and control: whether the community determines the process, or whether it is a diversion option controlled by prosecutors or judges. A program may be culturally rich but have little autonomy; another may have formal authority but little funding. Evaluations of effectiveness are uneven, and claims of reduced reoffending or increased satisfaction should be tested against independent evidence.

Historical Vocabulary
Restorative Justice, Indigenous Legal Orders, and Legal Pluralism

Definitions. Restorative justice is a contemporary approach emphasizing repair of harm and participation by victims, offenders, and communities. Indigenous legal orders are the laws, institutions, and processes of particular Indigenous peoples, grounded in their own sources of authority. Legal pluralism is the coexistence of more than one legal system within the same territory or population, whether through recognition, delegation, or separate jurisdiction.

Historical context. Restorative justice drew inspiration from Indigenous practices but developed within state systems; Indigenous legal orders predate those systems; and legal pluralism has existed in many forms in North America, from treaty-recognized spheres of authority to modern tribal courts alongside federal and state courts.

Potential misunderstanding. The terms are not interchangeable. A program that uses a talking circle but operates under a prosecutor's discretion is not necessarily an exercise of Indigenous law; a nation's court that applies written statutes and traditional principles may not be restorative in every case.

Section 130.16

Healing Lodges, Community Reintegration, and the Limits of Correctional Reform

Canada's Corrections and Conditional Release Act (CCRA) of 1992 included a group of provisions, sections 79 to 84, concerning "Aboriginal offenders." They require the Correctional Service of Canada to provide programs designed for Indigenous offenders, to consider Indigenous spiritual leaders and Elders, and to involve Indigenous communities. Section 81 authorizes the Minister to enter agreements with Indigenous communities and organizations to provide correctional services, including the care and custody of Indigenous offenders, which has been the legal basis for community-run healing lodges. Section 84 requires CSC, when an Indigenous offender requests it, to give the community the opportunity to propose a plan for release and integration. The statutory design thus contemplates a partnership between a federal correctional agency and Indigenous communities.

Healing lodges emerged from the recommendations of the 1990 report Creating Choices on federally sentenced women and from Indigenous advocacy for correctional spaces grounded in culture. Okimaw Ohci Healing Lodge, near Maple Creek, Saskatchewan, opened in 1995 as a women's facility, and Pê Sâkâstêw Centre in Maskwacis, Alberta, opened in 1997 for men. Both are operated by CSC with participation of Elders and community members. Community-operated lodges under section 81 agreements, such as the Stan Daniels Healing Centre in Edmonton and Waseskun in Quebec, are run by Indigenous organizations. Programming typically includes ceremonies, teachings, land-based activity, and relationships with Elders, together with correctional programs and supervision.

The distinction between government-operated and community-controlled lodges is crucial. A CSC-operated lodge may incorporate Indigenous culture and employ Indigenous staff, but it remains a federal institution under federal law and policy. A section 81 lodge is operated by an Indigenous organization, which has its own governance and cultural authority, but under agreement and funding terms set by the federal government and with continuing federal oversight. Neither is equivalent to an Indigenous nation's own correctional system under its own law. Understanding this difference is essential to evaluating claims about "Indigenous control."

The effect of the lodges on the larger problem is limited by capacity, access, and risk assessment. The Office of the Correctional Investigator has reported over many years that healing lodges hold only a small proportion of Indigenous people in federal custody, that admission depends on security classification and risk assessment tools criticized for bias, and that beds and funding are constrained. Its 2025–26 annual report states that access to culturally appropriate interventions, including healing lodges, remains constrained despite longstanding commitments, and that the gap between policy and experience is the defining challenge of federal corrections. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission (Call to Action 35) urged elimination of barriers to the creation of additional healing lodges. Other reforms, such as Elder-led programs in institutions and community release planning under section 84, face similar obstacles: communities may lack housing, treatment services, and employment for returning members, and agreements require sustained funding.

The broader question is whether such initiatives alter the causes of overrepresentation. Healing lodges can improve the conditions and meaning of a sentence and may support reintegration, but they act after conviction and generally concern the relatively small number of people who qualify. They do not address policing, bail, charging, or sentencing decisions that bring people into custody, nor the socioeconomic conditions behind offending. Rigorous evaluation, which compares outcomes for similar people in lodges and in other institutions, is necessary to know their effects, and the existing evidence is limited. They are therefore best understood as part of a broader reform effort, not as a solution on their own.

Section 130.17

Indigenous Justice in Mexico: Community Legal Systems and Constitutional Recognition

Mexico's approach to Indigenous justice must be understood on its own constitutional terms. The Mexican Constitution has recognized the country's pluricultural composition, based on its Indigenous peoples, since a 1992 reform, and a major reform in 2001 added the detailed provisions of Article 2. That article recognized the right of Indigenous peoples and communities to self-determination and autonomy, including the right to apply their own normative systems (sistemas normativos, often historically called usos y costumbres) to regulate and resolve internal conflicts, subject to the general principles of the Constitution, individual guarantees, human rights, and, in particular, the dignity and integrity of women. It also guaranteed Indigenous people full access to the jurisdiction of the state, including, in all trials and proceedings, the right to be assisted by interpreters and defenders familiar with their language and culture. The 2001 reform followed the Zapatista uprising of 1994 and the San Andrés Accords of 1996 and was criticized by the Zapatistas and many Indigenous organizations as falling short of those agreements.

The reform published in the Diario Oficial de la Federación on September 30, 2024, and in force from October 1, 2024, substantially revised Article 2. Its central change was to recognize Indigenous and Afro-Mexican peoples and communities as sujetos de derecho público, subjects of public law, with legal personality and their own patrimony, replacing the earlier status as entities of public interest. The text reaffirms self-determination and autonomy, adds rights including consultation on legislative and administrative measures that may affect them, and provides that Indigenous jurisdiction is exercised by community authorities in accordance with the constitutional framework and the legislation to be enacted. Congress was directed to issue implementing legislation; students should consult current Mexican sources for the status and content of those laws, because the practical force of the reform depends on them. The reform's recognition of community jurisdiction is not an equivalent of U.S. tribal criminal jurisdiction, which depends on federal statutes and judicial doctrine, nor of Canada's Gladue framework, which concerns sentencing in state courts; it is a constitutional recognition of community-based justice within a national legal system.

The reality of Indigenous justice in Mexico is regionally specific. In Oaxaca, a state with a very large number of municipalities, state law since the 1990s has recognized internal normative systems for choosing municipal authorities in a large majority of municipalities, and community assemblies, tequio (collective work), and service obligations are part of local governance; community authorities frequently resolve disputes, impose sanctions such as fines, community service, or temporary detention, and refer more serious cases to state authorities. In Guerrero, the Regional Coordinator of Community Authorities–Community Police (CRAC-PC), founded in 1995 in the Costa Chica and Montaña regions, created community police and a system of detention and "re-education," administered by community assemblies, as a response to crime and weak state protection; it has been praised for community safety and criticized and divided over procedures, authority, and relations with the state. In Cherán, Michoacán, Purépecha residents in 2011 expelled illegal loggers and criminal groups, formed a community guard, and, after litigation, obtained recognition of a community government chosen by its own assembly. In Chiapas, the Zapatista autonomous governments operate outside formal recognition by the state. These examples differ in legal status, organization, and relationship with the state, and no uniform Mexican Indigenous justice system exists.

Access to state justice is the other half of the picture. Indigenous defendants and victims often confront language barriers, lack of interpreters, poverty, and long distances to courts. Three Hñähñu (Otomí) women from Santiago Mexquititlán, Querétaro—Jacinta Francisco Marcial, Alberta Alcántara Juan, and Teresa González Cornelio—were arrested in 2006 and convicted in a case alleging the kidnapping of federal agents. Human-rights advocates identified serious deficiencies in the proceedings, including barriers to effective defense. Their legal outcomes were distinct: Jacinta was freed on September 15, 2009, after prosecutors withdrew their accusation; on April 28, 2010, the First Chamber of Mexico’s Supreme Court ordered the release of Alberta and Teresa, overturning their convictions. In February 2017, the federal prosecutorial authority publicly recognized the injustice and apologized to all three women. The case illustrates why acquittal or release, public acknowledgment, and meaningful reparation must be assessed separately. Mexico's criminal justice reforms of 2008 and its national code of criminal procedure include provisions on interpreters and on Indigenous defendants, and the Supreme Court has issued protocols for judges handling cases involving Indigenous people. The National Institute of Indigenous Peoples, created in 2018, supports Indigenous rights policy. These measures address access, but their effectiveness is contested and depends on resources.

Documentary references. For the distinct releases and proceedings, see the Centro Prodh case chronology (2013), the Mexican Supreme Court’s April 28, 2010 release decision, and the federal prosecutor’s 2017 acknowledgment and apology. Government pages may restrict automated retrieval; the case chronology is also documented independently by the human-rights organization.

Comparative reflection helps. Mexico's constitution names Indigenous peoples as holders of public-law status and community jurisdiction, but it also places that jurisdiction within a framework of national law and human-rights limits, and it relies on legislation and judicial review to define boundaries, especially in cases involving violence against women, sanctions such as banishment or detention, and conflicts between community and state authorities. Those themes echo debates in the United States and Canada about the rights of defendants and victims in Indigenous justice systems, although the legal mechanisms differ. A historian should therefore compare the questions asked, not the legal form, and should avoid describing Mexico as either more or less advanced than its northern neighbors. For the broader history of Indigenous Mexico and the northern borderlands see Chapter 141.

Section 130.18

Canada's Truth and Reconciliation Calls to Action and the Indigenous Justice Strategy

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada's 2015 Calls to Action included a group of calls, numbered 25 to 42, on "Justice." In summary, they urged policies guaranteeing the RCMP's independence when the government has its own interest in a case (25); cultural competency training for lawyers and courses on Indigenous law in law schools (27–28); commitments to eliminate Indigenous overrepresentation in custody within a decade, with annual progress reports (30), and to eliminate it among youth (38); adequately funded community sanctions as alternatives to imprisonment (31); amendments allowing judges to depart from mandatory minimum sentences and restrictions on conditional sentences (32); attention to fetal alcohol spectrum disorder in prevention and justice reform (33–34); removing barriers to healing lodges (35) and expanding culturally relevant programming in institutions and halfway houses (36–37); a national plan to collect and publish data on Indigenous people in the criminal justice system (39); Indigenous-specific victim programs (40); a public inquiry into the disproportionate victimization of Indigenous women and girls (41), which led to the National Inquiry discussed in Chapter 129; and recognition and implementation of Indigenous justice systems consistent with treaty and Aboriginal rights, the Constitution Act, 1982, and the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (42). Chapter 127 treats the Commission's work on residential schools.

A decade after the report, the justice calls remain a benchmark. The ten-year period set in Call 30 elapsed in 2025 without the elimination of overrepresentation; the Office of the Correctional Investigator's 2025–26 report found that overrepresentation in federal corrections continued to rise. Independent trackers have documented uneven progress on the Calls overall. The assessment of any specific call depends on its terms: some calls, such as the inquiry into missing and murdered Indigenous women (41), were acted on; others, such as Call 32 on mandatory minimum penalties, were addressed only partially by later legislation; others, such as elimination of overrepresentation, remain unmet. Students should avoid summarizing the whole group as either fulfilled or ignored.

On March 10, 2025, Justice Canada released Canada's first federal Indigenous Justice Strategy, which it described as responding to systemic discrimination and overrepresentation. The strategy followed roughly two years of engagement, including funding of about $11 million to 38 Indigenous communities, organizations, and governments to conduct their own engagement, and co-development tables with the Assembly of First Nations, Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami and the Inuit Justice Working Group (comprising the Nunatsiavut Government, Makivvik, Nunavut Tunngavik Inc., and the Inuvialuit Regional Corporation), the Manitoba Métis Federation, and the Délı̨nę Got'ı̨nę Government. It is organized around seven themes and includes distinct chapters for First Nations, Inuit, and Métis, reflecting a distinctions-based approach in which each people sets priorities. It presents priority actions, reported by the Canadian Broadcasting Corporation as numbering twenty-six, including support for First Nations-, Inuit-, and Métis-led victim services; exploring Indigenous community justice centres; expanding Indigenous control of community-run alternatives before and after sentencing; hiring and funding Elders and knowledge keepers within justice institutions; cultural learning for justice practitioners; and governance, accountability, and data-sovereignty mechanisms. Implementation is to proceed through regional tables negotiated with Indigenous partners.

Evaluating the strategy requires distinguishing commitments from results. Many actions are phrased as commitments to "explore," "seek," or "work with," and their realization depends on funding, provincial and territorial cooperation (since the administration of justice is largely provincial), and Indigenous partners' priorities. Some elements build on existing programs, such as the Indigenous Justice Program and community courts; others, such as the integrated governance and reporting mechanisms and distinctions-based data approaches, are newer. The evidence needed to judge effectiveness includes published implementation plans, budget allocations, the establishment and work of regional tables, indicators and data reports, and changes in outcomes such as custody admissions and victim service access. As of the chapter's writing in late 2026, students should consult Justice Canada for reports on implementation and treat the existence of the strategy as a policy statement rather than as proof of change.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Imposed Criminal Justice to Selective Recognition of Indigenous Legal Authority

What changed

  • Criminal ceremonial bans ended (Canada 1951; U.S. policy shifts in the 1930s and 1978 legislation), and Indigenous governments gained roles in policing and courts through self-determination laws and agreements.
  • Statutes and courts recognized limited tribal criminal authority over non-Indians (2013, 2022), judicial doctrine required consideration of colonial history in sentencing (1999, 2012), and Mexico recognized communities as public-law subjects (2024).
  • Inquiries (1989, 1991, 1996, 2007) documented discrimination and recommended Indigenous-controlled justice.

What remained continuous

  • Dependence on outside governments for authority, funding, and detention; limits on tribal and community jurisdiction.
  • Overrepresentation in custody, with high pretrial detention and supervision-related returns.
  • Indigenous communities' own legal teachings and efforts to maintain or revive them.

When and how change occurred. Change was uneven and reversible: authority expanded and contracted in the United States; Canadian reforms came through sentencing law and policy more than through transfers of jurisdiction; Mexico moved by constitutional amendment. Who experienced it. Communities with capacity to prosecute and police benefited most from jurisdictional restoration; people in custody experienced continuity. Why it matters. The pattern shows selective recognition, not an uninterrupted advance of sovereignty.

Section 130.19

Justice Reform, Institutional Oversight, and Indigenous Public Safety

Reform proposals in both countries concentrate on a limited set of institutional levers. Police accountability includes independent investigations of serious incidents, civilian oversight with Indigenous participation, data collection on stops and use of force, and training. Several Canadian provinces have civilian-led investigative bodies for police-involved deaths and injuries, such as British Columbia's Independent Investigations Office (established in 2012), although their powers and Indigenous participation differ. Prosecutorial decision-making includes guidelines on charging, bail, and plea practices, Indigenous Crown and federal prosecutors, and, in the United States, transparency about declinations, which the Tribal Law and Order Act required the Department of Justice to report. Tribal court funding and capacity concerns judges, prosecutors, public defenders, probation, and detention, which the Indian Law and Order Commission found inadequately funded in federal appropriations; federal programs include grants through the Department of Justice and the VAWA reimbursement program. Access to legal assistance includes legal aid, court workers, interpreters, and Gladue services. Community-controlled policing concerns stable funding, recognition of Indigenous policing as an essential service, and governance. Corrections reforms address classification, release planning, and healing lodges, and judicial oversight concerns appellate review and judicial education.

The institutional arrangements for these reforms differ across countries. In the United States, tribal governments, the federal government, and, in some places, states share responsibilities for investigation and prosecution; federal responsibility is substantial because of the Major Crimes Act, and tribes rely on federal funding for police, courts, and detention, with the Bureau of Indian Affairs and the Department of Justice as principal sources. In Canada, the federal government funds and operates federal corrections and supports First Nations policing, provinces and territories control policing, prosecution of most offences, and provincial corrections, and Indigenous governments and organizations participate through agreements and programs but typically do not hold independent criminal jurisdiction. These structures shape what a reform can achieve. A federal strategy cannot by itself change provincial bail practices; a tribal court cannot exercise authority Congress has not recognized.

Recent oversight reports offer evidence but also illustrate the problem of measuring results. The Bureau of Justice Statistics' March 2026 report documents patterns in the federal system and notes that rates of AIAN arrests, pretrial detention, imprisonment, and return to prison reflect the structure of federal jurisdiction. The Office of the Correctional Investigator's 2025–26 report, a ten-year review of 365 recommendations, concluded that a persistent gap remains between policy commitments and what is experienced on the ground, citing continued overrepresentation, inequitable risk assessment and classification, and constrained access to healing lodges, and it recommended that CSC provide a measurable public action plan by December 31, 2026. Indigenous organizations and the federal government have pointed to progress in program design. A careful reader distinguishes measured institutional results (admissions, release rates, victim outcomes, response times, program completion) from official intentions (strategies, frameworks, and commitments), and notes when only the latter are available.

Public safety and Indigenous authority intersect in practice. Indigenous communities have consistently requested both more effective responses to violence and more control over those responses. In the United States, VAWA 2022's reimbursement program and Alaska pilot seek to provide resources and extend reach. In Canada, the essential-service approach to First Nations policing and the strategy's emphasis on community-run alternatives pursue the same goals. In neither country are these developments sufficient to reduce overrepresentation by themselves, and evaluating them requires attention to both safety for victims and fairness for the accused.

Section 130.20

Criminal Justice, Indigenous Sovereignty, and the Future of Legal Pluralism

The history traced in this chapter yields several findings. Criminal justice was a central instrument of colonial governance because it allocated coercive power. Indigenous nations maintained diverse legal orders before and during colonization, and those orders adapted and survived in various forms. The expansion of outside criminal jurisdiction, through courts, police, and statutes, displaced Indigenous authority while also introducing public-safety institutions on which many communities came to depend. In the United States, authority was divided among tribal, federal, and state governments in ways that created enforcement gaps and were repeatedly altered by Congress and the Supreme Court. In Canada, authority over policing, prosecution, and corrections remained in federal, provincial, and territorial hands, and reform concentrated on police accountability, sentencing law, and culturally adapted services. In Mexico, a constitutional framework recognizes community jurisdiction within national law.

Across these histories, a persistent distinction emerges. A state may reform the justice system it administers over Indigenous peoples, by reducing discrimination, improving access, funding services, and incorporating Indigenous knowledge. Or it may restore authority to Indigenous governments to make and administer law. The first is not trivial: reductions in discriminatory practices and improvements in safety are significant. The second changes who holds power. Many measures described in this chapter combine the two. A Gladue report improves how a state court sentences, but the court remains a state court; a healing lodge operated under section 81 involves Indigenous organizations but under federal authority; a tribal court exercising special tribal criminal jurisdiction acts on Indigenous authority recognized by Congress and conditioned on federal requirements; a Mexican community assembly acts within a constitution that also constrains it.

How might multiple legal systems coexist within one territory? Historically and comparatively, arrangements include allocation by person (Indigenous law for members), by subject matter (family and local disputes to community authorities, serious violence to state courts), by consent of the parties, by territory, or by concurrency with coordination. Each raises questions that reform must answer. Who decides which forum hears a case? How are defendants protected against unfair process, and by what standard? How are victims protected from pressure and ensured services? What review exists, and who pays for courts, police, detention, and counsel? How will data be collected and governed? Safeguards found in the cases above include procedural guarantees and federal review in the VAWA framework, statutory duties to consider systemic factors in Canadian sentencing, independent investigation and inquiry mechanisms, and civilian and Indigenous oversight bodies. Evidence on how well these work remains incomplete.

Nor should the chapter be read as proposing a single model. Indigenous nations differ in size, resources, legal tradition, and priorities, and some prefer to build their own courts and police while others seek stronger partnerships with state authorities or primarily want effective protection. The historically important points are the importance of Indigenous authority, the need for community safety, the commitment to equal legal protection, and the importance of measurable accountability. These are not competing values. Sovereignty without capacity may leave communities unprotected, capacity without authority may reproduce dependence, and both without rights protections risk injustice for defendants and victims alike.

The final question is whether modern states are merely modifying colonial justice arrangements or enabling durable Indigenous control over legal institutions. The record to 2026 supports a cautious answer. In several respects, authority has been recognized and expanded: tribal sentencing powers, special tribal criminal jurisdiction, constitutional recognition in Mexico, and distinctions-based strategies in Canada. In others, authority has been redistributed to states, constrained by funding, or left dependent on discretionary agreements. Overrepresentation in custody persists, and oversight bodies describe a gap between policy and practice. The answer therefore depends on which institutions, in which places, are examined, and on whether future developments transfer decision-making power, resources, and accountability to Indigenous governments, a matter taken up again in Chapters 139 and 148.

Historical Debate

Should Indigenous Governments Exercise Primary Criminal Justice Authority Within Their Territories?

The question: Should Indigenous governments exercise primary criminal justice authority within their territories, and what institutional arrangements best reconcile that authority with individual rights, victim protection, and the responsibilities of federal, state, provincial, and national governments? The debate involves constitutional power, public-safety capacity, procedural fairness, and sovereignty, which should be kept distinct.

Interpretation A — Primary Indigenous authority: Advocates argue that criminal jurisdiction is an inherent attribute of nationhood that was never validly extinguished, that treaties and the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (which affirms the right to maintain juridical systems in accordance with international human rights standards) support it, that Indigenous governments are best placed to protect their communities and to design culturally grounded accountability, and that outside systems have failed on both safety and fairness.
Interpretation B — Shared authority with strong safeguards: Others argue that criminal power should be exercised concurrently with, or subject to review by, state, provincial, or federal institutions that guarantee uniform individual rights, that capacity gaps in courts, detention, and counsel would endanger both victims and defendants if responsibility shifted quickly, and that residents who are not citizens of a nation have legitimate concerns about being tried by governments in which they have no vote.
Interpretation C — Differentiated, nation-specific arrangements: A third view holds that no single allocation fits all nations; arrangements should be negotiated by nations according to their capacity and priorities, ranging from full tribal courts to cooperative agreements with outside police and courts, with independent monitoring.

Evidence: The evidence includes statutory and judicial history (the effect of Oliphant, VAWA, and Castro-Huerta), reports on declination and enforcement gaps, tribal court practice, Canadian inquiries and statistics, and Mexican community justice experience. Much of it is incomplete: systematic evaluation of outcomes in tribal courts, community justice programs, and special jurisdiction is limited.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns constitutional interpretation (is authority inherent or delegated?), terminology (what "sovereignty" implies for non-members), scale (how many nations have capacity), and evidence (what results show).

Why it matters: The answer affects who prosecutes crimes against Indigenous people, which rights defendants hold, and whether reform is understood as improving a system or transferring power.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that criminal justice became an instrument of colonial authority because it determined who could define crimes, enforce laws, conduct trials, and impose punishment, and that those powers were frequently transferred from Indigenous governments to colonial, federal, provincial, or state authorities. Indigenous peoples challenged that displacement through resistance, litigation, institutional reform, tribal courts and police, and the revitalization of their legal orders.

Division I established that Indigenous legal traditions were diverse, grounded in kinship, compensation, restoration, and also sanction, and that they differ from modern restorative programs. Colonial law then displaced them through measures such as the Courts of Indian Offenses, the Major Crimes Act, and the Indian Act, including bans on ceremonies, and through police institutions that served both control and public safety. The historical problem of unequal administration, including over-enforcement and under-protection, requires careful measurement.

Division II showed that United States criminal jurisdiction depends on location, the status of the accused and victim, the offense, and statute, and that Public Law 280 created further variation. Tribal authority was limited by the Indian Civil Rights Act, Oliphant, and Duro, partly restored by Congress in 1991, and expanded in sentencing by the Tribal Law and Order Act. VAWA in 2013 and 2022 recognized special tribal criminal jurisdiction over certain non-Indian offenders, with procedural requirements. McGirt confirmed the Creek Reservation's continued existence for Major Crimes Act purposes, while Castro-Huerta recognized concurrent state jurisdiction over non-Indian offenders against Indian victims; neither alone establishes tribal jurisdiction over every offender.

Division III turned to Canada, where policing is largely provincial, Indigenous policing depends on negotiated agreements under the 1991 First Nations Policing Policy, and public inquiries have documented failures of accountability. Ipperwash demonstrated the consequences of a land dispute treated as a policing problem and led to findings on police independence. Gladue and Ipeelee created a sentencing framework requiring attention to colonial and systemic background factors without creating an automatic discount. The Marshall case showed how wrongful conviction reflected systemic failure and why inquiries do not guarantee change.

Division IV examined correctional consequences. Statistics show overrepresentation in federal corrections in Canada, including approximately 28.6 percent of persons under federal correctional jurisdiction at the end of fiscal year 2025–2026, and a distinct pattern in the United States shaped by federal jurisdiction, but figures must be compared only with attention to population, year, and measure. Women, youth, and people in remote communities experience particular harms. Community justice, peacemaking, Indigenous courts, and healing lodges offer alternatives, but they differ in source of authority and in their capacity to affect the wider causes of overrepresentation.

Division V compared Mexico's constitutional recognition of community jurisdiction, strengthened by the reform in force from October 1, 2024, with Canada's Calls to Action and 2025 Indigenous Justice Strategy and with American tribal institutions. Mexico's framework is distinct and regionally varied, and cannot be equated with U.S. or Canadian arrangements. Canadian policy commitments must be tested against implementation evidence.

Across the chapter, several continuities appear: reliance on outside governments for authority and resources, persistent gaps between legal recognition and practical capacity, and the continuing importance of Indigenous legal teachings and community leadership. Several changes also appear: the movement from outright prohibition of Indigenous practices to selective recognition, the creation of Indigenous-led institutions, and the emergence of oversight and data that make disparities visible.

The chapter also emphasized that sovereignty and effective public safety are complementary objectives, and that victims and defendants must both remain visible. Discriminatory policing and incarceration do not negate the right of Indigenous victims to protection, and concern about violence does not remove the requirement of fair trials and lawful sentencing.

The central analytical distinction is between reforming a justice system administered over Indigenous peoples and restoring meaningful Indigenous authority to govern justice. Many initiatives combine the two, and the balance varies by country, nation, and institution. No single arrangement suits every Indigenous nation.

For these reasons, the chapter treats the present not as the culmination of a steady advance toward sovereignty but as a contested and incomplete process. The conditions that will determine its direction, including jurisdictional clarity, resources, accountability, and Indigenous participation, are taken up in the sovereignty chapters that follow.

Key Terms

Indian country
In U.S. federal law (18 U.S.C. §1151), reservations, dependent Indian communities, and Indian allotments; the geographic basis of much federal and tribal criminal jurisdiction.
Criminal jurisdiction
A government's authority to define, investigate, prosecute, adjudicate, and punish offenses, dependent on place, person, subject matter, and sentencing power.
Concurrent jurisdiction
Authority shared by two or more governments to prosecute the same conduct.
General Crimes Act
An 1817 U.S. statute extending federal criminal law to Indian country for offenses involving non-Indians, with exceptions for Indian-on-Indian crime.
Major Crimes Act
An 1885 statute giving federal courts jurisdiction over enumerated serious offenses by Indians in Indian country; now fifteen categories.
Public Law 280
A 1953 statute conferring criminal and some civil jurisdiction on certain states over Indian country, with mandatory and optional states and later provisions for consent and retrocession.
Courts of Indian Offenses
Reservation courts created in 1883 by the Department of the Interior, with a code criminalizing ceremonies and customs; some successor "CFR courts" remain.
Indian Civil Rights Act (1968)
U.S. statute applying many individual-rights protections to tribal governments and limiting tribal criminal sentences, later amended.
Implicit divestiture
The doctrine, applied in Oliphant, that tribes lost some inherent powers by virtue of their dependent status unless Congress affirms them.
Dual (separate) sovereignty
The principle that prosecutions by different sovereigns, such as a tribe and the United States, do not violate double jeopardy.
Tribal Law and Order Act (2010)
U.S. statute that increased permissible tribal sentences when safeguards are met, required reporting of federal declinations, and strengthened coordination.
Special tribal criminal jurisdiction (STCJ)
Tribal authority, recognized in VAWA 2013 and expanded in 2022 (25 U.S.C. §1304), over specified non-Indian offenders for covered crimes, subject to procedural protections.
Declination
A decision by a prosecutor not to pursue a case, important in U.S. Indian country because federal prosecutors handle many serious offenses.
First Nations Policing Policy
Canada's 1991 program funding policing agreements among the federal government, provinces or territories, and First Nations.
Police independence
The principle that police exercise investigative and enforcement discretion free from political direction in individual cases.
Section 718.2(e)
The Criminal Code provision directing courts to consider alternatives to imprisonment, with particular attention to the circumstances of Aboriginal offenders.
Gladue report
A pre-sentence document describing an Indigenous offender's background, community, and available alternatives, to assist courts applying the Gladue principles.
Proportionality
The principle that a sentence must be proportionate to the gravity of the offence and the offender's degree of responsibility.
Overrepresentation
A group's share of a justice or correctional population that exceeds its share of the general population; it must be specified by stage and measure.
Admission to custody
A count of entries into custody or supervision, which can exceed the number of individuals because people may be admitted multiple times.
Healing lodge
A correctional facility or program, federally operated or run under agreement by an Indigenous organization, incorporating Indigenous culture and Elders.
Section 81 agreement
An agreement under Canada's Corrections and Conditional Release Act allowing an Indigenous community or organization to provide correctional services.
Circle sentencing
A sentencing process, associated in Canada with R. v. Moses (1992), in which community members, victims, and offenders participate in discussion with the court.
Peacemaking
A process in which community members facilitate dispute resolution, exemplified by Navajo Nation peacemaking.
Restorative justice
A contemporary approach emphasizing repair of harm and participation by victims, offenders, and communities; distinct from Indigenous legal orders.
Legal pluralism
The coexistence of more than one legal system within the same territory or population.
Sistemas normativos
In Mexico, the internal normative systems of Indigenous communities recognized in Article 2 of the Constitution, historically called usos y costumbres.
Sujetos de derecho público
"Subjects of public law," the status given to Indigenous and Afro-Mexican peoples and communities by the 2024 reform of Article 2.
Distinctions-based approach
A Canadian policy approach that treats First Nations, Inuit, and Métis as distinct peoples with their own priorities.

This project asks you to build a dossier on how criminal justice authority operates in one Indigenous nation or region, drawing on library, government, and Indigenous community sources.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that law, police, and prisons are not neutral background to Indigenous history but arenas in which sovereignty was contested. The transfer of criminal authority from Indigenous governments to outside states was among the most consequential effects of colonization, and its legacy shapes who responds to a call for help on a reservation, how a judge sentences an Indigenous defendant, and who is in custody.

The chapter also reveals a pattern that recurs in later chapters on sovereignty: recognition is often partial, conditional, and dependent on resources. Tribal courts can try some non-Indians, but only for listed crimes and with prescribed protections; Canadian courts must consider colonial history, but sentencing cannot alone change who is arrested; Mexico recognizes community jurisdiction, but the effect depends on implementing law and practice. Understanding these conditions helps explain why movements for self-determination have focused on capacity as well as principle.

The history connects earlier chapters, from the Indian Act and the reservation order to boarding and residential schools, to the contemporary issues of missing and murdered Indigenous women, child welfare, land defense, and the international recognition of Indigenous rights. It also links Canada, the United States, and Mexico without equating them, showing that comparison is most useful when it asks similar questions of different legal systems.

Finally, the chapter leaves students with a standard for judging change: whether reforms improve the safety of Indigenous communities, protect the rights of victims and defendants, and transfer real decision-making power to Indigenous governments, and whether independent evidence shows that they do. That standard will remain relevant as the chapters on contemporary governments and the future of sovereignty examine how Indigenous nations continue to shape the legal institutions that govern them.
