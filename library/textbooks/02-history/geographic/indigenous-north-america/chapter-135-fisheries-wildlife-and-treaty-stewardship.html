Chapter Opening

In March 1957, the gates of The Dalles Dam closed on the Columbia River, and the rising reservoir submerged Celilo Falls within hours. For a very long time the falls had been one of the great fishing and trading places of the continent. Almost seventy years later, in 2024, the last of four dams on the Klamath River was removed, and salmon began to move into reaches that had been closed to them for a century. Between those two dates lies a history of loss, litigation, institution-building, and recovery.

Celilo Falls was not simply a good fishing site. Archaeological evidence indicates that people used the falls over many millennia, and by the historic period the surrounding stretch of the Columbia had become a seasonal gathering and trading center where peoples from the Plateau, the Columbia Gorge, and beyond met to fish, exchange dried salmon and other goods, and renew relationships. Its submergence was the culmination of a federal program of river development in which Indigenous nations were consulted weakly, if at all, and compensated in cash for something that cash could not replace. The Yakama, Umatilla, Warm Springs, and Nez Perce peoples had reserved the right to fish at their "usual and accustomed places" in the treaties of 1855. In 1957 the place itself disappeared beneath the water.

The Klamath River story in 2024 appears at first to run in the opposite direction. The removal of the lower Klamath dams was the largest project of its kind in the United States, and it followed more than two decades of organizing by the Yurok, Karuk, Hoopa Valley, and Klamath nations, whose members had watched fish kills, toxic algal blooms, and declining runs. But the parallel is closer than it looks. In both cases the central question was not only how many fish returned. It was who had the authority to decide how a river would be used, who would bear the costs of those decisions, and whether Indigenous nations would be treated as governments with rights or as stakeholders with opinions.

This chapter argues that the restoration of Indigenous authority over fisheries, wildlife, and habitats is both an ecological recovery process and an exercise of Indigenous sovereignty. It follows three living resources through the continent as principal case studies: salmon in the rivers and coasts of the Pacific Northwest, buffalo (bison) on the Great Plains, and caribou in the Arctic and Subarctic. It also examines Great Lakes fisheries, Atlantic lobster and eel fisheries, Arctic marine mammals, and the Cucapá fishing communities of the Colorado River Delta in Mexico, so that the chapter preserves the continental scope of the textbook rather than reproducing the borders of any one country.

The chapter must be read with two cautions. First, Indigenous harvesting systems were diverse, historically specific, and sometimes limited by ecology and circumstance. They are not best understood as a single timeless "Indigenous conservation ethic," and Indigenous governments today face genuinely difficult choices among immediate food needs, economic pressures, and long-term recovery. Second, restoration is neither inevitable nor complete. Many salmon runs remain depleted, buffalo herds occupy a fraction of the land they once did, and several caribou herds have declined sharply. This chapter distinguishes objectives from measured outcomes throughout.

Much of the modern history here turns on the institutional translation of rights into authority. A treaty clause reserving the right to fish does not by itself produce a fisheries biologist, a harvest-monitoring program, an enforcement officer, or a seat at the table where allocation decisions are made. Following the Belloni decision of 1969 and the Boldt decision of 1974, the Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission (1977), the Great Lakes Indian Fish and Wildlife Commission (1984), the InterTribal Buffalo Council (1992), and the wildlife management institutions created under the Nunavut Agreement (1993) all represented efforts to build the administrative capacity that makes legal recognition operational. The legal struggles over fishing rights are treated in Chapters 106 and 113; here the emphasis falls on what Indigenous nations built once rights were recognized.

Historians draw on an unusually varied evidentiary base for this subject: treaty texts and court records; reports and monitoring data produced by tribal natural resources departments, intertribal commissions, and wildlife management boards; government fisheries and wildlife surveys; archaeological and ethnographic evidence for earlier harvesting systems; oral histories and community knowledge; and published ethnographic research, as in the case of the Cucapá. Each has limits. Government population estimates for historical animals are uncertain, treaty texts survive in the languages and understandings of negotiators who did not always share them, and contemporary ecological outcomes are still unfolding. The chapter notes these limits where they matter.

Chapter 133 examined the knowledge systems that underlie Indigenous environmental stewardship, and Chapter 134 examined fire and landscape management. This chapter extends the discussion from landscapes to the living populations they sustain, examining the management, restoration, and governance of fisheries, wildlife, and habitats. It is organized in five divisions: historical foundations; fisheries and aquatic ecosystems; wildlife restoration; regional diversity and contemporary management; and the institutions, science, and political questions that will shape the future of stewardship. Chapter 136 turns next to industrial extraction, which has often been the principal threat to the resources discussed here.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how salmon, bison, caribou, and other living resources sustained Indigenous economic, cultural, and political systems rather than merely supplying food.
  2. Describe the customary institutions through which Indigenous societies governed harvesting and access, and distinguish them from modern government-administered wildlife conservation.
  3. Analyze how commercial exploitation, dispossession, river modification, and state regulation disrupted Indigenous resource systems and weakened Indigenous economic independence.
  4. Distinguish among inherent sovereignty, reserved treaty rights, Aboriginal and treaty rights in Canada, land-claim agreements, and Mexico's distinct legal framework.
  5. Trace the development of treaty fisheries management in the Pacific Northwest from the Belloni and Boldt decisions to the Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission and the Northwest Indian Fisheries Commission.
  6. Assess the Klamath River dam removals as an example of Indigenous-led restoration, distinguishing the completion of removal from the longer process of ecological recovery.
  7. Interpret the Marshall decisions of 1999 and explain why a treaty right to a moderate livelihood differs from an unrestricted commercial entitlement.
  8. Evaluate buffalo restoration as a cultural, ecological, economic, and political project, and compare conservation, commercial, and culturally oriented herds.
  9. Analyze the Porcupine caribou agreements as an example of transboundary governance, distinguishing advisory, regulatory, and harvesting authority.
  10. Compare wildlife governance under the Nunavut Agreement, the Great Lakes treaty commissions, Alaska Native co-management arrangements, and Cucapá fishing conflicts in Mexico.
  11. Evaluate how Indigenous knowledge and contemporary ecological monitoring interact in fisheries and wildlife management, including questions of data governance.
  12. Assess competing interpretations of co-management and explain under what conditions it strengthens or constrains Indigenous sovereignty.

Historical Context

The living resources discussed in this chapter are not distributed evenly across the continent, and that unevenness shaped Indigenous history. Most Pacific salmon populations are anadromous: they hatch in freshwater, migrate to the ocean, and return as adults to spawn, so that enormous quantities of fish arrive in particular rivers within narrow seasons. Plains bison lived in large herds on grasslands and moved in response to forage and weather. Barren-ground caribou migrate across tundra and northern forest between calving grounds and wintering ranges, and different herds have distinct ranges and population histories. Great Lakes fish, marine mammals, shellfish, deer, moose, waterfowl, and wild rice supported other seasonal economies. Chapters 7 through 12 introduced the long deep-time histories of these adaptations, and Chapter 13 and later chapters examined how agricultural, urban, and ceremonial societies built on them.

Colonization altered both the resources and the rules governing access to them. The commercial fur, hide, and fish trades, the spread of settler agriculture, the canning industry, river engineering, and the creation of parks, game laws, and fisheries regulations transformed ecosystems and displaced Indigenous harvesters. Conservation law itself became an instrument of exclusion in many places: regulations that limited Indigenous hunting and fishing were often enforced more strictly against Indigenous people than against commercial or sport harvesters, even where industrial activity was the larger cause of decline. Chapter 81 examined the destruction of the buffalo economy, and Chapters 85 and 86 examined Canadian and American administrative systems that reduced Indigenous economic autonomy. Those histories form the background of this chapter.

The legal terrain is not uniform. In the United States, treaties are understood in federal law as reservations of rights by tribal nations, and the Supreme Court has long held that treaty fishing and hunting rights survive even when exercised off reservation. In Canada, existing Aboriginal and treaty rights are recognized and affirmed by section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982, and modern land-claim agreements create their own management regimes. Mexico's constitution and agrarian system recognize Indigenous peoples and communal land tenure through different mechanisms, and Greenland's self-government arrangements place authority over living resources within a distinct political structure. This textbook does not treat any one of these frameworks as the template for the others, a commitment established in Chapter 1.

The period examined most closely here begins in the 1970s and extends to the present, an era in which self-determination policies, new court decisions, and modern land-claim agreements opened space for Indigenous nations to administer their own resource programs. It was also an era of accelerating ecological stress, including declining salmon runs, shrinking caribou herds, new industrial pressure on habitats, and a changing climate, treated comprehensively in Chapter 138. Students should therefore keep two histories in view: a history of legal and institutional achievement, and a continuing history of ecological uncertainty.

Core Vocabulary Preview

The following terms appear throughout this chapter. Several have different legal meanings in different jurisdictions, so students should note the jurisdiction attached to each definition. Ten further terms are defined in the Key Terms section at the end of the chapter.

Environmental stewardship
Ongoing practices of care, restraint, and responsibility toward land, waters, and other beings; Indigenous formulations vary by nation and are not exact equivalents of the English word.
Reserved treaty rights
In U.S. law, rights that a nation kept for itself in a treaty rather than received as a grant, such as the right to fish at usual and accustomed places.
Aboriginal rights
In Canadian law, rights arising from practices integral to a distinctive Indigenous culture before European contact (a different historical test applies to Métis rights), recognized by section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982.
Usufructuary rights
Rights to use or take resources, such as fish or game, from lands or waters that others may own, without owning the resource base itself.
Subsistence harvesting
Harvesting for food, material, ceremonial, and community needs, including sharing, rather than primarily for sale.
Commercial harvesting
Harvesting for sale in markets, which may be treaty-protected in some jurisdictions and heavily regulated in all.
Moderate livelihood
The Canadian treaty concept that harvesting may secure necessaries such as food, clothing, and housing, with some amenities, but not the open-ended accumulation of wealth.
Food sovereignty
The capacity of a people to define and control its own food systems, including the species, places, and practices on which it depends.
Fisheries management
The monitoring, regulation, and enhancement of fish populations and harvests, including research, allocation, enforcement, and habitat work.
Harvest allocation
The division of an allowable harvest among user groups, such as treaty tribes, commercial fleets, and recreational anglers.
Co-management
Formal arrangements in which governments and Indigenous institutions share responsibility for management, varying widely in who actually holds decision-making authority.
Adaptive management
Management that treats decisions as hypotheses, monitors outcomes, and revises practice as information accumulates.
Watershed
The land area that drains into a river or water body, the ecological unit within which fish habitat and water quality are shaped.
Anadromous fish
Fish, such as salmon, steelhead, and lamprey, that migrate from the sea to freshwater to spawn.
Spawning grounds
Stream, river, or lake habitats, often gravel beds with specific temperature and flow, where fish deposit eggs.
Fish passage
Structures and conditions, such as ladders, bypasses, and unobstructed channels, that allow fish to move past barriers.
Hatchery
A facility that spawns and rears fish for release; hatcheries can supplement harvests but raise questions about wild-fish genetics and habitat.
Dam removal
The demolition or breaching of dams to restore river flow and fish access, followed by years of physical and biological adjustment.
Conservation herd
A bison herd managed principally for the persistence of the species, its genetics, and its ecological role rather than for meat production.
Calving grounds
Areas to which migratory caribou return annually to give birth, a critical habitat in herd survival.
Migration corridor
A route along which animals regularly move between seasonal habitats and which can be broken by development or fencing.
Transboundary governance
Arrangements for managing resources, such as migratory herds, that cross political boundaries.
Habitat fragmentation
The division of continuous habitat by roads, dams, fences, or development into smaller, disconnected areas.
Ecological restoration
Assisting the recovery of an ecosystem's structure and function; it is a process with uncertain outcomes, not a return to a fixed past condition.
Population monitoring
Systematic counting, sampling, and observation of animal populations over time through surveys, tagging, harvest records, and community observation.

Division I — Historical Foundations of Indigenous Stewardship

Section 135.1

Wildlife, Fisheries, and Indigenous Civilization

Living resources were the foundation of Indigenous economies across most of the continent, but they were never only a source of calories. Salmon, buffalo, caribou, whitefish, seals, and wild rice organized the calendar, determined where people lived, shaped who worked together, and supplied the materials of clothing, shelter, tools, and ceremony. To say that these resources sustained political systems is to make a specific claim: the ability to harvest, preserve, store, and distribute a seasonal surplus allowed communities to support larger populations, ranked households, specialized labor, and diplomacy, and the rules governing access to harvests became part of how authority was exercised.

Salmon-centered societies of the Pacific Northwest illustrate the point. Because salmon return to rivers in concentrated runs, communities from the Columbia Plateau to the Northwest Coast and the Klamath Basin built weirs, traps, platforms, dip-net and reef-net fisheries, and drying and smoking technologies that converted a brief abundance into food that lasted through the year. Stored salmon could also be traded, and the Columbia River's fishing places, especially Celilo Falls, became centers of regional exchange. The settled villages, social ranking, and ceremonial exchange of the Northwest Coast, discussed in Chapters 10 and 33, rested on this seasonal productivity without agriculture.

On the Great Plains, bison supplied meat, hides, bone, sinew, and fuel. Communal drives into pounds and over jumps, and later mounted hunting, required cooperation and leadership, and dried meat and pemmican allowed storage and trade, a role that later made the northern Plains buffalo economy a foundation of the Métis and fur-trade world (Chapter 52). In the Arctic and Subarctic, migratory caribou provided food and, in the form of hides, some of the warmest clothing available. Inuit, Dene, Gwich'in, Cree, Innu, and other peoples organized seasonal movements, communal hunts, and sharing networks around herds. In the Great Lakes, lake sturgeon, whitefish, lake trout, walleye, and wild rice (manoomin) supported Anishinaabe and other communities, while marine economies on all coasts relied on shellfish, seals, whales, and fish.

Relationships with animals and fish were also moral and spiritual relationships. Many peoples held first-salmon or first-fruits ceremonies that acknowledged returning fish as participants in a reciprocal relationship, and many described animals as persons with whom humans had obligations. These beliefs differed from nation to nation and should not be reduced to a single "Indigenous spirituality." They mattered historically because they gave rules of conduct, such as restraint, proper handling, sharing, and respect, a place in religious life and in community discipline. Obligations to kin and community, rather than market incentives alone, shaped who harvested, how much, and what was shared.

Historical Vocabulary
Stewardship

Definition. In this chapter, stewardship refers to ongoing practices and obligations of care, restraint, and responsibility toward land, waters, and the living beings that depend on them, as understood and enacted by particular Indigenous communities.

Historical context. "Stewardship" is an English word with its own history, and it does not translate directly into Indigenous languages. Many nations have their own concepts of reciprocal obligation, such as the Mi'kmaw concept of netukulimk, which refers to using resources in ways that sustain both community well-being and the integrity of the environment. These concepts are tied to specific places, languages, and legal traditions.

Why it matters. Stewardship in Indigenous practice was embedded in kinship, ceremony, and customary law rather than in a separate government bureaucracy, and it persists in contemporary Indigenous resource institutions.

Potential misunderstanding. The term can suggest that humans own or manage nature from above, or that Indigenous peoples never harmed their environments. Neither is accurate. Stewardship describes responsibilities and practices, and historians must assess outcomes with evidence in each case.

Section 135.2

Indigenous Harvesting Systems and Environmental Governance

Indigenous societies governed harvesting through institutions that varied as widely as their economies. Among Northwest Coast peoples such as the Gitxsan and Wet'suwet'en, hereditary house groups held rights and responsibilities over specific fishing and hunting territories, validated in feasts. Coast Salish and Columbia River families held rights to particular fishing sites, and among the Yurok and neighboring peoples of the Klamath, families and communities built and maintained seasonal fishing weirs. In many places ceremonial leaders opened the season, and fish were not taken in quantity until first-run fish had been honored and allowed to pass upriver.

On the Plains, collective hunts required discipline, because a premature charge could scatter a herd and endanger the community's winter food. Societies with policing responsibilities, such as the Lakota akicita and comparable Cheyenne soldier societies, enforced rules during communal hunts and could punish violations. In the Subarctic and the Arctic, hunting groups used knowledge of herd movements, communal drive lanes, and caribou crossings, while community norms of sharing distributed meat widely so that success in the hunt benefited households beyond the hunter's. Among Great Lakes Anishinaabe communities, harvesting wild rice and fishing were guided by community knowledge of when and where harvests could proceed, and in some communities designated leaders helped decide when rice beds were ready.

Knowledge made these systems work. Harvesters observed run timing, animal condition, weather, ice, water level, plant phenology, and predator behavior, and transmitted this knowledge across generations through apprenticeship, story, and practice. Chapter 133 examines these knowledge systems in detail. The relevant point here is that management decisions, such as when to fish, which fish to let pass, which sites to rest, or how to distribute a hunt, were informed by close observation of local conditions and enforced through social obligation, kinship, and customary law.

Historians should not overstate the case. Indigenous practices of restraint were real, but so was variation in effectiveness, and archaeological evidence documents local depletion of some resources in some places and periods, while debates continue about the causes of particular declines. Some systems, such as family hunting territories in parts of the Subarctic, have been the subject of long scholarly argument over how far they predated the fur trade and how far they were reshaped by it. The accurate claim is that Indigenous nations maintained long-lived governance systems for living resources, and that many of the key features of those systems, including local control, seasonal restraint, sharing, and intergenerational knowledge, differ in kind from the centralized, rule-based wildlife administration that governments later imposed.

That difference is the key conceptual distinction for this chapter. Modern government conservation systems generally separate wildlife management from social and political life: they rely on codified regulations, licensing, enforcement agencies, and scientific bureaucracies, and they treat harvesters as users to be regulated. Indigenous governance, by contrast, embedded management in relationships among people and between people and other beings. The contemporary institutions examined later in this chapter often combine the two, using biologists and enforcement officers alongside elder councils, ceremonies, and customary norms.

Section 135.3

Colonial Expansion and the Disruption of Living Resources

Colonial expansion changed Indigenous relationships with fish and wildlife through several overlapping processes. Commercial hunting and fishing converted animals and fish into commodities for distant markets. The Columbia River's first salmon cannery opened in the 1860s, and industrial fishing with traps, fish wheels, and nets, followed by the growth of the canning industry, harvested salmon at scales that Indigenous fisheries had not approached. The Plains buffalo were reduced from a vast population, estimated in the tens of millions though with significant uncertainty, to a few hundred wild animals in the United States by the late 1880s through commercial hide hunting, military policy, and the pressures described in Chapter 81. Agricultural settlement, logging, mining, and municipal development degraded rivers and grasslands, and by the twentieth century hydropower dams, irrigation diversions, and flood-control works altered river flow, temperature, and fish access.

Dispossession and regulation compounded ecological loss. Treaties and statutes that shrank Indigenous land bases left communities with reduced access to hunting and fishing territory. Governments then created game laws, fisheries regulations, and protected areas that restricted Indigenous harvesting. In Canada, federal fisheries regulation in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries restricted Indigenous access to commercial fisheries in many regions and institutionalized a separate food fishery, and national and provincial parks closed traditional hunting grounds. The Migratory Birds Convention of 1916 between Canada and the United States prohibited spring hunting that had long been central to northern Indigenous communities, a restriction addressed only later by amending protocols. In the United States, states often applied hunting and fishing laws to treaty harvesters and arrested those who fished at traditional places, producing the confrontations treated in Chapter 113.

The consequences extended beyond subsistence. Where Indigenous communities could no longer rely on their own fisheries and hunting, they became more dependent on rations, wage labor, and government programs. The weakening of an economic base undermined political independence, a process visible across the reservation and reserve systems discussed in Chapters 84 through 86. Dietary change also followed: tribal health researchers link the loss of traditional foods to higher rates of diet-related disease, though causation is complex and involves many factors. In cultural terms, interrupted harvests meant interrupted ceremonies, language use tied to fishing and hunting, and the transmission of knowledge to younger generations.

It is important to avoid reading the story solely as destruction. Indigenous nations contested these changes through petitions, litigation, protest, negotiation, and the maintenance of harvesting by whatever means were available, sometimes in defiance of law. Some turned their knowledge to wage labor in the new fishing industries, and some Indigenous individuals preserved wildlife, as in the case of the Pend d'Oreille man Samuel Walking Coyote, who is credited in documentary accounts with capturing bison calves in the 1870s from which a surviving herd descended. These actions kept alive the resources and the claims on which later restoration depended.

Weakened economic and political independence, health and cultural effects, and later legal and institutional mobilization to restore rights and resources.

Historical complexity: These effects did not occur at the same time or to the same degree everywhere. Some Arctic and Subarctic peoples retained substantial control of their harvests into the twentieth century, while Plains and Pacific Northwest communities experienced severe disruption earlier. Ecological decline also had several causes, and distinguishing the weight of commercial harvest, habitat loss, and regulation requires species-by-species evidence.

Section 135.4

Treaties, Harvesting Rights, and Environmental Sovereignty

The legal structure of Indigenous harvesting rights rests on several distinct foundations that should not be conflated. The first is inherent sovereignty: the authority Indigenous nations held before colonial states existed and, in their own understanding, continue to hold. The second is treaty rights, the specific rights recognized or reserved in negotiated agreements. The third, in Canada, is Aboriginal rights, protected by section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982. The fourth is modern land-claim agreements, which define harvesting and management arrangements in detail. Each carries different consequences for who may harvest, where, how, and under whose regulatory authority.

In the United States, the Supreme Court's decision in United States v. Winans (1905) described a treaty as not a grant of rights to the Indians but a grant of rights from them, a reservation of those they had not granted. That reasoning supports treaty fishing at "usual and accustomed places" off reservation, including on lands that later passed to others, and the characterization of such rights as usufructuary, meaning rights to use and take resources rather than to own the land. Courts have held that states may regulate treaty fishing only to the extent reasonable and necessary for conservation and without discriminating against treaty fishers, a standard articulated in the Puyallup cases of 1968 and 1973. This is the framework within which the Boldt decision, examined in Section 135.6, arose.

In Canada, the Supreme Court in R. v. Sparrow (1990) held that Aboriginal rights existing in 1982 are constitutionally protected and that government infringement must be justified, with a priority for Indigenous food, social, and ceremonial fishing after conservation. Later decisions, including R. v. Van der Peet (1996), defined Aboriginal rights as practices integral to the distinctive culture of the nation, and the Marshall decisions of 1999, examined in Section 135.8, interpreted treaty rights arising from the Peace and Friendship treaties of the eighteenth century. On the Prairies, the Natural Resources Transfer Agreements of 1930 and the Numbered Treaties, discussed in Chapter 82, address hunting and fishing in different terms. Modern comprehensive agreements, from the James Bay and Northern Québec Agreement of 1975 to the Nunavut Agreement, establish their own management bodies. Chapter 117 treats Canadian constitutional politics in detail.

Mexico requires a distinct analysis. Mexico's constitution recognizes the pluricultural composition of the nation and, following reforms in 2001 and 2024, Indigenous self-determination, autonomy, and legal personality, while natural resource law is shaped by federal fisheries and environmental statutes, protected areas, and a long agrarian tradition of ejido and communal land tenure. Mexico also ratified ILO Convention 169 in 1990. Treaty-based reserved rights of the U.S. type are not the primary framework, and the Cucapá case in Section 135.15 shows how rights are contested through environmental regulation and permitting. Greenland's self-government since 2009 places authority over living resources within its own governing institutions, with Kalaallit and other Inuit forming the great majority of the population.

Two analytical points frame the rest of the chapter. First, the right to harvest is not meaningful if the resource is gone or the habitat inaccessible. A court, in the Washington culvert litigation that reached the Supreme Court in 2018, has had to consider whether the treaty fishing right implies an obligation not to block salmon from reaching spawning habitat, and the Court's equally divided affirmance left the Ninth Circuit's holding in place. The case illustrates why treaty rights and habitat protection are intertwined. Second, treaty rights are not governmental gifts and are not unlimited. They are rights held by nations that exist alongside conservation regulation, and the legal question is generally one of the extent and justification of limits, not of whether the rights exist.

Historical Vocabulary
Reserved Treaty Rights

Definition. In United States law, reserved treaty rights are rights that a tribal nation retained for itself when it ceded land or other interests in a treaty, such as rights to hunt, fish, and gather.

Historical context. The Stevens treaties of 1854–1855 in Washington, Oregon, and Idaho Territories guaranteed the right of taking fish at all usual and accustomed places, in common with citizens of the territory. Native negotiators often emphasized fishing as non-negotiable, and later courts held that treaties must be read as the Indians would have understood them.

Why it matters. The reservation framing places the source of the right in the nation itself, not in government generosity, and underlies the legal authority of tribal fisheries management.

Potential misunderstanding. Canadian "treaty rights" and "Aboriginal rights" are different legal categories under section 35, and Mexican Indigenous rights rest on other grounds. The terms should not be used interchangeably across jurisdictions.

Division II — Fisheries and Aquatic Ecosystems

Section 135.5

Salmon and the Indigenous Pacific Northwest

Most Pacific salmon populations (Chinook, coho, sockeye, chum, and pink) and steelhead are anadromous; some populations, such as landlocked sockeye, complete their life cycles in freshwater. Eggs laid in gravel beds hatch in freshwater, young fish migrate to the ocean, and adults return, usually to the stream where they were born. Most Pacific salmon die after spawning, delivering marine nutrients to river ecosystems and forests, while steelhead may survive spawning. The consequence of this life cycle is that salmon depend on whole watersheds: cold, clean, connected water from headwaters to estuary, and ocean conditions beyond any single nation's control. Damage in any part of the cycle reduces returns, which is why historical disruption of salmon was so broad.

Salmon fisheries underlay many Pacific Northwest societies. Coast Salish communities of Puget Sound and the Salish Sea used reef nets, weirs, and traps; Columbia River peoples such as the Yakama, Umatilla, Warm Springs, Nez Perce, and others fished from platforms at falls and rapids, with Celilo Falls as the best-known site. Peoples of the Klamath Basin, including the Yurok, Karuk, and Hoopa Valley, fished the river and its tributaries, and ceremonies such as the Karuk and Yurok world-renewal ceremonies are bound to place and season. In British Columbia, Fraser, Skeena, and Nass River nations such as the Stó:lō, Gitxsan, and Nisga'a likewise built economies and legal orders around salmon, and Canadian law has recognized Indigenous priority for food, social, and ceremonial fishing since Sparrow and has addressed commercial access through the Aboriginal Fisheries Strategy of 1992 and later agreements such as the Nisga'a Final Agreement.

Salmon decline had several causes that varied by river: industrial overharvest in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, logging and agricultural runoff, mining pollution, dams, hatchery effects on wild stocks, urbanization, and changing ocean and climate conditions. Dams on the Columbia, the Snake, and elsewhere blocked or degraded access to upstream habitat. Grand Coulee Dam, completed in 1942 without fish passage, cut off salmon from the upper Columbia, and the Colville Confederated Tribes held a Ceremony of Tears at Kettle Falls in 1940 before the flooding. Attribution of decline among harvest, habitat, hatcheries, and hydropower, often called the "four H's," remains contested in specific cases, as do the remedies.

For Indigenous communities, salmon decline was a loss of food, income, ceremony, and identity. Tribal research in the 1990s on fish consumption among Columbia River tribes found that members ate far more fish than the general population assumed in regulatory calculations, and this evidence contributed to stricter water-quality standards in Oregon. The finding shows how salmon connect to food sovereignty and to environmental governance: where regulators assume low fish consumption, pollution standards may inadequately protect people who rely on fish.

The geography of salmon harvesting also structured the geography of ownership and obligations. A productive fishing station was not interchangeable with an arbitrary place on a map. At a falls or constriction, the channel concentrated migrating fish and made a particular harvesting technology practical. Access was learned and negotiated through generations of relationships; an upstream community and a downstream community depended on the same migrating population but did not necessarily hold the same rights. The destruction of a station therefore affected more than the volume of fish caught. It altered the terms under which relatives assembled, people exchanged goods and news, and households taught children to harvest and preserve fish. Replacement access sites could restore some functions but could not reconstruct the original topography or its historical relationships.

Contemporary restoration must account for the diversity within salmon species as well as differences among species. Fish returning to separate tributaries may have different migration schedules, tolerances, and spawning conditions. A fishery conducted in a main river or marine channel may intercept several populations together, including a relatively abundant one and a depleted one. Managing that fishery involves determining which populations are present and what incidental mortality a vulnerable population can withstand. This helps explain why a tribal government may defend its treaty entitlement while accepting a limited fishing season. The legal entitlement and the biological opportunity are separate questions. The same distinction matters when comparing hatchery production with natural reproduction: a large return of hatchery-origin fish does not by itself demonstrate that self-sustaining wild populations have recovered.

The health of a salmon fishery is thus partly a measure of the health of a network that includes distant marine environments and the entire freshwater basin. Local control of a fishing site is indispensable, but it cannot substitute for coordinated decisions about upstream pollution, downstream passage, estuarine habitat, or fisheries operating far from the home river. That interdependence gave particular importance to the intertribal institutions discussed next.

Section 135.6

Treaty Fisheries and the Reconstruction of Fishing Authority

The history of fishing-rights protests and litigation belongs primarily to Chapter 113. This section concerns what followed: the transformation of legally recognized rights into functioning management institutions. In United States v. Washington (1974), Judge George Boldt interpreted the treaty clause securing fishing "in common with" citizens to mean that treaty tribes were entitled to a share, up to half, of the harvestable fish passing their usual and accustomed places, and he recognized the tribes as co-managers with the state. The Ninth Circuit upheld the decision in 1975, and the Supreme Court affirmed the core of the framework in 1979, clarifying that the share could be limited by the level needed for a moderate living. The decision also created an immediate institutional challenge: tribes now had to monitor runs, calculate harvests, enforce rules, and negotiate seasons with state and federal agencies.

The institutions that emerged were often intertribal. The Northwest Indian Fisheries Commission, organized in 1974 and now serving twenty treaty tribes of western Washington, provides biological, policy, and legal support to its members while each tribe retains its own treaty rights and governing authority. In the Columbia Basin, the Yakama, Umatilla, Warm Springs, and Nez Perce nations established the Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission in 1977 to coordinate technical, policy, and enforcement work. CRITFC works on harvest management, hatchery and habitat issues, law enforcement at treaty fishing sites, and participation in the forums, such as the long-running United States v. Oregon proceedings and the Pacific Salmon Treaty process, in which allocations and management plans are negotiated. Its 1995 restoration plan, Wy-Kan-Ush-Mi Wa-Kish-Wit ("Spirit of the Salmon"), presented a tribal vision for salmon recovery based on Columbia Basin nations' own priorities.

These commissions are service and coordination organizations, not substitutes for tribal governments. Authority to fish remains with the nations; the commissions provide shared scientific staff, enforcement capacity, and a unified voice in complex negotiations with several states, federal agencies, and Canadian entities. Their effectiveness has depended on funding, which comes from a mix of federal appropriations, agreements with agencies, and tribal resources, and on the willingness of other governments to treat tribes as co-managers rather than as stakeholders.

The institutions developed along with practical fisheries: tribes built hatcheries and rearing programs, operated fisheries for ceremonial, subsistence, and commercial purposes, created tribal fish-buying and marketing enterprises, and managed treaty fishing access sites constructed along the Columbia in partial compensation for inundated sites. Tribal harvest has often been conducted under conservation constraints, including closures in years of low returns. At the same time, harvest management does not solve the larger problem. Even a perfectly regulated fishery cannot rebuild a run whose habitat has been eliminated, a limitation that connects this section directly to dams and habitat in the next.

The Columbia River has its own litigation history. In 1968 Yakama fishers challenged Oregon’s regulation of fishing at off-reservation sites, and in 1969 Judge Robert Belloni’s decision in Sohappy v. Smith, heard with United States v. Oregon, established principles for protecting Columbia River treaty fisheries. The continuing United States v. Oregon proceedings became an important venue for managing these fisheries across jurisdictions. This history should be read alongside, rather than as a mere consequence of, the 1974 Boldt decision in western Washington. Both legal histories contributed to the emergence of tribal fisheries institutions, but the rivers, parties, and particular management arrangements were not identical (see CRITFC’s Fisheries Timeline).

A practical example comes from CRITFC’s explanation of a spring fishery closure in 2024. A technical advisory committee including tribal, state, and federal managers estimates the number of fish entering the river using counts at Bonneville Dam and information about catches farther downstream. Managers then have to evaluate allowable impacts, including impacts on stocks of conservation concern. The commission provides technical information and coordinates among its member nations; that does not mean the commission itself possesses all of their legislative powers. A closure is consequently both a biological decision made under uncertainty and an administrative decision in which different governments have legally defined roles.

This structure also makes it possible for tribal scientists to challenge assumptions that previously informed state management. Instead of relying exclusively on assessments prepared by agencies with different mandates, a nation can bring independently collected information to negotiations, ask how a model treats its harvest, or propose a restoration alternative. Scientific capacity becomes part of political capacity. It requires investments in people, instruments, long-term records, and public credibility that a judicial decision alone cannot supply.

Section 135.7

Dams, Fish Passage, and the Restoration of Rivers

Hydroelectric and irrigation dams reshaped the rivers of the Pacific Northwest in the twentieth century. On the Columbia, Bonneville Dam (1938), Grand Coulee (1942), The Dalles (1957), and later projects converted a free-flowing river into a chain of reservoirs. Dams flooded fishing sites, blocked or delayed migration, raised water temperatures, altered flow timing, and increased mortality of juvenile fish passing turbines or reservoirs. Fish ladders at some dams, juvenile bypass systems, barging of young fish, and large hatchery programs sought to offset losses, with mixed results that remain debated. The Columbia River Treaty of 1964 between Canada and the United States coordinated flood control and power but did not include Indigenous nations as parties, and recent modernization discussions have included Columbia Basin tribes and First Nations as participants. Whether to breach the four lower Snake River dams remains contested.

The Klamath River offers the principal case study. Hydroelectric dams built from the early twentieth century, including Copco 1 (completed 1918) and Iron Gate (completed 1962), blocked salmon from hundreds of miles of upstream habitat and degraded water quality in the reservoirs, where warm, nutrient-rich water fostered toxic algal blooms. In 2002, a large die-off of adult salmon in the lower river, in which tens of thousands of fish died, intensified Indigenous demands for river restoration. The Yurok, Karuk, Hoopa Valley, and Klamath Tribes, together with commercial fishing and conservation groups, pursued regulatory proceedings, litigation, public campaigns, and negotiation, and in the mid-2000s tribal delegations carried their case to shareholders of the dams' corporate owner. The Klamath Hydroelectric Settlement Agreement of 2010, amended in 2016, created a path for dam removal through a new nonprofit, the Klamath River Renewal Corporation, and federal regulators approved the surrender of the dam licenses in 2022. Copco No. 2 was removed in 2023. Reservoir drawdown for the remaining projects began in January 2024, and removal of J.C. Boyle, Copco No. 1, and Iron Gate was completed in 2024. The Klamath River Renewal Corporation announced the conclusion of the removal phase on October 2, 2024 (see Source Foundation).

The achievement should be described carefully. Removal restored passage to a long stretch of river and lets sediment, wood, and cold-water inputs move again, and reports of salmon entering former upstream reaches soon followed. But ecological recovery is a longer process. Salmon returns depend on ocean conditions, disease dynamics in the river, water temperature, flows allocated to irrigation in the upper basin, and the condition of tributary habitat. Scientific studies by agencies such as the U.S. Geological Survey are tracking sediment, water quality, and fish response, and the timeline for population recovery is measured in years to decades. Water allocation in the upper Klamath Basin, including the interests of the Klamath Tribes in endangered suckers and the demands of agricultural irrigators, remains unresolved.

Other restoration efforts offer comparison. The removal of two dams on the Elwha River in Washington, completed in 2014 and carried out in partnership with the Lower Elwha Klallam Tribe, produced early lessons about sediment release and fish recolonization. Pacific lamprey, an anadromous fish of great cultural importance to Columbia Plateau peoples and long neglected by fisheries agencies, has been the focus of tribal restoration programs. In each case Indigenous nations have been among the principal proponents and implementers, and their participation has shaped how success is defined, with measures that include cultural access and ceremony, not only fish counts.

Celilo and Klamath demonstrate different kinds of loss. At Celilo the construction of a reservoir submerged the physical location on which a fishing economy depended. On the Klamath, dams interrupted access to extensive upstream habitat and altered river conditions in ways that harmed fisheries downstream. Removing a barrier can restore connectivity, but the restoration of a submerged trading center on the Columbia would require a different set of decisions involving navigation, power generation, flood control, property, and regional economies. Students should not treat dam removal as a universal remedy detached from a particular river’s history.

The Klamath project also illustrates the importance of institutional design. A hydroelectric project is not simply a structure belonging to the government; it may be operated by a private utility under a public license. Surrendering that license, arranging ownership transfer, securing funding, establishing an organization to undertake removal, and allocating restoration responsibilities required separate legal and financial steps. KRRC was formed under the amended settlement to carry out the work. This organizational solution made a political agreement actionable and separated responsibilities for decommissioning from the long-term task of watershed recovery. Indigenous nations helped shape the political settlement but did not become the sole operators of every part of the project.

Restoration monitoring distinguishes short-term disturbance from long-term change. Sediment released from former reservoirs can temporarily affect water clarity and fish habitat, while channel adjustment and revegetation change conditions over subsequent seasons. Fish observed upstream demonstrate that passage has become possible; estimates of spawning success and juvenile survival over multiple generations are needed to evaluate population recovery. Indigenous communities may additionally evaluate renewed access to historically important places, availability of ceremonial fish, and participation of young people in restoration work. These measurements address different questions and should be interpreted together rather than reduced to a single claim of success.

Section 135.8

Indigenous Fisheries Beyond the Pacific Northwest

Indigenous fisheries extend across the continent. In the Great Lakes, Anishinaabe nations fished whitefish, lake trout, sturgeon, and walleye, and the treaty-rights implementation described in Section 135.14 followed from litigation in the 1970s and 1980s. In the northern interior of Canada, inland fisheries supported Cree, Dene, and Métis communities, but the federal marketing monopoly created in 1969, the Freshwater Fish Marketing Corporation, long limited fishers' control over sales and prices, and some provinces later withdrew. On the Atlantic coast, Mi'kmaw, Wolastoqiyik, and Peskotomuhkati peoples fished eels, salmon, lobster, and other species, and Indigenous communities now participate in the commercial fishing industry as licence holders, vessel owners, and processors.

The Marshall decisions of 1999 are central. Donald Marshall Jr., a Mi'kmaw fisherman, was charged for catching and selling eels without a licence. In R. v. Marshall (September 17, 1999), the Supreme Court of Canada held that the Mi'kmaq treaties of 1760–1761 protected a right to trade the products of hunting, fishing, and gathering in order to secure a moderate livelihood, which the Court understood as necessaries such as food, clothing, and housing with some amenities, not the accumulation of wealth. In a clarification issued in November 1999 when it declined to rehear the case, the Court emphasized that the right is subject to regulation justified on grounds such as conservation. The decisions did not create an unlimited commercial entitlement.

Implementation has been difficult. Disputes over access to lobster at Esgenoôpetitj (Burnt Church) in 2000–2002 involved confrontation between Indigenous fishers and federal officers, while federal programs after 1999 funded Mi'kmaw acquisition of licences and vessels. In 2020, when the Sipekne'katik First Nation launched a self-regulated moderate-livelihood fishery outside the commercial season, confrontations with non-Indigenous commercial fishers and attacks on Mi'kmaw property followed, and the federal government and Mi'kmaw leaders have since negotiated over how the livelihood right is to be exercised and regulated. Many Mi'kmaw communities have argued that their own governance, grounded in netukulimk, can regulate their fisheries, while the federal government has maintained that the Minister retains regulatory responsibility consistent with the 1999 decisions.

The Atlantic disputes illustrate a distinction between access to an industry and recognition of a political right. Government programs that finance boats, gear, training, and commercial licences can expand Indigenous participation in fishing markets. Such programs may provide employment and capital that communities previously lacked. But a licence issued under the ordinary commercial system is a permission administered by the state; it is not itself the source of a treaty right. Communities that insist on the authority to establish their own rules therefore raise a different issue from communities negotiating access to more commercial licences. They may pursue both objectives simultaneously.

The Marshall decisions concern a collective right held by treaty communities and exercised by their members; the treaty relationship and the relevant geographic connection matter. A finding about the rights of communities covered by the Peace and Friendship treaties does not establish an identical entitlement for every Indigenous person in Canada or in every fishing area. The Court’s November clarification also emphasized that regulation may be justified, not simply that any rule asserted in the name of conservation is automatically valid. The legitimacy of a restriction depends on the legal framework, its justification and application, and the particular fishery. The protection of stocks is a shared concern, but the distribution of the costs of a restriction and the decision-making process can remain disputed.

Fishing economies involve much more than the work on a vessel. Boat ownership, financing, maintenance, insurance, access to wharves, cold storage, processing, and bargaining with buyers all influence the income that harvesting can produce. A community acquiring vessels without access to buyers or training may find that its effective economic opportunity remains limited. A fishery organized through a communal enterprise can retain some revenues for community services while creating employment, but it also requires decisions about who receives access and how returns are distributed. The meaning of a moderate livelihood cannot therefore be inferred from the market price of a single species or the size of a single catch.

Inland fisheries likewise require access to infrastructure and markets. For remote communities, the cost of transportation and preservation can be a major constraint even where fish populations are healthy. A comparison between a Great Lakes commercial fishery, a remote northern inland fishery, and an Atlantic lobster fishery reveals why no single measure of commercial success describes all Indigenous fisheries. Ecological conditions, legal rights, distance to markets, and the organization of enterprises shape their different histories.

Division III — Wildlife Restoration and Terrestrial Ecosystems

Section 135.9

The Buffalo and the Reconstruction of Plains Communities

Chapter 81 treats the destruction of the buffalo economy in detail. Here it is enough to note the scale: a population usually estimated in the tens of millions, though with considerable uncertainty, was reduced by the 1880s to a few hundred wild animals in the United States, with small numbers surviving in captive and private herds. The destruction was not a purely ecological event. It removed the material basis of Plains economies, contributed to the political subordination of Plains nations, and was accompanied by the substitution of government rations for a food system that had supported mobility and independence.

Contemporary buffalo restoration therefore carries meanings that exceed species conservation. Buffalo are food, and tribal programs that supply meat to schools, elders, and ceremonies respond to concerns about diet and health. They are ceremonial and material resources, supplying hides, bones, and other materials for traditional production. They are an educational resource, allowing young people to learn hunting, butchering, and the cultural and linguistic knowledge attached to the animal. They support grassland restoration, because bison graze, wallow, and disturb soil in ways that shape prairie plant and animal communities. And they are economic assets, creating employment and enterprise on tribal land, though economic returns are rarely the primary goal.

Restoration relies on land. Tribal herds exist on reservation lands in the Dakotas, Montana, Wyoming, Oklahoma, and elsewhere, where fencing, water, forage, and veterinary care determine how many animals can live. Land recovery therefore intersects with restoration: in 2020 Congress directed that the National Bison Range in Montana be restored to the Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes, and the transfer into trust was completed in 2022. Not all land is tribal, and ranchers, agencies, and other governments hold many of the lands where historic ranges lay. Questions about bison status, such as whether they are wildlife or livestock, and about disease concerns such as brucellosis, shape where buffalo may go and who decides.

Restoration should not be described as the recreation of an unchanged precolonial economy. Contemporary herds are fenced and managed, markets and regulations are modern, communities are diverse, and Indigenous nations have chosen different purposes, from ceremony to conservation to enterprise. What continues is a relationship with the animal and an assertion of the right of Indigenous nations to decide how that relationship is lived today.

One consequence of restoration is the reconstruction of a community economy around an animal rather than simply the sale of meat. A herd program may employ staff to maintain fences and water facilities, monitor the range, organize harvests, process meat, and administer distribution. Tribal councils may choose to allocate part of a harvest to elders or schools rather than sell all of it. That decision has an opportunity cost: income forgone in the market supports another community objective. Accounting for such choices requires a broader definition of the benefits of a herd than the revenue of a livestock operation.

The relationship between buffalo and grassland is also dynamic. Grazing creates variation in vegetation height and structure; wallowing and other disturbance create local habitat conditions. The effects depend on soil, moisture, herd density, and range size. On restricted land, overuse can degrade forage rather than improve it, and herd managers must respond to drought and changing pasture condition. Restoration thus involves ongoing decisions about land and animals, not a single transfer of bison into a fenced enclosure. The question of how much land is available for herds connects ecological restoration directly to the history of allotment and land loss discussed in Chapter 86.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Buffalo Economy Before Destruction and During Contemporary Restoration

What Changed
  • Herds that ranged over vast open grasslands are now managed on bounded tribal lands with fences and veterinary oversight.
  • Harvesting for subsistence and trade in a continental market has given way to selective harvest, school meals, ceremony, and small enterprises.
  • Management involves tribal natural resources staff, genetic testing, and intertribal coordination, not only customary leadership.
What Stayed the Same
  • Buffalo remain central to ceremony, kinship narratives, and identity for many Plains nations.
  • Obligations of respectful use and sharing continue to guide how meat and materials are distributed.
  • Knowledge about the animal, its seasonal behavior, and its uses continues to be transmitted.

Pattern. Change was abrupt and violent in the nineteenth century and gradual and community-driven in restoration. Elders and ceremonial leaders often experienced continuity through ritual even during the absence of herds, while younger people experience restoration as a new relationship to practices their grandparents could not exercise.

Section 135.10

Intertribal Buffalo Restoration and Continental Cooperation

Nineteen tribal nations attended an organizing gathering in the Black Hills in February 1991; the InterTribal Buffalo Council (ITBC) was organized in 1992. Originally a nonprofit, it became a federally chartered Indigenous organization under section 17 of the Indian Reorganization Act in 2009. Its website displayed 86 member tribes when consulted in November 2026; the number is subject to change. The council is headquartered in Rapid City, South Dakota. Its role is to provide technical assistance, coordinate the transfer of animals from federal herds to tribal herds, and advocate for resources. Surplus animals from federal lands, including national park herds, have been moved through quarantine and transfer programs. Transfers from Yellowstone, for example, required quarantine to address brucellosis concerns before animals could be moved to tribal lands such as Fort Peck and Fort Belknap.

Intertribal cooperation also involves independent exchange among nations. Tribes transfer animals to one another, share veterinary and range-management knowledge, and coordinate on genetic management. Many bison herds carry cattle ancestry introduced through historical hybridization. Earlier marker-based tests did not detect introgression in some herds, but a 2022 whole-genome study detected cattle-derived segments in all bison genomes it sampled, including animals from herds previously regarded as free of detectable cattle ancestry. This result concerns the sampled genomes and the sensitivity of the method; it does not establish the ancestry of every individual animal or diminish the cultural meaning of buffalo restoration. Genetic stewardship, handling, and herd size therefore matter for conservation. Different herds also have different goals, and students should distinguish three categories. Conservation herds are managed to maintain genetic diversity and ecological function. Commercial herds are managed for meat sales. Culturally oriented herds are managed to supply ceremony, community food, education, and the continuation of relationships. Many tribal herds combine these purposes, and tensions among them are real: a ceremonial priority may conflict with the numbers needed for genetic diversity, and market opportunities may conflict with cultural limits on sale.

The 2014 Buffalo Treaty (associated with the Iinnii Initiative) expanded cooperation across the Canada–United States border. It was signed in September 2014 by a group of Indigenous nations and First Nations, including nations of the Blackfoot Confederacy, as a nation-to-nation agreement among Indigenous peoples, with no state as a party, and it has since been joined by other signatories. It commits them to cooperate in restoring buffalo as a relative, in cultural, spiritual, and ecological terms, and to work toward buffalo populations on shared landscapes. It illustrates both continuity with older Indigenous diplomacy and a modern use of treaty form. Related restoration includes the return of plains bison from Elk Island National Park in Alberta to the Blackfeet Nation in Montana in 2016, and the reintroduction of bison to Banff National Park in 2017 with Indigenous participation.

Obstacles remain: land and range to hold larger herds; fencing and water; funding and personnel; regulatory restrictions on movement; and the uncertain status of buffalo under state and federal law. Climate variability and drought affect forage. Despite these challenges, intertribal institutions have given Indigenous nations a collective voice in federal and state policy that individual nations could not have easily exercised.

The inaugural Buffalo Treaty signing on September 24, 2014, took place on the Blackfeet Reservation. Its eight founding signatory entities were the Blackfeet Nation; Kainai (Blood Tribe); Siksika Nation; Piikani Nation; the Assiniboine and Gros Ventre Tribes of Fort Belknap; the Assiniboine and Sioux Tribes of Fort Peck; the Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes; and the Tsuut’ina Nation. These names identify distinct political communities, including several connected through the Blackfoot Confederacy. The official treaty history documents subsequent gatherings and additional signatories, demonstrating that the agreement developed through continuing diplomacy rather than ending with its initial ceremony.

There is a significant difference between the ITBC charter and the Buffalo Treaty. The former supports an organization with a governing board and operational programs; the latter establishes commitments between nations. One can provide technical assistance for a transfer or advise on a herd’s management, while the other sustains political relationships, identifies collective objectives, and invites new partners. Neither eliminates the individual nation’s decisions concerning its herd. A cross-border transfer additionally requires compliance with the applicable animal-health and import rules. Intertribal agreement does not automatically repeal those rules, but it can give nations greater capacity to coordinate projects and seek changes in policy.

Herd management also reveals why a narrow genetic objective is insufficient for evaluating recovery. Maintaining variation and avoiding inbreeding are important biological objectives for relatively small herds. At the same time, a culturally oriented restoration program may seek opportunities for youth education, food distribution, ceremony, and changes in access to ancestral grasslands. A herd may satisfy several such objectives simultaneously. Whole-genome research on cattle ancestry informs conservation decisions, but determining which objectives to prioritize remains a matter for the responsible nations and managers. Genetic results should not be treated as a ranking of the cultural authenticity of communities or their animals.

Section 135.11

Caribou and the Indigenous Northern Interior

Caribou are the foundation of many northern Indigenous economies. Barren-ground herds make long seasonal movements between calving grounds on the tundra and winter ranges in northern forest, and Gwich'in, Dene, Inuit, Inuvialuit, Cree, Innu, Naskapi, and others harvest them at crossings, along migration routes, and on winter ranges. Meat, hides for clothing and shelter, bone, sinew, and antler all have uses, and caribou skin clothing remains valued for its insulation. Harvests are shared through networks of kinship and community, so that a successful hunt feeds households far beyond the hunter's own. In the Gwich'in tradition, the calving grounds of the Porcupine herd are described as a place where life begins and not to be disturbed.

Caribou are not a single population. The Porcupine herd, ranging across northeastern Alaska, northern Yukon, and the Northwest Territories, has fluctuated over several decades. The July 2025 photographic census, whose results were announced in January 2026, estimated approximately 143,000 animals, compared with 218,000 in 2017. The Vuntut Gwitchin First Nation highlighted the decline as a reason to protect habitat. The Bathurst herd in the central Arctic declined from several hundred thousand animals in the mid-1980s; the Northwest Territories government estimated 3,609 adult caribou in 2025, compared with 6,851 adults in 2022, leading to harvest restrictions and Tłı̨chǫ and other Indigenous-led monitoring and management efforts. The George River herd in Québec and Labrador, important to Innu and Naskapi communities, declined from several hundred thousand animals in the 1990s to an estimated 8,600 animals in July 2024. The Nunatsiavut Government reported some adult population growth from 2018 to 2024 but emphasized that the herd remained vulnerable and hunting was prohibited under provincial regulations at the time of its report. Woodland caribou in the boreal forest and southern mountains are generally smaller, more sedentary populations, many of them endangered; the West Moberly and Saulteau First Nations in British Columbia have led maternal penning and habitat recovery in the Klinse-Za program, formalized in a partnership agreement in 2020. Population trends differ by herd, region, and time, and causes include climate variability, predation, industrial disturbance, disease, and harvest, with the relative weight of each debated. Students must avoid generalizing from one herd to all caribou.

Northern Indigenous communities have been among the principal advocates of caribou protection. The Beverly and Qamanirjuaq Caribou Management Board, formed in 1982, brought together Indigenous and other users from several provinces and territories, and the land-claim agreements of the 1980s and 1990s, including the Inuvialuit Final Agreement of 1984, the Gwich'in comprehensive claim of 1992, and the Vuntut Gwitchin agreement concluded in the 1990s, created wildlife management bodies with Indigenous participation. National parks such as Ivvavik and Vuntut protect important habitat in Yukon and are tied to those agreements. The Gwich'in Steering Committee, formed in 1988, organized opposition to oil leasing on the Arctic Refuge coastal plain, a conflict that anticipates the extractive-industry histories of Chapters 136 and 137.

Population estimates for migratory herds require unusual care. During part of the year a herd may occupy a vast area, and animals do not respect survey boundaries. Photo surveys attempt to count aggregations when conditions make observation feasible; the timing, visibility, grouping of animals, and definition of the population influence the estimate. Some reports estimate adult animals, while others report total abundance. The Bathurst adult estimates and the Porcupine total estimates therefore cannot simply be placed in a table and interpreted as equivalent counts of all caribou. Changes across comparable surveys are useful evidence, but the methods and uncertainty matter as well as the headline number.

The three principal herd examples also show why a decline is not by itself evidence of one cause. A population can decline because adult survival falls, calf production changes, young animals survive less often, or individuals move into another herd’s range. Harvest, predation, weather, forage, and disturbance can act together. The 2024 George River report noted improvements in some demographic indicators despite the historically low overall population. Such improvements do not remove the need for caution. Conversely, the Porcupine decline between 2017 and 2025 demonstrates that a herd once regarded as relatively abundant cannot be assumed to remain so indefinitely.

For communities, harvest restrictions have uneven consequences. A household with regular wage income may be better able to purchase alternative food than a household depending on shared country food, especially where store prices are high. Reduced harvesting also reduces occasions for teaching navigation, processing, and language. Conservation decisions have to account for these social effects while responding to biological evidence. Indigenous leadership in monitoring and restraint can strengthen both the legitimacy and the relevance of a response.

Everyday Life and Social History

Harvest, Preparation, and Sharing in Northern Communities

Harvesting caribou involves far more than the hunt. Families travel to camps and crossing places, women and men often have different responsibilities in butchering, drying meat, preparing hides, and sewing clothing, and children learn by participating. Meat is shared with elders, with households that lack hunters, and across settlements, and community freezers, feasts, and distribution programs carry older sharing practices into modern settlement life. In many communities the cost of purchased food is high, so country food is a major source of nutrition as well as of cultural well-being.

Historians must read these practices through varied evidence: oral history, ethnography, harvest surveys, and community research. Written sources before the twentieth century often describe northern peoples through the eyes of traders, missionaries, and officials. Contemporary community-led harvest studies and elders' testimony before boards and commissions provide a fuller record of how caribou fit into daily life, and also record what is at stake when herds decline: not only food supply, but the continuation of skills, relationships, and language.

Section 135.12

Transboundary Caribou Management

The Porcupine herd crosses the boundary between Alaska and Yukon each year, so no single government can manage it alone. Two layers of agreement address this. The first is the Porcupine Caribou Management Agreement, signed on October 26, 1985, among Canadian governments and Indigenous organizations representing harvesting peoples, which established the Porcupine Caribou Management Board to coordinate management in Canada. The second is the 1987 Agreement between Canada and the United States on the Conservation of the Porcupine Caribou Herd, signed at Ottawa on July 17, 1987, which created the International Porcupine Caribou Board. The 1987 agreement recognized that the herd crosses an international boundary and that certain people in Canada and rural residents of Alaska have customarily harvested it, and it committed the two governments to cooperate on conservation of the herd and its habitat.

The architecture of the agreements reflects a deliberate balance. The Canadian board participates in domestic management with Indigenous harvesters at the table, and the international board brings Canadian and American appointees together to advise their governments. The treaty is explicit that cooperation should not alter domestic authority over management and should rely on existing structures, and it makes the international board advisory. This distinction is the key to interpreting the arrangement: it provides a forum for coordination, information sharing, and recommendations, but not an independent regulatory power over harvests or land use.

The agreement also reflects how Alaska and Canada define harvesters differently. In Canada the agreement refers to Native users defined in sections A8 and A9 of the 1985 agreement and other users identified under its section E2(e). In Alaska, it refers to rural residents under state and federal law, a category based on place of residence that includes many Alaska Native communities but is not Indigenous-specific. Differences like these show why transboundary governance must reconcile legal systems built on different principles, as discussed in Section 135.4. The treaty also requires impact assessment and consultation when activities in one country may significantly affect the herd or its habitat, and it prohibits commercial sale of herd meat.

The agreements' most visible stress test has been oil and gas development on the coastal plain of the Arctic National Wildlife Refuge, where much of the herd calves. Gwich'in communities in Alaska and Canada have opposed leasing, arguing that disturbance threatens the herd and with it their culture and food security, while other Alaska Native communities, including those whose corporations hold land or revenue interests, have supported development, a divergence examined further in Chapters 115 and 136. The episode shows that Indigenous communities are not unanimous on resource development and that a herd's conservation depends on decisions made in several capitals.

Section 135.13

Inuit Marine and Arctic Wildlife Management

Inuit, Inupiat, Yupik, and Kalaallit economies have long centered on marine and terrestrial wildlife: ringed and bearded seals, walrus, bowhead and other whales, polar bears, caribou, and Arctic char. Subsistence hunting and fishing, supported by knowledge of ice, currents, and animal behavior, remain central to food supply and identity, and sharing networks distribute meat and fish across households. Modern wildlife governance in the Arctic has been shaped by international agreements, including the 1973 agreement on polar bear conservation among Canada, Denmark, Norway, the Soviet Union, and the United States, and by marine mammal protection laws and whaling regulation.

The Nunavut Agreement of 1993, concluded between the Inuit of the Nunavut Settlement Area and the Government of Canada and leading to the creation of the territory of Nunavut in 1999, established an elaborate wildlife regime. Under Article 5, the Nunavut Wildlife Management Board is the main instrument of wildlife management in the settlement area. It sets harvesting limits, approves management plans, and funds research, while local Hunters and Trappers Organizations administer community harvesting and regional wildlife organizations provide a middle level. The agreement recognizes Inuit harvesting rights and establishes rules for basic needs levels and allocation where total allowable harvests are set. Inuit knowledge, including Inuit Qaujimajatuqangit, informs management alongside scientific research. Article 5.3 establishes different ministerial review procedures according to the decision and jurisdiction, including reasons, reconsideration, and limits on permissible restrictions of Inuit harvests. The arrangement is a treaty-established institution of public government with substantial independent decision-making responsibilities, while ultimate responsibility remains with government, a point revisited in the debate on co-management in Section 135.19. Chapter 116 treats the broader history of Inuit land claims.

Comparisons illuminate the range of arrangements. In Alaska, the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of 1971 extinguished aboriginal hunting and fishing claims, and subsistence is addressed by the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980, which provides a preference for rural residents. Alaska Native organizations, such as the Alaska Eskimo Whaling Commission, which manages bowhead whaling under a cooperative agreement with federal authorities within the framework of International Whaling Commission aboriginal subsistence quotas, and the Kuskokwim River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission, established in 2015 (followed by a memorandum of understanding with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in 2016), have created co-management structures under the Marine Mammal Protection Act and federal fisheries regimes. In Greenland, hunting and fishing are regulated by the self-governing Greenlandic authorities with participation of the hunters' organization KNAPK, and whaling for Kalaallit communities is conducted under International Whaling Commission aboriginal subsistence quotas. Each arrangement reflects different legal histories: land-claim agreements in Canada, settlement legislation in Alaska, and self-government in Greenland.

The Nunavut arrangement is best understood as a sequence of decisions established in a treaty, not as a single transfer of power. A total allowable harvest, when required, concerns the amount that can be taken without undermining the relevant management objectives. Allocation decisions concern the division of available harvest among those with recognized rights and interests. The agreement contains rules for determining Inuit basic needs and for addressing surplus harvests. At the community level, Hunters and Trappers Organizations exercise functions relating to harvesting by their members, while Regional Wildlife Organizations perform corresponding regional functions. Article 5 gives each level responsibilities that cannot be inferred merely from the title of the body.

Ministerial review must also be read within the agreement rather than described as an unlimited veto. Article 5.3 establishes procedural steps for different categories of decisions. Some decisions are subject to reconsideration after an initial rejection, and restrictions on Inuit harvesting must satisfy applicable treaty criteria. Consequently the Board’s recommendations and decisions do not all have the same status. Students should distinguish its decision-making functions from its advisory and research functions. Its institutional status is markedly different from that of the international Porcupine board discussed in the preceding section.

Alaska provides a comparison in which tribal organization developed through different federal laws. The Kuskokwim River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission was established in 2015 to pursue a greater role for the river’s tribes in fisheries management; its 2016 agreement with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service supplied a framework for cooperation. The commission brings together communities whose food supply depends on migrating fish, but whose fishing opportunities differ according to their location along the river. Scientific information about returns and the distribution of fishing opportunity are matters of practical concern to those communities. The federal partnership demonstrates a form of co-management, but its legal foundation and scope are not the same as the Nunavut treaty.

Marine mammal management introduces another scale of authority. Whales and other migratory marine animals cross local hunting territories and international waters. A community may organize its own sharing and harvesting practices while operating within national legislation and an international conservation regime. These different layers of authority can coexist, but their existence alone does not establish that the community has equal power in every decision. The Arctic comparison therefore illustrates several routes to environmental governance rather than a uniform model of Indigenous management.

Section 135.14

Indigenous Wildlife Management in the Woodlands and Great Lakes

Across the eastern woodlands and Great Lakes, Indigenous nations harvest deer, elk, moose, waterfowl, fish, and plants such as wild rice, and many hold treaty rights to do so beyond reservation boundaries. Ojibwe nations ceded large territories in treaties of 1836, 1837, 1842, and 1854 while reserving rights to hunt, fish, and gather. For much of the twentieth century states did not honor those rights, and arrests of Ojibwe harvesters led to litigation. In Lac Courte Oreilles Band v. Voigt, the Seventh Circuit in 1983 affirmed treaty rights in ceded territory in Wisconsin, and a period of intense conflict followed in the 1980s and early 1990s, including protests against Ojibwe spearfishing, until federal courts restrained harassment of treaty harvesters. Litigation in Michigan led to a 1985 consent decree and management through the predecessor Chippewa Ottawa Treaty Fishery Management Authority, whose regulations date to 1982. The successor Chippewa Ottawa Resource Authority (CORA) operates under the charter associated with the 2000 consent decree; a further Great Lakes consent decree followed in 2023.

The Great Lakes Indian Fish and Wildlife Commission was established in 1984 by Ojibwe nations to carry out the implementation of those treaty rights. It now serves eleven member tribes in Wisconsin, Minnesota, and Michigan, providing biologists, conservation wardens, policy specialists, and public information staff, and it supports member tribes in developing and enforcing their own harvest codes. Like CRITFC, it is a service organization: the treaty rights belong to the member nations, which retain authority to regulate their members. GLIFWC's work includes fisheries surveys, population monitoring of game and furbearers, wild rice restoration, and treaty harvest registration, and it has helped establish that tribal regulation can be professional, evidence-based, and in some cases stricter than state rules.

Wild rice, manoomin, is a living resource with political and legal dimensions. Its beds are sensitive to water levels, pollution, and development, and Ojibwe nations have worked on restoration and protection. In 2018 the White Earth Band of Ojibwe adopted a law recognizing legal rights of manoomin, an example of Indigenous law providing a new basis for protecting a species. Conservation can also be Indigenous-initiated: Red Lake commercial fishers voluntarily suspended their walleye fishery in 1997; a cooperative recovery agreement in April 1999 supported broader harvest closures, stocking and monitoring. Regulated fishing resumed in 2006. The community bore substantial economic and social costs during recovery.

Elsewhere, Cree harvesters of Eeyou Istchee in northern Québec operate under the James Bay and Northern Québec Agreement of 1975, which created a hunting, fishing, and trapping regime and a program supporting Cree hunters. Haudenosaunee communities pursue environmental work through bodies such as the Haudenosaunee Environmental Task Force. In Ontario, R. v. Powley (2003) recognized a Métis Aboriginal right to hunt for food, while moose and deer management intersect with First Nations treaty rights under the Robinson Treaties of 1850. Taken together these examples illustrate varied legal pathways to the same objective: Indigenous participation in regulating and sustaining wildlife.

The 1836 treaty fisheries in Michigan show how court-supervised management can develop through several successive institutional arrangements. The 1985 decree established a framework for resolving fishing disputes, but it did not end negotiations permanently. The 2000 decree expanded and refined the arrangements and included the CORA charter, and the subsequent 2023 decree addressed the continuing need to manage and allocate the fishery. The member tribes develop regulations and enforcement capacity through their own institutions. The successive decrees demonstrate that implementing rights requires repeated decisions as scientific evidence, fishing methods, ecological conditions, and institutional relationships change.

Red Lake provides a different kind of example because much of the lake lies within the jurisdiction of the Red Lake Nation. The collapse of the walleye population threatened not only food supplies but a long-established commercial enterprise. Commercial fishers voluntarily halted their harvest in 1997, before the April 1999 recovery agreement with Minnesota. The wider closure that followed imposed costs on recreational fishers and the community as well as commercial harvesters. Recovery efforts included stocking, protection of recovering stocks, scientific assessments, and planning across the different jurisdictions. Reopening in 2006 was accompanied by harvest management rather than a return to the former unrestricted levels of fishing.

This example illustrates a distinction important for the entire chapter. A population inhabiting waters within two jurisdictions is biologically interconnected even if jurisdiction is divided. Cooperation can enable both governments to manage total mortality more effectively. But the Red Lake Nation’s decision to suspend its own commercial fishery also demonstrates an independent act of governance. It was not simply a conservation restriction imposed on Indigenous harvesters by another government. Its history gives a concrete example of the political and economic costs a nation may accept to preserve the future of a resource.

Great Lakes stewardship also extends to ecological relationships that are not captured by fish abundance alone. Wetlands and water levels affect wild rice, and contamination can make fish unsafe to eat even where populations are plentiful. GLIFWC’s fish consumption guidance incorporates the needs and practices of member tribes in addressing contaminant exposure. Protecting treaty harvest therefore requires attention to the quality of resources as well as their quantity. A fish that cannot safely be consumed cannot fulfill the same role in a community’s diet and cultural practices.

Section 135.15

Indigenous Fisheries and Wildlife in Mexico and the Southern Borderlands

The continental scope of this textbook requires attention to Mexico, where Indigenous resource stewardship operates under a different legal and historical framework. Mexico's constitution recognizes Indigenous peoples and, since reforms in 2001 and later, their rights to self-determination, autonomy, and access to natural resources in their lands, subject to exceptions for strategic areas. A large share of Mexican land, often estimated at roughly half, is held in ejidos and agrarian communities, and many Indigenous communities govern forests, coasts, and fisheries through communal institutions. Fishing is regulated through federal law, permit systems, and protected-area designations administered by national agencies, and Indigenous communities often engage these systems through cooperatives, community permits, and negotiation, not through treaty reservations. Chapter 141 treats Indigenous Mexico and the northern borderlands more broadly.

The Comcáac (Seri) of coastal Sonora illustrate community-based marine management, with fishing, sea turtle conservation, and cultural uses in the waters of the Gulf of California. The Cucapá (Es Pei, "people of the river") of the Colorado River Delta in Baja California and Sonora provide the principal case. The Cucapá historically fished the delta's waters, hunted, and cultivated along the river, and a related people, the Cocopah, live on the Arizona side. Upstream diversion of the Colorado River, formalized in part by the 1944 water treaty between the United States and Mexico and by large dams and irrigation, reduced flows to the delta so greatly that the river often did not reach the sea, transforming the habitat and the fisheries on which the Cucapá relied.

In 1993 Mexico established the Upper Gulf of California and Colorado River Delta Biosphere Reserve, a conservation measure intended in part to protect endangered species such as the vaquita, a porpoise at the brink of extinction, and the totoaba fish. The reserve imposed restrictions on fishing that affected Cucapá fishers, who had relied on seasonal fisheries for corvina and other species. Cucapá communities have protested these restrictions, argued that they predate the reserve and that their fishing is a customary and territorial right, and sought permits and exemptions, and conflicts with federal authorities and with commercial and sport fishers have been recurrent. Ethnographic research, including a study published by Duke University Press, has documented how the dispute involves not just regulation but contested Indigenous identity and recognition, as authorities, scientists, and neighbors question or define what it means to be Cucapá.

Water diplomacy has offered limited openings. Binational agreements, known as minutes under the 1944 treaty, have allowed experimental environmental flows, including a pulse flow in 2014 that brought water to the sea for the first time in many years, and Minute 323 of 2017 established cooperative measures, including environmental water provisions, for a term ending December 31, 2026. These measures were negotiated between governments, and Indigenous participation has been limited. The case demonstrates three themes: that conservation regulation can restrict Indigenous livelihoods even when industrial water diversion is the larger cause of ecological decline, that Indigenous rights in Mexico are negotiated through environmental and administrative law, and that borders divide communities and ecosystems at once. Chapter 142 treats borders and Indigenous peoples.

The official history of the biosphere reserve is documented in Mexico’s National Human Rights Commission (Comisión Nacional de los Derechos Humanos, CNDH) Recommendation 8/2002. The document identifies June 10, 1993, as the date on which the designation decree was published and July 2, 1996, as the publication date of notice of the completed management program. The Commission investigated complaints about enforcement against the Cucapá community. Its account described an administrative proceeding involving a commercial fishing permit and competing interpretations of restrictions in the reserve’s core zone. This is evidence of a particular dispute at the beginning of the twenty-first century, not a description of the permit conditions that apply to all fishers today.

The Commission concluded that the authorities had infringed rights of the community and called for adjustments to the management program to address its economic and cultural needs. In its fourth recommendation, it asked the competent fisheries authorities to issue fishing permits to the community in the existing core zone, subject to scientific guidance about harvest volumes and revised management policies. It also called for Cucapá participation in relevant studies and social development programs. The recommendation was not a judicial decision overturning all reserve rules; it was a public human-rights finding and a request for remedial action within the applicable legal system. Its importance lies in the recognition that a conservation program must confront the rights and historical circumstances of the people it regulates.

The dispute is not evidence that species protection is unnecessary. Vaquita conservation, regulation of gillnet fisheries, and the protection of endangered marine life raise serious ecological questions. Different fishing methods, species, zones, and seasons may be regulated differently. A designation intended to conserve biodiversity can nonetheless distribute economic costs unequally if it restricts local customary livelihoods without adequately addressing the activities causing ecological decline. Analysis of such a conflict must identify the actual regulation, responsible institution, affected community, and environmental evidence rather than treat the community and conservation as two mutually exclusive interests.

The delta also exposes the relationship between water policy and fisheries management. A protected area can restrict the taking of fish, but that restriction does not restore the flow of water from distant upstream diversions. Minute 319 made possible the 2014 pulse flow, and Minute 323 continued environmental cooperation within the binational water regime. These measures offer ways to support habitat but are not substitutes for community participation in decisions about fishing. They also do not confer a permanent right to a particular volume of water or prove that the delta has recovered. The Cucapá case thus connects local customary practice, national law, and international water diplomacy without collapsing these distinct levels of authority.

Section 135.16

Habitat Restoration and Indigenous Landscape Management

The restoration of species depends on the restoration of places. Salmon require cold, connected rivers with shaded riparian margins, clean gravel for spawning, and healthy estuaries where juveniles feed. Bison need grasslands with functioning fire, grazing, and water regimes. Caribou need intact calving grounds, winter ranges, and migration corridors free of disruptive development. Wetlands filter water and support waterfowl, fish, and wild rice. Contaminants, invasive species, and habitat fragmentation can undo gains in harvest management, so Indigenous nations increasingly frame their goals in terms of whole landscapes.

Examples span the continent. The Nisqually Tribe worked with federal partners on a large estuary restoration in southern Puget Sound. Tribes in the Klamath Basin, including the Karuk and Yurok, combine cultural burning, discussed in Chapter 134, with riparian and fisheries work, since fire regimes affect forest structure, stream shade, and sediment. Pacific lamprey restoration, culvert replacement, wetland protection, and the removal of invasive species are routine work for tribal habitat staff. On the Plains, restoration of bison herds is paired with restoring native grasses and managing grazing and fire. In the Arctic and Subarctic, Indigenous organizations seek protected areas and land-use planning that safeguard calving grounds and corridors, and in the Great Lakes, wild rice beds depend on water quality that is threatened by mining and other development, a subject developed in Chapter 136.

Restoration is slow and uncertain. A dam can be removed in months, but forests, floodplains, and soils respond over decades, and success may be gauged by multiple indicators, such as fish returns, water temperature, cultural access, and community participation. Climate change complicates restoration by shifting temperatures, flows, and animal ranges (Chapter 138). Indigenous nations therefore emphasize monitoring, adaptive management, and long time horizons.

A watershed project may begin with the identification of a particular limiting factor. If a road crossing blocks access to a productive tributary, restoring passage can improve the amount of habitat available to fish. If the stream is too warm or lacks suitable spawning conditions, passage alone will not necessarily produce the intended biological result. Indigenous natural resources departments therefore often work across several kinds of projects, from negotiating access to land and monitoring water to revegetation and the protection of existing habitats. Restoration priorities have to be coordinated with the legal authority of landowners and governments outside tribal territory.

The Nisqually estuary restoration illustrates the importance of connections between fresh and salt water. Juvenile salmon may need productive shallow-water habitats as they adjust to marine conditions. Reconnecting tidal channels and restoring wetlands can support those functions, but the benefits depend on the species and population involved, the quality of the habitat, and other conditions elsewhere in the watershed. The same reasoning applies to a bison landscape: the availability of grass is not the only factor in maintaining a herd if its range is fragmented, water is limited, or animals cannot be transferred as needed.

For managers, identifying success requires a baseline and a time horizon. A count of restored acres is an indicator of work completed, not necessarily an indicator of ecological function. A rising number of juvenile fish may be encouraging without proving an increase in returning adults. A community may also regard access for ceremonial harvesting or the recovery of a culturally important plant as a meaningful outcome. A good evaluation identifies these different objectives explicitly, records the evidence for each, and revisits decisions as results emerge.

Section 135.17

Tribal Fisheries and Wildlife Departments

Environmental sovereignty requires practical capacity as well as legal recognition. In the United States, tribal natural resources departments typically include fisheries and wildlife divisions staffed by biologists, technicians, and enforcement officers, supported by hatcheries, laboratories, habitat restoration crews, harvest-monitoring programs, and community education. They report to tribal councils and often to committees of elders, harvesters, or community members, and they negotiate with state, federal, and sometimes international agencies. In Canada, First Nations fisheries and lands departments, Aboriginal Aquatic Resource and Oceans Management groups, and Indigenous Guardians and Watchmen programs perform comparable roles, and in Nunavut, Alaska, Greenland, and Mexico, equivalent functions are organized through the institutions described earlier.

Funding is a persistent problem. Many tribal programs depend on federal contracts and grants, such as agreements under the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975, and on specific programs, such as the fish and wildlife mitigation funded through the Bonneville Power Administration in the Columbia Basin, the Pacific Coastal Salmon Recovery Fund, and Canadian programs for Aboriginal fisheries and guardians. Short-term funding makes it hard to retain staff or conduct long-term monitoring. Building professional expertise has involved training Indigenous biologists, partnerships with universities and tribal colleges (Chapter 124), and the development of intertribal technical commissions that pool scarce expertise.

Accountability runs in two directions. Departments are accountable to external partners and funders through reporting and scientific standards, and to their own communities through council oversight, public meetings, and cultural protocols. Tension can arise when technical regulations clash with community practices, or when quotas affect families who depend on income from fishing. The institutions' legitimacy depends on their capacity to combine technical credibility with community trust.

The development of natural resources departments can be traced through the administrative requirements of treaty implementation. Before the modern self-determination era, many federal and state agencies treated Indigenous harvesters as subjects of regulations drafted elsewhere. When tribal governments asserted their own regulatory authority, they needed a way to draft rules, notify members, record harvests, collect ecological evidence, and participate in negotiations. These functions could be located within a single department or distributed among separate programs. The choice of structure depends on the nation’s constitution, governing bodies, resources, size, and the legal and ecological environment in which it operates.

CRITFC provides a concrete example of the advantages of shared infrastructure. Its fisheries science work supports the member nations in understanding fish populations and managing a river system whose ecological scale exceeds the boundaries of any one reservation. A technical program may provide stock information and assessments; policy staff may represent common positions in intergovernmental meetings; enforcement staff may help administer access and applicable rules at treaty fishing sites. Each of these functions has a distinct purpose. A scientific finding does not enact a tribal fishing regulation, and a policy position does not itself constitute a biological estimate. The governments must decide how information is incorporated into their own decisions and the wider management arrangements.

The institutional pattern can be compared with GLIFWC, which provides technical, enforcement, and policy support across the ceded territories of several Ojibwe nations. Sharing specialist personnel can make sustained research and enforcement more feasible than requiring every member government to maintain separate teams for every species. It also permits common methods for recording harvests and estimating populations. Yet the commission is accountable to its member nations; treaty rights do not become the property of a technical organization. This is why a distinction between shared operational capacity and the location of legal authority is essential.

Institutional development involves financial history. A grant may fund a particular restoration project for a limited number of years, while monitoring a population through several generations requires a continuing commitment. A department may need permanent personnel, training, vehicles, equipment, storage for samples, and reliable data systems. If the resources are financed through separate programs with different requirements, staff may have to spend considerable time administering funds rather than undertaking ecological work. Long-term funding and retention of expertise are therefore not peripheral management issues: they influence whether a nation can defend its positions with its own evidence and carry out its decisions consistently.

Accountability to the community requires more than reporting to another government. Harvesters may need to know how limits were determined, when rules apply, and how to raise objections. Elders and knowledge holders may have responsibilities that are not reflected in professional credentials. Institutional design must allow technical and community authority to interact while respecting the distinct roles of each. A department can thus become a site where a nation develops and exercises its own public administration, even while operating under complex external legal and financial constraints.

Section 135.18

Indigenous Science, Ecological Monitoring, and Adaptive Management

Contemporary Indigenous fisheries and wildlife management combines multiple ways of knowing. Community observation, oral history, and long experience on the land provide information about run timing, animal condition, ice, and change over generations. Indigenous institutions also conduct and commission surveys: electrofishing and netting, spawning ground counts, aerial and photographic surveys of caribou, GPS collaring and telemetry, genetic stock identification for fish, water-quality sampling, remote sensing, geographic information systems, and ecological modeling. In Nunavut, Inuit knowledge is formally part of the management process, and in the Porcupine and Bathurst herds, community monitors and harvesters contribute observations alongside agency surveys.

Adaptive management, the practice of adjusting decisions as monitoring reveals results, aligns closely with long-standing Indigenous practice of responding to observed change, though it is formalized differently. Different methods have strengths and limits. Aerial surveys cover large areas but provide snapshots; harvest data reveal use but not abundance; genetic tools identify populations but require laboratory capacity; community observation records patterns over long periods but may not be systematically documented. Some northern communities have raised concerns about collaring and handling animals, which shows that research methods themselves raise ethical and cultural questions. Importantly, Indigenous knowledge does not need external validation to be legitimate; the point of combining approaches is that decision makers benefit from multiple lines of evidence and that Indigenous nations decide how knowledge about their territories is used.

Data governance is therefore part of sovereignty. Nations decide who may collect, store, interpret, and publish information about fish, wildlife, and harvests, including sensitive information such as locations of spawning areas or hunting grounds. First Nations in Canada have articulated principles of ownership, control, access, and possession over community data, and such principles are increasingly applied to environmental data. The general discussion of Indigenous knowledge sovereignty belongs to Chapter 145.

Historical evidence about a fishery often begins with records created for a purpose other than conservation. Commercial landings can indicate changes in market activity, but a decline in landings may reflect a change in fishing effort, price, regulation, or available fish. Conversely, new equipment may maintain catches while abundance declines. Catch data therefore require interpretation together with information about effort and population surveys. Indigenous oral histories and the observations of harvesters can provide context for changes in fish size, timing, and distribution that the commercial records fail to describe. Neither kind of evidence should be treated as automatically complete.

Modern genetic stock identification demonstrates another difference between measurement and inference. A sample collected in a mixed fishery can be compared with reference populations to estimate which stocks contribute to the catch. The quality and coverage of reference data affect the reliability of the result. The technique can help managers understand which populations bear the effects of a particular harvest, but it does not settle the legal or ethical question of how that harvest should be allocated. Similarly, aerial photographs of caribou provide observations that must be interpreted using assumptions about aggregation, visibility, and the age classes included. The three caribou examples in Section 135.11 illustrate why an estimate should be accompanied by its survey date and definition.

An adaptive management approach can be illustrated by a tribal fishery considering a temporary closure. Managers identify the objective, such as maintaining sufficient spawning fish; collect evidence on abundance and harvesting; decide how much fishing can proceed under the applicable rules; and monitor the results. If returns differ from forecasts, later decisions may need to change. This process is not an experiment on a population without obligations to harvesters: a closure has economic and social consequences, and managers must consider the uncertainty of their evidence and explain the decision. Community knowledge can identify effects that a narrowly designed technical assessment overlooks.

The governance of data is especially important in an era when environmental information can be stored and distributed rapidly. Publishing the location of a sensitive harvesting site can make it vulnerable to outside exploitation, and a recording of knowledge intended for a particular audience may not be appropriate for unrestricted circulation. Nations may choose to share aggregated monitoring results with partner agencies while retaining control over detailed records. Research agreements can specify what information will be collected, who may access it, and what will be made public. These are decisions about institutional authority and responsibilities to communities, not merely about software or data storage. Section 135.19 turns to the political arrangements in which that authority is exercised.

Section 135.19

Conservation, Harvesting Rights, and Competing Authority

Conflicts over living resources persist. Treaty tribes, commercial fleets, sport harvesters, and governments compete for shares of limited fish. Endangered species listings, such as those of Columbia Basin salmon and of caribou, affect habitat decisions and harvest. Governments claim authority to restrict harvest for conservation, while Indigenous nations assert rights and their own regulatory authority. Habitat destruction by industries and infrastructure often lies outside the control of the harvesters most affected. And Indigenous governments themselves face difficult decisions: whether to close a fishery or caribou hunt in a low year when families depend on the harvest, how to weigh economic opportunities against habitat, and how to allocate scarce resources within their own communities. Several nations have chosen voluntary restraint, including tribal fishery closures and Dene caribou harvest reductions, and these decisions are exercises of governing authority.

The vocabulary of shared authority is imprecise, and students should distinguish its forms. Consultation means that government seeks input but decides. Cooperation involves coordination without shared decision-making. Advisory participation, as on the International Porcupine Caribou Board, gives a body the right to recommend. Delegated authority allows a government to authorize a body to decide within defined limits. Treaty-established public government is a distinct arrangement: the Nunavut Wildlife Management Board derives its mandate from Article 5 of the Nunavut Agreement and makes decisions subject to the agreement’s specific ministerial review procedures. Shared decision-making requires agreement of both parties or court-supervised allocation, as in Washington under the Boldt framework. The exercise of inherent sovereignty occurs when a nation regulates its own harvest and members under its own law, as tribal governments do through codes and as Mi'kmaw communities assert through their own regulation. The same word, co-management, may describe any of these.

Comparisons reveal that the term co-management covers arrangements with profoundly different histories. The international Porcupine board makes recommendations to the treaty parties; its authority does not include the power to enact a binding harvest regulation. The Nunavut Wildlife Management Board, by contrast, makes decisions within the procedures of a modern treaty. The tribal governments implementing treaty fisheries in Washington exercise their own regulatory authority within a court-supervised and intergovernmental system. These differences can be assessed by asking who can initiate a decision, who may reject it, who implements it, and who is accountable for the outcome.

Authority over harvesting and authority over habitat may be divided in different ways. A tribal government can restrict the catch by its members and invest in fishery science but may have little control over development on lands upstream. An international advisory board can recommend habitat protection but cannot itself prevent a national government from authorizing a project. An arrangement that effectively shares harvest decisions may thus leave major causes of decline outside Indigenous jurisdiction. This does not make harvest governance unimportant; it shows why its effectiveness depends on the wider political economy of land, water, and infrastructure.

The moderate-livelihood controversy in Atlantic Canada adds the problem of competing interpretations of the relationship between rights and regulation. Indigenous communities have challenged the adequacy of government licensing arrangements as the sole means of implementing treaty rights. Commercial harvesters may emphasize the need for conservation and a predictable regulatory framework, while federal authorities must address their legal obligations and responsibilities for managing the fishery. These positions cannot be evaluated solely by assigning each group an equal share of decision-making power. The treaty relationship is a legally significant distinction, and the history of exclusion has shaped the resources and institutional capacity available to each group.

The most useful measure of a co-management arrangement is not its title but its operation. A framework may improve information sharing yet leave final decisions unchanged; another may create meaningful opportunities to make decisions independently or jointly. Indigenous governments can use cooperation strategically without abandoning their claims to broader authority. Conversely, a formal seat on a board may yield little influence without staff, resources, access to information, and enforceable procedures. These conditions explain why the reconstruction of institutions discussed in Section 135.17 is inseparable from the legal analysis of this section.

Historical Debate

Does Co-Management Strengthen Indigenous Sovereignty or Preserve External Governmental Control?

The question: Since the 1970s, governments and Indigenous nations have created co-management arrangements for fisheries and wildlife. Do these arrangements enhance Indigenous authority, or do they bring Indigenous participants into governmental frameworks that preserve ultimate control with the state?

Interpretation A — Co-management strengthens sovereignty: Proponents argue that recognition as a manager, access to funding and data, and a formal role in decisions give Indigenous nations real authority they previously lacked, and that arrangements such as Boldt-era co-management and the Nunavut boards embed rights in durable institutions.
Interpretation B — Co-management preserves external control: Critics argue that when governments retain final authority, boards are advisory, funding is conditional, and Indigenous knowledge is processed through governmental categories, co-management can legitimize external control and shift Indigenous leaders into administrative roles that blunt claims to self-government.
Interpretation C — Outcomes depend on design: Others argue that the answer depends on specifics, including who appoints members, whether decisions bind governments, how funding is controlled, and whether the arrangement rests on treaty or agreement, and that co-management can coexist with parallel exercise of Indigenous regulatory authority.

Evidence: Statutes and agreements, board decisions and the rate at which recommendations are accepted, court rulings, funding records, and community assessments.

Points of disagreement: The debate concerns interpretation and terminology as much as facts: scholars and communities differ on whether the measure of sovereignty is decision-making power, institutional capacity, legal recognition, or cultural outcomes.

Why it matters: The answer shapes whether Indigenous nations pursue negotiated shared management or insist on regulatory authority of their own, and how courts and governments interpret treaty rights.

Section 135.20

Treaty Stewardship and the Future of Indigenous North America

The histories of salmon, buffalo, and caribou reveal a shared pattern even though each is distinct. Indigenous societies governed living resources as part of their political, economic, and religious lives. Colonial expansion depleted resources, fragmented habitats, restricted Indigenous harvesting, and weakened economic and political independence. Indigenous nations resisted through law, protest, negotiation, and persistence, and, with court victories and modern agreements, built institutions that translated rights into authority. Today they lead restoration programs that connect ecological recovery with food sovereignty, ceremony, language, and governance.

The meaning of this history is political. Healthy fisheries and wildlife populations make treaty and Aboriginal rights meaningful; capable institutions allow nations to exercise authority; and cultural continuity depends on the ability to harvest, prepare, and share foods in accordance with community law. These recoveries are partial. Many salmon runs remain low, buffalo occupy a fraction of their former range, several caribou herds have declined, and climate change adds new uncertainty. Extractive industries, examined in Chapters 136 and 137, present continuing pressure on habitats, and Chapter 138 examines climate adaptation.

Each of the three principal case studies demonstrates a different spatial problem. Salmon depend on a connected river and ocean system; their recovery requires intervention at multiple stages of a life cycle. Buffalo restoration depends on access to land and the ability to maintain herds under contemporary range and animal-health conditions. Caribou governance involves the migration of animals across extensive seasonal ranges and, in some cases, across international borders. The differences explain why a successful organization in one region cannot simply reproduce its management model in another.

Nevertheless, the cases share a historical lesson. A right reserved in a treaty may remain legally meaningful during decades of exclusion, but its exercise depends on both the survival of the resource and the capacity of the nation to act. Ecological recovery can create new opportunities for harvesting, yet the distribution of those opportunities depends on law and institutions. Likewise, community education and the sharing of food sustain relationships that cannot be measured by population estimates alone. The relationship between cultural continuity and environmental governance is therefore reciprocal: healthy resources support the community, and community institutions support the protection of those resources.

The following chapter examines the other side of that relationship. Mines, energy projects, and other extractive developments may promise employment, public revenue, or energy supplies while also imposing costs on fisheries, wildlife, and the territories that sustain them. Indigenous nations have responded in different ways, ranging from participation and negotiation to opposition. Understanding those choices requires the history developed here: the ecological systems at stake, the legal authority asserted, and the institutions through which nations evaluate and respond to proposed development.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the restoration of Indigenous authority over fisheries, wildlife, and habitats is both an ecological recovery process and an exercise of Indigenous sovereignty. It has examined salmon, bison, and caribou as principal case studies, supplemented by Great Lakes fisheries, Atlantic and Arctic resources, and the Cucapá of the Colorado River Delta.

Living resources sustained Indigenous civilizations as economic, cultural, and political foundations. Salmon supported seasonally concentrated fisheries and trade, bison anchored Plains economies, caribou sustained northern societies, and fish, marine mammals, and wild rice supported other regions. Relationships with animals involved reciprocal obligations expressed through ceremony and custom.

Indigenous societies governed harvesting through customary institutions, including family and house territories, ceremonial leadership, hunt societies, sharing norms, and intergenerational knowledge. These systems differed from modern bureaucratic wildlife conservation, though historians should avoid romanticizing them and should recognize regional variation and evidence of local depletion in some cases.

Colonial expansion disrupted these systems through commercial exploitation, agricultural and industrial development, dams, dispossession, and regulations that restricted Indigenous harvesting. The loss of resources weakened economic independence and political autonomy, though Indigenous communities contested these changes and preserved knowledge and claims.

Treaties and legal doctrines provide the foundation for contemporary authority. In the United States, treaty rights are understood as reserved by nations; in Canada, Aboriginal and treaty rights are constitutionally protected and modern agreements create management regimes; in Mexico and Greenland different frameworks apply. Treaty rights depend on the existence of resources and accessible habitat, and they coexist with justified conservation regulation.

In the Pacific Northwest, the Boldt decision and related rulings recognized treaty tribes as co-managers and prompted the creation of institutions such as the Northwest Indian Fisheries Commission and the Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission. These institutions demonstrate that legal rights became operational through administrative capacity.

Dams transformed rivers and fisheries, and Indigenous advocacy contributed to the Klamath dam removals completed in 2024. The achievement opened habitat but did not by itself restore the runs; recovery depends on water quality, flow allocation, disease, and ocean conditions, and will unfold over years to decades.

Beyond the Northwest, the Marshall decisions recognized a treaty right to a moderate livelihood in Atlantic Canada, with regulation permitted for justified purposes. The Great Lakes Indian Fish and Wildlife Commission implemented Ojibwe treaty rights, and Mexican Cucapá communities show how Indigenous fishing rights are contested under environmental and agrarian law.

Buffalo restoration involves food, ceremony, education, land recovery, and grassland ecology. The InterTribal Buffalo Council and the Buffalo Treaty illustrate institutional reconstruction and nation-to-nation cooperation, while distinctions among conservation, commercial, and cultural herds clarify competing objectives.

Caribou illustrate the complexity of migratory species. Herds differ in population trends, and the Porcupine agreements show how transboundary governance can rely on advisory cooperation rather than shared sovereignty. The 1987 agreement recognizes Indigenous harvesting while leaving regulatory authority with governments.

Arctic and Alaska institutions, including the Nunavut Wildlife Management Board, demonstrate substantial devolution with retained governmental authority. Debates about co-management concern whether such arrangements strengthen sovereignty or preserve external control, and answers depend on design, funding, and decision-making power.

Finally, the chapter has shown that tribal and First Nation natural resources institutions, Indigenous science, and habitat restoration together form part of the reconstruction of Indigenous economic systems, cultural relationships, and political authority. Recovery is incomplete and contested, and the chapters that follow examine the industrial and climatic pressures that will shape its future.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the recovery of fisheries, wildlife, and habitats is not only a conservation story. Rivers, herds, and fish are the material basis of Indigenous food systems, ceremony, and economic life, and the authority to manage them is an element of self-government. The institutions examined here, from intertribal commissions to wildlife boards and tribal departments, are among the most concrete expressions of Indigenous sovereignty in the modern era.

The chapter also reveals the limits of rights without resources. Treaty language, constitutional protection, and land-claim agreements made little difference where rivers were dammed, herds destroyed, or habitats fragmented. This is why the chapter pairs legal history with ecological history and why recovery must be measured in fish returns, herd health, habitat condition, and community access, not only in court victories.

The three principal cases illustrate different challenges: watershed governance for salmon, land access and herd management for bison, and transboundary coordination for caribou. Each shows that Indigenous nations act as scientists, regulators, negotiators, and cultural stewards, and that their approaches vary. Where governments share authority, the design of that sharing determines whether it strengthens or constrains self-government.

Later chapters carry these themes forward. Chapter 136 examines the extractive industries that threaten habitats; Chapter 137 examines infrastructure and land defense; Chapter 138 turns to climate change, which alters every ecosystem discussed here. Chapter 145 returns to knowledge and data sovereignty, and Chapter 148 considers the evolving relationships between Indigenous nations and states.

The central lesson is continuity through change. Indigenous nations did not simply endure the loss of living resources; they contested it, built new institutions, and now lead restoration. The story is unfinished, and recovery is partial. But in rivers reopened, herds returned, and harvests governed by Indigenous law, the chapter shows that the continuity of Indigenous nations and the health of the ecosystems they depend on are bound together.
