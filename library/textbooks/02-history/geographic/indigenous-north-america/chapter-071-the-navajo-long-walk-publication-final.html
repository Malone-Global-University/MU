The Navajo Long Walk

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

Between 1863 and 1866, thousands of Diné (Navajo) men, women, and children were driven from the country between their four sacred mountains and marched hundreds of miles to a military reservation on the Pecos River. Within weeks of the treaty ending their confinement, they were walking home. Both facts belong to the same history.

The Long Walk is among the best-known episodes in the history of Indigenous North America, and also among the most frequently flattened. In popular memory it often appears as a single march led by a single famous soldier, ending in a single defeat. The historical record is more complicated and more disturbing. The Long Walk was a series of forced journeys, made on different routes and at different times, by families who had been hunted, starved, and captured under a military strategy designed to make life at home impossible. Some Diné were never captured at all. Those who were deported entered a confinement the Diné remember as Hwéeldi, a name associated with suffering, and they survived it as families, clans, and a people with their own political voice.

This chapter takes up the story where Chapter 69 left it. That chapter examined the American conquest of the Mexican north and the transfer of jurisdiction that followed the U.S.–Mexico War. Here the question is what the United States did with that claimed jurisdiction in Diné country. The chapter's central problem can be stated plainly. How did the United States transform conflict with the Diné into a campaign of destruction and forced incarceration, and how did Diné resistance, cultural continuity, and diplomatic determination secure their return to ancestral homelands in 1868?

The answer begins with the Diné themselves. The Diné of the 1840s were not a unified state with a single ruler, and they were not a scattering of "raiders" in the colonial imagination. They were a large, regionally dispersed people organized through clans, extended families, and local leaders, who farmed, herded sheep and goats, wove, traded, and kept a profound ceremonial relationship with a landscape bounded by four sacred mountains. They were also participants in a long, mutually violent borderland economy of trade, raiding, captive-taking, and diplomacy with Pueblo, Hispano, Ute, and Apache neighbors. Section 71.1 and Section 71.2 establish what was at stake before the campaigns begin.

The chapter then follows the escalation of American military power: the treaties of 1846 and 1849, the founding of Fort Defiance in 1851, the crises of the late 1850s, and the Civil War, which both distracted and then militarized the Union's Southwest. Under Brigadier General James H. Carleton, the army first confined the Mescalero Apache at a new post, Fort Sumner, and then, in 1863, turned the same plan on the Diné. The principal weapon was not a decisive battle. It was the destruction of crops, orchards, and herds, which transformed a war the army could not simply win in the field into one that hunger could win for it.

The historical evidence for this episode is unusually layered. Military correspondence, treaty texts, and Indian Bureau reports record what officers intended and what they chose to report. Diné oral history, recorded in family memory and in collections made decades later, preserves experiences those reports omit or minimize: separation, sexual violence, the deaths of children, and the decisions of families who hid. A responsible account must hold both bodies of evidence in view, and must say honestly where they cannot be reconciled and where even the numbers are uncertain.

The chapter does not end with confinement. Bosque Redondo failed on the army's own terms: it did not feed its prisoners, it did not make them self-supporting farmers, and it cost far more than Congress was willing to pay. But its failure was not automatic. It took Diné leaders, Diné women, and ordinary families, who refused to accept a permanent exile and made that refusal known in 1868 to General William T. Sherman and Commissioner Samuel F. Tappan. The Treaty of June 1, 1868, which allowed them to return to a defined part of their homeland, was a diplomatic achievement and a coercive compromise at once. The sovereignty it addressed was not created by the treaty. It was the continuing authority of an Indigenous nation that the United States had tried to destroy and then had to negotiate with.

Three cautions govern what follows. First, the Long Walk was not one march, and no single distance, route, or death toll describes it. Second, the Diné and Mescalero Apache experiences at Bosque Redondo were different histories and must be kept apart. Third, the story is neither one of simple conquest nor one of invincibility. The campaigns inflicted devastating losses and forced thousands into captivity; the Diné survived as a people and negotiated their return. Chapter 71 is about the space between those two statements.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe Diné political organization, kinship, economy, and sacred geography before 1846, and explain why Diné leadership was dispersed rather than centralized.
  2. Trace the relationship between Diné communities and Spanish, Mexican, Pueblo, Ute, and Apache neighbors before and after the American occupation, distinguishing particular acts of violence from collective judgments about peoples.
  3. Analyze why the treaties of 1846 and 1849 recorded diplomatic commitments without producing lasting peace.
  4. Explain how the establishment of Fort Defiance and disputes over grazing lands produced escalating conflict in the 1850s.
  5. Assess the effects of the Civil War on American military capacity and Indigenous policy in New Mexico.
  6. Distinguish the policy-making responsibility of James H. Carleton from the operational responsibility of Christopher "Kit" Carson.
  7. Explain how the destruction of crops, orchards, and livestock functioned as an instrument of forced removal.
  8. Evaluate why surrender under threat of starvation should not be interpreted as voluntary migration.
  9. Reconstruct the Long Walk as a series of marches, and differentiate deaths on the marches from deaths during confinement and from broader estimates.
  10. Compare the experiences of the Diné and Mescalero Apache at Bosque Redondo.
  11. Interpret the Treaty of 1868 as both a diplomatic achievement and a coerced compromise, and distinguish Diné sovereignty from federal recognition of a reservation.
  12. Compare official military records with Diné oral history as evidence, identifying the strengths and limits of each.

Historical Context

The Diné speak an Athabaskan language, a family whose other branches are found in the western subarctic and, in the Southwest, among the Apache peoples (see Chapters 30 and 51). Diné history places the people's emergence and formative experiences in a homeland usually called Dinétah, in the region of the upper San Juan River in what is now northwestern New Mexico, and in a landscape marked by four sacred mountains. Archaeologists and historians continue to discuss when Athabaskan-speaking ancestors of the Diné and Apache arrived in the Southwest and by what routes. What is clear is that by the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries Diné communities had absorbed agricultural, ceremonial, and craft knowledge from Pueblo neighbors, acquired sheep, goats, and horses through the Spanish colonial economy, and expanded across a wide territory in what are now Arizona, New Mexico, Utah, and Colorado.

That expansion occurred in a region already shaped by Pueblo villages, Spanish and later Mexican settlements along the Rio Grande, and the mobile economies of Ute, Apache, and Comanche peoples (Chapters 43, 44, 50, and 51). Exchange, alliance, intermarriage, captive-taking, and raiding bound these societies to one another. Captives, livestock, and trade goods circulated in a regional economy in which almost every community both suffered and inflicted violence, and in which a thriving market in Indigenous captives persisted (see Chapter 53). Under this system Diné families lost relatives to New Mexican households, and New Mexican and Pueblo communities lost livestock and people to Diné raids.

In 1846, during the U.S.–Mexico War, American forces occupied New Mexico, and the 1848 treaty transferred the region to the United States (Chapter 69). From a Diné perspective, however, a foreign government's paper claim to jurisdiction did not erase a Diné political order that had never been conquered by Spain or Mexico. American officials nonetheless regarded Diné territory as part of the United States and treated Diné attacks on New Mexican settlements as a problem of "Indian relations" to be solved by treaties, forts, and, eventually, force. The chapter's first division explains how this clash of assumptions produced a frontier militarized well before the Civil War.

Two interpretive errors need to be avoided. The first treats the Diné as an undifferentiated "tribe" that either wholly resisted or wholly submitted. Diné leaders differed in strategy and in the regions they governed; some pursued peace for long periods, and others fought. The second treats the New Mexican population as a colonial monolith. Hispano settlers, Pueblo communities, and Utes had their own interests, vulnerabilities, and divisions, and some of their members profited from the war and the captive trade. The history of the Long Walk is, in part, a history of how a United States army exploited these regional rivalries while deciding for itself what outcome it wanted.

Diné Homelands and the Expansion of American Power

Section 71.1

Diné Peoples, Homelands, and the Sacred Geography of Dinétah

The people called Navajo in English and in the Spanish documents that first applied the term call themselves Diné, "the People." The name Navajo is an exonym of uncertain origin that has become common in federal law and everyday usage, and this chapter uses both terms, preferring Diné where the Diné point of view is central. The Diné language belongs to the Athabaskan family and is closely related to the languages of the Apache peoples of the Southwest, yet Diné and Apache communities developed distinct histories, ceremonial systems, and political relationships (Chapters 30 and 51).

Diné homeland geography is both physical and sacred. Diné tradition places the people within a country bounded by four sacred mountains, each associated with a direction, a color, and a set of ceremonial relationships. These are usually identified as Sisnaajiní (in the east, generally identified with Blanca Peak in Colorado), Tsoodził (in the south, Mount Taylor in New Mexico), Doko'oosłííd (in the west, the San Francisco Peaks in Arizona), and Dibé Nitsaa (in the north, in the La Plata range of Colorado). The mountains frame the landscape in which the Diné understand ceremony, prayer, and obligation. The country they enclose includes high desert, mesas, canyons, and forested ranges, with the Chuska Mountains and the canyons of Canyon de Chelly and Canyon del Muerto at its center. This is the "ancestral homeland" the chapter refers to, and its meaning cannot be reduced to the acreage defined by later treaties.

Diné social life was organized around matrilineal clans and extended families. Clan membership is traced through the mother, and clan relationships connected people across distant settlements and made hospitality and obligation possible among relatives who might never have met. Residence commonly centered on women's families, and women held and managed property, notably livestock and the products of weaving, to a degree that sharply contrasted with the legal position of women in the patriarchal households of New Mexican and American society. Women's authority within the family and the herd was a social fact, not an incidental detail, and it mattered when Diné women later pressed for return in 1868 (Section 71.18).

The Diné economy combined farming, herding, weaving, and trade. Families raised maize, beans, and squash, and in the canyons of the Chuska region they cultivated peach orchards, a fruit introduced through the Spanish colonial world. Sheep and goats, acquired through the Spanish economy, became central to family wealth and to a seasonal pattern of movement between summer and winter pastures. Weaving, practiced by women, produced goods for family use and for trade, and Diné textiles were exchanged across the Southwest. Because families relied on both fields and flocks, and moved among dispersed settlements, the Diné were politically and economically resilient and, as the army discovered, hard to subdue by a conventional campaign aimed at a capital or a stronghold.

Political life was similarly decentralized. Diné communities were led by naat'áanii, headmen whose authority rested on reputation, eloquence, kin networks, and the consent of those who chose to follow them. Leaders were often associated with a region and its families. There was no hereditary ruler, no standing national council with powers to bind every settlement, and no office that American officials could treat as the equivalent of a head of state. This structure had real strengths: it was flexible, it was hard to decapitate, and it permitted peace with some communities while war persisted with others. It also created the central diplomatic problem of the 1840s and 1850s. An agreement signed by one set of leaders did not bind other communities, and American and New Mexican officials repeatedly demanded accountability from men who lacked the authority to give it.

Historians reconstruct this world from Diné oral history and ceremonial knowledge, from archaeology in the San Juan basin and the canyons, from Spanish, Mexican, and American documents, and from ethnographic work done by outsiders and Diné scholars. Each has limits. The written record before 1846 was made almost entirely by Spanish and Mexican officials, missionaries, and soldiers, who described Diné communities mainly through their relations with New Mexican settlements. Diné history as the Diné themselves tell it is less reducible to such evidence, and in recent decades Diné historians have insisted that oral tradition, kinship memory, and women's experience be treated as primary evidence rather than supplements. This chapter attempts to honor that insistence while remaining careful about what each source can establish. What was at stake in 1863 was therefore not only territory but a way of life organized around kinship, livestock, fields, and a land that was understood as sacred.

Section 71.2

Diné Relations with Spanish and Mexican New Mexico

Long before 1846, the Diné lived in sustained relationship with Spanish and, after 1821, Mexican New Mexico. These relationships involved trade in livestock, textiles, hides, and other goods; military alliance and enmity; negotiated truces; and an enduring cycle of raiding, retaliation, and captive-taking. They were not the relationships of a "frontier tribe" with a distant authority. They were relationships among neighbors with competing interests and, often, kin ties.

New Mexico's economy relied on livestock, agriculture, and the exploitation of Indigenous labor. Hispano settlements and Pueblo communities along the Rio Grande and its tributaries held flocks and herds that were attractive targets for raiders from several peoples. Diné groups raided New Mexican livestock; New Mexican parties raided Diné flocks and took Diné people, especially women and children, for servitude. Spanish and Mexican officials sometimes sanctioned these expeditions and, through baptism and household service, folded captives into a system of dependent labor that historians describe through terms such as peonage and criadazgo (see Chapter 53). Captive-taking was thus not a Diné vice or a New Mexican vice. It was a feature of a regional system in which many peoples participated, and one that created grief and grievance in every community it touched.

The record of formal diplomacy is long. Spanish and Mexican officials negotiated with Diné headmen on many occasions, and these truces typically collapsed as the underlying conflicts over livestock, captives, and territory persisted. Spanish campaigns into the Diné country, including expeditions into the canyons of the Chuska region, left a memory of violence among the Diné, and Diné groups carried out their own raids. Pueblo communities such as Zuni, Laguna, and Jemez, Hopi villages, the Ute, and Apache peoples each stood in different relation to Diné communities: sometimes trading partners, sometimes enemies, sometimes both within a single decade. Many Pueblo and Ute communities would serve with American forces in the 1860s, motivated by their own grievances and opportunities.

Two cautions are needed. First, documented episodes of violence by particular Diné bands or New Mexican parties must not be converted into collective judgments about entire peoples. Both the later American claim that "the Navajos" were habitual raiders and the later romantic claim that Diné communities were uniformly peaceful distort the evidence. Different Diné communities had different relations with their neighbors, and many were engaged in farming and trade rather than raiding. Second, New Mexican communities were not uniformly hostile either; commerce, intermarriage, and personal friendship tied many families together. What the record supports is a borderland of mutual violence and mutual dependence in which the United States arrived in 1846 and, intentionally or not, took sides. The Americans inherited the complaints of New Mexican settlers, who presented them as grievances requiring redress, while they largely ignored Diné complaints about captives and stolen livestock. That asymmetry in whose losses counted shaped everything that followed.

Section 71.3

American Conquest, the 1846 Agreement, and the Treaty of 1849

American forces under Stephen Watts Kearny occupied New Mexico in August 1846. Kearny's proclamation promised protection to New Mexican residents against Indian raiders, a promise that placed the new government in the role of defender of the settlements against peoples such as the Diné, whom the proclamation treated as subjects of its authority. From the Diné point of view, an army they had never fought had suddenly claimed to be responsible for their conduct. Chapter 69 discusses the occupation itself; here its consequences for Diné relations matter.

On November 22, 1846, at Bear Springs, near present-day Fort Wingate, Diné leaders and a force under Colonel Alexander Doniphan concluded an agreement of peace and friendship. The agreement reflected the American desire to secure the Diné frontier while the army's attention lay elsewhere, and it reflected the willingness of some Diné leaders to end hostilities on terms that seemed to preserve their position. It did not produce a lasting peace. The signatories could not speak for all Diné communities, the underlying disputes over livestock and captives remained, and American authorities in New Mexico lacked the means, and often the intention, to restrain New Mexican raiders against Diné herds. Diné attacks on New Mexican settlements continued, as did New Mexican reprisals.

The next major diplomatic effort came in 1849. A military expedition under Lieutenant Colonel John M. Washington, accompanied by Indian Agent James S. Calhoun, marched into Diné country in late summer. During a parley in late August, a dispute over a horse said to have been taken from a New Mexican led soldiers to fire on the Diné present, and the elderly and respected leader Narbona was killed. Later accounts, in Diné memory and in the writing of some American observers, describe the desecration of his body. The killing of an older headman who had sought accommodation with the Americans left a deep mark on Diné memories of this period and on Diné trust in American promises.

Even so, on September 9, 1849, the expedition concluded a treaty in Canyon de Chelly with Diné leaders, among them Mariano Martinez and Chapitone, who were willing to continue negotiating. The treaty acknowledged the jurisdiction of the United States, promised an end to hostilities, and provided for the return of captives and stolen property, for American trade and military posts in Diné country, and for the future determination of boundaries. The commitments were framed as mutual, but their enforcement was not. A treaty drawn up by one side, in a language many Diné participants did not read, requiring headmen to return people and goods they might not control, imposed on its Diné signatories obligations they had no power to satisfy. The U.S. Senate gave its advice and consent on September 9, 1850, and the treaty was proclaimed on September 24, 1850; these later acts were separate from the 1849 field negotiation, and the text should be read as what it was: a record of what the American negotiators wanted recorded.

The treaty of 1849 therefore illustrates a pattern. Treaties could record diplomatic commitments without producing either lasting peace or uniform consent. Many Diné communities continued to graze their flocks and to defend their access to pasture; New Mexican raiders continued to take Diné livestock and people; and American officers continued to measure the Diné against a standard of control that the political structure of Diné society made impossible to meet. The next two decades would turn this structural mismatch into war.

Section 71.4

Fort Defiance, the 1850s Frontier, and Escalating Conflict

In September 1851 the army established Fort Defiance at the mouth of Cañon Bonito, in the heart of Diné country, as part of a broader American effort to place permanent garrisons on the Indian frontier of New Mexico. The post gave the United States a permanent military presence among the Diné, and it also took land. The pastures around Cañon Bonito had been grazed by Diné families, among them the herds of Manuelito's following, and the garrison claimed the best of them for its own animals and for hay. In effect, the army occupied the grazing grounds on which neighboring communities relied for their flocks.

The result was a continuing quarrel over pasture. Diné families objected to the army's claims; soldiers and officers regarded Diné animals grazing near the post as trespass. Over the 1850s, disputes over livestock multiplied. Soldiers shot or drove off animals; Diné and New Mexican parties continued to raid one another; and officers, agents, and local leaders negotiated, often at cross purposes. Some Diné leaders, notably Zarcillos Largos and others who favored accommodation, sought to maintain peace. Others, such as Manuelito, resisted the army's claim to the pasturage. The frontier was neither uniformly peaceful nor uniformly at war, but over time the incidents accumulated, and each one made the next harder to settle.

In 1858 a crisis exposed the fragility of the arrangement. The killing of an enslaved man who served an army officer at Fort Defiance, together with disputes over pasturage, led to demands for the surrender of the offender and to a military expedition into the Diné country under Colonel Benjamin Bonneville. A truce followed at the end of the year. This episode is usually told from the American side, which treats it as a Diné outrage met by a measured response. Diné accounts instead emphasize the army's seizure of grazing lands and the destruction of Diné livestock. The two framings are not simply opposed. They reflect the fact that each side counted its own losses and read the other's actions as aggression, and the surviving documents reflect principally the American officers' claims.

On April 30, 1860, Diné forces, in a very large body that contemporary and later estimates place in the hundreds, and under the leadership of Manuelito and Barboncito among others, attacked Fort Defiance in a determined assault that very nearly overran the post before it was repulsed. American authorities responded with campaigns under Colonel Edward R. S. Canby, and a truce was reached in early 1861. For Diné participants the attack was a military response to the occupation of their pastures. For American commanders it demonstrated that the Diné were a military threat requiring decisive action.

This history matters because it contradicts the notion that the Long Walk arose suddenly from the Civil War. The violence of the early 1860s developed from competing territorial interests, the permanent presence of troops on contested ground, an unresolved system of raiding and captive-taking in which American authorities took one side, and the failure of treaties to define who could speak for whom. It was a militarized frontier before Carleton arrived. What changed in 1862 and 1863 was not the existence of conflict but the willingness of the army's leadership to pursue it to the point of removing an entire people from their land.

The fort's effects also spread beyond its immediate stockade. Roads, supply contracts, horse pastures, and the demand for wood and hay tied the garrison to nearby settlements and grazing country. These logistical needs turned an asserted federal right of protection into recurring interference with Diné economic life. At the same time, individual officers and Diné leaders continued to negotiate, showing that the establishment of a post did not make war inevitable on every occasion. A historically precise account should follow how particular incidents accumulated: livestock taken or shot, a demand for restitution rejected, a truce agreed and then violated, or a retaliatory expedition sent into a district whose residents had not approved the original offense. These local sequences help explain how political distrust became institutionalized long before Carleton's 1863 removal orders.

Division II

The Road to Forced Removal

Section 71.5

The Civil War, New Mexico, and the Militarization of Indigenous Policy

In 1861 the American Civil War reached New Mexico. Many regular army officers resigned to join the Confederacy, regular troops were withdrawn or reorganized, and the Union military in the territory depended increasingly on volunteer regiments raised among the New Mexican population. In the winter of 1861–1862 a Confederate force under Henry Hopkins Sibley moved up the Rio Grande from Texas. After fighting at Valverde in February 1862, the Confederates occupied Albuquerque and Santa Fe, but a Union victory in the battles around Glorieta Pass in March 1862, in which Union troops destroyed the Confederate supply train, forced Sibley's army into retreat.

The Civil War in New Mexico was therefore a short and geographically limited conflict. But it had large consequences for Indigenous peoples. First, the Union army in New Mexico was reconstituted around volunteer units, among them the First New Mexico Volunteers, many of whose members had personal grievances over Diné and Apache raids and a direct interest in ending them. Second, the arrival in 1862 of the California Column, a force of volunteers under Brigadier General James H. Carleton, brought to New Mexico a commander of strong views and substantial resources. Carleton assumed command of the Department of New Mexico in the autumn of 1862 and ruled under conditions of wartime emergency that gave him extensive latitude.

American commanders interpreted the defeat of the Confederates as an opportunity. Having restored military capacity, they could now pursue the "Indian problem" systematically, rather than episodically, with forces larger than those that had been available to officers of the 1850s. It was a choice. Union officers might have used the recovered strength to negotiate, to restrain New Mexican raiders, or to strengthen existing treaties. Carleton chose to subdue and relocate. His reasoning combined genuine military concerns, a belief that mobile Indigenous peoples could be made peaceful only by concentrating and supervising them, and an assumption, common to his generation of officers, that Indigenous societies should be transformed into settled, agricultural, and Christian communities under American control.

It is important to keep the several conflicts distinct. The national Civil War was one conflict, between the Union and the Confederacy. The wars of the Diné, the Mescalero Apache, Pueblo communities, and New Mexican settlers were overlapping but separate conflicts with their own causes and chronologies, and they continued during and after the national war. Similarly, the Dakota War of 1862 and the events at Sand Creek in 1864 (Chapters 73 and 74) occurred in other regions during the same years, and they show that the Civil War era as a whole saw an intensification of military campaigns against Indigenous nations across the continent. Carleton's policy in New Mexico belongs to that wider pattern but also to a local history of frontier conflict that preceded it.

Section 71.6

James H. Carleton, the Mescalero Campaign, and the Bosque Redondo Plan

James H. Carleton, a career army officer, arrived in New Mexico with the California Column and replaced Edward R. S. Canby as department commander in the autumn of 1862. He had firm convictions about Indian policy and the authority to act on them. His first target was the Mescalero Apache, a people whose homeland lay in the mountains and plains of southern New Mexico, and whose communities had been in recurring conflict with New Mexican settlers and the army. Carleton ordered Colonel Christopher "Kit" Carson to lead the First New Mexico Volunteers into Mescalero country and instructed that Mescalero men be treated as enemies, with no negotiation to precede military pressure. Historians have generally read these orders as a deliberately harsh directive; Carson, for his part, accepted the surrender of Mescalero leaders who came to Fort Stanton seeking peace rather than conduct a campaign of extermination.

The Mescalero campaign must be understood as a distinct history. The Mescalero were an independent Apache people with their own leaders, territories, and strategies. Facing the army's pressure, many Mescalero leaders sought to end the fighting and to secure the safety of their families, and in late 1862 and early 1863 hundreds were moved from the Fort Stanton region to a new post, Fort Sumner, on the Pecos River. They were not a prelude to Diné history. Their experience, discussed more fully in Section 71.15, was their own.

Fort Sumner was developed in 1862–1863 at a place called Bosque Redondo, a stand of cottonwoods on the Pecos River in eastern New Mexico, selected by Carleton's officers as the site of a reservation. Carleton's plan was ambitious. He proposed to concentrate the Mescalero, and later the Diné, on a tract of arable land under military supervision, to feed them until crops could be raised, to teach farming, and to provide religious and secular instruction. He argued that confining Indigenous peoples in a single place would end the cycle of raiding and retaliation, free New Mexico's settlements from attack, and open the lands they had occupied to American mining and settlement. The plan treated the transformation of mobile, locally autonomous societies into dependent, supervised agricultural populations as both humane and practical. It did so without consulting the people whose lives it would reorder and without testing the site's ability to sustain thousands of people.

The Bosque Redondo proposal also rested on a narrow understanding of what counted as a viable livelihood. Carleton evaluated the land primarily as irrigable acreage and imagined that labor, seed, and military supervision would transform it into a productive community. That vision abstracted farming from the wider social and ecological systems that supported Diné and Mescalero households. For Diné families, cultivation in sheltered valleys worked alongside sheep grazing, seasonal movement, trading relationships, and access to several elevations and kinds of water. For Mescalero families, mobility across mountains and plains offered different resources at different seasons. A single, distant location on the Pecos could not replace those systems. The military officers designing the experiment held the authority to move people, but did not possess the knowledge needed to reproduce the lives they proposed to reorganize. Their failure to distinguish the two nations' economies was therefore a practical error rooted in political domination.

The distinction between a promised refuge and a guarded place of confinement is equally important. Military administrators argued that a reservation would shield settlements from raiding and shield Native prisoners from retaliatory expeditions. Yet the people being sent to the Pecos had not asked to live there; their departure from ancestral territory was compelled by armed operations and their freedom of movement was restricted. The selected site was vulnerable to incursions by other Plains groups and distant from reliable sources of the food and fuel needed by a growing population. The army thus created an apparent solution to a security crisis by moving that crisis onto a concentrated and dependent population. The following sections show how the policy's human costs became visible well before its formal abandonment.

Historical Vocabulary
Reservation and Forced Incarceration

Definition. In United States law, a reservation is a tract of land set aside for the use of an Indigenous nation, generally by treaty, statute, or executive order. In the nineteenth century the term could describe a homeland that a nation chose to retain or a confinement area to which a nation was forcibly moved.

Historical context. Bosque Redondo is a case in which the language of "reservation" described forced incarceration. Prisoners were removed from their homeland by military force, held under military guard, fed by army rations, and not free to leave. The 1868 Treaty, in contrast, defined a reservation within part of the Diné homeland, one that the Diné had negotiated to return to.

Why it matters. Using a single word for both situations obscures an essential difference between a place one is confined to and a place one is entitled to inhabit. This chapter uses "reservation" for the legal designation and "forced incarceration" or "confinement" for the experience at Fort Sumner.

Potential misunderstanding. Federal officials described Bosque Redondo as a protective and civilizing institution. Diné and Mescalero memory describes it as imprisonment. The two descriptions cannot both be treated as neutral.

Section 71.7

Kit Carson, the 1863 Campaign, and the Policy of Destruction

In June 1863 Carleton turned to the Diné. His orders directed military operations against them and set a deadline of July 20, 1863, after which Diné people who had not surrendered at designated posts were to be treated as hostile by the army. The terms offered to those who came in were removal to Bosque Redondo. Carleton's policy statements, written in advance of any campaign, are among the clearest records of intent in the history of the Long Walk, and they show that the removal was planned in advance rather than improvised in response to events.

Carleton appointed Kit Carson to command the field operations. Carson, a long-time resident of New Mexico, had served as a guide, trapper, Indian agent, and officer, and had a reputation for knowledge of the region. By most accounts Carson was reluctant to take the command, preferring to resign his commission rather than to lead the campaign; Carleton persuaded him to carry on. Responsibility has to be differentiated. Carleton, as department commander, devised the policy of removal, selected the destination, issued the orders, and pressed the campaign over objections from within and outside the army. Carson, as field commander, carried out those orders with a force that included New Mexico Volunteers and Ute scouts, and the operational decisions about how to apply pressure on Diné communities were his. The historian's task is to assign each man his own responsibility, not to treat one as an excuse for the other. Neither Carleton nor Carson acted alone: they commanded soldiers, civilian contractors, and Indigenous allies, each acting for reasons of their own.

The campaign was conducted by columns that moved through Diné territory from posts at Fort Defiance, renamed Fort Canby in the autumn of 1863, and elsewhere. Rather than seeking one decisive battle, which the dispersed Diné would not offer, the army sought the Diné's means of subsistence: fields of maize, orchards of peaches, stores of grain, and the herds of sheep and goats on which families depended. Troops cut down crops and orchards, burned stored food, and seized or killed livestock. They also took captives. Ute scouts and volunteer soldiers participated in these operations, and the plunder of livestock and the taking of captives were, for some of the participants, material incentives. A good deal of the evidence for this comes from the army's own correspondence, which reported with some satisfaction the quantities of corn and livestock destroyed.

The operational record should be read against the language of military success. Reports could present the number of livestock captured or destroyed as evidence that a district was being pacified. For a family whose flock represented food, clothing, exchange, and inherited wealth, the same figure documented the destruction of its capacity to survive. The gap between those perspectives was not a matter of vocabulary alone. American strategy operated through households rather than solely against armed opponents, because households produced the food that allowed mobile groups to stay beyond the garrisons' reach. That was the coercive mechanism: even when a soldier did not seize a family, a destroyed field could force its members into a military post weeks later. The seasonal timing mattered, since the loss of crops and herds before winter made replacement or flight especially difficult.

Historical Vocabulary
Scorched-Earth Warfare

Definition. A military strategy that seeks to defeat an adversary by destroying the resources, such as crops, livestock, shelter, and infrastructure, that sustain the adversary's population and fighting capacity.

Historical context. The term is modern, but the practice is old and was familiar to nineteenth-century armies, including in the American Civil War. In the Southwest in 1863–1864, it meant the systematic destruction of Diné fields, orchards, stores, and herds in order to make independent life impossible.

Why it matters. Because the Diné were dispersed and mobile, the army could not capture a capital or destroy a single force. Destroying the food supply turned the entire population into a target and made surrender a matter of survival.

Potential misunderstanding. The strategy is sometimes described as a way of avoiding bloodshed. It did reduce the number of battles, but it caused deaths through hunger, exposure, and disease, and it was aimed at civilians.

Section 71.8

Diné Resistance, Dispersal, and the Struggle to Remain at Home

The Diné did not meet the campaign with a single strategy. Communities responded according to their locations, resources, leaders, and judgments. Some fought; others moved their families and flocks to remote areas; others entered negotiations or sought safety with the army. It would be a mistake to imagine that any one individual commanded Diné resistance, as the army's reports sometimes suggest.

Several leaders are prominent in the record. Manuelito, a leader of the war at Fort Defiance and a figure of long-standing resistance, continued to evade capture and did not come in until late 1866, with a small following. Barboncito, associated with the Canyon de Chelly region, was also a leader of resistance, and later of negotiation; his career included capture, escape, and a return to the center of Diné diplomacy. Delgadito sought peace relatively early and later acted as an intermediary, carrying the army's terms to other communities. Ganado Mucho led a following that largely avoided confinement. These individuals differed, and so did the communities they represented. The leaders who accepted surrender and those who did not were all acting to preserve families and herds, though their judgments about how differed.

Armed resistance was one form of response. Diné parties attacked soldiers and sometimes raided settlements, and the army had to maintain garrisons and escorts. But the Diné also practiced a quieter form of resistance: dispersal and concealment. Families moved to the high country of the Chuska Mountains, to the canyons and mesas north and west of the Chuskas, and to remote areas along the San Juan River and the Colorado and its tributaries, beyond the reach of the army's columns. Some sought refuge among or near other peoples. They hid food, drove flocks into rough terrain, and tried to preserve children, seed, and ceremonial knowledge.

These efforts shaped the outcome. A very large number of Diné, including entire communities in the west and northwest of the homeland, avoided capture altogether and were never taken to Bosque Redondo. Their continued presence meant that the Diné homeland was never wholly emptied, that knowledge of the land remained in it, and that returning families in 1868 came back to a country that was still inhabited by relatives. It also meant that the Diné prisoners at Fort Sumner were not the whole of the Diné people, and that officials who presented the campaign as the removal of "the Navajos" were describing something that had not happened. The decisions of families who hid, families who surrendered, and communities that continued to resist outside confinement should be seen as different strategies in a single struggle to endure.

Diné strategies depended partly on access to a patchwork of local knowledge and reciprocal help. People knew which canyons could conceal small flocks, where water might remain during dry intervals, and which relatives could provide temporary shelter. These were social as much as geographical resources: a distant household could supply news, food, or refuge because clan and marriage ties joined people across the region. Flight also involved grave risks. Moving farther from a field could mean losing its harvest, and sheltering in a canyon could protect a family from a patrol while leaving it exposed to winter hunger. A choice to resist by evasion was therefore neither an easy escape nor proof that the army's campaign had no effect on people it failed to capture. Its significance lay in the continued existence of communities and relationships outside the prison system.

Division III

Destruction, Surrender, and the Long Walk

Section 71.9

Canyon de Chelly, 1864, and the Destruction of Diné Subsistence

Canyon de Chelly and its tributary Canyon del Muerto cut deep into the sandstone of the Chuska Mountains region, and for the Diné they were both productive farmland and a place of refuge. The canyon floors held cornfields and peach orchards, and the cliffs offered defensible positions. Earlier violence in the canyons, remembered in Diné tradition and associated with a Spanish expedition early in the nineteenth century, made the place a site of both vulnerability and memory. Carleton's staff understood its importance, and the army had long regarded the canyon as a stronghold the Diné might fall back upon.

In January 1864 columns entered the canyon system from different directions in winter conditions. Carson led troops toward the canyon, and a detachment under Captain Albert H. Pfeiffer passed through the canyon itself. Soldiers destroyed crops and orchards, seized and killed livestock, and took prisoners. Some Diné took refuge on defensible rock formations within the canyon system, where cold, hunger, and the lack of water eventually compelled many to come down and surrender. Reports from the campaign speak of the destruction of food and the capture of residents; they should not be read as a record of a decisive battle, because the principal injury was done to the canyon's capacity to feed people. Precise casualty and capture figures from the operation are the army's own and should be treated with caution.

The January expedition has to be distinguished from the broader 1863–1864 campaign. The army had been destroying food and livestock across Diné country since the summer of 1863. What Canyon de Chelly added was a demonstration, to the Diné and to the army, that no part of the homeland, including a place of great cultural significance and natural defensiveness, could shelter families from the army's campaign. The canyon stands in Diné memory both as a place of injury and as a place of return, and today it is a place of residence and farming for Diné families, a fact that is itself a historical conclusion about the campaign's limits.

The attack on food-producing landscapes undermined independent life even in regions the army could not permanently occupy. A family that has lost its fields and stores, whose animals have been taken, and who must find food through the winter, cannot long remain beyond the reach of an army that controls the posts at which rations are issued. This was the logic of the operation. It did not rely on a final victory; it relied on a slow collapse of the capacity to resist, which is why historians describe it as a campaign of destruction rather than one of battles.

The evidence for the canyon operation is strongest on the army's route and objectives and thinner on the experiences of families hiding above its paths. Reports by commanders can establish where detachments traveled and what they claimed to destroy, but do not capture every flight, loss, or decision made inside the cliffs. Diné testimony supplies a different record: the canyon was not an anonymous military objective but a place of gardens, orchards, burial, kinship, and shelter. A historical geography exercise should therefore read the army's movement across the canyon floor alongside the elevation of cliffs, the locations of cultivated patches, and the routes by which people could leave or remain concealed. That comparison explains how an invading force could inflict decisive economic injury without possessing the cultural landscape it attacked.

Section 71.10

Hunger, Surrender, and the Capture of Diné Families, 1863–1864

From the late summer of 1863 into the spring of 1864, families came in to Fort Canby and other collection points. They came for different reasons and under different degrees of compulsion. Some had been captured during military operations. Some surrendered because their crops, stores, and herds had been destroyed and they faced a winter without food. Some, hearing that those who came in would be fed, calculated that surrender was the only way to keep children alive. Some followed leaders who judged that continued resistance would bring further devastation. Some were brought in by intermediaries, including Diné who had themselves surrendered.

These categories matter. Negotiated surrender, in which leaders and the army reached an understanding, differs from capture, in which people were seized in operations, and both differ from movement undertaken under immediate threat of starvation, in which people went to the posts because there was nowhere else to find food. The army's records tend to merge them under the single heading of surrender. In Diné experience they were distinct. A family that gave itself up to obtain food or to keep its members together was making a choice in the narrowest sense of the word. It was not agreeing to removal from the homeland, and many families seem to have understood that they were seeking temporary refuge and rations, not a journey of hundreds of miles to an unfamiliar place. Carleton, for his part, insisted on terms that left no room for negotiating the destination: those who came in would go to Bosque Redondo.

By the winter and early spring of 1864 large numbers had gathered at Fort Canby in conditions of cold and shortage. The garrison could not supply them adequately; the army's own correspondence acknowledges the suffering of those held at the post. Officials at Fort Canby and Santa Fe understood that people who had been brought in destitute would arrive at Bosque Redondo in poor condition. The decision to move them anyway, in winter and early spring, was a decision made by American commanders under pressure to show that the policy was succeeding.

Surrender to obtain food or to preserve family members should therefore not be interpreted as consent to removal. The conditions under which people surrendered were conditions that the American campaign had created, and in which refusal meant hunger. In legal and moral analysis, consent given under such conditions is not freely given, and historians who describe the removals as "voluntary" or as "migration" misrepresent the evidence.

Collection points became an intermediate stage of the deportation system rather than a neutral pause between battle and travel. Army administrators had to count new arrivals, estimate rations, separate or group families for transport, and secure animals and wagons. These acts converted dispersed communities into manageable military columns. They also made delays dangerous. Each additional day at a crowded post exposed weakened people to cold and hunger while increasing pressure on commanders to move prisoners east before supplies were sufficient. A record stating that a group departed a fort on a given date consequently identifies an administrative achievement from the officer's perspective but tells little about the health, preparedness, or wishes of those made to march.

Cause and Effect

From Military Expansion to Forced Removal and Policy Failure

Long-term causes

A century of competition over land, livestock, and captives in a borderland economy; the extension of American jurisdiction in 1846; the placement of forts on contested pastures; a decentralized Diné political order that American negotiators could not match to their expectations.

Short-term causes

The recovery of Union military capacity after Glorieta Pass; Carleton's command and views; the Mescalero precedent at Fort Sumner; pressure from New Mexican settlers and officials; interest in the territory's mineral and agricultural resources.

Immediate trigger

Carleton's June 1863 orders and the deadline of July 20, followed by field operations under Carson.

Event or transformation

Destruction of crops, orchards, and herds; surrender and capture of families; deportation on multiple marches.

Immediate effects

Hunger, exposure, family separation, and death; the concentration of thousands of people at a site unable to feed them.

Long-term consequences

Illness, crop failure, and mounting cost at Bosque Redondo; congressional and public criticism; the 1868 treaty and return; enduring Diné memory of Hwéeldi and a treaty relationship defining a reservation within the homeland.

Historical complexity: The policy of removal was not inevitable. It reflected specific choices by Carleton and others at a particular moment. Its failure was likewise the product of specific conditions: the unsuitability of the site, the inadequacy of supply, administrative conflict, and Diné persistence. No single factor explains either the removal or its collapse.

Section 71.11

The Long Walk, 1863–1866: Routes, Conditions, and Death

The Long Walk was not a single event. Between 1863 and 1866, soldiers escorted Diné families from collection points, principally Fort Canby (Fort Defiance) and Fort Wingate, to Bosque Redondo in a series of separate groups, on varied routes, and at different times. Historians and museums distinguish the marches from the confinement and from the eventual return. Smithsonian sources document the forced removal of more than 10,000 people, a figure that includes people removed in several groups over several years, and that is not a count of any single march. Some Diné were deported in the first large movements of early 1864; others came in later in 1864 and 1865; and some surrendered as late as 1866.

Typical routes ran from Fort Canby by way of Fort Wingate, across the country west of the Rio Grande, and through or near Albuquerque and Santa Fe before turning east toward Fort Sumner on the Pecos River; others took different roads depending on the point of departure, the season, and the army's arrangements. Distances are often given as roughly 300 miles or more, but no single distance applies to all groups, and the figure varies by point of origin and route. Each of these journeys crossed difficult terrain, river crossings, high plateaus, and the Rio Grande valley in all seasons, but with the winter and spring of 1864 especially severe.

Conditions were harsh. Escorts of soldiers moved groups of prisoners on foot, with wagons for some of the sick or very young and for supplies. Food supplies were limited, and soldiers and prisoners alike experienced shortages; the prisoners had already been weakened by hunger. Clothing and shelter were inadequate for the cold, and many lacked shoes. Rivers and streams were crossed in cold weather. The army's pace was determined by military requirements, not by the capacity of the weakest. Children, pregnant women, older people, and the sick could not always keep up, and those who could not were sometimes left behind, an outcome the oral tradition remembers with great anguish. Deaths occurred from cold, hunger, exhaustion, illness, and, in some accounts, violence.

The numbers need careful handling. Estimates of deaths during the marches vary among sources and depend on which marches, which years, and which causes are counted. Estimates of deaths during the whole of the campaign and confinement, including deaths at Bosque Redondo, are higher and are often given in the thousands, but they too vary, and they include different categories of death. Students should not combine estimates without identifying what each includes: deaths on the marches, deaths at collection points, deaths during confinement, and the broader number of people who never returned are different quantities. The Smithsonian and New Mexico's historic-site authorities provide estimates that differ in scope, and this chapter does not average them. What the evidence establishes beyond reasonable doubt is that the marches and the confinement together caused very large mortality among a population that had been displaced by the army's campaign.

It is equally important not to portray every deportee as having followed one identical route or as having experienced the same hardship. Some marched in the dead of winter; others in warmer months. Some groups were moved with more provisions than others. Some were accompanied by relatives; others were separated. The Diné memory of the Long Walk, which is carried in the stories families tell, is thus both collective and particular: each family has its own account of who walked, who died, and who was lost.

The corridor between the western posts and the Pecos was not simply a line on a map. Each group needed access to water and places to halt, but those requirements varied with season, age, health, and the availability of livestock. Wagons could carry provisions and some sick people, though they could not eliminate the hardship of walking. The movement of soldiers, mules, prisoners, and supplies also put pressure on settlements and military depots along the road. When a route passed through an area with a history of conflict or captive-taking, a group had to contend with insecurity beyond the physical difficulty of travel. Reconstructing any particular march requires the departure roster, route reports, weather and ration records, and survivors' accounts where available, rather than retroactively assigning the most famous story to every person deported.

Section 71.12

Families Torn Apart: Captivity, Gendered Violence, and Separation

The social history of the forced removals is a history of families. Diné kinship, built around matrilineal clans and women's households, meant that the destruction of homes, flocks, and fields, and the separation of relatives, struck at the foundations of social life. Military correspondence counted prisoners, rations, and miles; it recorded far less about who was missing.

Women and children were especially vulnerable. Women, who held households together, tended children, and often managed flocks, bore the burdens of marching, caring for the sick and very young, and trying to find food. The evidence indicates that soldiers and civilians committed sexual violence against Diné women during the campaign and the removals; Diné oral history preserves this testimony, which official reports omit or minimize. The danger extended to kidnapping. In New Mexico the old trade in Indigenous captives, discussed in Chapter 53 and Section 71.2, persisted, and during the campaign New Mexican civilians and, in some cases, soldiers and allied parties took Diné children and women into servitude. Estimates of the number of Diné held in New Mexican households during these years run into the thousands but are uncertain and have been debated, and the number depends on how captivity is defined. What is clear is that the campaign opened new opportunities for the capture and enslavement of Diné people, in spite of efforts by some officials to restrict them and in spite of federal laws against peonage that came later.

The marches also separated families. People were divided between groups, lost on the road, left behind when they could not travel, or taken by others. Many relatives never saw each other again. Some children were taken into New Mexican homes and grew up there, and their descendants, and the descendants of those who were never found, are part of the story of the Long Walk's consequences. Return after 1868 did not reunite everyone.

Diné oral history preserves testimony that military correspondence often omits. Survivors and their descendants told these accounts over generations, and collections of them were recorded in the twentieth century, among them a volume published by the Navajo Community College Press in 1973. These accounts have distinct evidentiary roles. They were recorded decades after the events, and memory may compress, rearrange, or attach meaning to particular episodes; they are also shaped by their purposes of teaching and remembering. But they are the principal record of experiences the army had no interest in documenting, and they often correct or complicate official claims. Contemporary military reports, for their part, offer dates, locations, and numbers that oral tradition does not always preserve, though those figures reflect the army's perspective and aims. A historian should use the two kinds of evidence together, asking each different questions.

The long-term consequences of separation cannot be recovered solely from lists of prisoners. An official enumeration might record an adult and two children arriving at Fort Sumner but omit the relatives who died before the journey, those retained in New Mexican households, or those who had fled elsewhere. Clan relationships could preserve the memory of those absent even when administrative documents stopped following them. Later efforts to reconstruct kinship histories must therefore read lists as fragmentary evidence, not as a census of everyone affected. This difference is especially consequential when assessing the fate of Diné children whose names were changed or whose placements were described by outsiders as domestic arrangements. Such language can conceal the coercion that produced the separation.

Division IV

Hwéeldi: Incarceration and Survival at Bosque Redondo

Section 71.13

Bosque Redondo and the American Reservation Experiment

Bosque Redondo lay on the Pecos River in the plains of eastern New Mexico, far from the mountains, canyons, and pastures of the Diné homeland. The army's choice of site rested on its water, its stand of cottonwoods, its apparent arable land, and its location, which allowed the post at Fort Sumner to watch the prisoners and to guard against Comanche and other raiding from the east. It had not been chosen for the suitability of the land for several thousand Diné and Mescalero; this was a policy decided in advance and applied to a location selected for the army's convenience.

Military administration centered on Fort Sumner. The post's officers oversaw the issue of rations, the organization of work, and the surveillance of prisoners, and the army's concept was that those held at the reservation would become agricultural settlers. Plans called for farming settlements, an irrigation system fed by a ditch from the Pecos, planting of large fields of corn and other crops, and the distribution of food until harvests could support the population. Work was organized in part through the labor of those confined, and Diné men and women dug ditches, planted, and cultivated. The design assumed that people accustomed to a mixed economy of herding, farming, and trade in a variety of ecological zones could be made into irrigated farmers on land that officials had not adequately tested, under supervision by officers who regarded Diné society as something to be transformed.

The settlement's scale changed as deportation proceeded. What might have appeared workable for several hundred prisoners became wholly different when thousands of Diné arrived in 1864. A subsistence plan calculated for one population could not simply be multiplied when land, irrigation, fuel, and water were limited by the site itself. Families required space for shelter and sanitation, while livestock needed pasture, and cultivated fields needed irrigation works that could survive flooding or drought. These demands competed for the same scarce resources. As prisoners spread along the Pecos, distance from the fort and storehouses could make obtaining rations or medical assistance more difficult. The army's geographical control was thus also a problem of unequal access within the settlement.

Federal officials described the project using the language of improvement and civilization, but the people confined had already practiced complex agricultural, pastoral, and commercial livelihoods. Treating them as novices at production obscured the deliberate loss of their existing means of production. Many Diné were highly experienced cultivators and stockraisers; what they lacked at Bosque Redondo was suitable land, freedom of movement, familiar seed and stock, dependable water, and control over the terms of their work. The experiment's failure cannot be explained as a supposed incapacity of the prisoners to farm. It was the consequence of imposing a new, coercive economy in a place selected without their consent.

The term incarceration is useful precisely because it names the coercive relationship rather than the administrative aspiration. It does not mean that every aspect of life was identical to that of a modern prison: families built shelters, worked fields, exchanged goods, and continued social life. But soldiers controlled departure and arrival, administrators controlled the distribution of essential food, and the prisoners were hundreds of miles from the lands to which they sought to return. The distinction between a place people inhabit under their own political arrangements and a place from which they cannot freely depart helps explain why the Treaty of 1868 was remembered as liberation and return even though it also established a reservation.

Historical Vocabulary
Dinétah and Hwéeldi

Dinétah is a Diné term for the Diné homeland. It is used both for the region of the upper San Juan basin associated with early Diné history and, more broadly, for the Diné country within the four sacred mountains. Its meaning is geographic, historical, and sacred.

Hwéeldi is the name by which the Diné remember Bosque Redondo and the years of confinement at Fort Sumner. It is associated with suffering, hardship, and incarceration, and it is the term used in Diné historical memory and in contemporary Diné usage. Diné speakers also use other terms to describe the period of the removal and the dread it inspired.

Why it matters. The two terms mark a contrast central to the chapter: a homeland understood as sacred and sustaining, and a place of confinement remembered as one of suffering. Using Diné terms does not replace documentary evidence but reflects the way the Diné themselves name and remember these places.

Potential misunderstanding. "Bosque Redondo" is the army's name and the name used in federal records; it refers to a place. Hwéeldi refers to an experience and to its memory as well as to the place. The two are related but not interchangeable.

Section 71.14

Hunger, Water, Disease, and the Failure of the Bosque Redondo System

Everyday conditions at Bosque Redondo were harsh from the beginning. The water of the Pecos at this location was alkaline, and it caused stomach and bowel illness among those who drank it. The soil, though near water, proved difficult to farm. The first crops in 1864 were damaged by insects, and subsequent harvests suffered from drought, hail, flooding that damaged irrigation works, and poor-quality seed. The result was crop failure in successive years, a pattern that left the population dependent on rations from the army. Congress's later Bosque Redondo Memorial Act explicitly recognized drought, crop destruction, and alkaline water among the major conditions affecting the captives.

The scarcity of fuel compounded the problem. The cottonwoods near the post were quickly cut for firewood, and as supplies ran short, prisoners had to travel long distances to gather wood, and in some cases dug up mesquite roots for fuel. In the absence of timber, many families lived in shelters dug into the ground or constructed from whatever materials they could find, which gave poor protection against cold and wet. Exposure and the lack of adequate clothing, blankets, and fuel contributed to illness.

Disease followed. Dysentery and other intestinal illnesses were widespread; respiratory diseases and, at times, epidemic disease raged through a population that was already weakened by hunger and by the journey. Army surgeons also recorded venereal illness among prisoners and soldiers. Such records warrant examination alongside survivor testimony about sexual exploitation, but the disease reports alone cannot establish the circumstances of any individual assault. Food rations were inadequate and sometimes of poor quality, and delays in supply, administrative disputes, and the sheer size of the population strained the system. Comanche raids on livestock added to losses. The population confined at Bosque Redondo at its height was many thousands, a figure usually given as roughly 9,000 or so in the mid-1860s, far greater than the numbers for which the reservation had been planned or equipped.

The failure of the system was therefore structural. Environmental limitations interacted with inadequate funding, divided administration between the army and the Indian Bureau, and the design of the reservation itself, which placed thousands of people on land unsuited to their needs and expected them to become self-supporting there. The promises made by federal officials, that the confined people would be fed, sheltered, taught, and made prosperous, can be compared with the actual conditions recorded in the army's own reports. The gap between the two is the heart of the documentary record.

Evidence of failure accumulated in records that were often written to justify the program. An officer might attribute a poor harvest to weather, lack of equipment, or the supposedly inadequate effort of prisoners. Yet repeated crop failures under different conditions indicate a problem with the location and with the planning itself. Shortages could be intensified by corrupt contracting, delayed deliveries, transport distances, and uncertainty over which federal agency was responsible for supplies. The army could command labor, but it could not command rain, transform poor water into good water, or replace the surrounding ecosystems of the original homelands. Reading successive reports as a series rather than as isolated complaints reveals the persistence of these failures.

Section 71.15

The Mescalero Apache at Bosque Redondo and the Departure of 1865

The Mescalero were the first people confined at Fort Sumner, and their experience deserves separate treatment. A smaller population than the Diné, numbering several hundred, they had been moved from their homeland in the mountains and plains of southern New Mexico after the campaign of 1862–1863. At Bosque Redondo they were placed on the reservation before the arrival of the large Diné groups in 1864.

The Mescalero had distinct histories, economies, and ways of life. Their livelihoods, based on mobility, gathering, hunting, and raiding as well as some cultivation, were incompatible with the reservation regime of confinement, irrigated agriculture, and rations. The arrival of thousands of Diné, a people with whom the Mescalero had their own history of both contact and conflict, placed intense pressure on a small and already disrupted community. Tensions arose between the two groups over resources, space, and relations in the crowded settlement. Mescalero people also experienced the shared hardships of the site, including shortage of food and fuel, illness, and the difficulty of farming the land.

In November 1865, many of the Mescalero left Bosque Redondo, slipping away from the post, and returned toward their homeland. The departure was a form of resistance, an assertion that the Mescalero would not accept permanent confinement. The army pursued them with little success, and small numbers remained at or returned to the post. The Mescalero later negotiated their own relationship with the United States and received a reservation in their own country in the 1870s, an outcome related to the history discussed in Chapter 51 and later Apache chapters (Chapters 79 and 80).

The Mescalero departure demonstrated further the failure of the reservation experiment. It was the clearest sign that confinement could not be sustained by the army on the existing terms, and it deprived Carleton's design of its claim to success with the Mescalero. It should not be mistaken for a Diné event: the Mescalero left under their own circumstances and strategies, and their history is distinct from the later Diné negotiations of 1868.

Mescalero departure must not be read simply as an administrative embarrassment. For a community whose social and economic relationships extended into the Sacramento Mountains and surrounding country, leaving the Pecos meant attempting to recover access to familiar food, travel routes, relatives, and sacred places. The decision carried risk: those who left could face military pursuit or renewed conflict with settlers. But the choice also exposed a limit of Carleton's program, which assumed that the army could impose a permanent geography by transporting people to a distant post. The Mescalero had not surrendered their identity or their relationship to home merely because soldiers had confined them.

Their departure also complicates comparisons of mortality and population counts. Official reports sometimes counted both nations together at Fort Sumner, while later narratives focus almost exclusively on Diné experience. For a fair assessment, Mescalero numbers should be distinguished from Diné counts and should be followed across arrival, confinement, departure, and later negotiations where the evidence permits. Likewise, the fact that Mescalero communities regained a reservation in southern New Mexico later in the nineteenth century should not be projected backward as an inevitable result of their 1865 escape. At the time, leaving was a contested act of political agency under uncertain circumstances.

Section 71.16

Life in Captivity: Labor, Death, Cultural Continuity, and Survival

Diné society did not cease to function at Bosque Redondo. Families continued to organize themselves, to care for the sick and the young, to share scarce resources, and to maintain relationships. Clan relations remained a framework for mutual obligation among prisoners who had been dispersed from different parts of the homeland and who met at Fort Sumner as neighbors and kin. The reservation concentrated people from many communities in one place, and in some respects this forced proximity created new contacts among leaders and families.

Labor was a large part of daily life. Men and women dug irrigation ditches, planted and harvested crops, hauled wood and water, and built shelters. Women prepared food from rations that were unfamiliar and often inadequate, ground and cooked what was available, and tried to feed children and the elderly. Child welfare was a constant concern: children were especially vulnerable to illness and hunger, and parents' efforts to protect them are recurring themes in the oral tradition. Weaving continued where materials allowed, and textile historians have noted changes in materials and designs in this period, a reminder that crafts adapted under pressure rather than vanishing.

Ceremony, language, and social knowledge were maintained under difficult conditions. Diné people continued to pray, to conduct ceremonies as circumstances allowed, and to tell the stories that carried their history and law. The experience of Hwéeldi became itself part of that memory. Women's roles were central to preserving family relationships and cultural continuity: they were the center of households and the transmitters of much knowledge, and the survival of kinship ties through confinement depended in large part on their work.

Mortality estimates require care. Deaths on the Long Walk, deaths during confinement at Bosque Redondo, and broader estimates of the number of Diné who never returned are distinct categories. Different sources, including the Smithsonian and New Mexico historic-site authorities, offer figures that differ because they define the period and population differently, and some estimates are expressed as approximations. Official counts were incomplete, and the burial of the dead at the site and the movement of people in and out of the reservation made accuracy impossible. The chapter therefore does not offer a single death toll. It affirms that mortality was very high, that hundreds of deaths occurred on the marches and further deaths, in the thousands by some estimates, occurred during the whole period of removal and confinement, and that every estimate leaves out individuals whose deaths were never recorded.

The combination of concentrated confinement and incomplete official enumeration makes the demographic record particularly difficult. The number of people held could change with new arrivals, escapes, deaths, and reassignment of rations; a ration list was not necessarily a reliable census, and the date on which a person ceased to appear might not reveal why. A careful reconstruction distinguishes the number ever deported, the population present at a particular date, the number officially recorded as dead, and the estimates of people lost through circumstances beyond the record. The Smithsonian reports approximately two thousand deaths among Diné internees, while New Mexico historic-site materials use estimates with broader scope. Such figures should be presented with their source, period, and definition rather than added together.

Everyday survival included choices that seldom appear in the official administrative narrative. Families decided how to divide scarce food, how to keep children warm in an improvised shelter, and how to care for the sick when medical treatment was insufficient or mistrusted. Relationships among clans could provide assistance, but these obligations were placed under enormous strain by hunger and epidemic illness. Work in fields or ditches offered neither freedom nor guaranteed subsistence because the military retained control over the settlement and rations. Yet the preservation of speech, kinship knowledge, weaving skills, and ceremonies demonstrates that cultural continuity was not a passive byproduct of survival; it required sustained collective effort under conditions designed to transform Diné society.

Everyday Life and Social History

Family, Work, Food, and Memory under Confinement

The documents of the Long Walk are mostly written about people rather than by them. To recover everyday life, historians read the reports of officers and surgeons against the grain, listening for what they reveal about labor, food, illness, and the arrangement of households, and then place them beside oral history. What emerges is a picture of constant effort: ditches dug by hand, shelters improvised, food stretched, children watched over.

Women's work was the hinge. In Diné society women managed the household economy and its relationships, and at Fort Sumner they continued to do so, in far harsher circumstances and under constant threat. The continuation of ceremony and language, and the teaching of children, were everyday acts that carried a society through incarceration. This is why the history of Hwéeldi is not only a history of death. It is a history of how a people kept its social knowledge alive when its means of life had been destroyed, and why return in 1868 meant something far beyond the transfer of bodies to a place.

Division V

The Treaty of 1868, Return, and the Renewal of Diné Sovereignty

Section 71.17

The Collapse of the Bosque Redondo Policy and the Decision to Negotiate

By 1865 and 1866, Bosque Redondo had become unsustainable on every measure that mattered to American officials. It had not become an economically self-supporting agricultural settlement. The crops failed repeatedly, rations had to be purchased and hauled at great expense, and the cost of feeding thousands of people far exceeded what the army, the Indian Bureau, or Congress had anticipated. The Mescalero departure in 1865 undercut the claim that confinement could be maintained, and continued Comanche raids and the exhaustion of local resources added to the strain.

Administrative conflict deepened the crisis. Responsibility for the Diné was contested between the army and the Indian Bureau, and Carleton clashed with Indian Bureau officials who criticized his policy and questioned its results. Congress, through a Joint Special Committee investigating conditions among Indigenous peoples, examined the situation, and critics inside and outside government raised humanitarian objections and questions of expense. Carleton was relieved of his command in 1866. His successors and the civilian officials who followed him inherited a reservation they considered a failure, and growing numbers of officials concluded that the policy should be abandoned.

The United States government's reasons for changing policy were thus largely practical and financial, joined by humanitarian criticism. They were distinct from the Diné people's own long determination to return home. From the first months of confinement, Diné leaders and families had sought to go back, some by escaping, some by petitioning, and many by simply enduring and asking. The American recognition that Bosque Redondo could not be sustained did not originate the idea of return; it made the idea politically possible. Federal officials who had insisted that Bosque Redondo was a permanent solution now sought a way to end it that did not repeat the cost, and the question became where the Diné should go. That question was answered, in the end, in councils at Fort Sumner in the spring of 1868.

The process of reevaluation was incremental rather than an abrupt change of conscience. Each growing appropriation, each failed crop, and each report of illness made the promise of a self-supporting reservation harder to defend. Critics could agree that the system was failing without agreeing on why: some objected to its expense, others questioned coercive removal, and still others sought a cheaper means of supervising Indigenous people. This distinction matters because an official recommendation to close Bosque Redondo did not necessarily endorse Diné self-government or the restoration of ancestral land. The subsequent negotiations turned on whether fiscal retrenchment would produce another displacement or allow the people confined to return home.

The Diné had maintained that demand long before Washington concluded the experiment was unsustainable. Resistance outside the camp, petitions within it, escapes, and repeated appeals to the significance of homeland all formed part of the same political history. They kept return available as an objective when officials debated new reservations elsewhere. That persistence helps explain why the negotiations cannot be represented as an unsolicited gift from Sherman or Tappan. Federal commissioners possessed the authority to sign an agreement, but the content of the crucial concession—a reservation in the Diné homeland—was shaped by Diné refusal to accept another exile.

Section 71.18

Barboncito, Manuelito, Diné Women, and the Negotiations of 1868

In late May 1868, Lieutenant General William T. Sherman and Commissioner Samuel F. Tappan arrived at Fort Sumner as members of a federal peace commission to determine the future of the Diné. They met in council with Diné representatives, and the discussions are among the best-documented diplomatic exchanges of the era, preserved in transcripts made by the commissioners' staff. The record is American: it was made by American recorders, in English, with interpretation, and it should be read with attention to what it omits.

The commissioners came with options. The American government was considering relocating the Diné to Indian Territory, in the present state of Oklahoma, where other peoples had been removed, and it was also considering the possibility of a restored reservation in the Southwest. Sherman, who regarded the Diné country as marginal and the Bosque Redondo experiment as unworkable, was initially inclined toward relocation to the east. The Diné leaders rejected this. Barboncito, who emerged as the principal spokesman, spoke at length in the councils. His arguments, preserved in the transcript, rested on the sacred relationship between the Diné and their land, on the failure of the Pecos country to support them, and on the Diné desire to return to the homeland given to them, in Diné understanding, by their deities and enclosed by the four sacred mountains. He and other leaders rejected another removal and insisted on returning to their country; the Smithsonian documents this refusal of renewed eastern removal. Manuelito, who had come in late and who carried great weight among those who had resisted, also spoke for return and for the conditions Diné people would need to rebuild.

Diné women's advocacy was part of this effort, though the written record only partly shows it. Oral tradition and later scholarship, notably the work of Jennifer Nez Denetdale on Manuelito's family and on Diné women's historical roles, emphasize that women pressed leaders to secure return, that women's voices and the insistence of families lay behind the men's words, and that the continuity of kinship was a driving force. The surviving transcripts record principally male speakers, a limitation of the evidence, and the historian must be careful to avoid inventing words or scenes that the record does not support while also refusing to erase women from the story because the recorders did not write them down. What can be said is that Diné society's structure, with its matrilineal kinship, women's household authority, and women's ownership of flocks, made women's views inseparable from those of the community, and that tradition remembers their insistence.

The negotiations therefore resolved a contest of proposals: renewed removal to Indian Territory, continued confinement, or return to a homeland. Sherman, faced with the clear and unified Diné position, the practical failure of Bosque Redondo, and the expense of any alternative, came to accept the return of the Diné to a portion of their homeland. The result was not what the Diné had initially sought. The reservation would be smaller than their ancestral country, and many conditions were imposed. But it fulfilled the central demand, to go home, and it marks one of the few occasions in the history of nineteenth-century Indian removal in which a removed people secured a return to their homeland.

The negotiating record also reveals a difference between asking where the government could most cheaply move a population and asking where a people had the right to live. From a military perspective, the reservation's size, distance from towns, access to supplies, and administrative expense could be compared as practical variables. For the Diné representatives, the particular homeland was not interchangeable with an equivalent acreage in Indian Territory. Its sacred mountains, known places, fields, and family histories gave geography moral and political meaning. Recognizing that difference clarifies why concessions involving rations, tools, or transportation could not replace the demand for return. The final agreement reflected compromise, but not agreement that all possible destinations were equivalent.

Section 71.19

The Treaty of June 1, 1868: Homeland, Sovereignty, and Federal Obligations

The treaty signed at Fort Sumner on June 1, 1868, by Sherman and Tappan for the United States and by Barboncito, Manuelito, and other Diné headmen, in all more than two dozen, defined the terms of return. Its provisions can be summarized by theme, though students should consult the full text, available through the National Archives' DocsTeach collection and the Oklahoma State University Indian Treaties Database, and can check each article against the ratified text (15 Stat. 667).

Peace and territory. The treaty opens with a pledge of peace between the United States and the Diné. Article II sets aside a reservation defined by boundaries, a defined portion of the traditional homeland bounded in the north by 37° latitude, in the south by an east–west line through old Fort Defiance, and on the east and west by specified meridians, with Canyon de Chelly expressly included. This reservation was a small portion of the territory the Diné had occupied and used, and it excluded some of the most important lands, including the places of the four sacred mountains. It was, however, a homeland: it lay inside Dinétah, and it allowed families to return to the country they had known.

Administration and education. The treaty provided for an agent and agency buildings on the reservation. Article VI required the education of Diné children, a compulsory schooling provision that committed the Diné to send children of school age to school and the United States to provide teachers and a schoolhouse. This provision reflected the assimilationist assumptions of American Indian policy and would later shape the experience of Diné children, a subject taken up in Chapters 87 and 88.

Agriculture and material assistance. The treaty contained provisions for farming, for seeds and implements, for clothing, and for a distribution of sheep and goats to help families rebuild. Article XI required movement from Bosque Redondo to the treaty reservation under federal supervision and provided for subsistence en route and transportation for the sick and vulnerable. Article XII allocated a proposed $150,000 expenditure: roughly $50,000 for removal, up to $30,000 for 15,000 sheep and goats, and purchases of 500 cattle and one million pounds of corn, with the remainder available for maintenance. Those commitments were essential to survival after return; federal performance was uneven.

Restrictions and retained rights. Article IX set out restrictions on Diné conduct, including a commitment to end hostilities and to refrain from opposing certain developments such as the construction of railroads. It also preserved a right to hunt on lands outside the reservation so long as game could be found and peace was maintained. Article X required any future treaty cession of commonly held reservation land to obtain the agreement of at least three-fourths of the adult male Diné occupying or interested in it, a protection against future land loss, and one which later events often tested.

Permanent home. Article XIII provided that the Diné would make the reservation their permanent home, a provision imposing on Diné signatories a commitment not to establish permanent homes elsewhere and attaching penalties to relocation. It did not, by its own wording, give an unconditional guarantee against every future federal removal policy; the negotiated return and Article II reservation protections were the positive territorial commitments.

The treaty was both a major Diné diplomatic achievement and a coercive compromise. It was an achievement because the Diné, after years of destruction and incarceration, had persuaded American commissioners to abandon Bosque Redondo and a proposed Indian Territory removal and to restore them to part of their homeland, which relatively few removed peoples succeeded in doing. It was coercive because the Diné negotiated from a position created by the army's campaign: their fields destroyed, their numbers diminished by death, and their people held at gunpoint. The reservation was smaller than the homeland, the terms were written by American negotiators, and several provisions served American goals of control and assimilation.

It is important to distinguish the Diné nation's inherent sovereignty from the federal recognition of a defined reservation. The treaty did not create the Diné people or their political existence. It was an agreement between the United States and an Indigenous nation that existed before the United States and would continue after it; it defined a relationship, regulated certain conduct, and recognized a territory. Later chapters will examine how this treaty relationship shaped the Navajo Nation's legal standing (Chapters 97 and 118). The treaty was advised and consented to by the Senate on July 25, 1868, and proclaimed by President Andrew Johnson on August 12, 1868.

The legal text also allocated responsibilities in ways that deserve close reading. Article I promised peace and mechanisms for claims involving offenses by both sides, but enforcement largely passed through federal agents and officials. Article V allowed qualifying individuals to select farming parcels within the reservation, while Article VI required schooling for children aged six through sixteen and called for government-supported teachers and schoolhouses. Articles VII and VIII addressed seeds, implements, and annuity goods. These provisions mixed material support with a program of social transformation: the treaty furnished tools for rebuilding while encouraging individual agricultural tenure and federally supervised education. A close reading should therefore resist presenting every federal promise as either purely benevolent or purely punitive. Each was embedded in an unequal negotiation after incarceration, but each also formed part of the agreement Diné negotiators secured for return.

Primary Source Window V

The May 1868 Negotiations and the Treaty of June 1, 1868

Creators: William T. Sherman and Samuel F. Tappan for the United States; Barboncito, Manuelito, and other Diné headmen · Dates: Council proceedings, late May 1868; treaty signed June 1, 1868 · Place: Fort Sumner, New Mexico Territory · Audience: The federal government, the Senate, and the Diné · Type: A council transcript recorded by American staff, and a ratified treaty text. Both survive in federal records; the treaty text is available through the National Archives' DocsTeach collection and the Oklahoma State University Indian Treaties Database.

Context: The councils followed years of failed policy and were held on the grounds of the place of confinement, with the future of thousands at stake.

Paraphrase of the negotiations (editorial summary, not a quotation): Barboncito, speaking for the Diné, argued against any removal elsewhere and asked to return to the homeland, emphasizing its sacred meaning and the unsuitability of the Pecos country. Manuelito and other leaders supported return. Sherman proposed relocation to Indian Territory; the Diné declined. After deliberation, the commissioners agreed to a reservation in the Diné country.

Paraphrase of the treaty (editorial summary, not a quotation): Peace; a defined reservation (Article II); an agent and agency buildings; compulsory education of children (Article VI); farming assistance and supply of seeds, tools, clothing, and livestock; restrictions on opposing railroads and on hostilities, with a limited right to hunt beyond the reservation (Article IX); a requirement of broad Diné consent for any future land cession; and a commitment that the reservation be the Diné's permanent home (Article XIII).

Analysis: Together the documents show Diné leaders using argument, unity, and the force of kinship and sacred geography to alter American policy. They show the commissioners accepting the practical impossibility of Bosque Redondo. The treaty records both what the Diné gained, a homeland and a set of federal obligations, and what they gave up, including territory, autonomy in education, and freedom of movement.

Limitations: The transcripts were made by American recorders and translated; they do not preserve the full texture of Diné debate or the voices of women. The treaty text was drafted in English. Many Diné participants could not read it. It does not show how provisions were understood or what private discussions preceded the signatures, and it does not indicate how fully the United States later kept its promises.

Perspective: The federal record. Diné perspectives are recoverable from the councils' words as recorded and from oral tradition, which remembers the return as a deliverance.

Historical Debate

Was the Treaty of 1868 a Diné Diplomatic Victory, a Coerced Compromise, or Both?

The question: Historians and Diné writers differ in how they characterize the treaty, and the difference concerns interpretation more than evidence.

Interpretation A — A diplomatic victory: The Diné persuaded the United States to end the Bosque Redondo experiment, to forgo removal to Indian Territory, and to restore them to part of their homeland. Few removed Indigenous peoples achieved such an outcome, and it reflects Diné unity, leadership, and determination.
Interpretation B — A coerced compromise: The treaty was negotiated after years of destruction and incarceration. The reservation was smaller than the homeland, the terms included schooling and restrictions, and the United States wrote the text. On this reading, the treaty ratified an outcome shaped by American power.
Interpretation C — Both, understood as parts of a single outcome: Many scholars hold that the treaty cannot be classified in either way alone: it was a hard-won return achieved under duress. This chapter adopts that view while recognizing that different emphases are possible.

Evidence: The council transcripts, the treaty text, the reservation's boundaries, the conditions at Bosque Redondo, and the experience of the return all bear on the question.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and emphasis: how much weight to give the gains, how much to give the constraints, and what counts as victory when it follows catastrophe.

Why it matters: The interpretation affects how the Long Walk is remembered, whether as defeat, as survival, or as both, and how the treaty relationship is understood in later law and politics.

Section 71.20

The Return Home, Reconstruction, and the Meaning of the Long Walk

The journey home began in late June 1868; the Smithsonian records that thousands set out on June 28. In contrast to the marches of 1863–1866, this journey was a movement toward the homeland, and Diné memory preserves a sense of joy and relief along with exhaustion. A very long column of people, wagons, and livestock traveled westward across New Mexico toward Fort Wingate and the Diné country. Accounts of the return emphasize the feeling of recognizing the land as the people drew near their country, and the sanctity of the return in Diné tradition.

Reconstruction was hard. Returning families found devastated communities: houses burned or fallen, fields and orchards destroyed, herds depleted or gone. They had to rebuild shelters, replant, and restock flocks, with assistance from the government that was uneven and often inadequate. The distribution of sheep and goats in the years after the return was crucial, and Diné families, with their long experience of herding, rebuilt flocks relatively quickly, though under strain. Weaving, farming, and trade resumed. Ceremonies and social relationships were renewed, and families reunited, or learned that some members had not survived or could not be found.

Returning prisoners rejoined relatives who had never been confined. The Diné who avoided capture had remained in the homeland, preserving knowledge, tending whatever herds they had saved, and maintaining ceremonial life, and their presence made reintegration possible. The new reservation system also reorganized relationships: an agent, an agency, and a school system entered Diné life, and the Diné had to deal with a permanent federal presence, a topic developed in later chapters (Chapters 85 through 87 and 97). The reservation was enlarged by later executive orders, and the Navajo Nation today encompasses a far larger area than the treaty reservation, a reminder that the treaty fixed a starting point rather than a final boundary.

The Long Walk must be evaluated as a history of forced displacement and extraordinary suffering. The campaign of 1863–1864 used the destruction of food and the capture of families as instruments of conquest, the marches caused great loss of life, and Hwéeldi inflicted hunger, illness, and death on thousands. It was also a history of political survival, diplomatic persistence, and continued connection to ancestral homelands. The Diné were neither wholly conquered nor ever undefeated. They endured devastating losses, and they negotiated a return. The memory of the Long Walk remains central to Diné identity, and its history is now commemorated at sites such as the Bosque Redondo Memorial, in which Diné and Mescalero perspectives are presented.

Return did not restore every field, herd, or relative, nor did it erase the boundary imposed by the treaty. Families had to rebuild in places already altered by years of military operations and by the absence of many former inhabitants. The federal reservation was smaller than the wider sacred and economic homeland, and some Diné continued to live outside its lines. This difference later produced conflicts over movement, grazing, land use, and the enlargement of the reservation. At the same time, the homeward journey marked the failure of the attempt to sever the people permanently from the Four Corners region. It was a turning point in which practical work—planting, herding, weaving, and reconstructing households—became a form of political renewal.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Diné Society Before Removal, During Hwéeldi, and After Return

What Changed

  • The territory under Diné control contracted from the broad homeland to a defined reservation, later enlarged
  • Population and herds were sharply reduced by campaign, removal, and confinement
  • The Diné entered a permanent treaty relationship with the United States, including an agent, schooling, and federal obligations
  • The Long Walk and Hwéeldi became defining events in Diné collective memory

What Stayed the Same

  • Matrilineal clan relationships and the central role of women in households and in transmitting knowledge
  • Ceremonial life, language, and the sacred relationship with the land bounded by the four sacred mountains
  • Decentralized leadership grounded in reputation and kinship rather than a single ruler
  • The Diné identity as a people with a claim to a homeland, and the determination to return to it

Timing and experience: Change was abrupt for families who were captured and marched, and gradual for those who avoided confinement. Women and children bore especially heavy burdens, while continuity was carried disproportionately by those who maintained households, ceremonies, and language. Why the pattern matters: It shows a people changed profoundly yet continuous in identity and institutions, and it prevents both the claim that removal destroyed Diné society and the claim that it left no mark.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 71 has examined the Navajo Long Walk as a history of conquest, forced displacement, incarceration, resistance, and return. Its central argument is that the United States transformed a long-running frontier conflict into a campaign of destruction and forced removal, and that Diné leadership, women's advocacy, family persistence, and political determination secured a return to ancestral homelands in 1868.

The chapter began with what was at stake. The Diné were a large, regionally dispersed people organized through matrilineal clans and local headmen, with an economy of farming, herding, weaving, and trade and a sacred geography defined by four mountains. This decentralized structure made them resilient, but it also created persistent misunderstandings with American and New Mexican officials who expected to deal with a single authority.

Before 1846 the Diné lived in a borderland of trade, raiding, captive-taking, and diplomacy with Spanish, Mexican, Pueblo, Ute, and Apache neighbors, and the chapter cautioned against collective judgments about any of these peoples. American occupation in 1846 shifted claimed jurisdiction without ending Diné autonomy. The agreement of 1846 and the treaty of 1849 recorded commitments but could not bind dispersed communities, and the killing of Narbona in 1849 left a lasting mark on trust.

The establishment of Fort Defiance in 1851 placed an army garrison on contested pastureland, and disputes over livestock and grazing produced the crises of 1858 and the fighting of 1860. The violence of the early 1860s thus grew out of a militarized frontier and not from the Civil War alone. The war, however, reshaped military capacity. After the Union victory over the Confederate invasion, James H. Carleton assumed command in New Mexico and chose to pursue a policy of concentration.

Carleton first applied that policy to the Mescalero Apache at Fort Sumner and Bosque Redondo, then, in June 1863, to the Diné. Kit Carson commanded the field operations, but responsibility for the policy lay with Carleton. The campaign relied on the destruction of crops, orchards, and livestock, a form of scorched-earth warfare that culminated in the January 1864 expedition into Canyon de Chelly. Diné families responded in different ways: fighting, hiding, negotiating, or surrendering under threat of starvation. Surrender under such conditions was not voluntary migration.

The Long Walk was a series of marches between 1863 and 1866, on varied routes and dates, in which more than 10,000 people, by Smithsonian documentation, were removed. Estimates of deaths differ and measure different things, so this chapter has not offered a single toll. The removals separated families and exposed women and children to violence and captivity, experiences that Diné oral history preserves and military reports largely omit.

At Bosque Redondo, the army's experiment failed. Alkaline water, poor soil, crop failure, fuel shortage, illness, and inadequate rations produced extraordinary suffering, and administrative conflict and cost made the system unsustainable. The Mescalero departed in 1865, a distinct history that further exposed the experiment's failure. Yet Diné society continued to function: families, clans, ceremony, and language survived, sustained above all by women.

By 1868 American officials recognized that the policy had failed, but it was the Diné who gave the failure direction. Barboncito, Manuelito, other leaders, and Diné women insisted on return and rejected removal to Indian Territory. The Treaty of June 1, 1868, established a defined reservation, with provisions on education, assistance, restrictions, and a permanent home. It was a diplomatic achievement and a coercive compromise, and it recognized, rather than created, Diné sovereignty.

The return began on June 28, 1868, and reconstruction was difficult. The Long Walk remains a history of catastrophe and of endurance, and a foundation for later chapters on the reservation order, assimilation, and Indigenous self-determination (Chapters 84, 85, 87, 97, and 118).

Volunteer regiments raised among New Mexico's population for Union service, which also served in campaigns against Indigenous peoples.

The force of California volunteers under Carleton that entered New Mexico in 1862.

A strategy of defeating an adversary by destroying the crops, livestock, and other resources that sustain the population.

The place on the Pecos River where Fort Sumner was built and where Mescalero and Diné prisoners were confined.

The military post established in 1862 at Bosque Redondo.

Surrender without negotiated terms; the army's demand of Diné families, which left no room to negotiate their destination.

Knowledge of the past transmitted through speech, memory, and storytelling; the principal record of many Diné experiences of the Long Walk.

The treaty signed at Fort Sumner on June 1, 1868, restoring the Diné to a defined reservation in their homeland.
Indian Territory
The region in present-day Oklahoma to which several Indigenous nations were removed and to which Sherman proposed relocating the Diné.

The inherent political authority of a nation to govern itself, which predates and is distinct from federal recognition of a reservation.

Goods and food provided by the government under treaty or administration, often inadequately, to Indigenous people on reservations.

Chapter Reflection

The Long Walk matters because it shows, in concentrated form, the methods by which settler states pursued control over Indigenous nations in the mid-nineteenth century: treaties that could not bind, forts on contested land, campaigns that targeted subsistence, and concentration in places of confinement dressed in the language of civilization. It also shows the limits of those methods. Bosque Redondo failed on its own terms, and the failure was shaped by Diné persistence as much as by cost and climate.

The chapter also reveals how evidence shapes understanding. Military reports document intent and administration; Diné oral history preserves family experience and the violence that reports omit. Neither alone is sufficient. Reading them together, and acknowledging uncertainty about numbers and routes, is the method this textbook asks of its readers.

What followed was a treaty relationship that remains central to Diné life and to federal Indian law, a reservation that grew well beyond its original boundaries, and a memory of Hwéeldi that remains a foundation of Diné identity. The reservation order, schooling, and assimilation policies discussed in Chapters 84 through 87 build on the treaty framework created here, while Diné leadership in the twentieth century, including in the Second World War (Chapter 102), shows the continuity of a people who were never erased.

For this textbook, Chapter 71 stands as a case of what Chapter 1 called the persistence of Indigenous nations as political and historical communities. The Diné were devastated and were not destroyed. They returned, rebuilt, and kept governing their own lives, and their history is indispensable to understanding the continent.
