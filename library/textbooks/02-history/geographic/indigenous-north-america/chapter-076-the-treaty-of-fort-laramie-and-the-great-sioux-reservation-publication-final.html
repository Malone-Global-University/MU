The Treaty of Fort Laramie and the Great Sioux Reservation

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

In the summer of 1868 the United States abandoned and left to burn the forts it had built along the Bozeman Trail. On November 6, after the soldiers were gone, the Oglala leader Red Cloud came to Fort Laramie and signed a treaty. The paper he signed recognized a victory he had won in war. It also set out a plan for the federal supervision of Lakota life.

The documentary record of Red Cloud's signing is brief, but its sequence is clear and its meaning is not. Chapter 75 traced how Lakota, Northern Cheyenne, and Northern Arapaho resistance made the army's occupation of the Powder River country untenable. Chapter 76 begins where that story stops. It asks what happened when a military outcome was translated into the language of diplomacy and law. The Treaty of Fort Laramie of 1868 closed a road, withdrew garrisons, and recognized territory as Indigenous land in terms that have been cited in courtrooms and council halls ever since. In the same text, the United States promised agencies, schools, farmers, blacksmiths, annuity clothing, and rations, and it identified a fixed reservation on the Missouri River side of Dakota Territory as the permanent home of the signing bands. Both of these purposes were present in the same document, and most of the chapter's questions come from that fact.

The treaty is often summarized as a promise that was later broken. That summary is true, but it hides a great deal. The 1868 settlement was not a single grant of land, and its provisions did not all protect the same thing. Article II created the Great Sioux Reservation, a defined territory that included the Black Hills. Article XVI recognized a separate and differently described area, the country north of the North Platte River and east of the Bighorn Mountains, as unceded Indian territory, and it required the closure of the Bozeman Trail posts and road. Article XI reserved hunting rights outside the reservation under a condition tied to the buffalo. Article XII required the consent of three-fourths of the adult male Indians occupying or interested in the reservation before any cession of commonly held land could be valid. These categories had different legal bases, different geographies, and different histories, and this chapter keeps them apart.

Keeping them apart also corrects a common error about who made the treaty. The principal agreement was signed on behalf of many Lakota bands and several Dakota and other groups, but it was accompanied by separate agreements with the Crow Nation and with the Northern Cheyenne and Northern Arapaho, each of which had its own diplomatic history and territorial claims. The Crow, Cheyenne, and Arapaho were not appendages to a Lakota settlement. Their treaties were negotiated at the same place, in the same weeks, by the same federal commissioners, and they sometimes pointed in different territorial directions. The Lakota themselves did not act as one body. Spotted Tail's Brulé, Red Cloud's Oglala, and the Miniconjou, Sans Arc, Two Kettle, Yanktonai, and Hunkpapa leaders who signed at other posts held different views and made their decisions on different timetables. Many of the people who would later be most identified with Lakota resistance did not sign at all.

The treaty is also an unusual kind of evidence. The English text survives in the National Archives, in the Statutes at Large, and in published treaty compilations, and its precise language can be examined article by article. But it was negotiated through interpreters, signed by people who had very different relationships to written law, and explained to them in councils that were recorded by federal officials and journalists. Red Cloud's remarks in New York in June 1870, preserved in newspaper accounts, suggest that he understood parts of the agreement differently from the commissioners. Winter counts, tribal oral histories, agency correspondence, congressional documents, and the later findings of the Indian Claims Commission and the Supreme Court each preserve a different angle on the same text. This chapter treats each as evidence with its own strengths and limits.

The chapter also takes seriously the instruments through which the treaty was supposed to reshape Lakota life. Articles IV through X and XIII promised buildings, an agent resident on the reservation, a physician, a miller, an engineer, and other workers, schooling for children between six and sixteen, seeds and implements for those who farmed, clothing for thirty years, and limited food support for families who settled. Article VI created a system by which a family head might select a farm tract of up to 320 acres and hold it exclusively while cultivating it. These provisions were not the boarding-school system or the General Allotment Act, which belong to later chapters. They were, however, the first formal treaty framework for federal efforts to change how Lakota families fed themselves, held land, and raised children.

The central historical question is therefore this: how did the Treaty of Fort Laramie of 1868 transform an Indigenous military victory into a territorial and legal settlement, and why did its promises of protected lands, hunting rights, and continued sovereignty coexist with policies intended to restrict Indigenous independence? Answering it requires following the treaty through five stages: the military and political conditions that produced it; the negotiation and signing; the territorial categories it created; the obligations and administrative machinery it established; and the sequence of pressures, intrusions, and legislative acts, culminating in the Act of February 28, 1877, that undermined its territorial protections. A final section follows the treaty's consequences to the Supreme Court's decision of 1980 and to the Lakota and Dakota nations' continuing use of the treaty as a foundation of political and legal claims.

This chapter does not retell the military history on either side of the treaty. The campaigns of Red Cloud's War belong to Chapter 75, and the Great Sioux War of 1876–1877 and the Battle of the Little Bighorn belong to Chapter 77. The destruction of the buffalo economy is treated in Chapter 81, the wider reservation and reserve order in Chapter 84, and allotment and boarding schools in Chapters 86 and 87. Chapter 76 concentrates on treaty law, diplomacy, territorial rights, administration, and political consequence, because the instability of the 1868 settlement cannot be understood without close attention to what the document said and how it was carried out.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the outcome of Red Cloud's War shaped the diplomatic circumstances in which the Treaty of Fort Laramie of 1868 was negotiated.
  2. Describe the creation, membership, and program of the Indian Peace Commission and distinguish its humanitarian language from the coercive assumptions of its reservation policy.
  3. Reconstruct the sequence of signatures, ratification, and proclamation, and explain why signature, ratification, and implementation must be treated as separate events.
  4. Distinguish the Great Sioux Reservation of Article II, the unceded Indian territory of Article XVI, and the off-reservation hunting rights of Article XI as separate legal and geographic categories.
  5. Analyze the separate treaties with the Crow Nation and with the Northern Cheyenne and Northern Arapaho, and explain how they related to, but did not depend on, the principal Lakota treaty.
  6. Evaluate the treaty's provisions on peace, crime, and compensation, and assess whether they created reciprocal protection.
  7. Explain the agency system, the individual land-selection scheme of Article VI, and the education, subsistence, and annuity provisions, and distinguish them from later federal allotment and boarding-school policy.
  8. Analyze Article XII's three-fourths adult-male consent requirement as a legal protection and as a reflection of gendered assumptions in treaty-making.
  9. Compare the strategies of Red Cloud, Spotted Tail, and other Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho leaders toward the treaty, and explain why no single leader spoke for all.
  10. Trace the stages through which the treaty's territorial guarantees were undermined, from the Black Hills expedition of 1874 through the agreement of 1876 and the Act of 1877.
  11. Distinguish failure to implement a treaty, congressional legislation that conflicts with a treaty, and a constitutional claim for compensation, and apply those distinctions to United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians (1980).
  12. Assess the 1868 treaty as both an achievement of Indigenous diplomacy and sovereignty and a foundation of the federal reservation order.

Historical Context

The Lakota and the northern Plains. By the 1860s the Lakota, the western division of the Oceti Sakowin, or Seven Council Fires, were the dominant power across much of the northern Plains. The Lakota bands, known in English as the Oglala, Brulé, Miniconjou, Hunkpapa, Sans Arc, Sihasapa (Blackfeet), and Two Kettle, had moved west of the Missouri River in the preceding century and a half and had built a political and commercial order around horses, bison, kinship, and alliances with the Cheyenne and Arapaho. Chapters 49 and 50 describe the horse revolution on the Plains. The Lakota ranged over a vast country between the Missouri and the Bighorn Mountains, and the Black Hills (Pahá Sápa) stood at the center of their ceremonial and historical geography. The Lakota were not a state with fixed borders. Their territorial claims were expressed through patterns of hunting, camping, ceremony, warfare, and diplomacy, and those patterns overlapped with the territories of neighboring nations, especially the Crow.

The Treaty of Fort Laramie of 1851. The first Fort Laramie treaty, negotiated in September 1851 near Horse Creek, was the earlier framework against which the 1868 treaty must be read. In it, the United States secured the right to build roads and military posts across the Plains in exchange for annuities and recognition of the territories of the Lakota, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Crow, Assiniboine, Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara. The Senate amended the annuity term when it ratified the treaty, and the relevant nations were asked to accept the change. The 1851 treaty did not create reservations, and it was never a Lakota cession of land. It was, however, a diplomatic precedent: it showed that the United States would negotiate with Plains nations as political counterparts, and it left behind a set of territorial descriptions that later negotiators and later courts would revisit. The Oglala, Brulé, and other Lakota did not all sign it, and its boundaries were drawn according to American cartographic assumptions that fit poorly with the mobility of Plains peoples.

War, gold, and the Bozeman Trail. The next two decades brought a series of crises. The Dakota War of 1862 (Chapter 73) and the massacre at Sand Creek in 1864 (Chapter 74) hardened both American and Indigenous attitudes toward negotiation. The discovery of gold in Montana led to the opening of the Bozeman Trail across the Powder River country, a hunting ground that mattered greatly to Lakota, Northern Cheyenne, and Northern Arapaho communities. The army's three forts along that road became the focus of resistance in 1866 and 1867, as Chapter 75 explains. By 1868 the effort to hold the road had cost the United States dearly in money, men, and political credibility, and the transcontinental railroad advancing across Wyoming was beginning to offer a different route to the same goldfields.

Federal Indian policy. Federal policy toward Indigenous nations in the 1860s combined treaty-making with a growing conviction that the Plains peoples would have to be settled on defined reservations. The treaties negotiated at Medicine Lodge in 1867 with Southern Plains nations were part of the same effort. The Indian Peace Commission created in 1867 was the instrument through which that policy was to be extended to the northern Plains. The policy rested on a view of Indigenous societies that most Lakota people did not share, namely that their economic and political independence could be replaced, over a generation, by agriculture, schooling, and federal supervision. Chapter 84 compares the reservation and reserve order across the United States and Canada, and Chapter 82 treats the parallel Canadian treaty program. The reader should hold two things in mind: the United States was negotiating from a position of military frustration, and it was also pursuing a long-term program that would eventually bring far more force to bear.

Section 76.1

Red Cloud's War and the Political Conditions for Peace

The treaty of 1868 followed the United States' withdrawal from the Powder River country, and that order of events shaped everything about the negotiation. The Bozeman Trail forts, Fort Reno, Fort Phil Kearny, and Fort C. F. Smith, had been built in 1866 and 1867 to protect a road that crossed territory the Lakota, Northern Cheyenne, and Northern Arapaho used for hunting and regarded as theirs. Chapter 75 reconstructs the fighting that made those garrisons difficult to supply and dangerous to leave. For present purposes the relevant point is the result: by early 1868 senior federal officials had concluded that holding the road was costing more than the goldfields were worth, and that the forts could be given up without abandoning the broader project of controlling the Plains.

Several pressures converged. The first was cost. Maintaining three isolated garrisons, supplying them across hundreds of miles, and escorting wagon trains required troops and money that the army wanted for other missions, and Congress was increasingly unwilling to fund a war whose objectives were unclear. The second was the railroad. The Union Pacific was building across southern Wyoming, and military leaders, particularly William T. Sherman, regarded the protection of the railroad as a higher priority than the Bozeman Trail. A rail route made the overland road to Montana less necessary. The third was public opinion. Reports of the Sand Creek massacre of 1864 and of the army's setbacks on the northern Plains had produced a political climate in which a negotiated peace appeared both more humane and more economical than continued campaigning.

The Lakota, Northern Cheyenne, and Northern Arapaho had their own reasons to negotiate, and they entered the talks from a position of real strength. Their leaders had shown that the army could not hold the Powder River country on their terms. They also had practical concerns that diplomacy could address. Trade at Fort Laramie and other posts gave access to guns, ammunition, cloth, and metal goods, and recurring conflict made that trade precarious. Annuity goods, promised under earlier agreements, were also valued by some bands. Many Lakota leaders understood that treaty-making could secure what war had won, particularly if the agreement committed the United States to removing the forts. Different bands weighed these considerations differently. Some groups, especially those whose hunting grounds lay farthest from the Bozeman Trail and the posts, had less immediate stake in the negotiation, and some leaders distrusted any agreement with the United States.

The contrasting objectives of the two sides deserve statement at the outset. Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho leaders who favored a settlement sought the protection of their homelands, an end to military intrusion in the Powder River country, access to trade and supplies, and the continuation of hunting and mobility. Federal authorities sought peace, secure transportation routes along the Platte and the Missouri, reduced military expenditure, and more control over where Indigenous people lived and how they fed themselves. These aims overlapped on the question of ending hostilities, but they diverged on nearly everything else. A treaty that served both sets of purposes would necessarily contain provisions that each side valued for different reasons, and that each side would later read in different ways.

Historians draw on several kinds of evidence to reconstruct these conditions. Army reports and correspondence document the cost and difficulty of holding the forts. Congressional records and newspaper coverage show the shift in political opinion. Lakota winter counts, annual pictographic records kept by individual keepers, preserve Lakota perceptions of the events of these years, though entries vary from keeper to keeper and require interpretation. The records of the treaty councils themselves, taken down by federal secretaries and reporters, preserve fragments of what Lakota speakers said. Each source has a particular vantage, and none by itself fully explains why the treaty took the shape it did.

Section 76.2

The Indian Peace Commission and the American Program for Reservation Settlement

Congress created the Indian Peace Commission by an act of July 1867. Its charge was to investigate the causes of conflict with Plains nations, to negotiate peace, and to recommend a settlement. The commission's eight members came from different institutional worlds. Nathaniel G. Taylor, the Commissioner of Indian Affairs, and Samuel F. Tappan, a Colorado reformer who had criticized the army's conduct at Sand Creek, represented the civilian Indian-affairs and reform communities. John B. Henderson, a United States senator from Missouri, and John B. Sanborn, a Missouri lawyer and former general, brought legal and political experience. The four military members were William T. Sherman, commander of the Military Division of the Missouri; William S. Harney, a veteran officer whose name was associated for the Brulé with the attack on a Brulé village at Blue Water Creek in 1855; Alfred H. Terry, who commanded the Department of Dakota; and Christopher C. Augur, who commanded the Department of the Platte.

The mixture mattered. Civilian members and military members did not always agree about the causes of war or the proper remedy. Reformers such as Tappan emphasized white aggression and broken promises. Military officers emphasized the need to control transportation and to subdue resistance. Yet the commission as a whole arrived at a common conclusion: that the solution to Plains warfare was the settlement of Indigenous nations on defined reservations, where they could be taught agriculture, provided with schools and goods, and supervised by resident agents. The commission's public reports acknowledged that whites had provoked many conflicts, and they criticized the treatment of Indigenous nations in strong terms for the era. The commissioners also argued, however, that reservations and farming represented the only path by which Indigenous peoples could survive alongside the expanding American population.

The humanitarian language and the coercive assumptions of this program were inseparable. The commissioners believed they were offering an alternative to extermination, and many sincerely thought so. But the program presumed that Plains hunting economies were doomed, that Indigenous religious and family life would have to be reshaped, and that the federal government had the right and responsibility to direct that transformation. Indigenous consent was sought through treaties, but the alternative to agreement was left unstated, and sometimes stated, as continued war. The program also treated a fixed territory with fixed boundaries as the basic unit of Indigenous existence, a notion that bore little resemblance to the mobile political geography of the northern Plains. The tension between a genuine desire to end bloodshed and a determination to confine and transform Indigenous societies runs through the entire treaty.

The reservation program was concrete. It involved a defined territory; agencies staffed by federal employees; annuities of food, clothing, and equipment; instruction in farming and mechanical trades; schools; and legal mechanisms for resolving disputes. The commissioners also favored an eventual end to treaty-making with Indigenous nations as political equals, a position that Congress would adopt in 1871, as Section 76.17 explains. The commission's own work in 1867 and 1868 included negotiations with Southern Plains nations at Medicine Lodge and, in the north, the councils at Fort Laramie in the spring of 1868. Its recommendations shaped both the language and the structure of the agreement at Fort Laramie, and the treaty's articles on agencies, schools, farm tracts, and annuities are the commission's reservation program in legal form.

Section 76.3

The Fort Laramie Treaty Council of April–May 1868 and Its Signatories

The commissioners reached Fort Laramie in the spring of 1868, and the opening of signatures on April 29 marks the beginning of the formal treaty process. The date on the principal treaty is April 29, but that date records the opening of the document, not the moment at which every named leader signed. Signatures were collected over many months and at several locations. Brulé leaders, including Spotted Tail, were among the Lakota delegations present at Fort Laramie in the spring, and the signatures of Oglala and other headmen were added during the weeks and months that followed. Representatives of the Miniconjou, Sans Arc, Two Kettle, Hunkpapa, and Yanktonai peoples, and of the Santee Dakota, signed in councils held at other posts, including those along the Missouri River, through the summer and autumn. The treaty's formal title lists these groups together with the Arapaho. This staggered process is crucial to understanding the treaty, because it means that there was no single moment of agreement and no single body of consenting Lakota.

The negotiation required interpreters, and the quality and fidelity of interpretation was a persistent source of uncertainty. Lakota speech was rendered into English by interpreters who were often of mixed Lakota and European descent, were employed by traders or the army, and had their own interests. The proceedings were recorded in English by federal secretaries and reporters, so the surviving record of what Lakota speakers said is filtered through translation and through the priorities of those who wrote it down. Historians treat these records as important but incomplete evidence: they preserve fragments of Lakota statements, but they cannot establish with confidence how any given provision was explained or understood in councils that proceeded in two languages and across very different assumptions about law, land, and obligation.

The delegations themselves were diverse. Spotted Tail's Brulé had long maintained a policy of relatively close dealings with the United States, and Spotted Tail's presence at the council reflected a calculation that diplomacy offered better prospects than prolonged war. His relationship with American officials, however, was not simple deference; he had his own reasons, including the memory of the Blue Water Creek attack and his own experience of confinement at Fort Leavenworth after the 1855 conflict. The Oglala were in a different position. Red Cloud and the Oglala leaders most identified with the Powder River resistance did not sign in the spring, and Red Cloud's refusal to sign until the forts were gone is the best-known example of a leader using the timing of consent as leverage. Other leaders signed or declined for their own reasons, and the Lakota delegations did not form a united bloc.

Alongside the principal treaty, the commissioners negotiated two separate agreements at Fort Laramie in the same season. The Crow treaty was concluded on May 7, 1868, and the treaty with the Northern Cheyenne and Northern Arapaho on May 10. The National Archives catalogs these three documents as distinct instruments, and they should be treated as such. They were separate treaties with separate parties, they were not annexes to the Lakota treaty, and each addressed different territorial questions. Section 76.8 examines the Crow and Cheyenne–Arapaho agreements in detail. For now it is enough to note that the same commissioners were negotiating at the same fort with several nations whose claims overlapped, and that the arrangements reached with one nation had implications for the others.

The commissioners' own signatures on the principal treaty also deserve notice. Most of the commissioners signed, and their names appear with those of Lakota and other Indigenous leaders on the document's first pages. The treaty text begins by describing the parties and declaring that war would cease, and its formal language reflects the conventions of nineteenth-century diplomacy. The document's English phrasing, its structure of numbered articles, and its detailed financial and administrative provisions reveal how much of it was drafted by federal officials in advance. The negotiation, in other words, was less a conversation in which terms were worked out from scratch than a process in which a prepared text was explained, modified at the margins, and presented for signature. That fact bears on how historians read the question of consent, which Sections 76.4 and 76.13 address directly.

Section 76.4

Red Cloud's November 6 Signature: Consent, Translation, and Competing Interpretations

Red Cloud did not sign the principal treaty on April 29, and he did not sign it in the spring. His position, conveyed to federal officials through intermediaries, was that he would not agree to peace until the army abandoned the Bozeman Trail forts. The commissioners, and above all the army, had already accepted that the forts would be given up, and Article XVI of the treaty so provided. But Red Cloud wished to see the withdrawal carried out, not merely promised. When the posts were evacuated in the summer of 1868 and the buildings burned, as Chapter 75 recounts, the condition he had set was met. He came to Fort Laramie in the autumn and signed on November 6, 1868.

The timing is significant for two reasons. First, it shows that the treaty's central territorial provision was implemented before the leader most identified with it gave his consent. This sequence supports the interpretation, developed in Section 76.6, that Article XVI recognized the outcome of the war rather than granting a concession in advance. Second, it shows that Indigenous leaders understood treaty-making as a process in which consent could be withheld, delayed, and made conditional on observable action. Red Cloud's insistence on the evacuation was a form of negotiation: he used the timing of his signature to secure a concrete result before binding himself to a text.

Red Cloud was not the only Lakota leader whose approach differed from the others'. Spotted Tail, as noted above, had accepted the treaty process earlier. Other leaders held back. Sitting Bull of the Hunkpapa did not sign, and his resistance to the reservation order would become a central theme of the later Plains wars. The Lakota bands lacked a single authority capable of committing all of them to an agreement. Political authority among the Lakota rested on kinship, reputation, and consensus within bands and councils, and leaders who signed the treaty spoke for the people who followed them, not for the whole Lakota people. The federal officials who treated a leader's signature as binding on a wider group were applying a model of political authority that did not reflect how Lakota decisions were actually made. This gap would repeatedly complicate the treaty's implementation, and it was a root of the dispute over consent examined in Section 76.13.

Translation and legal language compounded the difficulty. The treaty was written in the technical English of nineteenth-century law, including phrases such as "absolute and undisturbed use and occupation" and "unceded Indian territory," and its provisions on agencies, land-books, and annuities presupposed administrative concepts that had no Lakota equivalents. Even a faithful interpreter would have struggled to convey the full legal weight of such terms, and the interpreters of the period were not always faithful, accurate, or disinterested. The result was a document that federal officials regarded as fully explained and that Lakota signers could reasonably understand in a narrower or different way.

Red Cloud's own later statements are the best surviving evidence of this gap. During a visit to the East in the summer of 1870, he addressed a public audience in New York, and a contemporary newspaper account of his remarks was preserved and is now presented in the Smithsonian's study of the Fort Laramie treaties. Those remarks, discussed in Section 76.17 and in the fourth Primary Source Window, indicate that he understood the agreement as concerned chiefly with peace and the withdrawal of the forts, and that he had not understood it to require the arrangements about residence and trade that federal officials soon sought to implement. Because the account is a retrospective statement preserved in a newspaper, it must be used with care. It is nonetheless the strongest evidence that the signer who is most closely identified with the treaty read it differently from the commissioners who drafted it.

The chapter must also distinguish three separate stages. Signature records an individual leader's consent, given at a particular time and place. Ratification is the act of the United States Senate, which approved the principal treaty on February 16, 1869. Proclamation is the presidential act that formally promulgated the treaty, which occurred on February 24, 1869. Neither ratification nor proclamation marks the date of any Lakota signature, and neither guarantees that the treaty's terms would be implemented. Implementation, the translation of promises into buildings, goods, and actual conduct, was a distinct process that unfolded over years and is the subject of Divisions III through V. A reader who collapses these stages into a single moment will misunderstand both the treaty's history and the later disputes about whether it was honored.

Division IIThe Great Sioux Reservation and the Geography of Treaty Rights
Section 76.5

Article II: Establishing the Great Sioux Reservation

Article II of the treaty defined the Great Sioux Reservation, and its boundaries must be understood precisely. The reservation began on the east bank of the Missouri River where the 46th parallel of north latitude crosses it. The boundary then ran down the east bank of the river to a point opposite the place where the northern line of the state of Nebraska meets the river, crossed the Missouri, and followed Nebraska's northern line westward to the 104th meridian west of Greenwich. From there the boundary ran north along that meridian to the 46th parallel, and then due east along the parallel back to the starting point. In addition, the article set apart all existing reservations on the east bank of the Missouri for the same use.

On a modern map, these boundaries enclosed most of what is now western South Dakota, including the Black Hills, together with the specified existing reservations east of the river. The reservation was a large territory by any standard. Its creation recognized the Lakota presence west of the Missouri in a form that American law could enforce, and its inclusion of the Black Hills reflected the commissioners' awareness, however imperfect, of the importance of that landscape. The Black Hills, called Pahá Sápa by the Lakota, were central to Lakota ceremonial life, and the treaty's inclusion of them within the reservation became, after 1874, the crux of the long dispute that followed.

The reservation must nonetheless be distinguished from the much larger Lakota homeland and sphere of influence. The Lakota ranged over a country that extended well beyond the reservation's boundaries: west to the Bighorn Mountains, south toward the Platte, north toward the Yellowstone. The reservation was a legal and geographic creation that contained part of that country, and a good deal of the Lakota-used territory fell outside it. The treaty's other territorial provisions, Articles XI and XVI, addressed some of that wider country in different ways, as the next two sections show. To say that the treaty "gave" the Lakota the Great Sioux Reservation, as popular summaries sometimes do, obscures the fact that the Lakota already used and claimed lands far beyond it, and that the reservation's boundaries were drawn by American officials to define the area within which the United States proposed to concentrate the signing bands.

Article II also contained a promise of protection. The United States agreed that the reservation was set apart for the "absolute and undisturbed use and occupation" of the Indians named in the treaty, along with other friendly tribes or individuals they might admit with the consent of the United States. The government further pledged that no persons except those designated in the treaty, and the officers, agents, and employees of the government authorized to enter Indian reservations in the discharge of their legal duties, would be permitted to pass over, settle upon, or reside in the territory. This was a significant legal protection. It created an affirmative federal obligation to exclude non-Indians, and it supplied the textual basis for the claim, advanced after 1874, that the government was bound to keep miners out of the Black Hills.

The legal protection and the program of concentration coexisted in the same article. The commissioners did not conceive of the reservation merely as a protective enclave. They conceived of it as the place within which the signing bands would live permanently, learn farming, and receive federal services, as Articles IV through X and XV elaborated. Article II's grant of "undisturbed" occupation was thus paired with a vision of settlement that would limit the mobility of the Lakota and bring them within reach of federal supervision. Students should see the reservation as simultaneously a recognized territory and a mechanism of confinement. The same boundaries that protected land against intruders also defined the area within which the United States intended to administer Lakota life.

Historical Vocabulary
Reservation, Unceded Territory, and Reserved Hunting Rights

Definition. A reservation is land set apart by treaty or statute for Indigenous occupation, described by fixed boundaries; in the treaty, the Great Sioux Reservation of Article II. Unceded Indian territory is land the treaty recognizes as Indian country that has not been ceded to the United States and that is not part of the reservation; in the treaty, the country described in Article XVI. Reserved hunting rights are rights to hunt on lands outside the reservation, retained by treaty under stated conditions; in the treaty, the rights of Article XI.

Historical context. The three categories rested on different articles, applied to different geographies, and were conditioned differently. The reservation was a place of residence. Unceded territory was a place from which non-Indians were to be excluded but in which Indigenous permanent settlement was not envisioned by the same article. Hunting rights depended on the continued presence of buffalo in sufficient numbers.

Why it matters. Calling all protected land "the reservation" misrepresents the treaty. It collapses three legal bases into one, hides the territorial claims of other nations, and obscures the fact that the treaty preserved Lakota rights outside the reservation boundary.

Potential misunderstanding. Modern readers may assume that "reservation" means all land in which Indigenous nations have rights. In the 1868 treaty it did not.

Section 76.6

Article XVI: Unceded Territory, the Powder River Country, and the Closure of the Bozeman Trail

Article XVI addressed the territory that had been the object of the war. In it the United States agreed that the country north of the North Platte River and east of the summits of the Bighorn Mountains "shall be held and considered to be unceded Indian territory." The article added that no white person would be permitted to settle upon or occupy any part of this country, or, without the consent of the Indians first had and obtained, to pass through it. It further provided that within ninety days after the conclusion of peace with all the bands of the Sioux Nation, the military posts then established in this territory would be abandoned and the road leading to them and to the Montana settlements would be closed.

This was a major concession by the United States, and it is best read as a legal recognition of the outcome of Red Cloud's War. Chapter 75 retains the responsibility of reconstructing the actual evacuation of the forts, and this section does not repeat that account. Its concern is the legal form in which the outcome was recorded. The treaty did not describe the Powder River country as a gift from the United States. It described it as unceded, which is to say land that the Indigenous nations had not surrendered, and it obliged the United States to keep non-Indians out and to dismantle the military infrastructure it had built there.

The unceded territory was not the same geographic category as the Great Sioux Reservation, and the difference is essential. The reservation of Article II lay east of the 104th meridian, on the Missouri River side, and was to be the permanent home of the signing bands. The unceded territory of Article XVI lay to the west and northwest, between the North Platte and the Bighorn Mountains, in the Powder River Basin and adjacent country. The treaty gave it a different legal description, with a different protection and a different relationship to Lakota residence. A reader who locates the Black Hills and the Powder River country in the same legal category will not be able to understand why the later taking of the Black Hills, which lay within the reservation, required a different legal process from the later loss of the unceded country, nor why the 1876 agreement addressed both.

The territorial histories involved were not Lakota alone. The Powder River country was also used by the Northern Cheyenne and Northern Arapaho, who were allies of the Lakota in the war and who negotiated their own treaty on May 10, 1868. It was also part of a landscape in which the Crow had long been present and which they regarded as their homeland. The 1851 treaty had recognized Crow territory in terms that overlapped with country the Lakota later contested. The Lakota and their allies had expanded into the Powder River country at the Crow's expense during preceding decades, and the Crow had their own reasons to regard that expansion as a violation. Article XVI's recognition of the country as unceded Indian territory therefore did not settle which Indigenous nation had the strongest claim, and the Crow's separate treaty of May 7 addressed that problem from another direction.

The protection Article XVI promised was also limited in the way that most nineteenth-century territorial guarantees were limited. Its terms depended on continued federal willingness to exclude settlers and to honor the closure of the road. The article assumed a condition of peace, since the evacuation was to follow within ninety days of peace with all the Sioux bands, and it said nothing about the position of the United States if conflict resumed. The Powder River country would again become a theater of military operations during the Great Sioux War of 1876–1877, treated in Chapter 77, and its status as unceded territory was one of the matters addressed in the 1876 agreement, as Section 76.19 explains.

Section 76.7

Article XI: Hunting Rights, Buffalo, and the Limits of Permanent Settlement

Article XI is the treaty's most intricate provision, because it combined a concession with a reservation of rights. In it the signing tribes stipulated that they would relinquish all right to occupy permanently the territory outside their reservation as defined in Article II. But they reserved the right to hunt on any lands north of the North Platte and on the Republican Fork of the Smoky Hill River, so long as the buffalo might range there in such numbers as to justify the chase. The article therefore did not abolish Lakota rights outside the reservation. It limited one kind of right, permanent occupation, and preserved another, hunting, under a stated condition.

The distinction between permanent settlement and hunting reflected a real difference in how the Lakota used land, and it also created ambiguity. The Lakota economy depended on seasonal movement: bands moved between winter camps, summer gatherings for ceremonies such as the Sun Dance, and hunting grounds that shifted with the herds. Temporary camps, seasonal travel, and hunting expeditions were integral to this economy, and the treaty's language did not define where hunting ended and occupation began. A band that camped for a season on the Powder River, hunted buffalo, and then returned to its agency might be engaged in a protected activity under Article XI or in unauthorized occupation, depending on who made the judgment and how the terms were construed. The treaty left this boundary undefined, and the consequence was that its practical meaning would be contested.

The hunting right was also conditional on the buffalo. The phrase "so long as the buffalo may range thereon in such numbers as to justify the chase" made the right depend on an ecological fact the signers did not control. As Chapter 81 describes, the buffalo herds declined sharply in the 1870s and 1880s under pressure from commercial hunting, railroad expansion, and military policy. The article did not say who would decide when the buffalo no longer justified the chase, and it created no mechanism to protect the herds. A right that was guaranteed so long as an animal population persisted could be undermined by actions that destroyed the population, and the treaty did not restrict such actions.

Article XI also contained a series of obligations on the part of the signers. They agreed to withdraw all opposition to the construction of the railroad then being built on the plains, to permit the peaceable construction of any railroad not passing over their reservation, and to refrain from attacking persons at home or traveling or from molesting wagon trains, coaches, and livestock belonging to Americans. They agreed not to capture white women or children, and not to kill or scalp white men. They also agreed to withdraw opposition to the military posts and roads then established, and not to object to future railroads, wagon roads, mail stations, or other works of utility or necessity ordered or permitted by the laws of the United States. If such works were built on the reservation, the government would pay damages assessed by three disinterested commissioners appointed by the President, one of whom would be a chief or headman of the tribes.

These provisions show the treaty's reciprocal and asymmetrical structure. The Lakota gave up opposition to infrastructure that served American expansion, including the railroad corridor across Wyoming and Nebraska and the posts along the Platte. In return they obtained the closure of the Bozeman Trail and the recognition of protected territory. Whether the exchange was balanced is a matter of interpretation, and Section 76.20 returns to it. What the article makes clear is that the treaty placed substantial limits on Lakota autonomy even as it preserved significant rights. The combination of reserved hunting and restricted permanent residence also created potential contradictions in administration. A family expected to make the reservation its permanent home could still, under Article XI, hunt hundreds of miles away for as long as the herds ranged there, and agents charged with distributing rations had no clear mandate for the many months when families were absent on legitimate hunts.

Section 76.8

The Crow, Northern Cheyenne, and Northern Arapaho Treaties: Different Nations, Different Settlements

The spring councils at Fort Laramie produced three treaties, not one, and the other two cannot be understood as footnotes to the Lakota agreement. Each nation negotiated for its own purposes. Each had its own political institutions, its own territorial claims, and its own history of relations with the United States, and the commissioners treated them as separate treaty partners. The National Archives catalogs the principal treaty of April 29, the Crow treaty of May 7, and the Northern Cheyenne–Northern Arapaho treaty of May 10 as distinct instruments, which is the correct way to treat them.

The Crow treaty of May 7, 1868, reflected the position of a nation that had often been in conflict with the Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho and had at times sought alliance with the United States against them. The treaty established a Crow reservation in what is now Montana and addressed the nation's earlier territorial recognition under the 1851 treaty. The Crow treaty was ratified July 25, 1868, and proclaimed August 12, 1868; these dates distinguish it from the principal Lakota instrument, which was not ratified until 1869. The treaty had implications for Crow sovereignty in two directions. On one hand, it provided formal recognition of a defined Crow homeland and of continued Crow hunting rights in specified circumstances. On the other, it narrowed the country the Crow could claim as their own, and it did so at the same time as Article XVI recognized as unceded Indian territory a region the Crow regarded as part of their historic range. The Crow had reason to see the Lakota treaty as ratifying their rivals' gains, and the two agreements together show how the same federal commissioners were reconciling, and sometimes failing to reconcile, overlapping Indigenous claims.

The May 10 treaty with the Northern Cheyenne and Northern Arapaho deserves detailed attention because those nations were independent diplomatic actors with their own interests. The Northern Cheyenne and Northern Arapaho were closely allied with the Lakota in the war for the Powder River country and shared its hunting grounds, but they were distinct nations with their own councils and leaders. Their treaty provided that they could choose to reside within the framework established for the Southern Cheyenne and Southern Arapaho, a reservation arrangement stemming from the Medicine Lodge treaties of 1867, or on the reservation set apart for the Sioux bands. It also retained qualified hunting rights. The treaty was ratified by the Senate on July 25, 1868, and proclaimed August 25, 1868, months before the principal Lakota treaty was ratified in February 1869, and so it entered into force on a separate timetable.

The two choices the treaty offered the Northern Cheyenne and Northern Arapaho illustrate how federal officials thought about Indigenous geography. In the commissioners' view, northern bands could be accommodated in one of two large reservation systems, one on the southern Plains and one on the Missouri side of Dakota, and the nations' own preference for the northern country was a secondary consideration. In practice, many Northern Cheyenne and Northern Arapaho preferred to remain near the Powder River and Tongue River country, and their relationship to the reservation system developed in ways that diverged from both options. Chapter 77 and later chapters examine their subsequent history.

The May 10 agreement also distinguished reservation residence from agency affiliation. Article II named three possible federal agency connections: the agency near Medicine Lodge Creek, the Missouri River agency near Fort Randall, or the Crow agency near Otter Creek on the Yellowstone. It contemplated different portions of the Northern Cheyenne and Arapaho choosing different arrangements, while retaining an expressly qualified right to roam and hunt as long as game remained abundant enough to support the chase. These provisions should not be reduced to an assertion that all northern bands agreed to settle immediately in either of two places. They demonstrate the government's attempt to channel mobile societies into multiple existing administrative systems, not the attainment of uniform community consent or residence.

The three treaties produced overlapping and sometimes inconsistent territorial expectations for several reasons. Each nation was negotiating separately, and the commissioners had no single map of Indigenous claims. The treaties' geographic descriptions used American conventions such as rivers, meridians, and mountain summits, while Indigenous territorial rights were rooted in patterns of use and in alliances and rivalries that shifted with time. The treaties also differed in their terms for hunting and residence, so that Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho hunters might all believe themselves entitled to hunt in the same country, while the Crow regarded the same country as properly theirs. The result was a treaty system in which several protections stood side by side without a mechanism for resolving the conflicts among them. That structural weakness would make the settlement more fragile than the text alone suggests.

Division IIITreaty Obligations, Federal Authority, and the Reservation Program
Section 76.9

Article I: Peace, Law, Compensation, and Federal Responsibility

Article I opened the treaty with a declaration that war between the United States and the signing bands would cease, and it followed with provisions about unlawful acts that deserve close reading. The article contemplated two kinds of wrongdoing: offenses committed by "bad men among the whites" against Indians, and offenses committed by "bad men among the Indians" against persons subject to United States authority. Each was addressed through a mechanism, and the mechanisms were not the same.

If an offense were committed against an Indian by a white person, the treaty provided that the offender would be arrested and punished under the laws of the United States and that the injured person would be reimbursed for the loss sustained. The chain of action began with proof made to the Indian agent and forwarded to the Commissioner of Indian Affairs in Washington, who was to cause the arrest. If an offense were committed by an Indian against a white, Black, or Indian person subject to United States authority and at peace with the United States, the signing Indians agreed that upon proof made to their agent and notice from him they would deliver up the wrongdoer to the United States to be tried and punished under its laws. If they willfully refused, the injured person was to be reimbursed from annuities or other money due to the Indians under the treaty or any other treaty.

The procedural asymmetry is significant. In the case of misconduct by a white person, the obligation lay on the federal government, which was to act through its own officials and courts. In the case of misconduct by an Indian, the obligation lay on the Indian communities themselves, who were to deliver the offender, and the penalty for refusal was financial and collective: payment from the annuities of the whole group. The treaty thus imposed a form of collective financial responsibility on the Lakota for the acts of individuals, while the corresponding responsibility of the United States was administrative and lay in the hands of its own agents. The provisions also treated the legal systems differently. Indigenous offenders were to be tried under American law, and the treaty did not provide for the prosecution of Indigenous offenders by Lakota institutions, nor did it recognize Lakota law as a basis for resolving disputes between Lakota and non-Indians.

The practical difficulties facing Indigenous families seeking redress were considerable. A Lakota family wronged by a white trader, soldier, or settler was required to bring proof to the agent, who in turn was to forward it to Washington, a process that depended on the agent's willingness, on the availability of interpreters, on the assembly of evidence in a form American officials would accept, and on the distance between the agency and any federal court. Article V, discussed in the next section, provided that the agent would take evidence in writing in cases of depredation and forward it with his findings to the Commissioner of Indian Affairs, whose decision, subject to revision by the Secretary of the Interior, would bind the parties. These mechanisms placed investigation, evidence-taking, and decision in the hands of federal officials, and Indigenous complainants had little opportunity to participate except as sources of testimony. Agents, who often relied on traders and interpreters with their own interests, could shape what was heard and what was recorded.

The deeper question is whether a system in which one party controlled most of the investigative and enforcement machinery could provide genuinely reciprocal protection. The treaty's language was reciprocal in form: wrongdoers on both sides were to be punished, and injured persons on both sides were to be compensated. In practice, reciprocity depended on the willingness of federal officials to act against their own citizens, and the historical record of that willingness is mixed at best. Historians of the period have documented numerous cases in which crimes against Indigenous people went unprosecuted, and the treaty's reimbursement mechanism provided no automatic remedy when federal officials did not act. At the same time, the provisions mattered: they supplied a textual basis for complaints, and Lakota leaders and agents did invoke them in the years after 1868. Article I is best understood not as an empty promise but as a framework whose effectiveness depended on the administrative will and capacity of the party that controlled most of its machinery.

Section 76.10

Articles IV and V: Indian Agencies, Administrative Control, and Federal Trusteeship

The treaty's administrative design centered on the agency. Article III first provided that if survey showed the reservation held less than 160 acres of tillable land for each person authorized to reside on it, and many persons wished to farm, the United States would set apart additional arable land adjoining the reservation as needed. Article IV then promised the buildings through which the reservation would be governed. The United States agreed to construct, at its own expense, at some place on the Missouri River near the center of the reservation, by August 1, 1869, a warehouse and storeroom for goods belonging to the Indians; an agency building as the agent's residence; a residence for the physician; and five further buildings for a carpenter, farmer, blacksmith, miller, and engineer. It also promised a schoolhouse or mission building once enough children could be induced to attend, and a steam circular sawmill with grist mill and shingle machine attached. The treaty set maximum costs for each building, which gives some sense of the modest scale of the physical establishment envisioned.

Article V defined the agent's role. The agent was to make his home at the agency building, reside among the Indians, and keep an office open at all times for prompt and diligent inquiry into complaints by and against the Indians. In cases of depredation on person or property he was to take the evidence in writing and forward it with his finding to the Commissioner of Indian Affairs, whose decision, subject to revision by the Secretary of the Interior, was to be binding on the parties. The article thus made the agent the central figure of reservation governance: investigator, recordkeeper, distributor of goods, and the point of contact between Lakota communities and the federal government. It also concentrated authority in the Interior Department's hierarchy and gave Lakota communities no formal role in selecting agents or in reviewing their decisions.

The agency system embodied the principle often called federal trusteeship, the idea that the United States held responsibility for the welfare of Indigenous nations and for the management of their property and affairs. The treaty did not use that term in its modern legal sense, but the structure of Articles IV and V presumed it. The government would hold the goods, employ the personnel, and supervise the process of agricultural and educational transformation, and Indigenous peoples would receive what was provided. This arrangement was compatible with the nineteenth-century concept of the treaty as a contract between nations and also with the administrative reality of guardianship. The tension between these two ideas, nation-to-nation agreement and federal supervision, runs through all of the later history of Indian affairs.

Agency location was a matter of immediate political importance. Article IV envisioned the principal facilities on the Missouri River near the center of the reservation, a location chosen in part for ease of supply by steamboat. But that location was far from the Powder River country and the Platte River, where many Oglala and Brulé families had lived and traded, and where Fort Laramie had been the center of commerce for decades. Many Lakota preferred to receive goods and trade near the Platte, closer to the hunting grounds and to traders they knew. Federal officials, for their part, wished to draw the Lakota toward the Missouri, farther from the overland routes and the railroad corridor. The dispute over where communities would receive their supplies, discussed in Section 76.15, began almost immediately and was one of the first ways in which the treaty's text and its practice diverged.

It is important to differentiate the treaty's stated obligations from later agency locations and administrative practices. The text of Article IV called for the Missouri River site, but agencies that served Lakota communities in the early 1870s were established elsewhere, some of them outside the geographic reservation. Residence at an agency, membership in a treaty-signing band, and participation in treaty rights were separate matters, and conflating them leads to confusion about who was entitled to what and where. The treaty's administrative framework was a design; the agencies that actually operated were shaped by negotiation, protest, military convenience, and the movement of Lakota families themselves.

Section 76.11

Article VI: Individual Land Selection, Agriculture, and the Reorganization of Landholding

Article VI set out the treaty's program for individual land selection, and it is one of the provisions most often misunderstood. It provided that any individual belonging to the signing tribes, or legally incorporated with them, who was the head of a family and wished to begin farming, could select, in the presence of and with the assistance of the agent, a tract within the reservation not exceeding 320 acres. Once the tract was selected, certified, and recorded in a book called the "Sioux Land-Book," it ceased to be held in common and could be occupied in the exclusive possession of the person and family as long as they continued to cultivate it. Any person over eighteen who was not the head of a family could in the same way select and have certified a tract for cultivation not exceeding eighty acres.

The mechanism was administrative. The agent, not the individual, kept the Land-Book. A certificate describing the tract and naming the selector, with an endorsement that the selection had been recorded, was to be delivered to the party entitled to it. The article authorized the President to order a survey of the reservation at any time, and it provided that when the reservation was surveyed, Congress would protect the rights of the settlers in their improvements and might fix the character of the title held by each. The United States was also given authority to pass such laws as it saw fit on the alienation and descent of property among the Indians and their descendants. The article thus left open important questions: what kind of title would settlers eventually hold, how that title could be transferred or inherited, and what authority Congress would exercise over it.

A separate patent-and-citizenship clause. Article VI goes further than selecting agricultural tracts inside the Great Sioux Reservation. It also provides a distinct route for a male treaty participant over eighteen who resides on qualifying land outside the permanent-home tract, provided that land is neither mineral land nor reserved for another federal purpose (apart from Indigenous occupancy). After making improvements valued at at least $200 and continuously occupying the homestead for three years, he could apply, with specified proof, for a patent covering 160 acres. The article states that a person receiving a patent under this provision would become a United States citizen while retaining the benefits guaranteed to Indians under the treaty. This was a conditional, individual route to citizenship attached to the off-reservation patent; it did not make every Lakota treaty signatory a U.S. citizen, nor did it automatically confer a patent on families selecting the 320-acre or 80-acre cultivation tracts inside the reservation. The distinction matters because the same article combines three legal mechanisms: interior reservation occupancy, possible future determination of title by Congress, and a separate homestead-patent provision with an expressly stated citizenship consequence.

The purpose of these provisions was to transform communal land relationships. Lakota landholding rested on collective use of hunting grounds and camping areas, and on rights of access and occupancy that were governed by custom and kinship, not by individual title. The commissioners and many reformers of the period regarded private property in land as a foundation of civilization, and the Article VI scheme was intended to create a class of Lakota farmers who would hold family tracts and build their lives around them. The provisions were an early expression of a policy goal that later legislation would pursue far more aggressively.

An essential distinction must be drawn here. Article VI's land-selection system did not immediately convert the entire Great Sioux Reservation into privately owned allotments. The reservation as a whole remained commonly held land, and only the tracts that individuals selected and recorded ceased to be held in common. The remainder was still collective territory, and Article XII, discussed in Section 76.13, protected that remainder by requiring three-fourths consent for any cession. The Article VI system was also different from the General Allotment Act of 1887, discussed in Chapter 86, which authorized the federal government to divide reservations into individual allotments, in many cases against the wishes of Indigenous nations, and to sell the surplus land. Article VI was voluntary in structure: a family head who wished to farm could select land, and the exclusive possession lasted only so long as cultivation continued. Students should not read the later allotment policy back into the 1868 treaty.

The provision also illustrates the treaty's gendered and familial assumptions. The treaty spoke of the "head of a family," a phrase often read in American administrative practice through male-centered assumptions, although Article VI itself did not expressly limit the 320-acre family-head selection to men. Lakota households, however, were organized around kinship networks in which women held substantial responsibilities and authority, including control over household goods and lodges. The Article VI scheme did not recognize the established roles of Lakota women in food production and household economy, and it imagined the farm as the property of a male head of household. The treaty's design therefore intersected with Lakota gender roles in ways that would produce friction as agents tried to implement it. In practice, relatively few Lakota families took up farming on the scale the treaty contemplated in the years immediately after 1868, as Section 76.16 explains.

Section 76.12

Articles VII–X and XIII: Education, Food, Annuities, and the Federal Assimilation Program

The treaty contained a series of detailed obligations that together made up the federal program of material support and cultural transformation. They differed in subject, duration, and condition, and they must be attributed precisely. Article VII addressed education. It declared that the necessity of education was admitted, especially for those who were or might be settled on the agricultural reservations, and it stated that the signers pledged themselves to compel their children, male and female, between the ages of six and sixteen to attend school. It made the agent responsible for seeing that the stipulation was complied with. In return the United States agreed that for every thirty children between those ages who could be induced or compelled to attend school, a house would be provided and a teacher competent to teach the elementary branches of an English education would be furnished to reside among the Indians. The article was to continue for twenty years.

Article VIII provided for agricultural supplies. When the head of a family or lodge had selected land and received the certificate described in Article VI, and the agent was satisfied that he intended in good faith to begin cultivating for a living, he was entitled to receive seeds and agricultural implements for the first year, not exceeding one hundred dollars in value, and for each succeeding year that he continued to farm for a period of three years more, seeds and implements not exceeding twenty-five dollars in value. Article IX allowed the United States, at any time after ten years, to withdraw the physician, farmer, blacksmith, carpenter, engineer, and miller provided for, in which case an additional ten thousand dollars per year would be devoted to the education of the Indians. Article XIII separately obliged the United States to furnish annually the physician, teachers, carpenter, miller, engineer, farmer, and blacksmiths contemplated by the treaty, with appropriations made as needed to employ them.

Article X was the treaty's principal provision for annuities and subsistence. In place of all sums of money and annuities promised under earlier treaties, the United States agreed to deliver at the agency house, by August 1 of each year for thirty years, specified articles of clothing: a suit of woolen clothing for each male over fourteen, specified garments or the materials for them for each female over twelve, and goods for younger children. It further provided that ten dollars per person would be appropriated annually for thirty years for those who roamed and hunted, and twenty dollars for those who engaged in farming, to be spent by the Secretary of the Interior on articles suited to their needs. The President was to detail an Army officer each year to attend and attest the delivery of goods and to report on their quantity, quality, and manner of delivery. The article also stipulated that each Indian over four years of age who settled permanently on the reservation and complied with the treaty would be entitled to a pound of meat and a pound of flour per day for four years, provided the Indians could not support themselves sooner, and that each lodge or family that moved to the reservation and began farming would receive an American cow and a well-broken pair of American oxen within sixty days.

These provisions had a double significance. For Lakota communities, they offered material support at a time when their economy faced new pressures. The treaty restricted permanent settlement outside the reservation, the buffalo were under growing hunting pressure, and trade routes were changing. Annuity goods and rations could supplement a hunting economy under strain, and the differential between ten dollars for those who roamed and hunted and twenty dollars for those who farmed shows that the treaty envisioned continued hunting for the time being. For federal officials, the same provisions were instruments for encouraging agricultural settlement and cultural change. Schooling was explicitly aimed at "civilization," the farming incentives rewarded sedentary cultivation, and the rations were tied to permanent residence. The same goods that sustained families were thus also levers intended to shape how they lived.

The chapter must distinguish these precise treaty provisions from the later federal boarding-school system. Article VII provided for schools on or near the reservation, taught by teachers who would reside among the Indians. It did not establish distant off-reservation institutions designed to separate children from their families, and the system of boarding and residential schools that grew up in the late nineteenth century, examined in Chapters 87 and 88, developed under different statutes, policies, and institutions. At the same time, Article VII's compulsory attendance pledge and its stated goal of "civilization" were early expressions of the educational ambition that the later system pursued with much greater intensity. The treaty's schooling provisions belong to the same historical lineage but are not identical to what followed.

Division IVIndigenous Consent and the Implementation of the Treaty
Section 76.13

Article XII: The Three-Fourths Consent Requirement and Protection against Future Land Cessions

Article XII is one of the treaty's central legal provisions, and its terms must be stated exactly. It provided that no treaty for the cession of any portion of the reservation "which may be held in common" would be of any validity or force as against the Indians unless executed and signed by at least three-fourths of all the adult male Indians occupying or interested in the reservation. It added that no cession by the tribe could be construed to deprive, without his consent, any individual member of the tribe of his right to any tract selected under Article VI. The requirement concerned adult male Indians, not chiefs, delegates, households, or all adults, and a historian who substitutes any of those categories misstates the provision.

The provision mattered because it created a formal restriction on the surrender of reservation territory. In an era when the United States frequently obtained land cessions through treaties signed by a handful of leaders, sometimes under pressure and often with dubious authority, a rule requiring broad popular consent among the people affected was a significant safeguard. It meant that a cession negotiated with a few headmen, or with a delegation selected by federal agents, would not suffice. The rule set a high procedural bar, and it gave Lakota leaders a legal argument against any future cession that did not meet it. That argument became central after 1876, as Section 76.19 explains.

The provision's authorship and logic reflected federal policy as well as Indigenous interests. The commissioners believed that large-scale consent would give future cessions greater legitimacy and would protect the United States from charges of fraud. They also believed that the three-fourths rule would shield individuals who had taken up farming from the loss of their tracts through tribal decisions, which is why the article's second clause protected Article VI selections. The rule thus served the policy of individualizing landholding while limiting the power of tribal leaders to sell communal land. Its protective function for the nation as a whole was real, but it was embedded in a framework that favored individual tenure.

The gendered nature of the requirement deserves analysis. Article XII specified adult male Indians, even though Lakota women possessed substantial social, economic, and political influence within their communities. Women controlled household production, owned and managed lodges, participated in family and band deliberations, and shaped the decisions of male relatives. The treaty's counting of adult men reflects the conventions of nineteenth-century American law and politics, which excluded women from formal political participation, and it did not correspond to the way Lakota political consensus was actually built. The effect was to define the consenting body in terms that were both narrower than Lakota practice and different from it. Whether Lakota communities would have counted consent differently is a question the sources cannot fully answer, but the mismatch illustrates how the treaty imposed American categories on Indigenous political life.

Students should also distinguish Article XII's collective rule from the individual protections of Article VI. The land-selection provisions protected a family's exclusive possession of a tract it cultivated, while the three-fourths rule protected the commonly held remainder of the reservation against cession. They addressed different property relationships, and the second clause of Article XII linked them by preventing a tribal cession from stripping an individual of a selected tract.

The legal importance of Article XII must be stated without overstating it. The article made treaty cessions invalid against the Indians unless the consent threshold was met. It did not, and could not by its own force, deprive Congress of whatever constitutional power it possessed to legislate regarding Indigenous nations. The question of the relationship between treaty requirements and congressional authority became acute in the 1870s, and it was addressed in later judicial decisions. In Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock (1903), a case concerning a different treaty, the Supreme Court held that Congress could enact legislation affecting Indigenous lands in a manner inconsistent with treaty provisions, a doctrine that has been sharply criticized. The 1877 act taking the Black Hills was an instance in which Congress legislated despite an unfulfilled Article XII condition, and the later conflict between treaty requirements and congressional authority is examined in Section 76.19.

Historical Vocabulary
Sovereignty, Treaty Consent, and Federal Trusteeship

Definition. Nation-to-nation treaty-making is the practice by which the United States negotiated written agreements with Indigenous nations as political entities. Ratification is approval by the Senate, followed by presidential proclamation. Three-fourths consent is the Article XII requirement that cessions of commonly held reservation land be signed by at least three-fourths of adult male Indians occupying or interested in the land. Federal trusteeship refers to the administrative authority and responsibility the United States claimed over reservation communities and their property.

Historical context. The Fort Laramie treaty used the language of a treaty between political parties while establishing an administrative apparatus of agents, warehouses, and supervised distribution. Both ideas, agreement between nations and federal guardianship, were present in the same document.

Why it matters. The coexistence of these ideas explains why Lakota leaders could invoke the treaty as a nation's contract while federal officials treated reservation residents as wards. Later legal conflicts turned on which idea would prevail.

Potential misunderstanding. Treaty-making did not mean that the United States regarded Indigenous nations as equals in every respect. Congress in 1871 ended further treaty-making without annulling existing treaties.

Section 76.14

Articles XV and XVII: Permanent Residence, Earlier Treaties, and the Meaning of Consent

Article XV contained the treaty's pledge about residence. The Indians agreed that when the agency house and other buildings were constructed on the reservation, they would make that reservation their permanent home and would make no permanent settlement elsewhere. But the article preserved the hunting right: they would still have the right to hunt on the lands north of the North Platte and on the Republican Fork of the Smoky Hill River, as set out in Article XI. The article thus restated the compromise of Article XI in the language of residence. The signers promised not to settle permanently outside the reservation, and the United States acknowledged that hunting beyond it would continue.

The promise of permanent residence was conditioned on the construction of the agency buildings, which is a significant detail. Article XV did not require the signers to move to the reservation immediately; it spoke of a future time when the agency house and other buildings were built. The United States' own performance of Article IV was thus a precondition of the Indians' undertaking. As Section 76.15 shows, the government did not complete the agencies on the schedule or in the places the treaty described, and many Lakota families continued to live and trade near the Platte and in the unceded country. The question of whether the Lakota had breached the residence pledge, or whether the United States had failed to meet the condition that triggered it, would recur in disputes about who belonged where.

Article XVII addressed the relationship between the new treaty and earlier ones. It declared that the execution and Senate ratification of the treaty would abrogate and annul all treaties and agreements previously entered into between the parties, but only "so far as such treaties and agreements obligate the United States to furnish and provide money, clothing, or other articles of property" to the Indians who became parties to the treaty, "but no further." The text is narrow. It replaced earlier obligations to supply money, clothing, and other property with the new provisions of Article X, and it did not indiscriminately cancel every earlier treaty provision. Provisions of earlier agreements that did not concern the furnishing of money, clothing, or property, including the recognition of territory in 1851, were not annulled by the language of Article XVII. Students should resist the common summary that the 1868 treaty "replaced" the 1851 treaty entirely.

Differences between legal obligations recorded in English and Indigenous understandings are a central difficulty. The treaty was, from the Lakota perspective of leaders like Red Cloud, an agreement obtained to secure the military evacuation of the Powder River country and a durable peace. Federal officials, by contrast, regarded it as a comprehensive statement of the terms of a permanent reservation settlement. These two readings were not necessarily incompatible, but they emphasized different provisions. A leader who regarded the treaty primarily as a peace agreement might not have attended closely to the articles on agencies, farming, and permanent residence, while the commissioners who drafted those articles regarded them as the heart of the settlement.

The leaders' approaches to the settlement differed in ways that illuminate the treaty's reception. Red Cloud used the timing of his signature as leverage, secured the evacuation of the forts first, and afterward argued that the agreement did not require the arrangements officials sought to impose. Spotted Tail, who had signed earlier, pursued accommodation with federal officials and attempted to secure the best terms for his Brulé followers within the reservation system, including favorable agency locations. Other Lakota leaders withheld their consent altogether, preferring the independent life of the unceded country and the buffalo hunt. These strategies were not simply degrees of acceptance or resistance. They were distinct political judgments about how best to protect their communities in a rapidly changing situation. The treaty could preserve certain collective rights while advancing the government's aim of restricting mobility because it was the product of these competing judgments, written in a form that served the drafters' purposes better than any single Lakota perspective.

Section 76.15

Red Cloud and Spotted Tail Agencies: Treaty Promises and Administrative Practice, 1868–1874

The agencies that actually served Oglala and Brulé families after 1868 were not located where the treaty's text envisioned. Article IV called for principal facilities on the Missouri River near the center of the reservation. The existing Upper Platte Agency moved from its Platte River administrative base to the mouth of Whetstone Creek on the Missouri River in December 1868 and was renamed the Whetstone Agency in 1869. It mainly administered Spotted Tail’s Brulé followers and also served other Lakota bands, including Oglala people remaining under its jurisdiction until a separate Red Cloud Agency was created in 1871. The Missouri River location was remote from the country where most Oglala and Brulé had lived and traded, and its distance from the hunting grounds and from Fort Laramie made it unattractive. Large numbers of Lakota people continued to come to the Platte, where they had long traded and where a federal presence was well established.

The result was a series of relocations as federal officials, agents, army officers, and Lakota leaders negotiated where supplies would be delivered. The Red Cloud Agency, serving Red Cloud's Oglala followers, was established in 1871 on the North Platte River near Fort Laramie, outside the reservation described in Article II. In 1873 it moved to the White River in northwestern Nebraska, again outside the reservation's boundaries, which followed the northern line of Nebraska. The Whetstone Agency moved from its Missouri River site in 1871 and continued to serve Spotted Tail’s Brulé followers; the name Spotted Tail Agency was adopted in 1874, and further moves brought the administration to the White River region near the Nebraska–Dakota boundary. Its later Rosebud name dates to 1878. The Nebraska State Historical Society and other local repositories hold records of these agencies and their relocations, and historians rely on them to trace the details. The pattern is clear in outline even where particular dates and sites require checking against agency records.

Agency location mattered politically for several reasons. Control of the point where goods were distributed gave agents leverage over the people who came to receive them. Proximity to military posts shaped how much force an agent could summon and how secure Lakota families felt. Access to trade determined whether families could exchange hides and other goods for the manufactured items they wanted. Location also affected the balance between competing leaders, since a leader whose followers gathered at a particular agency gained standing from the resources that flowed through it. Red Cloud and Spotted Tail each sought the location most favorable to their followers, and their competition with one another and with the agents shaped the agencies' development.

The records of these agencies reveal a pattern of requests, complaints, and negotiation. Lakota leaders asked for particular quantities and kinds of goods, objected to the quality of flour, beef, and clothing, and protested against agents they regarded as dishonest or unresponsive. Agents, for their part, wrote of the difficulty of distributing provisions to communities dispersed across a large area, of the unreliability of supply contracts, and of the challenge of counting a mobile population. Interpreters and traders exercised substantial influence, since they stood between agents and Lakota leaders and often had interests in how goods and information flowed. The reliability of agency records is variable, and their authors had reasons to emphasize some matters and omit others, so historians read them critically.

Students should recognize that agency residence, reservation membership, and participation in treaty rights were not interchangeable. A family that drew rations at an agency in northwestern Nebraska was living outside the Article II reservation, yet it was still a member of a band that had signed the treaty and was entitled to the treaty's benefits. Conversely, a band that never signed, and whose members lived in the unceded country, could still be treated by agents as a group to be drawn in. Questions of who counted as a treaty signatory, who counted as an agency Indian, and who was entitled to rations were sources of constant confusion and dispute, and federal officials sometimes used the distribution of goods to pressure families to move closer to the agency. This interplay of geography, administration, and political authority was an essential feature of the reservation system as it actually operated.

Section 76.16

Lakota Families after 1868: Buffalo Hunting, Subsistence, Community, and Cultural Continuity

The treaty did not cause all Lakota people to become permanently settled agricultural residents, and the evidence of the years after 1868 shows families making a wide variety of choices. Many continued to hunt buffalo, as Article XI allowed, moving between camps in the seasonal rhythm that had long structured Lakota life. Many also visited agencies to collect goods, to see relatives, and to trade, and then returned to the hunt. The pattern was not a simple transition from one way of life to another. It was an adaptive combination, in which families drew on the hunt, the agency, trade, and in some cases small-scale farming and livestock, depending on circumstances and the strategies of their leaders.

The household economy rested heavily on women's labor. Women processed buffalo meat, dried it for storage, prepared hides for clothing, lodge covers, and trade, and managed the movement and setting up of camp. Hide preparation was both essential to household needs and an important source of trade goods. Women also held responsibility for much of the transmission of cultural knowledge to children, including language, kinship obligations, and ceremonial protocol. The treaty's emphasis on male-headed farms did not match this division of labor, and when agents encouraged men to farm, they in effect proposed a reorganization of the household economy that would reduce the value of women's skills in processing the hunt. Families weighed these tradeoffs, and many adopted limited agricultural experiments or livestock keeping while preserving the larger structure of the hunting economy.

Ceremonial and community life continued as well. The Sun Dance, naming ceremonies, and the gatherings of bands and societies remained central, and the movement of families between camps and agencies often followed the timing of ceremonies as well as the availability of game. Winter counts continued to be kept by individual keepers, preserving a Lakota record of the years that is distinct from federal paperwork. These practices persisted despite the efforts of agents and missionaries to discourage them, and they illustrate that cultural continuity did not depend on formal political councils alone but on daily and seasonal decisions made by families.

The education provisions of Article VII had a limited immediate effect, though they pointed toward a major future conflict. Schools and teachers were to be provided where enough children could be gathered, but the number of children in school in the early years was small, and many families resisted efforts to shape children's activities, language, and religious life. The clash between families' wish to raise their children in Lakota language and ceremony and the agents' goals of instruction in English and farming was a recurring theme in the agency records, and it would become far more acute as the boarding-school system expanded, a development examined in Chapters 87 and 88.

The evidence for these everyday experiences is uneven and largely filtered through outsiders. Agency reports, military accounts, missionary writings, and travelers' descriptions record what officials observed and valued, and they typically say little about women's perspectives or about family decision-making. Lakota oral histories, winter counts, and later interviews preserve other views, but they too require critical interpretation. A key point emerges despite these limitations: Indigenous communities continued to negotiate their relationship with the reservation system through daily decisions about where to camp, whom to trade with, how to feed their families, and what to teach their children, not only through the formal councils that treaties required.

Everyday Life and Social History

Hunting, Agencies, Material Support, and Cultural Continuity

For a Lakota family in the early 1870s, the treaty's provisions were not abstractions but conditions that shaped the year. A family might winter near an agency, where rations and annuity clothing were available, then move in spring toward hunting grounds, trade at a post, return in late summer for the annual distribution of goods, and gather with relatives for the Sun Dance. Each part of this cycle involved decisions: whether to draw rations, whether to accept an agent's invitation to select a farm tract, whether to send children to a school, and whether to join leaders who urged closer cooperation with federal officials or those who urged distance.

Women's work held the cycle together. Preparing hides, drying meat, and managing the household were the basis of the family's economic position and also a source of trade goods that gave families some independence from agency rations. Because ration distributions were irregular and the quality of goods was often disputed, access to the hunt and to trade provided a margin of security. The social history of the treaty era shows families using every available resource to sustain themselves and their communities while the structure of the old economy came under mounting pressure.

Division VTreaty Violations, Territorial Reduction, and the Continuing Meaning of the 1868 Agreement
Section 76.17

Treaty Implementation, Federal Indian Policy, and the Limits of the Peace Settlement, 1869–1873

The Senate ratified the principal treaty on February 16, 1869, and it was proclaimed on February 24. The administration that would carry it out had just changed: Ulysses S. Grant took office as President in March 1869. Grant's approach to Indian affairs, which came to be called the Peace Policy, involved the creation of a Board of Indian Commissioners, composed of reform-minded civilians, and the practice of assigning the management of many agencies to religious denominations. The policy combined real concern for ending abuse and violence with the continuation of the goal of assimilation. It sought to reduce corruption in the supply of goods and to bring Christian influence to reservation communities, and it assumed that the Indigenous nations would eventually adopt farming, schooling, and Christianity.

Implementation of the treaty in the first years was uneven. Congress appropriated funds each year, and the agencies purchased and delivered goods, but agency reports and complaints from Lakota leaders record persistent shortages, delays in delivery, and disputes over quality. Personnel changed frequently, and agents differed in competence and honesty. The agricultural programs were small, and the schools and the buildings promised in Article IV were slow to appear or appeared in forms and places other than those the text described. The distribution of beef, flour, and annuity clothing to dispersed and mobile populations was difficult, and the system depended on contractors whose goods were sometimes of poor quality. These problems did not simply reflect bad luck. They arose from the structure of the system, in which Lakota communities depended on supplies controlled by the agent and the contractor, and in which appropriations depended on Congress.

In the spring and summer of 1870 Red Cloud traveled to Washington and then to New York with a delegation. He met federal officials, including the President and the Secretary of the Interior, and he addressed a public audience in New York in June. The contemporary newspaper account of his remarks, preserved and discussed by the Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian, is the principal surviving record of what he said. His statements indicate that he had understood the treaty primarily as an agreement about peace and the withdrawal of the forts, and that provisions about residence, agencies, and the Missouri River had not been explained to him as the commissioners later described them. Federal officials answered that the written treaty was clear and that he had accepted its terms. The exchange shows two parties disagreeing, in public, about what had been agreed, and it shows that the disagreement concerned the very provisions that implementation had begun to put into practice.

The legal framework of federal-Indigenous relations also changed in this period. In an act of March 3, 1871, attached to an Indian appropriations measure, Congress provided that no Indian nation or tribe within the territory of the United States would thereafter be acknowledged or recognized as an independent nation, tribe, or power with which the United States might contract by treaty. The act also provided that existing treaties lawfully made and ratified were not invalidated or impaired. The act thus ended the making of new treaties but did not annul those already in force, and the 1868 treaty remained in effect. The change had practical consequences, however. Future dealings with Indigenous nations would be carried out through agreements ratified by both houses of Congress, or through statutes, and the House of Representatives, which had been critical of the treaty system, gained a role it had not formerly had. The shift also reinforced the presumption that Indigenous nations were subjects of federal administration, though Indigenous leaders continued to treat existing treaties as binding agreements.

The first years after ratification thus revealed the settlement's characteristic weaknesses. The agencies were not where the text placed them. Promised goods were delivered irregularly. Disputes about interpretation surfaced in public. And the legal framework within which the treaty would be enforced was shifting in a direction less favorable to Indigenous nations. None of these developments by itself destroyed the settlement, but together they limited its capacity to deliver what Lakota leaders understood it to promise, and they created the conditions in which a crisis over the Black Hills could produce a sharp breach.

Section 76.18

The Black Hills Gold Expedition of 1874 and the Crisis of Treaty Protection

The Black Hills, Pahá Sápa, rise from the plains of western South Dakota as an island of forested uplands, and they hold a place in Lakota history and ceremony that has no precise parallel in American law. Lakota accounts treat them as a sacred landscape, associated with origins, ceremony, and the renewal of life, and as a place of refuge and resources, including game, timber, and lodgepole pine. Under Article II of the 1868 treaty, they lay within the Great Sioux Reservation, and the United States had promised that no unauthorized persons would be permitted to enter. The history of the Black Hills after 1868 is largely the history of the failure of that promise.

Rumors of gold in the Hills circulated for years. In 1874 the army sent an expedition into the Black Hills under Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer, ostensibly to reconnoiter a site for a military post and to explore the country. The expedition left Fort Abraham Lincoln in Dakota Territory in July, with a large force of soldiers, wagons, scientists, journalists, and miners. It entered the Hills, which lay within the reservation, without Lakota consent and in tension with Article II's restrictions on entry by those not authorized. Reports from the expedition, including newspaper dispatches and Custer's own reports, described the country in glowing terms and told of traces of gold found in the streams. Those reports spread quickly and set off a rush of interest.

The expedition's treaty status is a matter of interpretation. The government's officers and agents were among those the treaty allowed to enter reservations in the discharge of duties enjoined by law, and the army presented the expedition as a legitimate reconnaissance. Lakota leaders and many later historians regard it as a violation of the spirit and probably the letter of the treaty, since the purposes of the expedition included the exploration of mineral resources and the examination of the country for settlement, and since it entered without the consent of the Indians whose reservation it was. The treaty did not contain an express clause prohibiting military reconnaissance, and the legal question is therefore more complicated than some summaries suggest. The political question is plainer: the expedition opened the Black Hills to wide public attention and created pressure to open them to settlement.

The pressure came from several sources. Miners and speculators organized parties to enter the Hills, some of them in defiance of army efforts to keep them out. Newspapers and territorial politicians in Dakota Territory, Nebraska, and elsewhere promoted the opening of the country, seeing in it an economic opportunity during a depression that followed the Panic of 1873. Some federal officials sympathized with these demands. The army initially removed some unauthorized parties in 1874 and 1875, but its enforcement was limited, and as the number of intruders grew, the government's commitment to the exclusion promise wavered. A scientific party under Walter P. Jenney examined the Hills' geology and mineral resources in 1875 and confirmed that gold was present, which further encouraged entry.

The government's obligation under the treaty was clear: to exclude unauthorized entrants. The extent to which its actual policies failed to protect the reservation is documented in the correspondence of army officers and federal officials. Late in 1875, evidence indicates that senior officials decided that the army would stop vigorously removing miners, while still seeking to acquire the Hills by agreement. A commission under Senator William Allison met Lakota leaders in the autumn of 1875 to negotiate a purchase or lease, and the negotiations failed in the face of the leaders' unwillingness to sell. Soon afterward, federal officials issued an ultimatum to bands living outside the agencies to report to them by the end of January 1876, a step that helped lead to war. This section concerns the political and legal crisis created by the Black Hills discoveries. The military campaigns that followed belong to Chapter 77.

Cause and Effect

Why the 1868 Settlement Failed to Hold

Long-term causes

The expansion of American settlement and transportation across the Plains; the federal commitment to reservation concentration and assimilation; the dependence of Lakota communities on federal supplies; and the treaty's own ambiguities about residence, hunting, and consent.

Short-term causes

Weak implementation of agency and delivery obligations in 1869–1873; the Panic of 1873 and the demand for new sources of wealth; the end of treaty-making in 1871; and the declining capacity of the Grant administration to resist pressure.

Immediate trigger

Custer's 1874 expedition and the circulation of reports of gold, which converted private rumor into public demand to open the Black Hills.

Transformation

The government stopped enforcing exclusion, failed to buy the Hills by consent, and pursued acquisition by pressure and legislation, culminating in the 1876 agreement and the 1877 act.

Immediate effects

War in 1876–1877, the loss of the Black Hills and of hunting rights, and tighter federal control over Lakota communities.

Long-term consequences

A century of litigation, the 1889 division of the reservation, and continuing Lakota claims to the Black Hills.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the failure. The treaty's text, federal administrative weakness, economic crisis, political pressure, and decisions by particular officials all mattered, and alternatives existed at each stage.

Section 76.19

The Agreement of 1876, the Act of 1877, and the Loss of the Black Hills

The war that began in 1876 transformed the political context of the Black Hills question. Chapter 77 treats the campaigns, including the Battle of the Little Bighorn. For the purposes of treaty law the important point is how the federal government used the war to pursue its objective. Congress, in the Indian appropriations legislation of the summer of 1876, tied further appropriations for the subsistence of the Lakota to their relinquishment of rights in the Black Hills and in the unceded territory and to their agreement to a route for travel to the Hills. Rations, in other words, were made contingent on agreement, at a time when many Lakota families depended on rations. Critics have long described this as coercion.

A federal commission, sometimes called the Manypenny Commission after its chairman George W. Manypenny, traveled to the agencies in September 1876 and presented an agreement for signature. It proposed that the Lakota give up the Black Hills and their claims to the unceded territory and hunting rights outside the reservation, in exchange for continued subsistence. The commission obtained signatures at several agencies, but not from three-fourths of the adult men. The Smithsonian's presentation of the findings in the later Supreme Court litigation states that approximately ten percent of adult male Lakota signed. Article XII required three-fourths of the adult male Indians occupying or interested in the reservation, so the threshold was not met by a wide margin.

Congress proceeded anyway. The Act of February 28, 1877, ratified the agreement as if the consent requirement had been satisfied, and it implemented the transfer of the Black Hills and the removal of the Lakota claims to additional hunting territory. The act has often been described as a "taking," and its passage over the unfulfilled consent requirement is the core of the Lakota argument that the treaty was violated. From the standpoint of the United States, Congress had power to legislate regarding Indian affairs, and it was not bound to treat the Article XII threshold as limiting its own authority. This divergence is the legal heart of the later dispute. A later Supreme Court decision, Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock (1903), concerning the Kiowa and Comanche Medicine Lodge Treaty of 1867 rather than the Sioux agreement, would hold that Congress could act notwithstanding a comparable three-fourths-consent clause and treated that legislative judgment as largely beyond judicial review. The case illustrates a broader doctrine of congressional power, not a ruling on the validity of the 1877 Black Hills legislation itself. Chapter 77 and later chapters follow the immediate aftermath.

The Lakota never accepted the transfer, and they pressed claims for decades. After many years of litigation through the Indian Claims Commission and the Court of Claims, the Supreme Court decided United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians on June 30, 1980. The Court held that the 1877 act effected a taking of tribal property that required just compensation under the Fifth Amendment, on the ground, found by the lower court, that Congress had not made a good-faith effort to give the Lakota the full value of the land. The Supreme Court reproduced the Court of Claims’ denunciation that began, "A more ripe and rank case of dishonorable dealings"; this characterization originated with the lower court, not as new language written by the Supreme Court in 1980. The award consisted of the value of the land as of 1877, together with interest.

It is essential to distinguish what the 1980 decision did and did not do. The Court's judgment was a compensation decision. It did not declare the Black Hills the property of the Lakota, and it did not order their return. The Lakota nations, many of whom regard the Black Hills as sacred land that cannot be sold, have declined to accept the money, which has remained in an account and has continued to grow with interest. The legal dispute is thus not resolved by the recognition of a taking. Three questions remain distinct: whether the United States failed to carry out treaty promises, whether Congress's later legislation conflicted with treaty provisions, and whether the Constitution required compensation for the resulting taking. The court addressed the third. The Lakota demand for restoration of the land concerns a fourth and different question, and it remains a living political issue.

Historical Vocabulary
Treaty Violation, Congressional Abrogation, and Compensable Taking

Definition. Treaty violation is a failure to carry out the commitments a treaty contains, as when federal officials do not exclude intruders or deliver promised goods. Congressional abrogation refers to later legislation that conflicts with or overrides treaty provisions. A compensable taking is, in the constitutional sense, the appropriation of property that requires just compensation under the Fifth Amendment.

Historical context. The Black Hills sequence involved all three: tolerated intrusion, an act of Congress that conflicted with Article XII, and a judicial finding that the act was a taking.

Why it matters. The distinctions explain why recognizing a taking did not resolve the dispute. A court can order payment for property taken without deciding that the taking was lawful in treaty terms or that the land should be returned.

Potential misunderstanding. The 1980 decision is sometimes summarized as the Court finding that the Black Hills belong to the Lakota. It did not.

Section 76.20

From the Great Sioux Reservation to Continuing Treaty Sovereignty

The consequences of the treaty for Indigenous political authority, landholding, and relations with the United States unfolded over generations. In the short term, the treaty and the events that followed placed Lakota communities under tighter federal supervision, reduced their territory sharply, and ended their claim to the unceded country and the hunting grounds. In the longer term, the settlement became the foundation of both the reservation order and of a body of Lakota and Dakota legal and political claims. The treaty's two faces, as recognition of sovereignty and as framework for confinement, persisted together.

The Great Sioux Reservation itself did not survive intact. By an act of March 2, 1889, Congress divided the reservation into smaller units, creating separate reservations including Pine Ridge, Rosebud, Standing Rock, Cheyenne River, Lower Brule, and Crow Creek, and opened a large portion of the remaining land to non-Indian settlement. The March 1889 act was expressly conditional on consent meeting the three-fourths rule of Article XII. A commission obtained signatures later in 1889 that President Benjamin Harrison accepted as sufficient; his proclamation of February 10, 1890, brought the act into force. Historians have scrutinized the circumstances of the signatures, including federal pressure and the meanings conveyed to different communities, while the presidential report represents the official determination rather than an independent demonstration of fully informed consent. The division formed part of the broader assault on communal land examined in Chapter 86 and was followed by the disasters of the early 1890s examined in Chapters 89 and 90. This chapter treats the 1889 division as a bounded consequence rather than a subject of full narrative.

The treaty nonetheless remained a living legal and political instrument. Lakota and Dakota nations have continued to invoke it in courts, in negotiations, and in political protest. The Black Hills claim became one of the longest-running land disputes in American history, and the rejection of the 1980 monetary award by many Lakota communities expressed the view that the land was not for sale. Activists in the twentieth century also cited the treaty. The Trail of Broken Treaties and the occupation at Wounded Knee in 1973, examined in Chapters 111 and 112, invoked the 1868 agreement and the demand that the United States honor its treaty commitments. The treaty's continuing significance rests on the principle that treaties are the supreme law of the land and on the Lakota nations' own understanding of the agreement as a compact between peoples.

The conclusion must distinguish legal recognition of protected lands from the effective preservation of those protections. The treaty recognized the Great Sioux Reservation, the unceded territory, and the hunting rights in clear language, and its text bound the United States to defend them. But the machinery of enforcement was in the hands of the party with the strongest interest in their reduction, and when pressure mounted the protections gave way. Recognition on paper did not translate into protection in practice, and the history of the Black Hills is the most prominent illustration. At the same time, the recognition mattered. It supplied the legal and moral foundation for claims that survived the loss of the land and that continue today.

The treaty can be evaluated as both an achievement of Indigenous sovereignty and a foundation of the federal reservation order. As an achievement, it recorded the success of Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho resistance in forcing the United States to abandon the Bozeman Trail, recognized territory, preserved hunting rights, and established a consent requirement. As a foundation of the reservation order, it created the agency system, the program of agricultural and educational transformation, and the fixed boundaries within which the United States later sought to confine Lakota life. These two functions were not opposed alternatives that history later resolved in one direction. They coexisted within the same document, and the treaty's later history reflects the way the United States gradually emphasized one and abandoned the other.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Lakota Life before and after the Treaty

What Changed

  • Lakota land use was described and constrained by treaty boundaries and by the concept of a reservation as a permanent home.
  • Federal agencies and agents became central to the distribution of goods and the resolution of disputes.
  • Treaty provisions linked material support to residence, farming, and schooling.
  • After 1877 the Black Hills and the claims to unceded territory and additional hunting country were lost, and in 1889 the reservation was divided.

What Stayed the Same

  • Kinship, band relationships, and Lakota political deliberation continued.
  • Ceremonial life, the Sun Dance, and the keeping of winter counts persisted.
  • Women's work remained central to household economies.
  • Lakota attachment to the Black Hills and the claim to treaty rights endured.

Why the pattern matters: Change was uneven. It was most immediate for families dependent on agencies and least immediate for those who continued the hunt. Continuity of culture and political claim coexisted with fundamental changes in land and administration. A limited later comparison appears in Chapters 84 and 118.

Historical Debate

Was the 1868 Treaty Primarily a Recognition of Indigenous Victory or the Foundation of Reservation Confinement?

The question: Historians differ about how to weigh the two dimensions of the treaty. Did it chiefly record an Indigenous military and diplomatic triumph, or did it chiefly establish the framework through which the United States later restricted Lakota independence?

Interpretation A, victory and recognition: Emphasizing Lakota power, some historians stress that the United States withdrew from the Powder River country, closed the Bozeman Trail, recognized unceded territory, and accepted a consent requirement. On this reading the treaty reflected the strength of the Lakota and their allies, and the later violations were departures from a settlement that had favored them.
Interpretation B, confinement and colonial design: Emphasizing the structures of settler colonialism, other historians stress that the treaty's articles on agencies, farm tracts, schools, and permanent residence were the commissioners' reservation program in legal form. On this reading the concessions on the forts were tactical, and the long-term design aimed at concentration and transformation.
Interpretation C, simultaneity: Many scholars hold that the treaty was both, and that the two functions were inseparable. The same document recorded the victory and established the framework of supervision.

Evidence: The treaty text, commissioners' reports, army correspondence, the sequence of signatures, Red Cloud's later statements, agency records, and the later legislative history.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and emphasis more than the facts. It turns on how much weight to place on Indigenous agency versus structural constraint, and on whether the treaty's drafters intended its protections as durable or as expedient.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how one understands both the Lakota achievement and the responsibility of the United States, and how one reads the continuing claims.

Student task: Construct the strongest argument for each interpretation using specific articles and events from this chapter, then explain which evidence would best distinguish them. Consider whether the question assumes that victory and confinement were mutually exclusive.

The final question of the chapter is the one with which it began: how could a treaty record a major Indigenous victory and yet create a political framework through which the United States subsequently reduced Indigenous independence and territory? The answer lies in the combination of a negotiated text that served two incompatible purposes, an enforcement system controlled by one party, the pressure of economic and political interests on that party, and a legal framework that gave Congress the means to override its own commitments. The treaty's history does not show that the Lakota victory was an illusion or that the treaty was meaningless. It shows that legal recognition and effective protection are different things, and that Indigenous sovereignty had to be defended repeatedly after the paper was signed.

Chapter Summary

The Treaty of Fort Laramie of 1868 grew out of the military outcome of Red Cloud's War. Lakota, Northern Cheyenne, and Northern Arapaho resistance made the Bozeman Trail forts costly and untenable, the railroad reduced the road's importance, and federal officials turned to negotiation. Indigenous leaders sought protection of their homelands and an end to military intrusion; federal authorities sought peace, secure transportation, lower expenditure, and greater control over where Indigenous people lived and how they fed themselves.

The Indian Peace Commission, created in 1867, brought together military officers, federal Indian officials, and civilian policymakers. It favored reservations, agriculture, annuities, schools, and resident agents as the solution to Plains warfare. Its public language acknowledged white aggression and expressed humanitarian concern, but its program rested on the assumption that Plains economies and political independence would have to give way to federal supervision and cultural transformation.

The negotiation unfolded over many months. The principal treaty was first signed on April 29, 1868, but different Lakota bands and other Dakota groups signed at different times and places. Red Cloud signed on November 6, 1868, after the forts had been abandoned. The Senate ratified the treaty on February 16, 1869, and it was proclaimed on February 24. Signature, ratification, and implementation were separate stages. The Crow treaty of May 7 and the Northern Cheyenne–Northern Arapaho treaty of May 10 were independent instruments with their own parties and territorial implications.

The treaty recognized three different territorial categories. Article II established the Great Sioux Reservation, including the Black Hills. Article XVI recognized the country north of the North Platte and east of the Bighorn Mountains as unceded Indian territory and required the closure of the Bozeman Trail posts and road. Article XI relinquished permanent occupation outside the reservation but reserved hunting rights as long as the buffalo ranged in sufficient numbers. These categories had different bases and geographies and involved the territorial histories of several nations.

The treaty also set up a federal administrative program. Articles IV and V created agencies and the role of the resident agent. Article VI offered individual land selection of up to 320 acres for heads of families and 80 acres for other adults over eighteen, recorded in the Sioux Land-Book, a scheme distinct from the later General Allotment Act. Articles VII through X and XIII promised schooling, agricultural supplies, annuity clothing, limited rations, and federal personnel. These provisions sustained families under pressure and also served as instruments of agricultural and cultural change.

Article XII required that cessions of commonly held reservation land be signed by at least three-fourths of the adult male Indians occupying or interested in it. The provision was a significant legal protection and also reflected the gendered conventions of American law. Disagreements about consent, translation, and interpretation, including Red Cloud's 1870 statements, complicated implementation from the beginning, as did agency placement outside the reservation, uncertainty about hunting versus occupation, and irregular delivery of promised goods.

Lakota families responded to the reservation system through daily decisions. Many continued to hunt, moved between camps and agencies, relied on women's labor in hide and food processing, maintained ceremonial life, and experimented selectively with farming and livestock. The treaty did not make Lakota people into settled agricultural residents overnight, and cultural continuity was sustained by family and community choices as well as by formal councils.

The settlement's territorial guarantees were undermined in stages. The 1871 act ended new treaty-making without annulling existing treaties. Custer's 1874 expedition and the gold rush that followed created pressure the government did not resist. In 1876 a commission obtained a contested agreement that did not meet the Article XII threshold, and Congress enacted the Black Hills taking on February 28, 1877. The 1889 act divided the Great Sioux Reservation. In United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians (1980) the Supreme Court recognized a compensable taking but did not return the land, and the Lakota have continued to press their claim. The treaty remains both a record of Indigenous achievement and the foundation of the reservation order.

Chapter Reflection

The Treaty of Fort Laramie matters because it shows how a military outcome becomes a legal order, and how the form of that order can both preserve and endanger what was won. The Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho compelled the United States to withdraw from the Powder River country, and the treaty wrote that fact into law. In the same text, the United States set out a reservation system that would define its relations with Indigenous nations for generations. Reading the treaty only as a victory or only as a design for confinement misses how the two were joined.

The chapter also illustrates how much depends on precision. The distinctions among the reservation, the unceded territory, and the hunting rights; among signature, ratification, and implementation; and among violation, abrogation, and taking are not technical niceties. They determine what was promised, to whom, by what process, and with what remedy. The story of the Black Hills cannot be told accurately without them.

The treaty's afterlife connects this chapter to the rest of the textbook. The Great Sioux War of 1876–1877 in Chapter 77, the collapse of the buffalo economy in Chapter 81, the reservation order in Chapter 84, allotment and boarding schools in Chapters 86 and 87, the events of Wounded Knee in 1890 and 1973, and the modern sovereignty era all draw on the framework established in 1868. The treaty is not a closed episode but a foundation that Lakota and Dakota nations have continued to invoke.

Finally, the chapter shows Indigenous agency operating through diplomacy as well as war. Red Cloud's insistence on the evacuation, Spotted Tail's negotiation of agency arrangements, the refusal of other leaders to sign, and the daily choices of families all shaped the settlement's history. Understanding the treaty means understanding both what the United States wrote and what Indigenous people did with, and in spite of, what was written.
