Climate Change and the Indigenous North

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

Between 2019 and late 2024, the Yup’ik people of Newtok, Alaska, moved their community nine miles across the Ninglick River to a new site named Mertarvik, house by house and barge by barge. By 2025, public records and reporting indicated that the last residents had moved from Newtok by the end of 2024. The move was widely described as the first full community relocation in Alaska driven principally by climate-related land loss, and just as widely described as unfinished.

The Newtok relocation begins this chapter because it condenses the chapter’s central problem into a single, documented case. A river eroded the bank on which a village stood; ground that had once been frozen year-round became less stable; a community voted, planned, negotiated land, sought money from a patchwork of agencies, and began to move. Yet the case cannot be read as a story of a river and a village alone. It also involves a federal land exchange, a tribal government and a village corporation, a school district, a regional health consortium, construction seasons measured in weeks, and a housing shortage that kept families on the wrong side of the river for years. Climate change entered this history as an environmental force, but what it encountered was a set of political and economic arrangements shaped long before.

This chapter examines northern North America, the Arctic and Subarctic homelands of the Iñupiat, Yup’ik, Siberian Yupik, Unangan, Inuit, Kalaallit, Inughuit, Gwich’in, Dene, Innu, Cree, and many other peoples, as a region experiencing rapid environmental transformation. The most widely cited syntheses of Arctic observation, including NOAA’s annual Arctic Report Card, describe a region warming far faster than the global average. The 2025 Report Card, covering October 2024 through September 2025, reported the warmest Arctic surface air temperatures since records begin in 1900, a record-low winter sea-ice maximum in the satellite era, and ten consecutive years that rank as the ten warmest on record. These are measurements of ice, air, water, and ground. They are also measurements of the conditions under which hunters travel, elders teach, villages sit on their land, and governments plan.

The chapter’s argument is that climate change in the Indigenous North is best understood as a historical transformation of societies and not only a physical transformation of ecosystems. Northern peoples adapted to extraordinary environmental variability for millennia, as the earlier chapters on Arctic and Subarctic adaptation (Chapters 11 and 35) show. What distinguishes the present is the combination of three conditions: a pace of warming exceptional in the instrumental record; a settlement pattern that, over the twentieth century, concentrated people and essential services in fixed villages; and a legal and financial landscape in which communities often hold limited authority over the infrastructure, funding, and land decisions on which adaptation depends. Environmental vulnerability, in short, is produced by the interaction of climate with history.

It follows that outcomes are not predetermined. Indigenous communities across the North have responded in strikingly varied ways: some by relocating, some by fortifying and staying, some by building community-run monitoring programs, some by drafting regional climate strategies, and some by declaring climate emergencies and redirecting energy planning. These choices reflect differing hazards, different political institutions, different relationships to land and water, and different histories of dispossession and negotiation. A chapter that presented Newtok as the template for all northern communities would misrepresent a region of exceptional diversity, and the chapter is organized specifically to prevent that error.

The evidence base is likewise plural. Satellites have tracked sea ice continuously since 1979. Instrumental records, aerial photographs, sediment cores, and engineering surveys document ground and shoreline change. Alongside them stand the observations of hunters, harvesters, and elders, recorded in community-based monitoring programs and research partnerships, which register changes in ice thickness, snow conditions, animal behavior, and the predictability of seasons at scales no satellite resolves. Treating these as complementary forms of evidence, each with strengths and limits, is a methodological commitment carried over from Chapter 1 and Chapter 133.

To keep the analysis disciplined, the chapter introduces a Six-Dimension Indigenous Climate Resilience Framework: environmental security, territorial security, infrastructure capacity, economic security, cultural continuity, and political authority. The framework is an instructional device developed for this textbook, not an externally established scientific index. It asks, for each case, not merely whether a hazard exists but whether communities can maintain access to functioning ecosystems and ancestral lands, whether essential systems can withstand change, whether livelihoods and adaptation can be financed, whether knowledge and language can be transmitted, and whether Indigenous institutions can determine and carry out their own responses.

The chapter has five divisions of four sections each. The first establishes the historical and scientific foundations. The second explains the transformation of sea ice, permafrost, coastlines, and ecosystems. The third, the longest, turns to community experience, including detailed treatment of Newtok and Mertarvik and comparative treatment of Kivalina, Shishmaref, Shaktoolik, Inuit Nunangat, and Greenland. The fourth examines Indigenous-led monitoring, engineering, regional strategy, and the interior case of the Vuntut Gwitchin First Nation. The fifth considers shipping and resource development, circumpolar diplomacy, climate justice, and the relationship between environmental resilience and political sovereignty.

Chapter 138 closes Part XXIII. Chapter 133 opened that part by examining Indigenous knowledge of environments; Chapters 134 through 137 followed with fire, fisheries and wildlife stewardship, extractive industry, and pipelines. This chapter shows how knowledge, institutions, engineering, finance, and political authority converge, or fail to converge, under unprecedented pressure. It also looks ahead to Part XXIV, beginning with Chapter 139, which examines the contemporary Indigenous governments responsible for implementing many of the decisions described here. Contemporary conditions in this chapter are dated explicitly; where this textbook describes a project as ongoing, readers should consult current sources, since conditions change faster than any printed account.

Historical Context

Students arrive at this chapter having studied northern peoples at several earlier points. Chapter 11 examined Arctic and Subarctic adaptations in the ancient period, and Chapter 35 treated Inuit, Yupik, Unangan, and Arctic worlds on the eve of colonization, including the technologies of ice and sea that made life possible. Chapters 99 and 100 followed Alaska Native communities and Inuit North America through twentieth-century colonial administration. Chapters 115 and 116 examined Alaska Native land claims and the creation of Nunavut. Chapter 133 introduced Indigenous environmental knowledge, and Chapters 136 and 137 examined extractive industry and pipeline conflict. This chapter draws on that material selectively and does not retell it.

Three background points are essential. First, the North is not a single environment. The Arctic includes sea-ice coasts, tundra, river deltas, and mountain valleys; the Subarctic includes boreal forest, lakes, muskeg, and large river systems. Peoples inhabited each with different technologies and economies: marine hunting of seals, walrus, and whales along Arctic coasts; caribou hunting across tundra and forest margins; salmon and freshwater fishing along rivers; and trapping, gathering, and trade across the interior. Seasonal mobility, the movement of households between camps as animals and conditions changed, structured much of this life, and it was supported by dense webs of kinship and sharing.

Second, northern societies were not static. They experienced earlier climatic shifts, including episodes of warming and cooling over centuries, and they adjusted settlement, hunting, and exchange accordingly. The Thule Inuit expansion across the North American Arctic, discussed in earlier chapters, is one of several movements that archaeologists associate with changing environmental and social conditions, although the causes of particular migrations remain debated. This history of adaptation is evidence of capacity, but it does not mean that adaptation to present conditions is simple. Earlier adaptation took place among mobile populations with varying combinations of mobile and permanent settlements, substantial material investments, and territorially specific rights and relationships. Present adaptation takes place within settlements that hold schools, clinics, airports, power plants, and water systems, and within legal regimes that distribute authority among tribal, municipal, regional, provincial or state, and national governments.

Third, the late twentieth century produced a new political environment. Land-claims settlements in Alaska (1971) and in Canada’s northern regions created regional and village institutions, discussed in Chapters 115 and 116. Nunavut was established in 1999. Self-governing First Nations in the Yukon negotiated agreements in the 1990s. These arrangements gave many northern peoples new tools, but the tools differ by region and by country. Meanwhile, international institutions, notably the Arctic Council (1996), created channels for Indigenous participation in circumpolar governance. Against this background, the scientific recognition of Arctic warming, dramatized by the Arctic Climate Impact Assessment of 2004, reached communities that already carried the burdens of fixed settlement and limited fiscal capacity.

Geographical scope follows the textbook’s continental approach. The chapter ranges across Alaska, the Yukon, the Northwest Territories, Nunavut, northern Quebec, Labrador, and Greenland. It does not treat national borders as the organizing frame, and it notes where the same people and the same ice cross them. At the same time, it recognizes that the three states involved, the United States, Canada, and the Kingdom of Denmark with its self-governing Greenland, provide very different legal, fiscal, and institutional settings for adaptation. Differences in outcomes across those settings are an important part of the analysis.

The Six-Dimension Indigenous Climate Resilience Framework

This chapter uses a six-part analytical framework to compare cases. The framework appears here, recurs in the community case studies, and returns in Section 138.20. It is an MGU instructional framework, not a scientific index, and should not be treated as a measurement tool. Its purpose is to prevent two reductions: treating climate change as a purely physical problem, and treating adaptation as a purely technical one.

1 · Environmental security

Can communities maintain access to functioning ecosystems? Healthy, predictable ice, ground, water, and animal populations on which livelihoods and diets depend.

2 · Territorial security

Can communities remain connected to ancestral homelands? Physical presence, land tenure, travel routes, and the capacity to use territory as the community chooses.

3 · Infrastructure capacity

Can essential community systems withstand environmental change? Housing, water and sanitation, power, airstrips, roads, schools, and clinics.

4 · Economic security

Can communities sustain livelihoods and finance adaptation? Wage and harvesting economies, public funding, and capital for construction and maintenance.

5 · Cultural continuity

Can knowledge, language, foodways, and practices be transmitted? Intergenerational learning, ceremony, and land-based skills.

6 · Political authority

Can Indigenous institutions determine and implement their own responses? Decision-making power, jurisdiction, and control over funds, data, and planning.

The dimensions interact. A community may retain strong cultural continuity while lacking infrastructure capacity, or may have capable political institutions but insufficient funding. The sixth dimension is distinctive: it holds that a community’s ability to decide matters independently of the physical outcome. Two communities that both relocate may differ greatly depending on who made the decision and who controls the new site. Environmental resilience and political sovereignty, the chapter will argue, are related but not identical.

Section 138.1

The Indigenous North in an Age of Climate Transformation

The Arctic and Subarctic are sometimes described, in outside accounts, as remote peripheries at the edge of national territories. For the peoples who live there they are centers: homelands with names, histories, routes, and obligations. Iñupiat communities of Alaska’s northern and northwestern coasts, Yup’ik and Cup’ik communities of the Yukon–Kuskokwim Delta and Nelson Island, Siberian Yupik communities of St. Lawrence Island, Unangan communities of the Aleutian and Pribilof Islands, Inuit communities across the Inuvialuit Settlement Region, Nunavut, Nunavik, and Nunatsiavut, Kalaallit and Inughuit communities of Greenland, Gwich’in and Dene communities of the Yukon, Northwest Territories, and Alaska interior, and Innu, Cree, and other peoples of Labrador and northern Quebec belong to a northern world connected by language families, trade, kinship, and shared environments. Many of these connections cross the boundaries of Alaska, Canada, and Greenland, boundaries that are younger than the relationships they divide.

Life across this region was organized around seasonal cycles. In coastal communities, the movement from open water to ice, and back, structured the year: whaling and sealing in particular seasons, travel across sea ice to hunting grounds and neighboring communities, fishing through the ice, and the storage and sharing of food for the dark months. In river and forest settings, the seasons governed caribou migrations, salmon and whitefish runs, berry and plant gathering, trapping, and travel by boat in summer and by snowmobile, dog team, or on foot across frozen rivers and lakes in winter. Households and communities moved between locations as these cycles dictated. Social organization, including kinship, the sharing of harvests, and the transmission of knowledge between generations, was integrated with this seasonal mobility.

Snow, ice, rivers, tundra, forest, and sea therefore served as more than scenery. They were infrastructure, in the broad sense of the term: the surfaces and systems that carried travel, trade, and food. Sea ice was a highway and a hunting platform; frozen rivers were winter roads; permafrost kept ground firm beneath houses, cellars, and graves; snow cover insulated the land and shaped animal movements. When these elements change, the effects are not restricted to ecology. They alter what is possible and safe for people, and they alter the knowledge that older generations hold about reading conditions, because that knowledge was formed in a more predictable environment.

Northern peoples have adapted to environmental change for millennia. Archaeological and oral historical evidence, discussed in Chapters 11 and 35, shows movement, innovation in technology and settlement, and responses to shifting climates and animal populations. It would be a mistake, however, to conclude that because adaptation is an old skill, contemporary climate change is simply another variation. The scientific literature on Arctic change describes the current rate of warming as exceptional within the instrumental record, and it attributes the long-term trend principally to human-driven greenhouse gas emissions, a causal conclusion established by the broad consensus of climate science. Distinguishing anthropogenic climate change from the natural variability to which earlier peoples adapted matters for historical reasons too: the present change was not produced by northern communities, whose aggregate contribution to global emissions is small, even though isolated diesel-dependent settlements may have high per-capita fuel consumption, and it unfolds within institutional conditions those earlier generations did not face.

For this reason, northern environmental change constitutes a historical transformation of societies as well as a physical transformation of ecosystems. It affects the conditions under which governments plan, communities decide where to live, families teach children, and nations assert authority over land and water. The remainder of this division establishes how colonial history shaped those conditions, what the science says, and how different forms of evidence document change.

Section 138.2

Colonial Settlement and the Historical Foundations of Climate Vulnerability

Why do many contemporary northern communities experience climate hazards differently from their ancestors? A central part of the answer lies in how, and why, people came to live where they now live. Before sustained colonial administration, many northern households moved seasonally across wide territories. They might winter in one location, fish or hunt in another, and gather in larger groups at particular times. A hazard such as a storm surge, a shifting river channel, or an unstable bank could be answered by moving camp, and moving some camps could be less costly than relocating a modern serviced village, although permanent dwellings and storage facilities could involve substantial investments. Mobility was a form of risk management.

Over the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, missions, trading posts, whaling stations, government schools, health services, and later housing and administrative programs pulled and pushed households toward fixed locations. In Alaska, the siting of schools and post offices, the legal requirements of compulsory education, and the logic of delivering services efficiently encouraged year-round occupation of particular places. In Canada, the policies discussed in Chapter 100, including the establishment of permanent Inuit settlements in the mid-twentieth century, the administration of schooling, the economics of the fur trade’s decline, and in some cases the relocation of families by government, moved people from camps into communities where services could be concentrated. These processes varied in coercion and in speed, and Indigenous people made their own choices within them, including choosing to settle for the sake of schooling, health care, and wage employment. But the overall effect was a transformation of settlement patterns.

The consequences are now infrastructural. A village is not only a cluster of houses. It includes a school, a clinic, a power plant with fuel storage, a water and sanitation system, an airstrip that is often the only year-round link to the outside, a landfill, and roads or boardwalks. These facilities are expensive to build in remote conditions and cannot easily be moved. A community that once could relocate its camp now holds capital investments that bind it to a location, and public funding for services is often tied to the existing site. When erosion or flooding threatens that site, the community faces a choice that earlier generations did not: abandon substantial infrastructure, pay to defend it, or fund a replacement settlement.

The siting of many villages also reflected the needs of outsiders. Locations were chosen for access to barge landings, for proximity to trading routes, or for administrative convenience, not always for long-term environmental stability. Government and mission planners did not necessarily consult residents about where the village should sit, and they could not have anticipated the rate of present change. Alaska’s experience with boarding and day schools, discussed in Chapter 99, and Canada’s experience with residential schools, discussed in Chapters 87 and 88, also removed children from seasonal land-based learning during formative years, with effects on the transmission of the very skills that support flexible response to environmental change. The point is not that colonial administration caused climate change. It is that administration shaped the exposure of communities to climate hazards and constrained their options for responding.

Political and legal arrangements matter as well. The Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of 1971 vested land in village and regional corporations rather than in tribal governments, and it did not create reservations in most of the state, as Chapter 115 explains. Village corporations received land selected under statutory rules, sometimes in places chosen for economic prospects or access, and tribal governments (federally recognized villages) have different authorities from the corporations. Relocation requires land, and the Newtok case shows how acquiring a site can depend on complex exchanges involving corporations, federal agencies, and wildlife refuges. In Canada, the northern land-claims agreements of Chapter 116 created regional governments and institutions, some with substantial resources, others with limited fiscal capacity relative to infrastructure needs. In each case, how much a community can do about a climate hazard depends on who owns the land, who controls the budget, and which government has the authority to approve and finance construction.

Historical Vocabulary
Climate Vulnerability and Adaptive Capacity

Definition. Climate vulnerability is the degree to which a community is likely to be harmed by climate-related hazards. It depends on exposure to the hazard, sensitivity to harm, and the community’s capacity to respond. Adaptive capacity is that capacity: the combination of resources, knowledge, institutions, and authority that allows a community to anticipate, absorb, or recover from change.

Historical context. Vulnerability is not an inherent property of a place or people. In the North it has been shaped by settlement history, infrastructure investment, land tenure, and funding structures. A community on thawing ground with limited funding and little control over land decisions is more vulnerable than an identical hazard would make a community with abundant resources and authority.

Why it matters. The concepts direct attention from hazards alone to the social and political conditions that determine outcomes, which is the central argument of this chapter.

Potential misunderstanding. “Vulnerable” can slide into implying that Indigenous people are passive or weak. As used here, vulnerability describes conditions produced by history, and adaptive capacity includes Indigenous knowledge, social organization, and political action.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Seasonal Mobility to Permanent Settlement

What changed

  • Households that once moved between seasonal camps came to reside year-round in fixed villages, in many cases over the course of the mid-twentieth century.
  • Essential services were concentrated in settlements: schools, clinics, power plants, water systems, and airstrips.
  • Wage employment, imported goods, and public programs became part of household economies, increasing the cash costs of harvesting and living.
  • Decisions about land and construction increasingly involved external agencies and funding programs.

What stayed the same

  • Seasonal harvesting, sharing, and travel on the land and sea continued to organize social and cultural life.
  • Kinship networks and community decision-making persisted, and have been central to relocation planning.
  • Knowledge of place and conditions continued to be passed on, even as colonial schooling interrupted it.

Pace and experience. Change occurred unevenly, over decades, differing by region; elders have often experienced the transition personally. Children and younger adults inherit settled life as the baseline, which makes adaptation to environmental threats to settlements especially consequential for them. Why the pattern matters: it shows that today’s hazards meet communities already reshaped by earlier policies, and that responses may involve rediscovering flexibility without discarding the services settlement made available.

Section 138.3

Arctic Amplification and the Science of a Warming North

Students need a working understanding of the science to follow the historical developments in this chapter, without a full course in climatology. The basic causal chain is as follows. Human activities, principally the burning of fossil fuels and land-use change, have increased concentrations of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere, which trap heat and raise global average temperatures. The Arctic has warmed faster than the global average, a pattern called Arctic amplification. Scientific assessments, including the Arctic Report Card series, describe Arctic warming as more than twice the global rate over recent decades; some analyses cite larger multiples depending on period and method, and readers should note that the exact ratio depends on the definition of the Arctic region and the time frame used.

Several mechanisms contribute. One is the ice–albedo feedback: snow and ice are bright and reflect most incoming sunlight, but open ocean and bare ground absorb more. As ice and snow retreat, more heat is absorbed, which causes further warming and melting. The 2025 Report Card noted, for example, that loss of late-spring snow cover exposes dark surfaces earlier, warming the land. Other contributors include changes in atmospheric and ocean heat transport, in cloud and water vapor, and in the vertical structure of the lower atmosphere, which in the Arctic tends to confine warming near the surface. Ocean heat also matters: the Report Card documented some of the warmest Arctic August sea-surface temperatures in the instrumental record in 2025, though with strong regional differences. The scientific details remain an active research area, but the broad conclusion, that the Arctic is warming faster than the planet as a whole, is well established.

Careful reading of Arctic science requires several distinctions. First, climate trends are long-term patterns, typically measured over decades, whereas weather events are short-term conditions. A cold winter in a given place does not refute a long-term warming trend, and a single devastating storm does not by itself prove it, although a changing climate can alter the likelihood and character of storms. Second, observed changes are measured and documented, while projections are model-based estimates of the future under assumed emissions scenarios; this chapter treats projections as projections and does not present them as accomplished facts. Third, regional warming differs from global averages, and within the Arctic there are marked differences between regions and seasons. Fourth, human-driven change must be distinguished from natural variability, such as decade-scale oscillations in atmospheric circulation; scientific attribution studies indicate that natural variability alone does not account for the observed long-term warming.

The record is extensively documented. The satellite record of sea ice begins in 1979. NOAA’s 2025 Report Card, the twentieth in the series, reported that the March 2025 maximum sea-ice extent was the lowest of the 47-year satellite record, that the September 2025 minimum was the tenth lowest, that all nineteen lowest September minimums occurred within the previous nineteen years, and that the end-of-summer ice extent in 2025 was roughly 28 percent smaller than in 2005 and considerably thinner and younger. It reported surface air temperatures from October 2024 through September 2025 as the warmest since records began in 1900, with the previous ten years the ten warmest, and it estimated that the Greenland Ice Sheet lost about 129 billion tons of ice in 2025. These findings should be read with their dates: they describe a specific reporting period, and subsequent reports will update them. They should also be read alongside the methodological point that, as the lead editor noted, data do not interpret themselves; their meaning for communities depends on local conditions.

Science does not make decisions for communities, and its role in this chapter is limited. Climate science explains why northern conditions are changing and bounds what may be expected. The historical question is how Indigenous communities, with specific histories and institutions, experience, interpret, and act upon those changes. The next section turns to the evidence through which change becomes visible to scientists and to the people living with it.

Subsequent satellite observations sharpen the distinction between individual years and a long-term trend. NSIDC identified the likely 2026 annual minimum on September 12 at 4.60 million square kilometres, sharing tenth place in the record of lowest minima. Its October analysis gave a September monthly mean of 4.81 million square kilometres, thirteenth lowest. A daily minimum and a monthly average answer different questions; both can be correct without contradiction. NSIDC calculated a downward September trend through 2026 of 11.6 percent per decade relative to the 1981–2010 mean. The return of the minimum to the same rank as 2025 does not demonstrate recovery. A year-to-year fluctuation is superimposed on the multidecadal contraction of ice. For communities, the relevant measurements also include when nearshore ice forms, how it attaches to land, and whether travel routes remain usable. These conditions cannot be inferred from a single circumpolar number. [S03, S04]

NSIDC also reported that the March 2026 maximum was statistically tied with the 2025 record low. Winter maxima and summer minima measure different seasonal processes; both should be considered when comparing years. [S45]

Section 138.4

Knowing the Changing North: Historical Evidence and Environmental Observation

How do historians and communities reconstruct environmental change? Several kinds of evidence are available, and each answers a different question. Satellite observations provide continuous, pan-Arctic measurements of sea-ice extent, snow cover, vegetation greenness, and surface temperature, beginning for sea ice in 1979. Their strength is breadth and consistency over time; their limitation is spatial resolution and the fact that they measure the surface from above, so they do not directly reveal whether ice is safe to cross, how thick a particular channel is, or how an animal is behaving. Instrumental temperature records from weather stations extend further back, in some places to the early twentieth century, but stations are sparse in the Arctic, and their placement, often at airports in villages, introduces local biases that researchers adjust for.

Aerial photography and historical maps allow measurement of shoreline and landscape change. Comparing photographs of a village from several decades apart can document erosion rates, as engineers and agencies have done at Newtok and elsewhere in Alaska. Their limitation is irregular coverage and the fact that maps record what mapmakers chose to represent. Sediment, ice-core, and tree-ring records reach back centuries to millennia, enabling comparison of present conditions with earlier climates, though they carry dating uncertainties and often represent regional rather than local conditions. Engineering surveys and borehole measurements track ground temperatures and the depth of the seasonally thawed active layer in specific locations.

Community observations, oral histories, harvesting records, and intergenerational knowledge provide a different kind of evidence. Hunters, elders, and harvesters observe where and when ice forms, what color and texture signal danger, how snow drifts and winds behave, where animals appear and disappear, how birds time their arrival, and how the taste or condition of fish and game differ across years. Many Indigenous knowledge holders speak not only of what changed but of the loss of predictability: weather signs that elders learned to read no longer reliably forecast conditions. Community harvest surveys and calendars record quantities and timing of harvests and can be compared across years. This evidence has high spatial and temporal detail for specific places, and it integrates multiple variables as they bear on practical decisions. Its limitations are those of any observation-based source: it is tied to particular observers and locations, it may be shared under protocols that govern how it is recorded and used, and its record may not extend into time frames beyond living memory unless supported by recorded oral tradition.

These forms of evidence are best understood as complementary, not as competitors. A satellite record can show that ice extent in a sea region has declined; a hunter can explain that a particular route used for generations became unsafe in early winter, that the ice edge now forms later and breaks up earlier, or that a seal haul-out has moved. When the two agree, as they often do, they reinforce each other; when they differ, the difference can reveal scale effects or point to variables that one method does not capture. Chapter 133 examined Indigenous environmental knowledge as a system, and Chapter 145 will examine the governance of knowledge, including questions of data control. In this chapter the emphasis is on how evidence of different kinds documents change and informs adaptation, and on who decides what counts and how it is used.

Source criticism applies to all of it. Scientific datasets reflect the choices of the institutions that fund and maintain them, and recent reporting has noted that Arctic observation faces data-continuity challenges, a reminder that the record depends on sustained public investment. Governmental reports reflect agency priorities. Journalism often concentrates on dramatic cases. Community testimony recorded by outside researchers may be shaped by the questions asked. The historian’s task is to ask who produced each source, for what purpose, with what methods, and what it leaves out.

Section 138.5

The Transformation of Sea Ice and Northern Mobility

For coastal Inuit, Iñupiat, Yupik, Inughuit, and Kalaallit communities, sea ice is simultaneously a transportation network, a hunting environment, a cultural landscape, and an economic foundation. It connects villages to one another and to hunting grounds; it supports camps, whaling, sealing, and fishing; it provides a platform on which travel by snowmobile, dog team, and on foot becomes possible in winter and spring; and it is a setting for knowledge, since reading ice is among the most demanding and prized forms of expertise. Inuit terms for different types and conditions of ice, recorded in dictionaries and by researchers, reflect the practical importance of distinguishing among them. Scientists, too, distinguish between landfast ice, anchored to the shore or seabed, and drifting pack ice, because the first provides a more stable platform and also protects shorelines from waves.

Observed changes include shifts in the timing of freeze-up (the formation of stable ice in autumn) and breakup (its dissolution in spring), thinner and younger ice, a shorter season in which ice is usable, and, in many places, reduced predictability. Hunters in widely separated regions have described the same pattern in community research and testimony: ice that forms later than it once did, leads and cracks appearing in places where ice was formerly reliable, and spring breakup arriving sooner. At the pan-Arctic scale the satellite record documents the decline summarized in Section 138.3, including the record-low March maximum of 2025. At the community scale, what matters is not the pan-Arctic number but the number of days when a particular route can be traveled safely.

The consequences are practical and cumulative. Delayed freeze-up shortens the winter travel season and can leave communities waiting before they can reach caribou, seals, or fishing sites. Thin or unstable ice raises the risk of breaking through, of being carried away on a detached floe, or of being stranded. Earlier breakup can end spring hunting before households have completed their harvests. Access to particular hunting grounds can be lost altogether when a route becomes impassable. Hunters respond in several ways, including changing routes, relying more on boats in open water, delaying travel, abandoning some trips, or using new information technologies; but each adaptation carries costs, such as additional fuel, equipment, and risk, and not all households can bear them equally. Search-and-rescue incidents, discussed in Section 138.9, are one measure of the hazard.

Inuit communities in Alaska, across Inuit Nunangat, and in Greenland all describe versions of these changes, though conditions differ. Research with hunters in northwestern Greenland, for example, has documented how shorter sea-ice seasons have affected dog-sledding and the hunting of marine mammals from the ice, prompting adjustments in equipment and in the species and places pursued. Studies in Nunavut, Nunatsiavut, and elsewhere in the Canadian Arctic have likewise recorded the adaptation of travel routes and timing. These accounts should not be generalized without care: some places experience new open-water opportunities, some experience a loss of stable ice, and many experience both in different seasons.

The loss of sea ice is not simply the disappearance of frozen water. It changes how people move through, understand, and use their territories. A route that grandparents traveled in a particular month may no longer be reliable; the knowledge of reading that route may become less applicable; and the skills that young hunters acquire may need to be rebuilt with new reference points. Territory, in this sense, is not only a location but a set of practiced relationships with conditions. When those conditions shift, territorial security, the second dimension of the framework, is affected even if no one has left home.

The historical record of ice use also reveals differences within a single season. A household may depend on a frozen corridor to reach a hunting camp while a different household needs an opening in the ice to launch a boat. Changes in the location and stability of leads can redistribute access rather than simply reduce it. When travel becomes uncertain, households with reliable boats, fuel, communications equipment and wage income may have more alternatives than households without those resources. The distribution of adaptation costs therefore follows social and economic relationships as well as the changing geography of ice.

Satellite-derived extent is calculated across grid cells meeting an ice-concentration threshold; it is not a measure of the load-bearing strength of any particular route. Landfast ice can deteriorate even where a regional extent figure appears relatively stable. This explains why the historical development of local monitoring, including SmartICE, is not redundant with the satellite record. Communities require observations at a scale relevant to harvesting and travel. A scientifically informed map and a hunter's judgment answer different questions; neither can safely be substituted for the other. [S03, S22]

Section 138.6

Permafrost Thaw and the Transformation of Northern Landscapes

Permafrost is ground, soil, rock, or sediment that remains at or below freezing for at least two consecutive years. In much of the Arctic and Subarctic it underlies large areas, from continuous permafrost in the far north to discontinuous and sporadic patches farther south. Above it lies the active layer, which thaws each summer and refreezes in winter. Many permafrost soils contain ground ice, sometimes in large volumes. When permafrost warms or the active layer deepens, ground ice melts, the ground surface can sink, and water can drain or pond in new ways. Thermokarst describes the landscapes of pits, sinkholes, slumps, and ponds produced by thaw of ice-rich ground, and ground subsidence describes the sinking itself.

Thaw is not uniform. Gradual deepening of the active layer can slowly destabilize foundations, causing differential settlement, cracked walls, and leaning buildings. Abrupt thaw, in contrast, involves rapid, localized collapse, as when a hillside slumps, a lake drains suddenly, or a riverbank retreats because thawing ice-rich sediments slide away. Researchers distinguish these processes because they differ in predictability and in the kinds of damage they cause. Warming ground temperatures have been measured at monitoring sites in Alaska, northwestern Canada, and elsewhere, and the Arctic Report Card has documented the warming of permafrost across many sites in recent decades, although values vary by location.

The consequences for community infrastructure are well documented. Houses and public buildings founded on ice-rich permafrost can sink and tilt. Roads and airstrips, essential for supply and medical evacuation, can develop ruts and depressions requiring repair. Water and sewer lines, especially buried pipes and utilidors, can shift and break. Fuel tanks and pipelines can be damaged. Cemeteries and archaeological sites, which preserve community history and ancestral burials, can be disturbed as ground collapses or erodes; this is a loss of cultural heritage that does not appear in infrastructure budgets. Studies by Alaska agencies and researchers have estimated substantial future costs of permafrost-related damage to public infrastructure statewide, though such estimates depend on assumptions about emissions, design, and maintenance, and should be read as estimates rather than forecasts of certain cost.

Examples span the region. In western Alaska, Newtok’s site on ice-rich ground adjacent to an eroding river bank was a principal factor in the community’s decision to relocate, as Section 138.10 describes; agency assessments have repeatedly ranked Newtok among the communities most threatened by permafrost thaw and erosion. In the Mackenzie Delta region of the Northwest Territories, communities such as Tuktoyaktuk, on the Arctic coast, have experienced erosion and ground instability that affect homes, a cemetery, and ice cellars used for storing food; and the all-season road connecting Inuvik and Tuktoyaktuk, completed in 2017, crosses permafrost terrain that requires continuing maintenance. In Nunavut, communities have documented building settlement and airstrip damage, and in the northern Yukon, thaw has altered landscapes and drainage in ways that affect travel and harvesting. These cases illustrate varied settings; they do not imply that every northern community faces the same ground conditions, since some lie on bedrock or gravel where thaw is less consequential.

Existing infrastructure deficiencies can magnify the consequences. Houses in many northern communities are overcrowded and in need of repair, and water and sanitation systems may be incomplete. A community with limited housing stock is less able to absorb the loss of buildings to thaw, and a village without a reliable alternative route has little margin if its airstrip is damaged. Thaw is therefore a stressor interacting with long-standing infrastructure gaps, not an isolated cause, a point developed in the causal analysis below.

Thaw also changes the chemical and biological history of watersheds. NOAA's 2025 assessment examines orange discoloration in northern rivers as iron is mobilized from soils and rock and subsequently oxidizes. Some affected waters in Alaska have shown concentrations of metals of concern to aquatic organisms. The processes vary by geology and drainage: oxidation of sulfide minerals and microbial iron mobilization in wetlands are not the same mechanism, and discoloration alone does not establish a particular cause. For fishing communities, the issue is not simply the appearance of a river but the possible effects on habitat, water quality, and the reliability of harvests. [S05]

Infrastructure inventories must therefore extend beyond buildings. A water intake, drainage ditch, fuel containment system and buried utility may be physically connected, so damage at one site can interrupt several services. Engineers can test ground temperatures and design for anticipated settlement, but designs have to be maintained and reassessed throughout their service lives. Northern governments face a choice between paying recurring repair costs and investing earlier in relocation or replacement. Historical budgets and maintenance logs can show whether a settlement's vulnerability grew through accumulated deferred work rather than through a single sudden disaster.

Section 138.7

Coastal Erosion, Flooding, and the Changing Arctic Shoreline

Arctic coastlines are changing through the combined action of several processes, and understanding that combination is necessary to understand why certain communities face acute hazards. Longer open-water seasons expose coasts to waves and storms for more of the year. Sea ice and landfast ice historically protected shorelines from wave action in autumn storm seasons; when ice forms later, storms strike an unprotected beach. Thawing permafrost weakens bluffs and banks, so that the same waves remove more material. River processes, including channel migration and spring flooding, erode river banks in deltas and along river villages. Storm surge, the elevation of water level by storm winds and low pressure, can drive water far inland across low terrain. Relative sea level also varies locally: global sea-level rise contributes, but local land movement, including subsidence of land or, in some places, rebound after glacial retreat, produces regional differences.

The chapter must not present global sea-level rise as the sole explanation for every northern erosion problem. In many Alaska communities the immediate drivers are storm waves, river erosion, and thawing ground, and in some places local land subsidence is important. In parts of Greenland and the Canadian Arctic, where land is rising as ice sheets and glaciers lose mass, relative sea level may be falling even as global sea level rises. Clarity about causes matters for adaptation, because different drivers call for different responses: a wall may help against waves but not against thawing ground beneath it, and relocating away from a river is a different problem from raising a village above rising seas.

Alaska’s coastal and riverine villages provide the clearest documentation. A federal Government Accountability Office report in 2003 found that the great majority of Alaska Native villages, 184 of 213 at that time, were affected to some degree by flooding and erosion, and a 2009 GAO report identified a smaller number as imminently threatened, with Newtok, Kivalina, Shaktoolik, and Shishmaref singled out as likely to need relocation. State and federal assessments since then have repeatedly ranked communities by risk, and the Denali Commission’s 2026 statewide threat assessment process continues this work. Kivalina, Shishmaref, and Shaktoolik sit on low barrier islands or exposed shore, and their situations are examined in Section 138.11. In the Yukon–Kuskokwim Delta, villages such as Newtok, Kipnuk, and Kwigillingok lie on low, river-threaded ground exposed to storm surge and river erosion.

Extreme events underline the stakes. Remnants of Typhoon Merbok struck western Alaska in September 2022, and, in October 2025, the remnants of Typhoon Halong, which had reached major intensity in the western Pacific, brought storm surge and high winds to southwestern Alaska. Reporting indicates the storm destroyed much of Kipnuk and Kwigillingok, displaced roughly 1,500 or more people in the largest Alaska air evacuation in at least half a century, and caused deaths and missing persons. These storms were not unprecedented in kind, and scientists are cautious about attributing any single storm to climate change, but the reduced presence of protective sea ice, higher sea-surface temperatures, and degraded shorelines are conditions under which such storms can be more damaging. The aftermath demonstrated an institutional problem addressed in Section 138.11: disaster-recovery programs are designed to repair and rebuild in place, not to relocate communities.

Federal assessments of flooding and erosion in Alaska also changed the political visibility of the problem. The Government Accountability Office's 2003 and 2009 studies brought together reports from dispersed villages and agencies. Their significance was not that they discovered hazards unknown to residents; it was that they documented a statewide administrative problem, including mismatches between agency eligibility rules and community needs. A village may be exposed to a recurring hazard yet lack a formally declared disaster that unlocks certain funds. Conversely, a disaster declaration may support reconstruction but not the purchase and development of a replacement site. This distinction became increasingly important after the 2025 storm. [S07, S08]

Section 138.8

Wildlife, Fisheries, Vegetation, and Changing Ecosystems

Northern Indigenous economies rest on relationships with animals and plants, and climate change is altering these relationships in complex and uneven ways. Caribou and reindeer are central to economies and identities across the tundra and forest margins. Many migratory tundra caribou herds have declined from peak abundance in the early twenty-first century, although causes vary by herd and are debated; climate-related factors, such as changes in snow, ice-crust formation, vegetation, insect harassment, and disease, interact with industrial development, predation, and harvest. Seals, walruses, polar bears, and bowhead whales depend on sea ice and productive marine ecosystems; changes in ice affect their habitat, the timing of their migrations, and hunters’ ability to reach them. Salmon and Arctic char, the basis of fisheries from Alaska rivers to northern Canada, respond to river and ocean temperatures, flow patterns, and ocean conditions; some Alaska salmon runs have faced significant shortfalls in recent years that communities have linked to warming and other factors, a causal question under active research. Migratory birds are shifting in timing and range. Tundra vegetation has changed too: the Arctic Report Card notes that maximum tundra greenness in 2025 was the third highest in the 26-year satellite record, continuing a sequence of high values since 2020, reflecting shrub expansion and a longer growing season.

Some species are moving northward. This borealization of Arctic marine and terrestrial ecosystems means that species typical of more southerly waters and forests appear farther north, sometimes competing with or preying on Arctic species. New species may create opportunities, such as new fisheries, but can also bring unfamiliar parasites and diseases. Seasonal mismatches arise when the timing of events such as plant growth, insect emergence, and animal movements shifts at different rates, with consequences for breeding and feeding. Warmer winters can produce rain-on-snow events that form ice layers, making forage inaccessible for caribou and muskoxen. Marine ecosystem change affects the food webs on which seals, whales, and fish depend.

The effects of climate change must be distinguished from other influences. Industrial development, including roads, mines, and energy infrastructure, discussed in Chapters 136 and 137, fragments habitat and alters migration routes. Harvesting pressure, both Indigenous and non-Indigenous, and wildlife management decisions, discussed in Chapter 135, affect populations. Commercial fishing, shipping, and noise affect marine species. Attributing a particular decline solely to climate change, or ignoring climate change in favor of other factors, would be an error; the evidence in most cases points to interacting causes. This is also why Indigenous participation in co-management, as Chapter 135 describes, remains central: communities hold detailed knowledge of local animal conditions and have direct stakes in management decisions.

Ecological change does not produce identical outcomes for every species or community. A coastal Iñupiat community dependent on bowhead whales and seals confronts different challenges from a Gwich’in community dependent on the Porcupine caribou herd, and both differ from a Yup’ik delta community relying on salmon, whitefish, and waterfowl. Some changes create openings: longer open-water seasons may permit boat-based harvests and new commercial fisheries, and some communities have taken part in them. Others close options. The task for historians is to describe this variation accurately, avoiding both uniform decline narratives and unqualified opportunity narratives.

The northern Bering and Chukchi seas offer a documented example of ecological reorganization. NOAA's 2025 assessment describes the movement of species associated with warmer waters into northern marine ecosystems over the 2002–2022 study period. Borealization is not merely the arrival of individual fish: changes in prey, predators and the timing of biological production can reorganize food webs. For communities whose knowledge and equipment are fitted to particular harvesting grounds, opportunities to catch newly available species coexist with uncertainty over established resources. Markets, licenses, processing facilities and household preferences determine whether a newly abundant species can become a useful resource. [S01]

Terrestrial records require equal care. Satellite observations in the 2025 assessment found exceptionally high average tundra greenness, but areas with vegetation loss or disturbance remained. Greenness measures reflectance related to vegetation productivity; it is not a direct measure of edible plant abundance, winter forage or caribou health. Warmer growing seasons can increase vegetation while ice crusts or deep snow impede access to forage during winter. The same climatic trend can therefore have contrasting consequences over a complete annual cycle. A history of wildlife management should compare observations from harvesters, repeated herd surveys and habitat records rather than attributing every fluctuation in population to temperature alone. [S06]

For caribou-dependent communities, herd boundaries are also political geography. The Porcupine herd migrates across Alaska and northwestern Canada; its seasonal range intersects different management jurisdictions. Harvest regulations and industrial land decisions may be made in one jurisdiction while their consequences appear elsewhere. Cooperative management therefore requires both ecological observation and institutional agreements across borders. Chapter 135 treated the historical development of stewardship; here the important point is that environmental change can make existing agreements harder to implement by altering the timing or distribution of animals. A conservation decision should distinguish a change in the ability to find animals from a demonstrated decline in the number of animals.

Section 138.9

Food Security, Subsistence, and Everyday Life

Northern Indigenous food systems are best understood as interconnected systems and not only as lists of foods. They include harvesting, which depends on knowledge, equipment, fuel, safe travel, and time; processing and storage, including drying, freezing, fermenting, and the use of ice cellars; sharing, through kinship and community networks that distribute harvests to elders, households without hunters, and neighbors; and the transmission of knowledge and skills across generations. Country food or traditional food, the animals, fish, birds, and plants harvested from land and sea, sits within a mixed economy in which households also buy imported groceries, often at very high prices in remote communities, and pay for fuel, snowmachines, boats, ammunition, and other equipment with wage income and transfers. Community freezers, harvesters’ support programs, and sharing networks connect the two.

Access to country food has economic, cultural, nutritional, and political significance. It provides nutrient-rich food that is often more affordable, over time, than imported alternatives. It sustains cultural practices, language, and identity; the vocabulary of hunting, butchering, and sharing carries knowledge. It supports health and well-being by providing physical activity, time on the land, and a sense of purpose. It also carries political meaning: the concept of food sovereignty, used by Inuit and other Indigenous organizations, refers to the right of peoples to define and control their own food systems, and goes beyond food security, which concerns reliable access to sufficient, safe, and nutritious food. Food insecurity is substantial in many northern communities according to public-health surveys in Canada and elsewhere, reflecting high costs, low incomes, and limited access, conditions that predate climate change but are compounded by it.

Climate change affects this system at several points. Changing ice, snow, and water conditions make travel more dangerous and more costly. Animals may be farther away, appear at unfamiliar times, or decline in number. Warmer temperatures can compromise the storage of meat and fish in ice cellars and drying racks, creating spoilage and food-safety concerns, as communities in the Mackenzie Delta and elsewhere have reported. Fuel and equipment costs rise when hunters must travel farther or switch to boats. Households with fewer resources are least able to adjust. Evidence from community-based research in several northern regions indicates that safety concerns, equipment costs, and altered seasonal patterns shape how often people can go out on the land, though the specific effects differ by place and season.

The consequences extend beyond diet. Personal safety is one: injuries and deaths on thin or unstable ice and in sudden storms have been documented, and search-and-rescue volunteers in northern communities carry heavy burdens. Mental health and community well-being are another: reduced access to the land can erode a sense of identity and capacity, and the loss of familiar landscapes through erosion or relocation produces grief that researchers and community members describe in terms of loss of place. A third concerns the next generation. Younger people acquire land-based skills by traveling and hunting with elders over many seasons. If conditions are riskier, if schooling and wage work consume time, and if equipment is expensive, fewer young people may gain full experience, and the knowledge that guides adaptation may be transmitted more thinly. Many communities respond with land-based education programs, youth camps, and mentorship, efforts that Chapter 146 examines as part of broader cultural resurgence.

For the framework, food is a point at which multiple dimensions converge: environmental security (access to functioning ecosystems), economic security (the cost of harvesting and purchasing food), cultural continuity (transmission of knowledge), and political authority (the capacity to manage fish and wildlife and to define food policy). A community’s response to food-system change therefore cannot be reduced to supplying food; it must protect the conditions that allow people to feed themselves in culturally meaningful ways.

Food-sharing systems can distribute climate risk, but they are not inexhaustible reserves. When fuel prices rise or unsafe ice reduces the number of successful journeys, the costs of supplying relatives increase as well. Community freezers and organized distribution may make harvests available to elders or households without equipment, yet they require dependable electricity, maintenance and administration. In this sense, northern food security is an infrastructure question and a cultural question simultaneously. Evaluating a food program requires asking not only how many kilograms were distributed but whether local harvesting, decisions about appropriate foods, and relationships of sharing were sustained. Everyday Life and Social History

Obtaining and Sharing Food in a Changing Northern Environment

Consider a household in a northern coastal or river community, described here in general terms drawn from documented patterns and not as a specific family. A harvest begins with a decision: whether conditions are safe, whether a trip is affordable, and who will go. Fuel, ammunition, and maintenance must be purchased, often at remote-community prices. Travel requires knowledge of ice, water, or snow conditions. The harvest, if successful, is processed by family members, with different tasks assigned by age and skill, and portions are shared with elders and with households that lack hunters. Some is stored for winter. Youth learn by taking part.

Climate change enters at each step: the trip may be delayed or cancelled because ice is unsafe; the route may be longer; the meat may be harder to preserve; and the elder who would once judge conditions may find that familiar signs no longer apply. Households with more income and equipment adapt more easily, while those with less may rely more heavily on store-bought food. Community freezers and harvest-sharing programs, often supported by Indigenous governments and organizations, help redistribute food and reduce these inequalities.

Section 138.10

Newtok and Mertarvik: The History of Community Relocation

Newtok, a Yup’ik village on the Ninglick River in the Yukon–Kuskokwim Delta, sat on low, ice-rich ground in a landscape where the river was eating away at the bank. Agency assessments in the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries documented erosion that threatened homes, the school, and community facilities, and those assessments were reinforced by residents’ own observations over years. Newtok’s housing was overcrowded, and flooding contributed to mold in many homes. By the 1990s, community leaders had concluded that staying was not viable and began planning relocation. The decision was therefore community-initiated, though its feasibility depended on partners and funds outside the community’s control. The case matters because it became the most advanced example in Alaska of an entire community relocating, and because its complications illuminate what relocation demands.

The first problem was land. Relocation required a site that was safer, large enough, and legally available. The community identified a location on Nelson Island, about nine miles from Newtok across the river, at a place called Mertarvik, a Yup’ik name often translated as a place for getting water from the spring. The land lay within the Yukon Delta National Wildlife Refuge system and associated Alaska Native corporation lands, so acquiring it required a land exchange among the village corporation and the federal government. Congress authorized the exchange in Public Law 108-129 on November 17, 2003; the conveyance of the Mertarvik site followed in 2004. This step shows how Alaska’s land-tenure system, created by the 1971 settlement act discussed in Chapter 115, shapes adaptation: the community needed federal action and the cooperation of corporate landholders before it could build anywhere.

The second problem was coordination. In 2006 the Newtok Planning Group was established as an interagency forum bringing together tribal, state, federal, and nonprofit participants, a structure that later served as a model for other communities facing relocation. Participants included the State of Alaska, the Denali Commission, the Alaska Native Tribal Health Consortium (which provided project management), the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, the Bureau of Indian Affairs, and others. Yet coordination did not solve funding. At the outset, no comprehensive federal or state program covered the full cost of relocating a village, so the community and its partners assembled funding piece by piece, from housing, transportation, water and sanitation, energy, and disaster-related sources. Reporters and researchers have described years of stalled progress, partly because of limited funding and partly because of a period of internal conflict between two tribal governing bodies in Newtok, the Newtok Traditional Council and the Newtok Village Council, over control of the relocation effort, which the Village Council eventually assumed. This illustrates the sixth dimension of the framework: political authority within the community itself was necessary for effective implementation, and its disruption had real costs.

Construction proceeded in phases. Infrastructure at Mertarvik was built first: a barge landing, roads, a community building, an evacuation facility, water and sanitation systems, and the first houses. Reporting indicates that in 2018 a community center and several houses were constructed, and families began moving in 2019 as homes became available. The movement was gradual because housing was the binding constraint. Three different organizations designed and built houses at Mertarvik, each with different designs. Funding for further housing was often uncertain; at one point the relocation coordinator told reporters the community lacked money to build additional houses. By April 2024, a tribal relocation coordinator counted about 220 people living in Mertarvik and about 129 still in Newtok. In late September 2024 the season’s last barge delivered 18 small temporary houses for those who remained, and Newtok’s public services were then decommissioned: power and water were shut down in late 2024. A 2025 state agency update reported that no Newtok resident remained in Newtok, a statement later cited in a March 2026 Federal Communications Commission order that waived rules to allow a telecommunications provider to shut down the Newtok cell tower and activate one at Mertarvik.

Public services moved too, though unevenly. Newtok’s school closed at the end of the 2023–2024 school year; students began classes in August 2024 in a temporary location at Mertarvik; and a new school building, with a reported 2025 construction estimate of roughly $56 million, was reported as scheduled for completion in 2026. The clinic operated from a temporary building. The school district reports that its temporary classrooms occupy the evacuation center and that completion of the new school is expected by the end of 2026; opening of the permanent building has not been confirmed in this account. A May 2025 joint investigation projected total relocation costs above $150 million; separate KYUK reporting in July 2025 emphasized the difficulty of reconciling expenditures across agencies. Neither figure constitutes an audited final cost.

The case must be read for what it shows beyond the construction of a replacement village. The relocation of residents is not the same as the completion of a fully functioning replacement community. By the end of 2024, residents had moved, but journalists, researchers, and residents described continuing problems: a housing shortage, temporary facilities, the need for lasting public services, the challenges of maintaining utilities, and the difficulty of building an economy and municipal capacity in a new place. A joint investigation by the Washington Post, ProPublica, and KYUK, published in 2025, reported that although Newtok had been envisioned as a model for dozens of other Alaska communities, its participants faced a new set of difficulties, including those in housing and services. The case also revealed grief and ambivalence: some residents described mixed feelings about leaving a place of ancestral ties even as they welcomed better housing, and others reported being healthier and happier in new homes. Community cohesion was tested by years of living between two sites. Newtok demonstrates the possibility of community-led relocation, and it demonstrates that doing so required more than three decades, substantial public funds, and an unusual degree of community determination. It is a model of what is possible, and also of what other communities should not be required to endure.

Land acquisition had a lengthy institutional prehistory. The state's relocation history records a corporation resolution in November 1996 authorizing negotiations with the Fish and Wildlife Service, followed by an intent-to-exchange agreement in December 1997. The legislation of 2003 and the recorded conveyance in 2004 converted a community preference into a legally usable land base. The distinction matters: the tribal government's authority to represent residents did not itself give it title to the federal refuge land. Nor did the transfer automatically supply a surveyed community layout, safe water or construction finance. The 2017 community layout plan approved by the Village Council was another step in a long sequence of implementation. [S09, S10, S11, S12]

The Newtok Planning Group's history also records disruption during the 2013 tribal governance controversy, followed by the resumption of group meetings in 2014 while the administrative appeal continued. A final decision by the Interior Board of Indian Appeals followed in 2015. This episode cannot be reduced to community disunity: public agencies required a recognized counterpart for financial and administrative decisions. An interruption in recognition could delay construction even where the environmental hazard did not diminish. [S11]

Federal policy began to respond more explicitly to these problems. On November 30, 2022, the Department of the Interior announced a $135 million commitment to community-driven relocation and associated assistance. Newtok, Napakiak and the Quinault Indian Nation in Washington were each selected for $25 million in initial implementation funding. The award to Newtok was not the total price of moving its community; it added a dedicated source of funding to the existing collection of grants. The relevant policy question after 2022 was whether these new measures could become sufficiently comprehensive, stable and coordinated to support threatened communities over many years. [S13]

By December 2024 the last residents had moved, according to the Bureau of Indian Affairs and the local school district. A 2025 investigation and subsequent KYUK reporting nevertheless documented the continuing housing problem. Temporary houses solved an urgent need without necessarily providing adequate permanent living space. A school can similarly operate in an interim building while construction proceeds. Thus resident movement, permanent housing availability, full utility service and long-term fiscal viability are separate milestones. The FCC's 2026 telecommunications transition concerned one service in this wider process, not certification that all relocation work was complete. [S14, S15, S16, S17, S18]

Section 138.11

Kivalina, Shishmaref, and the Politics of Remaining or Moving

Newtok is the most advanced case, not the typical one. Other Alaska communities identified as imminently threatened have followed different paths. Alaska’s institutional record describes three broad responses, and the choice among them is political and cultural as well as technical.

The Kivalina lawsuit illustrates the difference between a scientific dispute and a dispute about an available remedy. In 2012 the Ninth Circuit held that the Clean Air Act and the regulatory authority it provides displaced the federal common-law nuisance claim, including the damages sought by the village and city. The court did not decide that Kivalina faced no erosion hazard or that greenhouse gases did not contribute to warming. A community may have extensive evidence of injury yet lack a viable cause of action under the legal theory it invokes. Kivalina's experience thus connects local environmental history to the institutional history of climate litigation. [S19]

The advisory result at Shishmaref in 2016 followed earlier votes and planning. The certified 94–78 result, rather than the preliminary 89–78 tally circulated in some reporting, indicates both a majority for moving and substantial support for staying. Agency plans had to respond to this divided community, the suitability of potential sites and the cost of maintaining services in the meantime. An evacuation road or replacement school in a safer location can improve safety without resolving the question of where all households will ultimately live. [S20, S21]

In the aftermath of ex-Typhoon Halong, the decisions of Kipnuk and Kwigillingok became subjects of Alaska legislative resolutions in 2026. The documents recognize community authority to choose relocation and urge support. The October 2026 reports describe continued recovery and temporary accommodation; they do not establish completed replacement villages. A decision to move after disaster must be distinguished from the separate work of determining title, designing infrastructure and preserving access to harvest areas. [S36, S37, S38]

Alaska’s planning records identify relocation votes in May 1973 and July 2002; contemporary reporting gives the 2002 tally as 161–20. The state transportation department also documents a separate airport relocation feasibility study for Kivalina. An airport study is a planning activity, not a commitment to construct an airport or relocate an entire settlement. [S21] [S46] [S47]

Historical Vocabulary
Protection in Place, Managed Retreat, and Community Relocation

Protection in place means defending and reinforcing an existing settlement with seawalls, revetments, berms, drainage, or elevated structures. Managed retreat means moving vulnerable buildings and infrastructure away from hazardous areas, often incrementally and within or adjacent to the existing community. Community relocation means establishing a replacement settlement elsewhere and moving the community there.

Why it matters. The terms correspond to different costs, timelines, and cultural implications; a community may use more than one over time. Potential misunderstanding. Relocation is often treated as an engineering decision. It is also a decision about ancestral ties, subsistence access, governance, and community identity. “Climate displacement” by contrast refers to movement driven by environmental pressure; communities that decide to move may reject this term if it implies they have lost agency.

Kivalina, an Iñupiaq community of roughly 400 residents on a barrier island on the Chukchi Sea coast, has pursued relocation for decades because of erosion, storm exposure, overcrowding, and limited space. A relocation master plan was released in 2006, and a preferred site at Kiniktuuraq, several miles away, has been under consideration; planning has been interrupted by cost and site-suitability issues, and many sites examined were rejected for erosion risk or expense. An approximately eight-mile evacuation road constructed in 2020, according to Alaska DOT improved access toward the mainland. Cost estimates for relocation have varied widely, from about $100 million to $400 million or more, and reporting in 2024 and 2025 indicated that no comprehensive funding package for the entire relocation had been secured. In 2008 Kivalina’s tribal government and city also filed a federal lawsuit against energy companies seeking damages related to climate change; the case was dismissed, and the dismissal was affirmed on appeal in 2012. The suit is part of the history of Indigenous climate claims in Section 138.19, and its dismissal illustrates how difficult it has been to obtain compensation through domestic courts.

Shishmaref, an Iñupiaq community on Sarichef Island off the Seward Peninsula, has lost shoreline over decades, and it has combined shoreline protection with relocation planning. Residents have voted on relocation more than once, including in 2002 and again in August 2016, when the certified advisory vote was 94–78 in favor of relocation. The community conducted a relocation site-selection feasibility study, but, as the GAO observed in 2009, agreement among federal, state, and village officials on a site that is safe, sustainable, and compatible with subsistence use proved difficult. Shishmaref illustrates how a community may remain divided about moving, since attachment to the island and its access to marine hunting grounds weigh against its hazards, and how decisions may change over time as conditions and finances change.

Shaktoolik, on a low spit in Norton Sound, has built a protective berm of gravel and sand and has also considered eventual relocation to higher ground. Storm damage to the berm has been documented, and the community has used state planning grants to develop approaches combining nearer-term protection, emergency shelter, and long-term relocation options. Shaktoolik shows that protection and relocation are not mutually exclusive: communities may protect what they have while planning for a longer-term move, a strategy that reflects risk, resources, and preferences.

Why do some communities pursue protection and others relocation? Several factors recur. Geography: a barrier-island community has little land into which to retreat, whereas a village on a river bank may be able to move inland on higher ground. Hazard type: wave action may be countered by structures, but thawing ground or flooding on a delta may not. Cost: protective works are expensive and require maintenance, but relocation costs are larger and often exceed the dedicated funding available. Governance: tribal governments, city governments, and village corporations each hold different authority, and agreement among them may be needed. Community preference: attachment to ancestral places, access to harvest areas, and the sense of obligation to ancestors weigh heavily. And time: after a disaster, a community that had hesitated may decide to move.

The October 2025 storm made that last factor vivid. Within months of the remnants of Typhoon Halong, tribal members in Kipnuk voted overwhelmingly to relocate, with results announced in March 2026, and residents of Kwigillingok also voted to move to higher ground inland, about 27 miles from the existing site according to reporting. Nearly all residents of both villages had been displaced to Anchorage, Bethel, and elsewhere. Yet federal and state disaster-recovery programs are designed to repair and rebuild, and officials have said that those programs will not relocate the communities; a tribal disaster declaration for Kipnuk was approved by FEMA in 2026 to fund infrastructure repair. A year after the storm, reporters described parallel rebuilding and relocation efforts that had stalled. The structural gap that Newtok exposed, namely the absence of a program that funds the relocation of a community, therefore persists.

Treating relocation as a purely engineering decision is hazardous. It risks overlooking what community members emphasize: that remaining connected to ancestral territory is central to identity, that new sites must provide access to harvest areas, that relocated communities need governance, economic opportunity, and cultural continuity, and that communities want to determine their own course. The Alaska village protection and relocation programs, including the Denali Commission’s Village Infrastructure Protection program and the state’s planning grants, provide an institutional record of the alternatives; they also show that funds for planning have been more available than funds for implementation.

Section 138.12

Climate Change Across Inuit Nunangat and Greenland

Alaska is only one part of the Indigenous North. Inuit Nunangat, the Inuit homeland in Canada, comprises four regions established through modern land-claims agreements: the Inuvialuit Settlement Region in the western Arctic, Nunavut, Nunavik in northern Quebec, and Nunatsiavut in Labrador. Each has its own government or governing institutions, its own agreements with Canada, and its own environmental conditions. The Inuvialuit region includes Beaufort Sea coastal communities such as Tuktoyaktuk, where erosion, permafrost thaw, sea ice, and infrastructure have been central concerns. Nunavut, a territory of widely scattered communities reached mainly by air and sea, faces challenges in harvesting, transportation, housing, and food security, including the high cost of food and equipment. Nunavik’s communities experience seasonal change, damage to infrastructure including airstrips and roads, and changing access to the land. Nunatsiavut’s coastal livelihoods depend on marine environments and sea ice, and the region has been a center of community-based ice-monitoring work discussed in Section 138.13.

The comparison with Alaska illustrates institutional difference. Canada’s Inuit regions hold land-claims agreements that give Inuit organizations ownership of land and resources, roles in wildlife and environmental co-management, and funding through settlement and government transfers, as Chapter 116 describes for Nunavut. These arrangements provide institutional channels for climate adaptation that differ from the village-corporation and tribal-government structure of Alaska. They do not eliminate infrastructure gaps, however, and some communities face housing shortages and high costs. Moreover, the relocation trajectory of Newtok does not represent northern Indigenous communities generally: in much of Inuit Nunangat, communities are responding through infrastructure adaptation, monitoring, planning, and regional policy rather than wholesale relocation, although some sites face significant erosion.

Greenland, Kalaallit Nunaat, presents another institutional setting. It is a self-governing part of the Kingdom of Denmark, with a government under a 2009 self-government arrangement that the majority Inuit population has used to assert growing authority over its economy and policy. Greenlandic communities rely heavily on fishing and hunting, and the economy depends on fisheries and, increasingly, discussions of mining, tourism, and infrastructure. Research from northwestern and western Greenland provides documented examples of how hunting and fishing communities experience and respond to changing sea ice. In Qaanaaq and the Avanersuaq region, home to the Inughuit, hunters who rely on sea ice for sledding and hunting have described shorter ice seasons and increased risks, shifts from dog-sled travel toward boats in some periods, and altered access to hunting grounds. In western Greenland, communities such as Qeqertarsuaq on Disko Island, where winter ice has long supported fishing and travel, have faced reduced ice in recent years, affecting both subsistence and commercial activities. Meanwhile the Greenland Ice Sheet’s mass loss, which the 2025 Report Card estimated at around 129 billion tons that year, has consequences for global sea level and for local meltwater, glacier, and fjord conditions.

In Greenland, environmental transformation and economic development are intertwined. Reduced sea ice can extend open-water seasons for fishing and shipping, expanding some economic opportunities, while the decline of ice-based hunting threatens cultural practices. Debates about mining and other development are therefore bound up with climate, and with questions of Greenlandic self-determination within the Kingdom of Denmark. Heightened international attention to Greenland in 2025 and 2026 has added geopolitical dimensions, which Section 138.17 and Chapter 148 consider; this section notes only that Greenlandic and Inuit organizations have emphasized that decisions about the island’s future rest with its people.

The chapter’s approach in this section is deliberately comparative and cautious. The communities of Inuit Nunangat and Greenland differ among themselves in geography, hazards, governance, and economy; they share a heritage of Inuit language and culture and a strong tradition of regional and circumpolar political organization, as Section 138.15 and Section 138.18 describe. It would be inaccurate to treat the Alaska relocations as the template for all of them.

Greenlandic fisheries demonstrate why climate adaptation cannot be described solely as a struggle against loss. In a 2012 study of Qeqertarsuaq, James Ford and Charlotte Goldhar examined vulnerability and adaptation in a resource-dependent West Greenland community. Local livelihood choices were constrained by access to equipment, economic institutions and environmental change. A later multi-site study of Greenlandic fisheries drew on interviews and workshops with fishers and other stakeholders across offshore and coastal settings. Participants reported altered ice cover, changes in the distribution of familiar species, a northward shift in shrimp fishing and emerging fisheries, alongside unfamiliar bycatch. Such accounts record opportunities and problems as they are experienced in particular communities rather than imposing a single national narrative. [S32, S33]

Changes in fishing geography also have a distributional consequence. A commercially licensed vessel can follow resources differently from a small boat serving a nearby settlement; harbor facilities, processing capacity and access to capital affect who can exploit a change in species distribution. A shift that increases national fisheries revenue may therefore create costs in a particular village. Historical analysis needs to consider the place of municipal services, fishing regulations, household economies and harvesting practices. Greenland's self-government, Naalakkersuisut, exercises extensive domestic responsibilities, including fisheries policy, within the Kingdom of Denmark, while foreign and security affairs involve the Kingdom's constitutional arrangements. These different levels of authority should not be conflated when assessing northern climate policy.

Section 138.13

Indigenous Environmental Monitoring and Community-Based Research

The previous divisions described change and its consequences. This division turns to Indigenous capacity: the institutions, technologies, and policies through which northern peoples are responding. One of the most significant developments of recent decades has been the growth of Indigenous-led environmental monitoring. Community-based monitoring refers to programs in which community members collect, interpret, and use environmental information, often in partnership with scientists. Such programs draw on long traditions of observation but add instruments, digital tools, and structured records.

Examples are varied. Community weather stations and ice-thickness sensors supply local data on conditions. Wildlife observation programs record animal numbers, health, and behavior, and harvest monitoring documents what is taken and when. Mapping projects record travel routes, hazards, and place names, and digital communication systems allow hunters to share information on conditions in real time. In Alaska, the Indigenous Sentinels Network, which involves tribal environmental staff in monitoring and which the 2025 Arctic Report Card featured, brings Indigenous observers into surveys of coastal and marine conditions, including work on fur seal rookeries on St. Paul Island in the Bering Sea. In Canada, SmartICE, developed in partnership with Inuit communities in Nunatsiavut and elsewhere, combines community-operated sensors and satellite data to produce information about ice thickness and safety that travelers can use. The Arctic Eider Society, working with communities in Nunavut and Nunavik, developed the Siku platform, a social network through which Inuit and other northern users record and share observations of sea ice and wildlife. Many other regional and local programs exist, and this list is illustrative and not exhaustive.

What distinguishes these programs from conventional research is governance. Indigenous participation is not limited to providing data; it extends to determining research priorities, interpreting results, controlling sensitive information, and communicating findings. Inuit organizations have articulated principles for research in their regions, including the National Inuit Strategy on Research released by Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami in 2018, which calls for Inuit direction over research affecting Inuit Nunangat. The purposes are practical as well as principled. Local priorities, such as ice safety for travel, are not always those of external researchers. Communities may hold sensitive information about harvesting areas, sacred sites, or animal dens that they do not wish to disclose widely. And research that returns results to the community in usable form can support decision-making.

Monitoring has policy consequences. Documentation of conditions supports requests for funding, evidence in planning and permitting processes, and negotiation with agencies. Real-time ice information can reduce accidents. Long-term records that combine instrument readings and community observations can strengthen the evidentiary case for adaptation measures. At the same time, programs face constraints: reliance on short-term grants, difficulties in maintaining equipment in harsh conditions, limited broadband and power, and shortages of trained staff. The 2025 reporting on Arctic science observed that sustaining long-term observation requires public investment, an observation that applies to community monitoring as well.

This chapter focuses on monitoring as an instrument of adaptation and governance and does not repeat the broader treatment of Indigenous environmental knowledge in Chapter 133. Nor does it develop the question of knowledge sovereignty, including data ownership and the governance of genetic and digital information, which Chapter 145 addresses. The key point here is that monitoring is one means by which Indigenous communities generate their own evidence, and thereby strengthen the sixth dimension of the framework, political authority.

Monitoring institutions have an operational history as well as a research function. SmartICE combines community observations and locally operated technology for information about ice conditions. The question of who trains operators, decides where measurements are needed and maintains equipment is as important to long-term operation as the hardware itself. Locally developed information can inform trips and help document changing conditions over successive years, but no device certifies a route as universally safe. Information must be interpreted with attention to local conditions and uncertainty. [S22]

Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami's 2018 research strategy placed Inuit governance of research, ethical conduct, and the alignment of funding with Inuit priorities within a broader institutional program. Section 138.14

Climate Adaptation Through Infrastructure, Engineering, and Energy

Northern Indigenous communities and their partners have also developed physical responses to changing conditions. Foundations and buildings can be designed to cope with thawing or unstable ground, for example by elevating structures on pilings or adjustable foundations, using thermosyphons or insulated pads to keep ground cold, and building lighter, more adaptable houses. Drainage can be improved to prevent water from ponding around structures. Coastal protection includes seawalls, rock revetments, gravel berms, and planned elevation of key facilities. Housing can be designed for energy efficiency and resilience, addressing both climate adaptation and the chronic overcrowding and poor condition of housing that many communities report. Water and sanitation systems can be redesigned to avoid vulnerable buried lines and to improve reliability. Transportation networks can be improved through all-season roads, upgraded airstrips, and safer boat and snowmobile routes. The Mertarvik housing designs, discussed in Section 138.10, and the Alaska Native Tribal Health Consortium’s work on water and sanitation illustrate such measures in practice.

Energy is a second field. Northern communities have traditionally depended on diesel generators for electricity and on heating oil, brought by barge or aircraft at high cost and with spill and supply risks. Reducing this dependence addresses two aims at once. It is climate mitigation, since reducing fossil fuel use lowers greenhouse gas emissions, and it is climate adaptation and energy security, since local renewable energy can lower costs and reduce vulnerability to supply disruptions. Wind, solar, small hydroelectric, biomass, and battery storage, linked in community microgrids that can operate independently of a larger grid, have been developed in a number of Alaska and Canadian northern communities. Kotzebue in northwest Alaska has operated wind turbines for many years, and several Yukon and Northwest Territories communities, including the Vuntut Gwitchin First Nation at Old Crow, have pursued solar projects intended to reduce diesel use. The distinction between adaptation and mitigation is analytically useful, but in this context they overlap, because the same project may serve both purposes.

Economic and institutional constraints are severe. Construction costs in remote communities are high because materials and labor must be transported over long distances, often by barge during a brief shipping season or by air. Short construction seasons compress schedules, and delays can push projects into the next year. Financing typically depends on grants from several programs, each with its own rules and reporting requirements, and communities with limited administrative capacity find application and compliance burdensome. Maintenance is a long-term challenge: facilities built with capital grants may lack ongoing operating funds, so that systems deteriorate. Communities often depend on outside contractors, which can limit local employment and control, though many Indigenous governments and corporations have built construction and engineering capacity. And regulatory and funding programs often favor repairing or replacing facilities in place over relocating them, a pattern noted in Section 138.11.

These constraints help explain a recurring pattern in the historical record: planning funds have been more readily available than implementation funds, and the gap between adaptation needs and adaptation financing is large. The Alaska Division of Community and Regional Affairs’ unmet needs reporting and federal assessments have repeatedly documented funding shortfalls for threatened communities. In Canada, national adaptation policy, including Canada’s National Adaptation Strategy released on June 27, 2023, acknowledges northern and Indigenous communities’ needs, and Inuit organizations have argued that funding must flow in ways that respect Inuit decision-making. Whether stated commitments translate into funded projects is an empirical question that students should assess by examining budgets, project completion, and community assessments rather than announcements alone.

Capital funding and operating finance solve different problems. A grant may pay for a treatment plant, but a community still needs trained personnel, replacement parts and an affordable energy supply. Similarly, a protective berm has a design life and may require rebuilding after a severe storm. A relocation program needs to budget for the interval during which two settlements are operating, including transport and duplication of essential services. The 2022 Interior initiative provided targeted relocation assistance, but its initial awards did not remove the necessity of coordinating infrastructure, housing, land and operations across agencies. [S13]

Community energy projects can alter these relationships. A power purchase agreement, in contrast to a grant for equipment alone, sets the commercial terms under which a utility buys electricity from a producer over an agreed period. In an isolated grid, the utility must keep generation and demand balanced; battery storage and control systems can permit diesel generators to operate less frequently, without implying that an intermittent renewable source meets every hour of demand. Local ownership may provide a source of revenue and a role in planning beyond the direct savings in fuel. Old Crow illustrates how an Indigenous government can negotiate such an arrangement within a territorial regulatory system. [S29, S30, S31]

Section 138.15

Inuit Climate Governance and the National Inuit Climate Change Strategy

In Canada, Inuit have developed a coordinated approach to climate policy through national, regional, and community institutions. Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami (ITK), the national organization representing Inuit across Inuit Nunangat, released the National Inuit Climate Change Strategy on June 7, 2019. The strategy sets out five priorities: knowledge and capacity, aimed at strengthening Inuit-directed research and decision-making; health, well-being and the environment, addressing the human and ecological consequences of environmental change; food systems, protecting access to culturally important and nutritious foods; infrastructure, improving the resilience of northern community systems; and energy, supporting sustainable energy development and security.

The strategy is best understood as an exercise in political organization and collective agenda-setting, not simply environmental advocacy. By articulating common priorities across four regions that have separate land-claims institutions, ITK presented a unified Inuit position to the federal government and to other actors. This builds on a political tradition of Inuit organization that includes the land-claims negotiations discussed in Chapter 116 and the circumpolar work of the Inuit Circumpolar Council discussed in Section 138.18. It also reflects a principle that Inuit expect to be partners in policy and not merely recipients of programs, a principle expressed in the Inuit–Crown Partnership Committee and related initiatives, and in federal policy documents on Inuit Nunangat.

The relationships among institutions matter. National Inuit organizations such as ITK set shared agendas and engage the federal government. Regional organizations and governments, including those of the Inuvialuit, Nunavut, Nunavik, and Nunatsiavut, administer land-claims agreements and deliver programs. Territorial and provincial governments hold responsibilities for health, education, and infrastructure in their jurisdictions. Researchers and universities collaborate under Inuit research principles. Local communities hold knowledge and make daily decisions about travel and harvesting. A strategy that spans these levels depends on coordination, funding flows, and mutual accountability, none of which is automatic.

Distinguishing announced objectives from funded programs and demonstrated outcomes is essential for historical assessment. A strategy document records intentions and demands; it does not by itself deliver infrastructure or change budgets. Students should therefore ask which elements of the strategy have been matched by dedicated funding, which have been incorporated into federal or regional programs, and what evidence exists of outcomes in communities. Because implementation has evolved since 2019 and varies among regions, this textbook does not claim comprehensive outcomes; it directs students to ITK’s own reports, federal budget documents, and regional assessments for current status.

The 2019 strategy was part of a longer sequence of Inuit political organization. The 2018 National Inuit Strategy on Research had already identified research governance and funding as matters requiring Inuit leadership. The climate strategy brought together these concerns with health, food, housing and energy, making climate policy a cross-sector governmental responsibility. Its energy priority calls for increased Inuit ownership and control of energy systems; its infrastructure priority links policy to the vulnerability of the existing built environment. These priorities demonstrate why a response confined to scientific monitoring would not satisfy the strategy. [S23, S24, S25]

Policy relationships changed further with Canada's final National Adaptation Strategy in June 2023 and its 2026 progress report. The latter describes actions underway and includes Indigenous research protocols and data sovereignty among its objectives. It also identifies ongoing development of regional Inuit climate planning, including a Nunavut Inuit climate action plan scheduled for 2026. The federal account establishes that the institutional agenda was being pursued; it does not establish that all Inuit priorities were fully financed or achieved. ITK's own project materials identify community renovations and retrofits while emphasizing the need for stronger government policy. Comparing these records shows how implementation can advance in particular projects while remaining incomplete at a national scale. [S25, S26]

Historical evaluation must separate three kinds of institutional evidence. An adopted strategy establishes the political objective and the participating institutions. A budget or signed funding agreement establishes that resources have been authorized, subject to its conditions. A completion report, community assessment or sustained operating record establishes something about implementation. None of these documents alone proves the success of the whole strategy. This method is equally applicable to relocation, research and food-security programs, and helps students distinguish the history of Indigenous climate governance from a catalogue of government announcements.

Section 138.16

Gwich’in, Dene, and Subarctic Climate Adaptation

A chapter limited to coastal Inuit communities would misrepresent the Indigenous North. The continental interior, the Yukon, the Mackenzie River basin, the Northwest Territories, northern Quebec, and Labrador, is home to Gwich’in, Dene, Innu, Cree, Inuit, and other peoples whose environments include boreal forest, rivers, lakes, wetlands, and tundra. Climate change appears there in different forms. Wildfire regimes are shifting, with larger and more intense fires in some areas; the 2023 fire season in the Northwest Territories forced the evacuation of most of Yellowknife and other communities, an event that illustrated the vulnerability of northern settlements with few road connections. River flooding, including ice-jam floods during breakup, affects communities along the Mackenzie and its tributaries. Winter roads, seasonal routes over frozen ground and lakes that supply remote communities and mines, have shorter seasons in a warming climate, raising costs and complicating logistics. Freshwater ecosystems, fish, and caribou habitat are changing, and forests are transforming as the climate shifts. Community energy systems, dependent on diesel in many places, face cost and supply issues.

The Vuntut Gwitchin First Nation and its community of Old Crow in the northern Yukon provide a principal case. Old Crow is the Yukon’s only community not accessible by road; people and goods arrive by air, and, seasonally, by river. The Vuntut Gwitchin, whose name means “people of the lakes,” have long relied on the Porcupine caribou herd, fish, waterfowl, and muskrat from the nearby Old Crow Flats wetlands, one of the large wetland complexes of the North where scientists have documented changes in lake and drainage patterns associated with thaw. The Vuntut Gwitchin Final Agreement, part of the Yukon land-claims framework of the 1990s, and the First Nation’s self-government agreement gave it authority over land, resources, and governance, and established a national park and other protected areas in its territory, discussed in earlier chapters on land claims. These institutions provide a base for climate governance.

In May 2019, the Vuntut Gwitchin First Nation declared a climate emergency through the Yeendoo Diinehdoo Ji’heezrit Nits’oo Ts’o’ Nan He’aa Declaration. The declaration, issued under the leadership of Chief Dana Tizya-Tramm, linked climate change to threats to the land, the caribou, and the way of life of the Vuntut Gwitchin, and committed the First Nation to action, including on energy, community planning, and stewardship, while calling on other governments to act. Its significance lies partly in its placement within a self-governing First Nation’s own authority. A government that controls land, resources, and public services can translate a declaration into policy in ways that an advocacy group cannot. The First Nation has pursued renewable energy projects aimed at reducing dependence on diesel, which links the declaration to mitigation as well as adaptation. The case also connects to the Gwich’in people’s longstanding concern for the Porcupine caribou herd and its calving grounds, a concern that features in Chapter 136’s discussion of resource development.

The interior cases show how ecological consequences connect with governance capacities established through modern land claims and self-government arrangements. They also show that adaptation in the Subarctic has features distinct from coastal relocation: it emphasizes wildfire preparedness, river and flood management, winter-road alternatives, energy transition, and stewardship of caribou habitat. Regional organizations such as the Gwich’in Tribal Council, the Dene Nation, and the Arctic Athabaskan Council advocate on these matters and participate in circumpolar forums discussed in Section 138.18. This textbook does not treat any one First Nation as representative of the others; differing agreements, resources, and priorities produce differing approaches.

The First Nation's General Assembly resolutions of 2019 explicitly refer to the May declaration, and subsequent resolutions return to the need for clean energy and climate action. These are governmental records, not simply statements of personal concern by a chief. They illustrate how an Indigenous political institution can connect a declaration to continuing agenda-setting through resolutions and administration. The declaration and later resolutions are separate documents; their objectives should not be described as outcomes already achieved. [S27, S28]

Old Crow's solar development supplies a concrete institutional example. In 2018 the Vuntut Gwitchin Government and ATCO Electric Yukon announced an electricity purchase agreement for a First Nation-owned 940-kilowatt solar array with a 25-year purchase term. The arrangement was enabled by Yukon's independent power production policy. The project, completed in 2021, includes battery storage; Canadian Utilities projected a reduction of 189,000 litres of diesel use annually. That figure is a project expectation, not an independent measurement of each year's actual fuel savings. The distinction between power capacity in kilowatts and generated energy over time is important in assessing such claims. [S29, S30, S31]

The history is also a reminder that mitigation is not a substitute for adaptation. A solar plant can reduce dependence on fuel delivered from outside the community, while flood exposure, wildfire risk, changes in wetlands and harvesting conditions remain. Conversely, a community might improve emergency evacuation or water treatment without materially reducing emissions. Old Crow connects both responsibilities through an institution that can set priorities for local investment. The specific self-government and land-claim arrangements of the Vuntut Gwitchin First Nation should not be generalized to all Dene or Gwich'in communities.

Section 138.17

Arctic Shipping, Resource Development, and Changing Geopolitics

Environmental change is altering the Arctic’s accessibility, and with it the interests of states and corporations. Longer open-water seasons and thinner ice have increased the feasibility of seasonal shipping through the Northwest Passage and along Russia’s northern coast, though ice conditions remain variable and hazardous, and the volume of transit traffic remains small relative to other global routes. Regional marine traffic, including supply vessels, fishing fleets, tourist cruises, and resource-related shipping, has grown in some areas. Northern ports and infrastructure have drawn investment and planning. Commercial fisheries may expand northward as species shift. Mineral development, oil and gas exploration, and tourism are all affected by changing access. At the same time, search-and-rescue, spill-response, and emergency capacity in the Arctic remain limited, and a serious incident could overwhelm local resources.

New activity can create employment, business opportunities, and infrastructure investment, and many Indigenous communities and corporations have sought to participate: through ownership of shipping and fishing enterprises, partnerships in mining and energy ventures, tourism businesses, and service contracts. It can also create environmental risks, including spills, noise disturbance of marine mammals, ship strikes, introduction of invasive species, and disruption of ice-based travel and hunting by icebreaking and traffic. Chapters 136 and 137 examined extractive and pipeline conflicts in detail; here the point is that climate change can open new frontiers for such development while also undermining the stability of northern communities.

Jurisdictional disputes accompany these changes. Canada regards the waters of the Arctic archipelago, including the Northwest Passage, as internal waters, while the United States and some other states have treated the passage as an international strait, a disagreement that has persisted for decades without resolving everyday cooperation. Other disputes involve continental-shelf claims and the delimitation of maritime boundaries. The Kingdom of Denmark, Russia, Canada, the United States, and Norway all assert interests, and geopolitical attention to the Arctic, and in particular to Greenland, intensified in 2025 and 2026. These are matters for Chapter 148 as well; this section addresses their relationship to Indigenous rights.

A central distinction is between state claims to Arctic sovereignty and Indigenous relationships with ancestral lands and waters. States claim sovereignty on the basis of international law and effective governance. Inuit organizations have emphasized that Inuit have used and occupied the sea ice, coasts, and waters for millennia, and that Inuit Nunangat includes marine areas. The Inuit Circumpolar Council’s 2009 circumpolar declaration on Arctic sovereignty asserted Inuit rights and called for Inuit participation in decisions affecting the Arctic. The argument is not necessarily that Indigenous peoples oppose state sovereignty; many Inuit organizations assert that sovereignty must be exercised in partnership with Inuit and consistent with their rights. The distinction nevertheless shows that territorial authority in the Arctic involves more than two parties.

Increasing accessibility also changes the geography of public responsibility. A ship can travel through waters used by several communities without creating permanent employment in any of them, while an accident may require local emergency personnel to respond. Shipping governance must therefore address route information, communications, emergency planning, ecological monitoring and meaningful participation in decisions. The relevant economic question is who receives revenue and who bears environmental and infrastructure costs. Inuit Circumpolar Council's 2009 sovereignty declaration placed Inuit participation within the political debate over Arctic territorial and maritime authority. [S34]

Historical Debate

Economic Opportunity or Renewed Territorial Pressure?

The question: Does a more accessible Arctic primarily offer Indigenous communities new economic opportunities, or does it primarily intensify external pressure on Indigenous territories and waters? Scholars, Indigenous leaders, and policymakers differ, and many hold that the answer varies by place and by who controls the terms of development.

Interpretation A — Opportunity under Indigenous control: Proponents argue that reduced ice and expanded access can bring jobs, revenue, services, and infrastructure to communities with few economic options, especially where Indigenous governments and corporations own resources or hold co-management rights and can shape projects.
Interpretation B — Renewed territorial pressure: Others argue that increased access resembles earlier waves of external interest in northern resources, bringing environmental risk, disruption of hunting and travel, and jurisdictional contests in which Indigenous communities have limited power, with benefits that may flow mainly to outside actors.

Evidence: Both views cite documented cases: Indigenous-owned enterprises in fisheries, shipping, and tourism on one side, and concerns about spills, marine mammal disturbance, and decision-making processes on the other. Evidence on net economic effects at community level is uneven and often context-specific.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns the distribution of benefits and risks, the adequacy of consultation and consent, and the interpretation of present trends; it does not usually concern whether the physical changes are occurring.

Why it matters: How historians weigh these interpretations affects whether new Arctic activity is seen as an extension of Indigenous economic agency or of a longer pattern of external pressure on Indigenous territory.

Student task: Identify two kinds of evidence that would help assess which interpretation better fits a particular community, and explain how the terms of ownership and consent would change your conclusion.

Section 138.18

The Arctic Council and Indigenous Climate Diplomacy

Indigenous participation in circumpolar governance has developed through institutions that grew out of Cold War and post-Cold War Arctic cooperation. The Arctic Council was established in 1996 by the Ottawa Declaration as a high-level intergovernmental forum for cooperation on environmental protection and sustainable development among Arctic states. Its member states are Canada, the Kingdom of Denmark (including Greenland and the Faroe Islands), Finland, Iceland, Norway, Russia, Sweden, and the United States. A distinctive feature is the category of Permanent Participants, reserved for Indigenous organizations: the Aleut International Association, the Arctic Athabaskan Council, Gwich’in Council International, the Inuit Circumpolar Council, the Russian Association of Indigenous Peoples of the North, and the Saami Council. Participation by Russian institutions has been complicated since the 2022 invasion of Ukraine and the member states’ decision to pause Council activities involving Russia, a reminder that circumpolar cooperation depends on political conditions.

Permanent Participants have the right to full consultation in the Council’s negotiations and decisions, and they participate in working groups, task forces, and research assessments, including those of the Arctic Monitoring and Assessment Programme (AMAP) and other groups. The 2004 Arctic Climate Impact Assessment, a landmark synthesis of Arctic warming and its consequences, drew on Indigenous knowledge and participation, and later assessments have continued to incorporate Indigenous observations and priorities. Indigenous organizations also use the forum for diplomacy: presenting community concerns, shaping research agendas, and linking local observations to international policy.

It is important to describe the limits accurately. Permanent Participant status is not the same as decision-making authority. Decisions in the Arctic Council are made by consensus of the member states, and the Council is a forum for cooperation and assessment, not a body with legislative power or binding regulatory authority over the Arctic. Indigenous organizations have used their consultative position to influence agendas and products, but the capacity to do so depends on funding, staffing, and the willingness of states to listen. Questions of representation also arise: the organizations represent peoples across state borders, but they do not replace the governments of individual Indigenous nations or the institutions described in earlier sections.

Organizations beyond the Council are also active. The Inuit Circumpolar Council, founded in 1977 in Utqiaġvik, Alaska, represents Inuit of Alaska, Canada, Greenland, and Chukotka, and has been a leading voice on climate, sovereignty, and human rights, including in the 2005 petition discussed in Section 138.19. The Arctic Athabaskan Council and Gwich’in Council International represent interior Athabaskan and Gwich’in peoples across borders. The Aleut International Association represents Unangan and Aleut communities in Alaska and Russia. These bodies show that Indigenous political organization in the North has long been transnational, a pattern that Chapter 143 treats as part of the wider history of Indigenous internationalism and that this chapter reserves for that discussion.

Cooperation after 2022 illustrates the difference between continuity of an institution and continuity of all its activities. The interruption of normal meetings after Russia's invasion of Ukraine did not dissolve the Council. Scientific and working-group cooperation gradually resumed in modified forms, including virtual meetings. The Kingdom of Denmark assumed the 2025–2027 chairship with Greenlandic leadership. In June 2026 the Council reported three strategic consultations that brought groups of state representatives, Permanent Participants and working-group chairs together for joint discussions for the first time since the interruption. These consultations were a step toward sustained cooperation, not evidence that all pre-2022 political arrangements had been restored. [S35]

Indigenous organizations face a distinctive difficulty when international institutions are disrupted. Their constituencies and ecological relationships cross state boundaries; interruption of diplomatic cooperation can restrict the exchange of environmental observations and participation in shared policy work even where the underlying environmental problems continue. This makes institutional continuity a dimension of adaptation capacity, as distinct from the formal sovereignty of any Arctic state. The Council's consultative arrangements provide an important channel for influence, but they cannot replace Indigenous governments or finance community infrastructure by themselves.

Section 138.19

Climate Justice, Human Rights, and the Politics of Responsibility

Climate justice is the proposition that the burdens and benefits of climate change and of responses to it are distributed unequally, and that fairness requires attention to historical responsibility, vulnerability, and the rights of affected peoples. For Indigenous peoples of the North, it has meant framing environmental change as a violation or threat to rights to culture, property, livelihood, health, and self-determination, in addition to being an environmental problem.

A landmark in this history is the 2005 petition to the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights, filed by Sheila Watt-Cloutier, an Inuk leader from Nunavik who then chaired the Inuit Circumpolar Council, on behalf of Inuit of the Arctic regions of Canada and the United States. The petition, submitted on December 7, 2005, argued that United States greenhouse gas emissions and the resulting climate change were violating Inuit human rights recognized in inter-American instruments, including rights to culture, property, health, life, physical integrity, security, and means of subsistence. It presented Inuit dependence on ice, snow, and wildlife as the basis of their culture and argued that the loss of these conditions threatened cultural continuity.

The petition’s historical significance should be distinguished from its procedural outcome. On November 16, 2006, the Commission informed the petitioners that it would not process the petition, concluding that the information provided did not enable it to determine whether the alleged facts would characterize a violation of the rights protected by the American Declaration. The Commission nonetheless held a hearing on climate change and human rights on March 5, 2007 at the petitioners’ request, giving the issue a platform. The petition did not produce a binding judgment, and it did not establish United States liability. Its importance lies elsewhere: it helped to frame climate change as a human-rights question, it brought Inuit voices to international attention, and it influenced later arguments and campaigns. Students should be careful not to overstate its legal effect.

Other approaches have also been pursued. The Kivalina lawsuit of 2008 sought damages from energy companies in United States federal court and was dismissed, as noted in Section 138.11. Indigenous delegations have taken part in United Nations climate negotiations, and the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (2007), discussed in Chapter 143, provides a framework of rights invoked in climate arguments. Questions of responsibility remain contested in policy debate: how historical emissions should be reckoned, who should fund adaptation and relocation, whether displaced communities hold specific rights to resettlement support, how to ensure equitable access to climate funding, and how self-determination should shape adaptation. Different governments, institutions, and advocacy groups propose different answers, and this textbook describes these approaches without adjudicating among policy proposals.

The chapter does not treat all positions as equivalent in factual terms. The scientific conclusion that human activities are warming the planet is established by an overwhelming body of evidence, and claims denying it are not supported by the evidence. Disagreements about responsibility and policy, by contrast, involve values, law, economics, and politics and are legitimately debated. Distinguishing these categories is part of responsible historical writing about a contested contemporary subject.

The chronology of the Inuit petition is particularly instructive. Counsel involved in the proceedings reported the November 16, 2006 refusal to proceed as a rejection without prejudice; on March 5, 2007, Sheila Watt-Cloutier and legal representatives addressed a Commission hearing on climate and human rights. A hearing can establish an international forum for testimony and debate without producing an enforceable remedy. The case therefore belongs in the history of legal mobilization and political agenda-setting, not in a list of successful damages judgments. [S39]

Relocation adds a practical dimension to rights claims. A program that provides temporary shelter can reduce immediate danger while leaving families unable to return to ancestral lands or maintain community institutions. Rights-based evaluation asks who decides the destination, whether community membership and governance continue, and whether the financing and land arrangements support that decision. It does not assume that every community must move, or that a community's choice to remain transfers responsibility for every subsequent hazard to its residents. The comparison with Kivalina and Newtok shows that legal claims, political negotiation and administrative funding are distinct routes through which Indigenous communities pursue protection.

Climate Change, Indigenous Sovereignty, and Historical Continuity

This section synthesizes the chapter and concludes Part XXIII. The cases examined differ in environmental pressure and in political response, and comparing them through the six-dimension framework clarifies what they share and where they diverge.

Three patterns emerge. First, hazards are real and documented, but the forms they take, and the outcomes they produce, depend heavily on institutions. Newtok’s relocation was possible because the community pursued it for decades, secured land, assembled partners, and eventually resolved internal governance disputes; it was prolonged because comprehensive, coordinated relocation financing was unavailable for much of the planning period. Kivalina and Shishmaref, facing similar hazards, have followed different trajectories shaped by geography, cost, and community preference. Inuit Nunangat has responded principally through regional policy and infrastructure planning, drawing on land-claims institutions. Old Crow has used the authority of a self-governing First Nation to declare an emergency and pursue energy change. Greenland’s responses are bound up with economic development within a self-governing framework. The differences show that climate vulnerability and adaptation are historical and political outcomes, not simply environmental ones.

Second, adaptation requires resources, but resources alone are insufficient. The framework’s sixth dimension, political authority, repeatedly proved decisive: the ability of Indigenous institutions to determine and implement their own responses affected the pace, character, and legitimacy of adaptation. Communities with authority over land, funds, data, and planning could act on their own priorities; communities dependent on programs designed elsewhere often found their options limited. Environmental resilience and political sovereignty are therefore interconnected but distinct. A community may be physically secure yet dependent on outside decisions, or politically autonomous yet exposed to hazards. A satisfactory future requires both, and neither guarantees the other.

Third, Indigenous responses to climate change continue a long history of adaptation while operating under new conditions. Northern peoples have repeatedly modified their settlement, technology, and economies in response to environmental and social change. What is new is the scale and speed of change, the investments tied to fixed settlement, and the dependence of adaptation on external funding and law. Indigenous knowledge, monitoring, engineering, regional strategies, and diplomacy are not departures from tradition but extensions of it into present circumstances.

The concluding argument is that the future of the Indigenous North depends not only on controlling environmental hazards but also on the ability of Indigenous peoples to determine their own adaptation priorities. That conclusion carries a caution for historians and students: outside researchers cannot substitute their preferences for the decisions of Indigenous governments and residents. Some communities will choose to move; others will choose to stay and defend; still others will combine strategies, change course, or decline options that outsiders consider obvious. The legitimacy of such decisions rests in the right of self-determination, and the task of other governments, institutions, and scholars is to support, fund, and respect them.

This conclusion also prepares for Part XXIV. Decisions about relocation, infrastructure, energy, monitoring, and diplomacy are carried out by governments: tribal governments, First Nations governments, regional Inuit organizations, and the institutions of self-governing territories. Chapter 139 therefore turns to the contemporary governments of Indigenous North America, examining their institutions, responsibilities, legal authority, and policy challenges, including those that climate change has made more urgent.

The comparison also reveals that environmental security can improve while other dimensions remain insecure. Moving residents away from an eroding riverbank may reduce direct exposure while creating housing and fiscal pressures. A renewable energy project may strengthen economic security without altering coastal exposure. A regional strategy may strengthen political authority before its infrastructure objectives are realized. These are not contradictions; they are reasons to assess each dimension separately and then consider their interactions. The framework is most useful when students identify evidence for each conclusion rather than assign numerical scores that the sources cannot support.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Indigenous Adaptation Across Generations

What changed

  • The pace of environmental change and its human cause: contemporary warming is attributed principally to human greenhouse gas emissions.
  • The settlement pattern: fixed villages with essential infrastructure replaced much seasonal mobility.
  • The institutions of adaptation: land-claims corporations, tribal and First Nation governments, regional organizations, and international forums.
  • The tools: satellites, sensors, digital platforms, engineered foundations, and renewable energy.

What stayed the same

  • Detailed observation of land, ice, water, and animals as the basis of decision-making.
  • Sharing, kinship, and community cooperation in times of stress.
  • The importance of connection to ancestral territory.
  • The assertion of Indigenous authority over land and community decisions.

When and for whom: Change has been gradual in some respects (settlement over decades) and abrupt in others (storms, evacuations). Coastal communities on eroding shores have experienced the most direct disruption, while communities with more land and resources have experienced greater continuity. Why the pattern matters: it shows adaptation as a continuing practice and not a new invention, one now exercised under conditions that make political authority and resources decisive.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined climate change in the Indigenous North as a historical transformation of societies and not only a physical transformation of ecosystems. Its central argument is that environmental change interacts with existing social and political conditions, so that vulnerability depends on land tenure, infrastructure, funding, and political authority as well as on climate. Outcomes are not inevitable; they have varied widely among communities.

The first division established foundations. The Arctic and Subarctic are interconnected Indigenous homelands whose peoples built economies around seasonal cycles of ice, rivers, tundra, forest, and sea. They have adapted to environmental variability for millennia, but present anthropogenic warming is unusual in pace and arises in a setting of fixed villages and concentrated infrastructure created largely during the twentieth century. Colonial settlement, schooling, administrative centralization, and land-tenure arrangements shaped where communities sit and how much authority they hold to respond.

The science of Arctic amplification explains why the North is warming faster than the global average, through feedbacks such as the ice–albedo effect. NOAA’s 2025 Arctic Report Card documented the warmest Arctic air temperatures since 1900 for October 2024 through September 2025, a record-low March sea-ice maximum in the 47-year satellite record, and a decade of the ten warmest years on record. Different forms of evidence, including satellite data, instrumental records, engineering surveys, and Indigenous observation, document change at different scales and are best used together.

The second division described ecological transformation. Sea ice, a transportation network, hunting platform, and cultural landscape, has become thinner, later to form, and earlier to break up, altering mobility and access. Permafrost thaw destabilizes ground beneath houses, roads, airstrips, and cemeteries, with consequences magnified by existing infrastructure deficiencies. Coastal erosion and flooding result from combined processes, including longer open-water seasons, storm exposure, thaw, and river action, and cannot be attributed to global sea-level rise alone. Wildlife, fisheries, and vegetation are changing unevenly, and climate effects must be distinguished from industrial development, harvesting, and management influences.

The third division, the longest, turned to communities. Food systems, which link harvesting, sharing, knowledge transmission, and economy, are affected by safety, cost, and ecological change, with consequences for health, identity, and the next generation. The Newtok–Mertarvik case traced a Yup’ik community’s decades-long effort to relocate: federal land-exchange authorization in 2003 and conveyance in 2004, the Newtok Planning Group in 2006, phased moves beginning in 2019, and, by 2025, the departure of all residents from Newtok. The case showed that relocating residents is not the same as completing a functioning community, and that funding structures and local governance shaped the pace of change.

Kivalina, Shishmaref, and Shaktoolik illustrated protection in place, managed retreat, and community relocation, and the October 2025 destruction of Kipnuk and Kwigillingok, followed by relocation votes in 2026, showed both the role of disasters and the continuing gap in relocation funding. Inuit Nunangat and Greenland extended the analysis beyond Alaska, showing different institutions and responses, and warning against treating Newtok as typical.

The fourth division examined Indigenous institutional capacity. Community-based monitoring programs, such as the Indigenous Sentinels Network, SmartICE, and Siku, generate Indigenous-controlled evidence. Infrastructure and energy adaptation face high costs, short construction seasons, maintenance burdens, and funding structures that favor repair over relocation. The National Inuit Climate Change Strategy of June 7, 2019 expressed five Inuit priorities and demonstrated collective agenda-setting, though announced policy must be distinguished from funded programs and outcomes. In the interior, the Vuntut Gwitchin First Nation’s May 2019 climate emergency declaration showed a self-governing First Nation using its authority to link caribou, land, and energy to climate governance.

The fifth division considered sovereignty and geopolitics. A more accessible Arctic brings opportunities and pressures, and state claims to sovereignty coexist with Indigenous relationships to ancestral lands and waters. The Arctic Council’s Permanent Participant arrangement gives Indigenous organizations consultation rights but not formal decision-making authority. The 2005 Inuit petition to the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights framed climate change as a human-rights issue; the Commission declined to process it, though it held a hearing in 2007, and the petition produced no binding judgment of liability.

The six-dimension framework, environmental security, territorial security, infrastructure capacity, economic security, cultural continuity, and political authority, was used to compare cases. It is an instructional tool and not a scientific index. The comparison showed that political authority repeatedly shaped the pace, character, and legitimacy of adaptation, and that environmental resilience and political sovereignty are interconnected but distinct.

The conclusion is that the future of the Indigenous North is not exclusively a question of environmental survival. It is a question of Indigenous self-determination in a changing world: whether Indigenous nations can decide, fund, and carry out their own adaptation priorities. Contemporary conditions described in this chapter are dated and subject to change, and Chapter 139 turns to the governments that implement many of these decisions.

Key Terms

Arctic amplification
The tendency of the Arctic to warm faster than the global average, driven by feedbacks such as loss of reflective snow and ice and changes in heat transport.
Anthropogenic climate change
Climate change caused principally by human activities, especially the burning of fossil fuels and land-use change, as distinguished from natural variability.
Permafrost
Ground that remains at or below freezing for at least two consecutive years.
Active layer
The surface layer above permafrost that thaws each summer and refreezes in winter.
Thermokarst
Landscape of pits, slumps, sinkholes, and ponds formed when ice-rich ground thaws.
Ground subsidence
Sinking of the ground surface, often caused by melting ground ice, that can damage buildings, roads, and utilities.
Albedo
The fraction of incoming sunlight a surface reflects; snow and ice have high albedo, open water and bare ground low.
Sea-ice extent
The total area of ocean covered by ice above a threshold concentration, measured by satellites since 1979.
Landfast ice
Sea ice anchored to the shore or seabed that remains stationary, providing travel platforms and shoreline protection.
Freeze-up
The autumn formation of stable ice on seas, lakes, and rivers.
Breakup
The spring deterioration and movement of ice on seas, lakes, and rivers.
Coastal erosion
The removal of shoreline material by waves, currents, storms, thaw, and river action.
Storm surge
A rise in sea level produced by storm winds and low pressure that can flood low-lying coasts.
Cryosphere
The parts of Earth where water is frozen, including sea ice, glaciers, ice sheets, snow, and permafrost.
Glacial retreat
The shrinking of glaciers as melting exceeds accumulation, contributing to sea-level rise and altering rivers and landscapes.
Borealization
The northward shift of species and ecosystem characteristics typical of more southerly regions into Arctic environments.
Subsistence economy
An economy in which households harvest, process, and share food and materials from land and sea, usually alongside cash income; in Alaska law the term has specific regulatory uses.
Country food
Animals, fish, birds, and plants harvested from the land and sea, also called traditional food, central to northern nutrition and culture.
Food sovereignty
The right of peoples to define and control their own food systems, including harvesting, distribution, and policy.
Food security
Reliable access to sufficient, safe, and nutritious food; narrower than food sovereignty.
Ecological resilience
The capacity of an ecosystem to absorb disturbance and retain its basic structure and functions.
Climate adaptation
Adjustments in practices, infrastructure, and institutions to reduce harm from, or take advantage of, climate change.
Climate mitigation
Actions that reduce greenhouse gas emissions or enhance their removal, thereby limiting future warming.
Managed retreat
Planned movement of vulnerable buildings and infrastructure away from hazardous areas, often incrementally.
Protection in place
Defending an existing settlement with structures such as berms, revetments, drainage, or elevation.
Community relocation
Establishing a replacement settlement elsewhere and moving a community to it, as at Mertarvik.
Climate displacement
Movement of people driven by climate-related hazards; communities that choose to relocate may reject the term if it implies loss of agency.
Climate justice
The principle that climate burdens and responses should be fairly distributed, attending to historical responsibility, vulnerability, and rights.
Climate vulnerability
The degree to which a community is likely to be harmed by climate hazards, determined by exposure, sensitivity, and capacity to respond.
Adaptive capacity
The resources, knowledge, institutions, and authority that allow a community to anticipate, absorb, or recover from change.
Community-based monitoring
Environmental observation programs in which community members collect, interpret, and use information, often in partnership with scientists.
Indigenous knowledge
Systems of understanding developed and transmitted within Indigenous communities across generations, including knowledge of ice, weather, animals, and land.
Climate governance
The institutions, policies, and decision-making processes through which communities and governments address climate change.
Infrastructure resilience
The ability of essential systems such as housing, water, power, and transportation to withstand and recover from environmental stress.
Self-determined adaptation
Adaptation in which Indigenous governments and communities set priorities and control decisions, resources, and implementation.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 138 concludes Part XXIII by showing what happens when knowledge, institutions, engineering, finance, and political authority meet an environmental change of unusual speed. Chapter 133 introduced Indigenous knowledge of environments, and the intervening chapters examined fire, wildlife stewardship, extraction, and pipelines. Here the lesson is that knowledge alone does not secure a community’s future; it must be joined to resources and to the authority to act.

The chapter also reveals how recent history is entangled with older history. Fixed settlements, land-tenure arrangements, funding structures, and administrative boundaries, many created during the colonial and administrative eras studied in earlier volumes, shape which hazards become disasters and which responses are possible. Climate change did not create these conditions, but it exposes them. Understanding that connection prevents both fatalism and simplification: the outcomes depend on choices that governments, institutions, and communities make.

Continuities run through the chapter. Northern peoples have long observed, adapted, shared, and asserted authority over their territories, and these practices persist in monitoring programs, regional strategies, community votes, and diplomacy. At the same time, the chapter has shown the costs of delay and the persistent gap between planning and implementation, from Newtok’s decades of effort to the stalled recovery in Kwigillingok. These findings extend into the volume’s final chapters, which examine governments, borders, internationalism, knowledge sovereignty, and the future of nationhood.

The chapter’s closing argument is that the future of the Indigenous North is a question of self-determination as much as environmental survival. Whether communities stay, retreat, or move, the legitimacy and success of their choices depend on who makes them and who controls the means to carry them out. With that in view, Chapter 139 turns to the governments that bear responsibility for those decisions.
