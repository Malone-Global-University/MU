Chapter Opening

In June 1990, a single member of the Manitoba legislature, holding an eagle feather, withheld the consent that the Assembly needed in order to move quickly on a national constitutional agreement. The moment was brief, public, and procedural. Behind it lay two decades of First Nations argument about who had made Canada, on what terms, and whose consent a Canadian constitution required.

On June 12, 1990, Elijah Harper, an Oji-Cree member of the Legislative Assembly of Manitoba for the northern riding of Rupertsland, declined to give the unanimous consent that the Assembly required to set aside its notice rules and take up the Meech Lake Accord without delay. Television cameras recorded the exchange, and Harper became one of the most recognizable political figures in Canada. The Accord, a package of constitutional amendments negotiated in 1987 and designed chiefly to secure Quebec’s acceptance of the Constitution Act, 1982, needed approval from every provincial legislature and from Parliament before a deadline in late June 1990. Harper’s refusal was not the only reason it failed. But it dramatized a claim that Indigenous leaders had been making in different forms since the late 1960s: that the constitutional future of Canada could not legitimately be settled by federal and provincial governments alone.

This chapter explains how that claim arose, how it was organized, and what it achieved. Its subject is the transformation of First Nations demands for recognition, treaty enforcement, and political self-government into a major constitutional struggle within Canada between 1969 and 1996. The chapter begins with the constitutional framework that Canada inherited from the nineteenth century, a framework in which Parliament held jurisdiction over “Indians, and Lands reserved for the Indians” while Indigenous nations had not been parties to the constitutional bargain that created the federation. It then follows the confrontation over the federal government’s 1969 White Paper, the creation of national and regional political organizations, the legal opening provided by the Calder decision of 1973, the campaign to influence constitutional patriation, the entrenchment of Section 35 in 1982, the unfinished conferences on self-government, the failures of the Meech Lake and Charlottetown accords, and the Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples.

The chapter’s central argument is that the constitutional struggles of the 1970s through the 1990s secured recognition of existing Aboriginal and treaty rights within Canada’s constitutional order, but left unresolved fundamental questions about Indigenous nationhood, self-government, treaty implementation, and the distribution of political authority between Indigenous nations and the Canadian state. Two points follow from that argument and recur throughout. First, constitutional recognition was not a gift introduced by Canadian political leaders. It resulted from organized First Nations opposition to assimilationist policy, sustained advocacy for treaty rights, domestic and international political mobilization, and the particular opportunity created when the federal government and the provinces set out to rewrite the Constitution. Second, recognition did not create Indigenous rights. Section 35 recognized and affirmed rights that, in the view of Indigenous nations and in the reasoning of later courts, existed before 1982.

The story is also one of disagreement within Indigenous politics. First Nations whose relationships with the Crown were governed by numbered treaties often emphasized treaty implementation and a continuing nation-to-nation relationship. First Nations in British Columbia, where most of the province was never covered by land-cession treaties, emphasized Aboriginal title. Indigenous women challenged discriminatory provisions of the Indian Act and, at times, the positions of the national organizations. Inuit and Métis leaders pursued priorities that overlapped with, but were not identical to, those of First Nations chiefs. Haudenosaunee communities and other nations questioned whether any Canadian constitution could define their status. The chapter treats these differences as central evidence about the nature of Indigenous constitutional politics rather than as obstacles to a single narrative.

The evidence is unusually rich. Constitutional politics generated government white papers, committee transcripts, conference records, treaty texts, court judgments, newspaper reporting, television footage, organizational policy statements, and the writing of Indigenous leaders and intellectuals. It also left silences. The formal record of first ministers’ conferences captures the speeches of leaders far better than it captures the community meetings, kitchen-table discussions, and elders’ counsel through which many Indigenous positions were formed. Historians must read the documentary record alongside oral history, organizational archives, and later memoirs, asking in each case who produced the record and for what purpose.

The chapter sits at a joint in the textbook. Chapter 116 explained how Inuit secured a land-claims settlement and a new public territory. Chapter 117 widens the lens to the constitutional order of Canada itself: who held authority, whose rights were recognized, how Indigenous nations took part in national constitutional negotiations, and why recognition did not fully resolve the question of self-government. It prepares for Chapter 118, which examines how courts subsequently interpreted Indigenous sovereignty, jurisdiction, and constitutionally protected rights, and for Chapter 139, which examines the institutions of contemporary Indigenous governments. Where those chapters own a subject, this chapter introduces it only as far as the constitutional narrative requires.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the nineteenth-century constitutional framework, including the Royal Proclamation of 1763, historical treaties, and Section 91(24) of the Constitution Act, 1867, and why First Nations were not parties to the federal bargain.
  2. Analyze the White Paper of 1969 and the Red Paper of 1970 as competing statements about equality, citizenship, treaty obligations, and Indigenous political status.
  3. Trace the development of national and regional First Nations political organizations from the late 1960s to the creation of the Assembly of First Nations in 1982.
  4. Distinguish among Aboriginal title, Aboriginal rights, treaty rights, and the inherent right of self-government, and explain why these categories are related but not identical.
  5. Explain the significance of Calder v. Attorney-General of British Columbia (1973), including the differing conclusions of the judges and its influence on federal land-claims policy.
  6. Reconstruct the Indigenous campaign and the political negotiations of 1980–1981 that led to the restoration of Aboriginal and treaty rights in the patriation package.
  7. Interpret the text of Section 35 and related provisions, identifying which subsections were part of the 1982 wording and which were added by the amendments agreed in 1983 and proclaimed in 1984.
  8. Evaluate the roles of Indigenous women and women’s organizations in constitutional equality, Indian Act reform, and debates over membership, and distinguish federal Indian status from First Nations citizenship.
  9. Compare the recommendations of the Penner Report with the federal government’s 1995 Inherent Right Policy.
  10. Assess why the constitutional conferences of 1983–1987 and the Meech Lake and Charlottetown accords did not produce an express constitutional definition of self-government, and why their failure did not repeal Section 35.
  11. Evaluate competing interpretations of whether Section 35 transformed Indigenous–Crown relations, distinguishing recognition, implementation, and enforcement.
  12. Connect the constitutional movement of 1969–1996 to the unresolved questions of self-government, treaty implementation, jurisdiction, and fiscal authority addressed in later chapters.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did Indigenous leaders in the late 1960s and 1970s come to seek constitutional protection rather than relying on changes in federal policy?
  2. How did the White Paper’s language of equality differ from the Red Paper’s language of Citizens Plus, and what different conceptions of citizenship and nationhood did each express?

  4. What mechanisms connected the Calder decision, the patriation project, and Indigenous mobilization to the inclusion of Aboriginal and treaty rights in the Constitution?

Historical Context

Students approaching this chapter need to hold in mind three layers of background. The first is the diversity of Indigenous political relationships with the Crown. Chapters 25 through 36 and 43 through 60 traced how Indigenous nations negotiated with French, British, and later Canadian authorities. By the nineteenth century, those relationships took several legal forms: the peace and friendship treaties of the Maritime region, which did not involve land cessions; the Robinson Treaties of 1850 and other land-cession agreements in what is now Ontario; the eleven numbered treaties negotiated between 1871 and 1921 across the Prairies, northern Ontario, parts of British Columbia, and the Northwest Territories; and large areas, especially in British Columbia and much of Quebec and the North, where no land-cession treaty had been made. Indigenous constitutional politics inherited this uneven map.

The second layer is the apparatus of federal administration. After Confederation in 1867, Parliament legislated for status Indians under the Indian Act, first consolidated in 1876, and a federal department administered reserves, band membership, and many aspects of daily life. The Act was amended repeatedly. Through the early twentieth century it restricted political organization, prohibited certain ceremonies, and, from 1927 until the 1951 revision, barred the raising of funds to pursue claims. The residential school system and enfranchisement policies, treated in Chapters 85 through 88, belong to the same administrative world. By the 1960s many First Nations leaders had experience with a state that regulated Indigenous life while denying a political voice in the rules that governed it. Status Indians gained the federal vote without losing status in 1960.

The third layer is the changing political climate of the 1960s. The federal government, led after 1968 by Pierre Trudeau’s Liberals and their vision of a “just society,” was committed to individual equality, to a reformed federation, and to bilingualism. The decade also saw rising Indigenous organizing, the Quiet Revolution in Quebec, a worldwide language of decolonization, civil-rights movements in the United States, and the emergence of a modern Indigenous leadership trained in political advocacy. The simultaneous pressures of Quebec nationalism and Indigenous nationalism would shape constitutional politics for three decades. Quebec’s demands for recognition as a distinct society and Indigenous demands for recognition as distinct peoples sometimes ran in parallel and sometimes collided, as the Meech Lake episode would show.

Readers should also bear in mind the terminology problem introduced in Chapter 1. The words used in this period were contested. Federal law spoke of “Indians,” “Eskimos,” and, increasingly, “native” and “aboriginal” peoples; Indigenous organizations adopted “First Nations” in the 1970s and 1980s; and the Constitution Act, 1982 used “aboriginal peoples of Canada” to include Indian, Inuit, and Métis peoples. This chapter uses “Indigenous” as its general term, “First Nations” for the peoples historically described as status Indians and their governments, and “Aboriginal” where the constitutional text, a legal institution, or historical usage requires it. Chapter 116 treated Inuit land claims, and this chapter returns to Inuit and Métis participation only as far as constitutional negotiations require.

Division I

The Constitutional Foundations of the Indigenous Rights Struggle

Section 117.1

First Nations, Confederation, and the Canadian Constitutional Order

The Canadian constitutional order that First Nations confronted in the 1960s had been built from several layers laid down over two centuries. The oldest was the practice of treaty-making and diplomacy examined in earlier volumes. The Royal Proclamation of October 7, 1763, issued by King George III after the Seven Years’ War, declared that lands not ceded to or purchased by the Crown were reserved to the Indigenous peoples living on them, forbade private purchase, and required that any cession be made at a public meeting by the Crown. Historians disagree about how far the Proclamation reflected British imperial interest and how far it reflected a response to Indigenous diplomacy, a debate Chapter 58 considered in the context of Pontiac’s War. What is clear is that Indigenous nations, colonial officials, and later courts all treated it as a founding document of the Crown’s relationship with Indigenous peoples. It is specifically named in Section 25 of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms.

A second layer was the series of historical treaties. In what is now southern Ontario and the upper Great Lakes, Crown officials negotiated land-cession agreements throughout the early nineteenth century, including the Robinson Treaties of 1850. After Confederation, the government of Canada negotiated Treaties 1 through 11 between 1871 and 1921, extending across the Prairies and into northern Ontario, northeastern British Columbia, and the Northwest Territories. These agreements are documented in written texts drafted by Crown negotiators, but Indigenous oral accounts of the negotiations often describe broader commitments than the written wording records, including promises concerning education, health care, hunting and fishing, and the continuing nature of the relationship. Many First Nations understood the treaties as sacred covenants made with the Crown, sometimes confirmed through ceremony. The Maritime peace and friendship treaties of the eighteenth century, by contrast, did not ordinarily cede land, and most of British Columbia and substantial areas of Quebec and the North were never covered by land-cession treaties.

The third layer was the Constitution Act, 1867. Section 91(24) assigned to the federal Parliament legislative authority over “Indians, and Lands reserved for the Indians.” Other provisions gave the provinces control of most public lands and natural resources within their boundaries, which meant that questions about Indigenous land frequently involved both orders of government and sometimes put them in conflict. In the Prairie provinces, the Natural Resources Transfer Agreements of 1930 transferred Crown resources from Ottawa to the provinces while preserving a right of Indians to hunt and fish for food on certain lands, a provision that later became constitutionally important. In 1888 the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council, in St. Catherine’s Milling, described Aboriginal title in terms that made it dependent on the will of the Crown. That characterization shaped Canadian law for generations and was a principal target of Indigenous legal arguments in the twentieth century.

The central problem for later constitutional politics was that Confederation was negotiated by delegates of the Province of Canada, Nova Scotia, and New Brunswick. Indigenous nations took no part in the Charlottetown and Quebec conferences of the 1860s, and the 1867 Act assigned to Parliament authority over them without consulting them or defining what that authority meant for their continuing political existence. Many First Nations never accepted that they had been incorporated into Canada by consent. Some, notably Haudenosaunee communities, maintained that they were allies of the Crown rather than subjects of the Canadian state. Others accepted treaty relationships with the Crown but understood them as agreements between nations, not as grants of jurisdiction to a federal legislature.

This section therefore requires students to distinguish two claims to sovereignty. Canadian law asserted Crown sovereignty over the territory, a position grounded in assertion, colonial practice, and the exercise of institutional authority, and not in any single act of Indigenous consent. First Nations asserted continuing inherent political authority that predated European arrival and that treaties, where they existed, had never surrendered. The constitutional struggle examined in this chapter was in large part a struggle over whether and how those two claims could be reconciled within a single constitutional order. Courts would eventually adopt language acknowledging that Aboriginal societies existed before Crown assertion of sovereignty, but they also held that Crown sovereignty itself could not be challenged in Canadian courts. Chapter 118 examines that jurisprudence.

Section 117.2

The Indian Act, Treaty Obligations, and the Demand for Constitutional Recognition

The Indian Act formed the everyday constitutional reality of most First Nations communities for a century. It defined who was a status Indian, set up elected band councils under the supervision of the Department of Indian Affairs, regulated reserve land, and, for many decades, restricted activities ranging from ceremonial gatherings to the hiring of lawyers. It also created a set of legal categories that cut across Indigenous communities. Women who married men without Indian status lost their own status and band membership under the Act’s marriage provisions, while status men who married non-status women conferred status on their wives. Enfranchisement provisions could strip individuals of Indian status and associated community and treaty-linked rights in exchange for certain political and civic privileges, historically including eligibility for the federal vote. This legal process must not be confused with Canadian citizenship itself: in 1960, registered First Nations people obtained the unconditional federal franchise without being required to surrender status. These rules, discussed further in Section 117.12, determined who could live on a reserve, vote in band elections, and share in band resources.

The Act also displaced or marginalized many existing political institutions. Elected band councils sat alongside hereditary and traditional governing systems, including the Haudenosaunee Confederacy council, hereditary chiefs among the Gitxsan and Wet’suwet’en and other Northwest Coast nations, and clan-based and treaty-based leadership on the Prairies. Federal officials in some communities installed elected systems over the objections of traditional leaders, producing disputes about legitimacy that persisted into the constitutional era. Band governments exercised delegated authority under federal law, subject to ministerial oversight, and could not easily be understood as expressions of inherent Indigenous self-government. When later constitutional discussions asked who spoke for First Nations, this layered legacy meant that elected chiefs, hereditary chiefs, treaty organizations, and women’s groups each had a plausible claim to part of the answer.

Treaties continued to matter to First Nations even as the federal government administered them narrowly. Treaty nations pointed to specific promises, such as the “medicine chest” clause of Treaty 6 and commitments on education, and argued that federal programs for health and schooling were treaty obligations and not discretionary benefits. Federal policy, by contrast, increasingly treated services to Indians as a matter of policy and welfare, and many officials regarded the treaties as historical instruments of limited current effect. This gap between treaty understandings and administrative practice provided the political energy for the later insistence that treaties be protected by the Constitution.

Two developments of the mid-1960s set the stage for the confrontation of 1969. The first was the Hawthorn Report. Commissioned by the federal government and led by anthropologist Harry Hawthorn, the two-volume Survey of the Contemporary Indians of Canada (1966–1967) documented widespread poverty, inadequate services, and exclusion from the economic life of the country. Its most influential recommendation was that Indians be regarded as “citizens plus”: that is, as Canadian citizens who also held additional rights arising from their treaty and historical relationships with the Crown. The report urged greater Indigenous control over local services and the expansion, not the abolition, of the special status. The second development was a series of national consultations on the Indian Act in 1968 and 1969, in which Indigenous participants made recommendations. Many later concluded that the government’s policy had been written before the consultations, and that the resulting proposals ignored what they had said.

Together these developments explain why constitutional recognition emerged as a demand. If federal policy could be rewritten at the will of a minister, and if a majority government could propose to dismantle the legal basis of the relationship, then no statute or policy was secure. Indigenous leaders therefore began to search for a form of protection that Parliament could not alter by ordinary legislation. That search led, over a dozen years, to the Constitution itself.

Historical Vocabulary
Aboriginal Peoples of Canada

Definition. In Section 35(2) of the Constitution Act, 1982, “aboriginal peoples of Canada” includes the Indian, Inuit, and Métis peoples of Canada. The phrase is a constitutional term of art; it names three distinct categories of peoples, not a single population.

Historical context. “Indian” was the term used in treaties and in the Indian Act and remains a legal term in federal statute. “Aboriginal” became common in Canadian government usage in the 1970s and 1980s and was adopted in the Constitution after Indigenous organizations lobbied to ensure that Inuit and Métis peoples were included. First Nations people increasingly rejected “Indian” as a self-description, and “First Nation” became the standard political term, although it has no single statutory definition. By the twenty-first century “Indigenous” became widely preferred, particularly after Indigenous internationalism and the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, a subject examined in Chapter 143.

Why it matters. Constitutional text, case law, and government policy use “Aboriginal” in specific ways. Students must read it as a legal category in documents from the period, while recognizing that many Indigenous people prefer other terms.

Potential misunderstanding. The constitutional category does not imply that First Nations, Inuit, and Métis share identical histories, rights, or political priorities. As this chapter shows, their organizations frequently disagreed.

Section 117.3

The White Paper of 1969 and the Threat of Assimilation

On June 25, 1969, Jean Chrétien, minister of Indian Affairs and Northern Development in Pierre Trudeau’s Liberal government, tabled the Statement of the Government of Canada on Indian Policy. It is universally known as the White Paper. The document grew out of the government’s commitment to a “just society” based on equality of individual rights and out of a sincere, if narrowly framed, diagnosis that the legal separation of Indians from other Canadians was a source of disadvantage. Its authors regarded the Indian Act and the special status of Indians as the problem, not the protection.

The proposals were sweeping. The statement called for the removal of all legislative and constitutional distinctions based on Indian status, so that Indians would be treated under the law like other Canadians. It proposed repeal of the Indian Act, the gradual dismantling of the Department of Indian Affairs, and the transfer of responsibility for services to the provinces, on the same basis as for other residents. It proposed that title to reserve lands be transferred to Indian bands or individuals, so that they could be controlled and owned by Indigenous people. It promised funding to improve economic conditions and a commissioner to inquire into outstanding claims, while stating that lawful obligations arising from treaties would be reviewed with a view to bringing them to an end. A Crown appointment of an Indian Claims Commissioner in 1969, with a limited mandate, was part of the same policy moment.

The document’s language of equality was central to its appeal. Its introduction declared that the government’s policies must lead to the “full, free and non-discriminatory participation of the Indian people in Canadian society.” Many Canadian officials saw this as progressive, a rejection of the paternalism of earlier eras. The prime minister reinforced the point in public remarks later in 1969, arguing that a society could not be built on historical grievances and that treaties between sections of one society were difficult to sustain in principle. Indigenous leaders heard in those remarks a rejection of both treaties and the Aboriginal claims that had not yet been addressed by treaty at all.

Indigenous reaction was swift and largely hostile. Leaders pointed out that the proposal had been unveiled as a decision of government after consultations in which they had asked for increased Indigenous control and the strengthening of treaty rights, not abolition of the legal relationship. They argued that eliminating Indian status without a negotiated settlement would terminate, in effect, the collective legal basis for treaty rights, reserve land, and federal responsibility. They also noted that the proposal to transfer services to the provinces would place Indigenous communities under governments that had never been parties to the treaties and that had often contested Indigenous land claims. Many compared the proposal with American termination policy. The comparison has some force, since both proposals sought to end special federal relationships in the name of equality. But historians caution against equating them. The White Paper was a proposal that was withdrawn before it was implemented; American termination, examined in Chapter 104, was enacted in statutes affecting particular tribes. The Canadian proposal also arose in a different legal setting, where the Crown’s treaty obligations and the federal jurisdiction in Section 91(24) were constitutional questions.

The White Paper became a defining confrontation because it forced a generation of Indigenous leaders to articulate what they wanted to preserve. Opponents did not defend the Indian Act as it stood. Many demanded its radical revision. They defended instead a political status rooted in treaties, in the Crown’s historical relationship with Indigenous nations, and in the claim that Indigenous peoples were nations whose rights could not be abolished by a minister’s statement. In doing so, the controversy created a durable alliance among First Nations organizations that had previously worked separately, and it persuaded many leaders that only constitutional protection could prevent a repetition. The reaction also drew non-Indigenous allies, including churches and civil-society organizations that had supported the government’s goals of equality but concluded that the proposal was disrespectful of Indigenous consent.

Section 117.4

The Red Paper of 1970: Citizens Plus and the Defense of Treaty Rights

The most influential Indigenous response came from Alberta. The Indian Association of Alberta, under the presidency of Harold Cardinal, a young Cree leader from Sucker Creek who had published The Unjust Society in 1969, prepared a formal reply. Presented to the prime minister and cabinet on June 4, 1970 by the Indian Chiefs of Alberta, it was titled Citizens Plus and became known as the Red Paper. Its title deliberately borrowed the Hawthorn Report’s phrase and turned it into a political principle: Indians were entitled to the full rights of citizens, and in addition they held rights because they were Indians, rights that arose from their treaties and from their historical place in the country.

The Red Paper defended the treaties as the foundation of the relationship. It argued that the treaties were not antiquated documents but continuing agreements whose terms the federal government was obliged to honour, and that Indian status was the legal embodiment of those agreements, not a badge of inferiority. It rejected the proposal to transfer responsibility to the provinces, pointing out that the treaties had been made with the Crown in right of Canada and that provincial governments had no place in that relationship. It also called for a thorough revision of the Indian Act with Indigenous participation, for recognition of Indian control of Indian education, for the establishment of mechanisms to adjudicate treaty claims, and for the transformation of the Department of Indian Affairs into one that served as an instrument for Indian development, not for Indian control.

Two features of the Red Paper deserve emphasis. First, it was not only a negation. It set out a positive account of the relationship between Indigenous peoples and Canada: a relationship of distinct communities with distinct rights, who nonetheless were citizens of the country and entitled to its services and protections. This was a vision of what later writers would call differentiated citizenship. Second, it was argued in the language of equality turned back on the government. If equality meant fair treatment of the commitments the Crown had made, then dismantling those commitments was not equality but breach. The Red Paper thus contested the meaning of equality rather than rejecting it.

Other organizations produced parallel statements. The Union of British Columbia Indian Chiefs, formed in 1969, and the National Indian Brotherhood, which presented its own critique, joined Alberta in rejecting the White Paper. Opposition came also from the Federation of Saskatchewan Indians, the Indian Association of Manitoba, and Ontario and Quebec organizations. Their positions differed in emphasis. Treaty organizations insisted on treaty implementation, while British Columbia groups stressed unresolved land claims that the White Paper had barely acknowledged. But the shared opposition made the White Paper unworkable in practice. The government did not abruptly abandon its goals, but it recognized that it lacked consent, and by 1971 the Trudeau government had abandoned the proposal amid sustained opposition. The experience persuaded many federal officials that policy imposed on First Nations would encounter sustained resistance.

The withdrawal did not settle the underlying disagreement. The government remained committed to the principle of individual equality and wary of any recognition of collective rights that might conflict with it. First Nations leaders remained committed to treaty-based and nation-based distinctness. The result was that both sides moved their dispute to a new venue. The federal government began to deal with Indigenous organizations as consultative partners, funding them through the Secretary of State’s programs and, in 1973, adopting the National Indian Brotherhood’s Indian Control of Indian Education policy paper of 1972 as the basis for education policy. Indigenous leaders began to look beyond ministerial promises to courts, to international forums, and eventually to the Constitution.

Division II

Indigenous Political Organization and Constitutional Mobilization

Section 117.5

National Indigenous Organizations and the Emergence of Constitutional Advocacy

The White Paper controversy accelerated a process of organization already under way. The National Indian Brotherhood (NIB), founded in 1968 after the dissolution of the earlier National Indian Council, became the main national voice for status Indian provincial and territorial organizations. The split that produced the NIB also left non-status Indians and Métis without a place in it; preparatory organizing continued in 1970, and the Native Council of Canada was formally established in 1971 to represent Métis and non-status Indians. The Inuit Tapirisat of Canada was established in 1971 to speak for Inuit, a subject examined in Chapter 116. The Native Women’s Association of Canada, founded in 1974, brought together Indigenous women’s groups, many of which had worked on membership and equality issues for years. The Métis National Council followed in 1983 after Métis leaders of the western provinces separated from the Native Council. The institutional separation of these bodies mattered later, because each claimed to represent different Indigenous constituencies in constitutional negotiations.

Regional and provincial organizations were equally important. The Indian Association of Alberta, the Federation of Saskatchewan Indians, the Indian Association of Manitoba, the Union of Ontario Indians, the Grand Council Treaty 3, the Indians of Quebec Association, the Union of Nova Scotia Indians, and the Union of British Columbia Indian Chiefs each reflected distinct regional histories. The NIB under George Manuel, who became president in 1970, pursued an international as well as a national strategy. Manuel, a Secwépemc leader from British Columbia, helped found the World Council of Indigenous Peoples in 1975 and argued in The Fourth World, a book he wrote with Michael Posluns, that Indigenous peoples constituted a distinct category of nations whose claims could not be reduced to those of minority populations within states. That international thinking would later inform the Constitution Express.

Federal funding shaped the organizations. Beginning in the late 1960s, the Department of the Secretary of State and the Department of Indian Affairs provided core funds to Indigenous organizations, enabling them to hire staff, conduct research, and travel. This support made national advocacy possible, but it also created dependence on the government they sought to influence, and critics sometimes argued that funding rules favoured organizations willing to work within official channels. Indigenous leaders themselves debated whether funding compromised independence, and the question recurred whenever governments altered financial support.

The most consequential restructuring took place in 1982. The NIB had been a federation of provincial and territorial organizations, and increasingly chiefs, who held elected office in their communities and argued that they were the proper representatives of First Nations governments, pressed for a body in which chiefs themselves would be the members. In April 1982, at an assembly in Penticton, British Columbia, the NIB was reconstituted as the Assembly of First Nations (AFN), an assembly of chiefs of First Nations communities, with a national chief elected by the assembled chiefs. Delbert (Del) Riley led the National Indian Brotherhood from 1980 to 1982. David Ahenakew, a Cree leader from Saskatchewan, became the first national chief of the reconstituted Assembly of First Nations and served from 1982 to 1985. Georges Erasmus, a Dene leader from the Northwest Territories, was elected national chief in 1985, and Ovide Mercredi, a Cree lawyer from Manitoba, in 1991. The change moved national representation from organizations toward the chiefs as holders of community mandates, and it shaped the AFN’s claims to speak for First Nations governments in constitutional negotiations.

This institutional approach differed from that of grassroots activists and traditional leaders. Direct-action groups, urban Indigenous organizations, and Haudenosaunee confederacy leaders often regarded national organizations dependent on federal funding as inadequate vehicles for sovereign nations. Some insisted that treaty relationships belonged to the Crown and the specific nation, not to a national lobby. The students of this chapter should therefore read “First Nations constitutional politics” as a field of multiple actors who used different strategies, from legal argument and lobbying to demonstrations and international appeals, and who did not always agree.

Section 117.6

Treaty Nations, Unceded Territories, and Competing Constitutional Priorities

One of the most important facts about Indigenous constitutional politics is that its participants did not stand in identical legal positions. The Prairie First Nations that signed Treaties 4, 6, 7, and 8 negotiated with the Crown in the nineteenth century and remembered those negotiations in oral tradition, in ceremonies, and in treaty-based organizations. For them, the constitutional question was often how to secure implementation of obligations already undertaken and how to prevent unilateral federal or provincial changes. They tended to emphasize that the treaties were agreements between nations and that the proper partner for them was the Crown in right of Canada. Treaty-based leaders in Alberta, Saskatchewan, and Manitoba were deeply suspicious of constitutional language that might subordinate treaty rights to provincial authority or leave them undefined.

First Nations in British Columbia stood differently. With limited exceptions, such as the Douglas treaties on Vancouver Island and Treaty 8 in the northeast, the province’s territory was not covered by land-cession treaties. Instead, Indigenous nations had long asserted continuing Aboriginal title, and had met resistance from colonial and provincial governments that denied that title existed. Their political priority was recognition of that title and the right to negotiate over land, resources, and jurisdiction, and many were wary of constitutional language that might treat treaties as the only source of rights. The Union of British Columbia Indian Chiefs, founded to coordinate such claims, became the organizational centre of the most visible constitutional campaigning, although other British Columbia nations, including the Nisga’a, pursued title by litigation as well.

Differences extended beyond these two models. In the Northwest Territories, Dene leaders argued that Treaties 8 and 11 had been understood as peace and friendship agreements and had not extinguished their rights to land; their 1975 Dene Declaration affirmed that the Dene were a nation and demanded to be treated as one. The Mackenzie Valley Pipeline Inquiry led by Justice Thomas Berger (1974–1977) publicized that argument. In the Yukon, Indigenous leaders presented the 1973 claim Together Today for Our Children Tomorrow. In northern Quebec, the Cree and Inuit negotiated the James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement of 1975, a comprehensive modern settlement that preceded Section 35 but that later became an example of the “land claims agreements” protected by the Constitution. Haudenosaunee communities in Ontario, Quebec, and New York held that they were nations with their own confederacy and law, and some of their leaders were skeptical of participating in Canadian constitutional processes at all. In the Atlantic region, Mi’kmaq, Wolastoqiyik, and Passamaquoddy leaders relied on the peace and friendship treaties, which in some cases protected hunting and fishing rights but did not cede land.

These distinctions produced different positions on key constitutional questions. Treaty nations emphasized treaty federalism, a term some Indigenous and non-Indigenous writers use for the idea that treaties created a continuing relationship between Indigenous nations and the Crown and a shared jurisdictional framework. They were more likely to ask for guarantees of treaty implementation and for recognition that treaty rights could not be amended without Indigenous consent. Nations without treaties asked for recognition of Aboriginal title and for processes in which land and governance could be negotiated. Nations with strong claims to sovereignty asked whether recognition in a Canadian constitution would compromise those claims or confirm them. Proposals on self-government and on amendment of the Constitution drew sharply different reactions depending on where one stood. The preservation of existing agreements, including modern land-claims agreements, was a shared concern, but its meaning varied.

The upshot is that Indigenous constitutional politics involved negotiation within First Nations movements as well as between Indigenous and Canadian governments. Leaders sometimes compromised on language to hold coalitions together, as the later “existing Aboriginal and treaty rights” formulation illustrates. Disagreement also reflected the unequal resources of organizations: the Prairie treaty organizations and the British Columbia chiefs were well-funded and organized, while smaller or remote communities had fewer opportunities to participate in national debates.

Section 117.7

Aboriginal Title, the Calder Decision, and the Expansion of Legal Recognition

The Nisga’a of the Nass Valley in northwestern British Columbia had pressed their claim to their territory for nearly a century. In 1913 they had sent a petition to the British Privy Council; in the 1960s, under the leadership of the Nisga’a Tribal Council and its president Frank Calder, who was also a member of the British Columbia legislature, they went to court. They sought a declaration that their Aboriginal title had never been lawfully extinguished. The British Columbia courts rejected the claim, and the case reached the Supreme Court of Canada, which decided Calder v. Attorney-General of British Columbia on January 31, 1973.

The result requires careful description because it is often simplified. Seven judges heard the case. Six addressed the substantive question of Aboriginal title and divided three to three. Three judges, led by Justice Emmett Hall, concluded that Aboriginal title existed as a legal interest derived from prior occupation and had not been extinguished, because extinguishment required clear and plain legislative intention. Three other judges, led by Justice Wilfred Judson, accepted that Aboriginal title could exist in law but concluded that colonial legislation had extinguished the Nisga’a’s title. The seventh judge, Justice Louis-Philippe Pigeon, decided against the Nisga’a on the procedural ground that they had not obtained the provincial authorization required to sue the Crown, and did not address the substantive question. The outcome was therefore a dismissal of the appeal, but six of seven judges had treated Aboriginal title as a legal concept that did not depend on Crown grant, and half the court would have recognized it as still existing. The effect was to shift the legal landscape: the government of Canada could no longer assume that Aboriginal title had no legal foundation.

The federal government responded with the comprehensive land-claims policy announced in August 1973. The policy distinguished “comprehensive” claims, based on Aboriginal title in areas not covered by treaty, from “specific” claims, which concerned grievances over the fulfillment of treaties, the Indian Act, and other legal obligations. The government declared that it was prepared to negotiate settlements of comprehensive claims, and for many years the policy required that Aboriginal title be ceded or surrendered in exchange for defined rights and benefits, a condition that Indigenous nations criticized and that was revised in later decades. Early agreements included the James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement of 1975. The American Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of 1971, treated in Chapter 115, offers a contrasting model in which the settlement used regional corporations and extinguished claims by statute; Canadian settlements gradually developed distinct features, including self-government provisions.

Students should draw several connections from Calder to the constitutional movement. First, the decision gave Indigenous leaders a legal argument that their claims were rooted in law and not only in political grievance, and that argument could be taken to negotiations. Second, it prompted federal officials to establish institutions, including an Office of Native Claims, to handle claims, giving Indigenous organizations an expectation that rights could be negotiated. Third, by showing that existing Aboriginal rights might survive independently of treaty, it encouraged the use of the word “Aboriginal” alongside “treaty” in later constitutional drafting. And fourth, it strengthened the argument that rights recognized in law needed protection from legislative extinguishment. The case also illustrates a limit: a legal victory in principle did not give the Nisga’a their land. Their claim was settled by negotiation in the Nisga’a Final Agreement of 2000, a development addressed in Chapter 119.

This section intentionally does not trace the complete history of land-claims litigation, which Chapters 118 and 119 develop. The significance of Calder for constitutional politics is that it transformed the status of Aboriginal title from a contested political assertion into a recognized, if disputed, legal category, and thereby helped make Aboriginal rights a plausible subject for constitutional text.

Section 117.8

Constitutional Patriation and the Indigenous Rights Campaign

The opening for constitutional entrenchment came from an unrelated political project. When Canada was created in 1867, its basic constitutional statute, the British North America Act, was an act of the United Kingdom Parliament, and amending it required a request to Westminster. Federal and provincial governments had tried repeatedly to agree on a domestic amending formula that would allow Canada to “patriate” the Constitution, and had repeatedly failed. In 1980, after the Quebec referendum of May 20 on sovereignty-association was defeated, Prime Minister Trudeau, who had campaigned for a renewed federalism, resolved to patriate the Constitution and to add a Charter of Rights and Freedoms. When provincial premiers refused to agree, the federal government in the fall of 1980 announced it would proceed with a resolution to the British Parliament without provincial consent.

For First Nations, this created a crisis and an opportunity. The proposed federal resolution of October 1980 contained a provision that Charter rights should not diminish rights that pertained to Aboriginal peoples, but it made no wider recognition of Aboriginal or treaty rights, and Indigenous peoples had not been invited to take part in drafting. Many leaders feared that patriation would remove the Crown’s role as a distinct party to treaties and leave Indigenous nations at the mercy of a federation in which they were not partners. Treaty nations in particular reasoned that obligations undertaken by the Crown could not be transferred to a new constitutional order without their consent.

The response was a campaign on several fronts. Indigenous organizations appeared before the Special Joint Committee of the Senate and House of Commons on the Constitution in the winter of 1980–1981, where their testimony helped persuade the government to add a provision recognizing and affirming Aboriginal and treaty rights. The most visible effort was the Constitution Express, organized by the Union of British Columbia Indian Chiefs and supported by many other Indigenous leaders and communities. In November 1980 hundreds of participants travelled by train from British Columbia to Ottawa, gathering supporters along the way, to present their demands; further delegations travelled to the United Nations and to Europe, and representatives lobbied members of the British Parliament and officials in London. The Indian Association of Alberta and other organizations pursued parallel strategies, including a lawsuit in the English courts. In January 1982, the English Court of Appeal in Ex parte Indian Association of Alberta held that the Crown’s treaty obligations to Indians had come to rest with the government of Canada, so that the United Kingdom was no longer responsible for them, a decision that weakened the argument for British intervention but did not alter Indigenous determination to be heard in Canada.

Indigenous leaders argued that constitutional reform could not legitimately proceed while disregarding Indigenous nations and treaty relationships. Their argument combined legal and moral claims. Legally, treaties were agreements with the Crown, and the transfer of constitutional authority from the United Kingdom to Canada needed to preserve those obligations. Politically, a constitution that purported to be the foundation of the country needed the participation of the peoples whose lands it governed. The campaign also reflected a more modern understanding of the international dimension: Indigenous delegations told international audiences that Canada’s constitutional change was proceeding without the consent of the original peoples, and sought support from human-rights organizations and foreign parliamentarians.

Not all Indigenous leaders agreed on tactics. Some national leaders worried that the Constitution Express risked alienating British and Canadian officials; others thought public confrontation was the only way to be heard. Some Indigenous communities and organizations, especially in the Prairies, favoured lobbying in Ottawa and negotiating directly with federal officials. Despite such differences, by the end of 1980 the question of Aboriginal rights had become a visible part of the patriation debate, and the federal government had committed to include some form of recognition. The key question was what form it would take, and whether the provinces would accept it.

Historical Vocabulary
Patriation and the Amending Formula

Definition. “Patriation” refers to the transfer to Canada of the power to amend its own constitution, which had until 1982 remained in the hands of the United Kingdom Parliament. An “amending formula” is the set of rules governing how constitutional changes may be made.

Historical context. The Constitution Act, 1982 included an amending formula in Part V that requires, for most amendments, resolutions of the Senate, the House of Commons, and the legislatures of at least seven provinces representing at least half the population. Some matters require unanimity. Indigenous peoples were not given a formal role in the formula, but Section 35.1, added in 1984, commits governments to convene a conference including Aboriginal representatives before certain amendments.

Why it matters. The amending formula determines who has a veto over change, and the exclusion of Indigenous peoples from it meant that constitutional protection could be won or lost in negotiations among governments.

Potential misunderstanding. Patriation did not create Canada’s independence, which was a gradual process, nor did it by itself change Indigenous rights; it changed where and how the Constitution could be amended.

Everyday Life and Social History

Constitutional Politics Beyond the Conference Table

Constitutional history is often told through the speeches of chiefs and prime ministers, but the movement depended on the labour of many people whose names rarely appear in conference records. Community meetings, band council resolutions, and gatherings of elders were the places where positions on treaties, title, and self-government were debated before being carried to regional and national assemblies. Organizations relied on administrators, researchers, interpreters, translators, drivers, cooks, and volunteers, and on the willingness of communities to raise money and host travellers. Participants in actions such as the Constitution Express were often away from home, work, and family for extended periods.

Women played a particularly important role in sustaining these networks. Women’s groups organized on membership and equality issues, while women in communities carried out much of the practical work of hospitality, fundraising, and care that political mobilization required. Elders gave counsel about ceremony and protocol, and in many Prairie communities treaty days and pipe ceremonies connected the constitutional debate to older practices of agreement. Young people entered political life through these campaigns, and many later leaders trace their formation to the 1970s and 1980s.

The costs were real. Dependence on federal and provincial funding constrained organizations; remote communities often had limited access to national debates; and the political divisions described in this chapter caused conflict within families and communities. Historians reconstructing this everyday history rely on oral histories, organizational archives, community newspapers, and memoirs, and they must be careful not to treat the published record of leaders as the whole story.

Division III

Constitutional Recognition and Its Limits

Section 117.9

The Constitutional Negotiations of 1980–1981

The federal government’s unilateral resolution drew immediate challenge. Eight provinces, which became known as the “Gang of Eight,” opposed the plan, and several of them took it to the courts. On September 28, 1981, the Supreme Court of Canada in the Patriation Reference gave a split answer: the federal resolution was legal, but constitutional convention required a substantial degree of provincial consent. That ruling pushed the prime minister and the premiers back to the table. Meanwhile the Special Joint Committee on the Constitution had completed hearings, and in January 1981 the federal government accepted an amendment adding a provision that recognized and affirmed the Aboriginal and treaty rights of the Aboriginal peoples of Canada, a change that Indigenous witnesses had urged and that reflected the lobbying of Indigenous organizations and their allies, including opposition members of Parliament and church and civil-society groups.

The decisive negotiations took place at a first ministers’ conference in Ottawa in early November 1981. In the final days, the federal government and nine of ten provincial governments, excluding Quebec, agreed on a compromise package. It included an amending formula, a Charter of Rights and Freedoms subject to a legislative override clause now called the notwithstanding clause, and other changes. The compromise, reached in a late-night negotiation among representatives of the federal government, Ontario, and Saskatchewan and then approved by the other first ministers, omitted the clause on Aboriginal and treaty rights and weakened the guarantee of sexual equality in the Charter. Quebec’s premier, René Lévesque, rejected the agreement, and Quebec’s refusal to sign the Constitution became a central grievance of Quebec politicians for decades.

For Indigenous leaders, the removal of the Aboriginal rights provision came as a shock. Within days, Indigenous organizations, women’s groups, opposition parties, churches, and civil-society groups mounted an intense lobbying effort. Women’s organizations protested the weakening of the equality guarantee, and Indigenous groups, working with federal New Democratic Party members and other supporters, demanded the restoration of the Aboriginal rights clause. The campaigns used telegrams, telephone calls, press conferences, and direct approaches to premiers. Under this pressure, the premiers and the federal government agreed to restore the Aboriginal rights provision and the equality guarantee. However, several provincial governments insisted on adding the word “existing” before “Aboriginal and treaty rights,” reflecting concerns that undefined rights might later be asserted against provincial resources and jurisdiction. The revised provision, now numbered Section 35, was incorporated into the package that was sent to the United Kingdom.

Students should distinguish three levels of negotiation in these months. The first was the formal bargaining among federal and provincial governments, in which Indigenous peoples had no seat and which determined the final text. The second was Indigenous participation through hearings, delegations, and lobbying, which influenced what governments were willing to accept. The third was the wider public campaign in Canada and abroad, which shaped the political cost of excluding Indigenous peoples. Indigenous leaders could not veto the package, but they affected its content. The restoration of the clause thus illustrates both the strength of Indigenous mobilization and its limits: Indigenous leaders could prevent omission, but could not control the wording or the later interpretation.

The Parliament of the United Kingdom enacted the Canada Act 1982, which included the Constitution Act, 1982 as a schedule. Queen Elizabeth II proclaimed the Constitution Act, 1982 in Ottawa on April 17, 1982. Indigenous peoples did not give the Constitution unified endorsement. Some leaders welcomed the recognition as a major victory, others regarded it as a hollow gesture adopted without their consent, and some saw it as an assertion of Canadian jurisdiction over Indigenous peoples that they could not accept. These responses foreshadowed continuing debate over what the new constitutional text meant.

Cause and Effect

Why Indigenous Rights Entered the Constitution

Long-term causes

The treaty tradition and the Royal Proclamation; the absence of Indigenous participation in Confederation; the legal uncertainty of Aboriginal title after St. Catherine’s Milling; decades of federal administration under the Indian Act; and the persistence of Indigenous political claims despite restrictions on organizing.

Short-term causes

The 1969 White Paper and the national mobilization against it; the Red Paper and treaty advocacy; the growth of national and regional organizations; the Calder decision of 1973 and federal claims policy; and the growing capacity of Indigenous leaders to work in legal and international forums.

Immediate trigger and context

The federal decision in 1980 to patriate the Constitution and adopt a Charter, with the risk that Indigenous nations would be excluded and that the Crown’s treaty relationship would be affected; the Constitution Express and lobbying campaigns; and the political bargaining among governments in 1981.

Effect

Aboriginal and treaty rights were recognized and affirmed in Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982, protected from ordinary legislative amendment, and placed outside the Charter’s section 1 limitations and the notwithstanding clause.

Long-term consequences

A new legal basis for litigation and negotiation; the expectation that governments would consult Indigenous peoples in constitutional change; continuing disputes over the meaning of “existing” rights; and unfinished negotiation over self-government.

Historical complexity

No single actor produced the result. Federal reformers, provincial premiers worried about resources, Quebec’s refusal to sign, the courts’ ruling on provincial consent, women’s organizations, and Indigenous organizations all affected the outcome. The Constitution was not a gift: it resulted from Indigenous political pressure exercised at a moment of opportunity.
Section 117.10

Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982

Section 35 appears in Part II of the Constitution Act, 1982, titled “Rights of the Aboriginal Peoples of Canada.” The placement matters. The Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms occupies Part I, and its rights are subject to “reasonable limits” under Section 1 and, in some cases, to legislative override under the notwithstanding clause in Section 33. Section 35 is not in the Charter and is not subject to either mechanism. Under Section 52, the Constitution is the supreme law of Canada, so that legislation inconsistent with Section 35 is, to that extent, of no force. These features gave Section 35 a force that ordinary legislation and policy did not have, though courts later developed doctrines under which governments could justify some infringements, a subject for Chapter 118.

The original 1982 text of Section 35 had two subsections. The first recognized and affirmed “existing aboriginal and treaty rights of the aboriginal peoples of Canada.” The second defined “aboriginal peoples of Canada” as including the Indian, Inuit, and Métis peoples. Subsections (3) and (4) were added by the amendments agreed in 1983 and proclaimed in 1984 (Section 117.13). The inclusion of Métis in the definition was the result of lobbying by Métis leaders, including Harry Daniels, who argued that Métis peoples were Aboriginal and had distinct rights. The inclusion of Inuit reflected their organizational presence and the settlements discussed in Chapter 116. The definition did not decide who would be members of each group, which individuals would hold rights, or what rights each group could claim.

Section 25 of the Charter and Section 35 should be distinguished. Section 25 provides that the Charter’s guarantees shall not be construed so as to abrogate or derogate from any Aboriginal, treaty, or other rights or freedoms that pertain to Aboriginal peoples, including those recognized by the Royal Proclamation of 1763. It is a shield: it prevents Charter rights, such as individual equality rights, from being used to diminish Aboriginal and treaty rights. Section 35 is a source of constitutional protection for those rights themselves. Section 25 was amended in 1983 to refer also to rights existing under land-claims agreements. Together, the two sections reflect the compromise that Indigenous rights would have their own constitutional space, apart from but connected to the Charter’s individual-rights framework.

The constitutional verbs matter. Section 35 “recognizes and affirms” existing rights. It does not “grant” or “create” them. The wording follows the Indigenous argument, and the reasoning of the courts, that Aboriginal and treaty rights have independent sources: in Aboriginal societies’ prior occupation and law, and in treaties with the Crown. The Constitution acknowledges what exists; it does not bring it into being in 1982. Accordingly, any description of Section 35 as having “given” Indigenous peoples rights is inaccurate, and this chapter avoids it. The legal achievement of constitutional entrenchment was real nevertheless. Rights that could previously be limited or extinguished by ordinary legislation, as in the case of Parliament’s powers over Indians, were now protected against that form of alteration, and governments had to justify any infringement.

Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982

Creators: The Parliament of the United Kingdom, enacting the Canada Act 1982 at the request of the Parliament of Canada, with the text negotiated by Canadian federal and provincial governments · Date: Proclaimed April 17, 1982; subsections (3) and (4) added by amendment proclaimed June 21, 1984 · Type: Constitutional text

Original document access (historical and official editions):

  • Constitution Acts, 1867–1982 — official Department of Justice consolidation and amendment notes

Context: The provision was drafted in the final stages of the patriation negotiations, was removed and then restored in November 1981 with the word “existing,” and was amended in 1984 after the 1983 First Ministers’ Conference. Students should consult the official consolidation maintained by the Department of Justice of Canada.

35. (1) The existing aboriginal and treaty rights of the aboriginal peoples of Canada are hereby recognized and affirmed.

(2) In this Act, “aboriginal peoples of Canada” includes the Indian, Inuit and Métis peoples of Canada.

(3) For greater certainty, in subsection (1) “treaty rights” includes rights that now exist by way of land claims agreements or may be so acquired.

(4) Notwithstanding any other provision of this Act, the aboriginal and treaty rights referred to in subsection (1) are guaranteed equally to male and female persons.

Subsections (1) and (2) were in the 1982 text. Subsections (3) and (4) were added by the Constitution Amendment Proclamation, 1983, which took effect in 1984. Wording reproduced from the constitutional text; students should check it against the official version.

Analysis: “Recognized and affirmed” indicates acknowledgment of pre-existing rights. “Existing” introduced uncertainty about which rights were covered, since courts would need to decide whether rights had been extinguished before 1982. The provision lists three peoples without defining their rights or the content of self-government. Subsection (3) confirms that modern land-claims agreements generate constitutionally protected treaty rights, and subsection (4) ties the guarantee to equality between men and women.

Limitations: The text does not state what Aboriginal rights are, whether they include self-government, how conflicts with provincial or federal law are to be resolved, or how rights are enforced. Those matters were left to negotiation, legislation, and judicial interpretation.

Perspective: The language reflects compromise among federal officials, provincial governments, and Indigenous lobbying, but Indigenous nations did not draft the final wording, and it does not represent a single Indigenous view. The text says nothing about the diverse legal traditions of Indigenous peoples.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the provision say about the source of Aboriginal and treaty rights?
  2. Who created the text, and under what circumstances did the word “existing” appear?
  3. What possible purposes shaped the wording, and how might different governments have read it?
  4. What can historians learn from the text about the 1982 compromise?
  5. What can historians not establish from the text alone, particularly about self-government?
Section 117.11

The Meaning of Existing Aboriginal and Treaty Rights

Entrenching Section 35 settled that Aboriginal and treaty rights had constitutional status. It did not settle what they were. The phrase “existing Aboriginal and treaty rights” was ambiguous in several respects. “Existing” might mean rights that were already recognized by governments in 1982, rights that had not been extinguished by that date, or rights as they might be defined later. The word “Aboriginal” did not say whether rights included title to land, hunting and fishing, cultural practices, or self-government. And “treaty rights” left open how the written texts of treaties, which Crown officials had drafted, related to Indigenous understandings of the agreements.

These ambiguities reflected a political compromise. Indigenous leaders pressed for broad language and feared that “existing” could be used to deny rights that governments had refused to recognize, or that had been regulated or limited by statute. Provincial governments, especially those with substantial natural resources, wanted assurance that Section 35 would not make unspecified rights enforceable against provincial laws. Federal officials favoured provisions that could be clarified by negotiation. The agreement to schedule constitutional conferences to “identify and define” rights, discussed in Section 117.13, was meant to bridge these differences. It assumed that political negotiation would specify the content of rights that Section 35 acknowledged.

The distinction between recognition of a right and determination of its content, geographic application, and limits is central to understanding what Section 35 did. Recognition meant that a right could not be ignored as a matter of constitutional law. But the content of any particular right, its geographic range, the people who held it, and the circumstances in which governments could regulate it, remained to be determined. Courts took up this task. The Supreme Court’s decision in R. v. Sparrow (1990) concerned Ronald Sparrow, a member of the Musqueam Nation charged with fishing with a net longer than his licence allowed. The Court held that “existing” rights were those that had not been extinguished before April 17, 1982, that regulation could not by itself extinguish a right after that date, that Section 35 should be interpreted generously, and that government infringement of a right required justification. Earlier, in Guerin v. The Queen (1984), the Court had recognized a fiduciary relationship between the Crown and Indigenous peoples in connection with reserve lands. These cases are introduced here only to show how recognition began to acquire legal consequences. Chapter 118 examines the doctrine in detail.

The interpretive uncertainty produced two opposing concerns among First Nations. One was that governments and courts might interpret “existing” narrowly and freeze rights in the form they had taken under colonial regulation. The other was that attempting to define rights in the Constitution might fix them in a form that limited their growth. Some leaders therefore preferred an open-textured provision to be developed through negotiation and litigation; others wanted explicit definition, especially of self-government. The tension between these approaches explains why the constitutional conferences described below became so contentious.

Section 117.12

Indigenous Women, Constitutional Equality, and Political Membership

Women’s activism formed a distinct strand of Indigenous constitutional politics. Under the Indian Act’s rules, which dated in various forms from the nineteenth century and were reaffirmed in 1951, an Indian woman who married a man without Indian status lost her own status and her band membership, along with the right to live on the reserve, to inherit family property there, and, in some cases, to be buried in the community. A non-Indian woman who married a status man acquired status. Status men who married non-status women kept their status and conferred it on their wives and children. The effects were uneven and severe: women and their children could be cut off from their communities and from the legal rights associated with status.

Indigenous women organized to change these rules. Groups such as Indian Rights for Indian Women, founded in the late 1960s with the leadership of Mary Two-Axe Earley, a Mohawk woman from Kahnawà:ke, campaigned for reform. In 1973, in Attorney General of Canada v. Lavell and Isaac v. Bédard, the Supreme Court of Canada upheld the Indian Act’s marriage provisions against challenge under the Canadian Bill of Rights, by a narrow margin. Women of the Tobique reserve in New Brunswick organized to protest housing and marriage rules, and in 1979 women marched to Ottawa. Sandra Lovelace, a Maliseet woman from Tobique who had lost her status, brought a complaint to the United Nations Human Rights Committee, which concluded in 1981 that Canada’s treatment of her had violated her rights as a member of a minority under the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights. That finding added pressure on the federal government.

During the patriation campaign, Indigenous women’s organizations, including the Native Women’s Association of Canada, joined other women’s groups in demanding that the Constitution guarantee equality between men and women, and many worked also for recognition of Aboriginal rights. When the November 1981 compromise weakened sexual equality in the Charter and removed Aboriginal rights, the campaigns to restore both overlapped. Women’s lobbying contributed to the restoration of Section 28, which guarantees the Charter’s rights equally to male and female persons. Indigenous women then pressed for similar protection within Part II. In the 1983 constitutional amendments, Section 35(4) was added to guarantee Aboriginal and treaty rights equally to male and female persons.

The issue raised a serious conflict of principle. Some First Nations leaders and organizations argued that the authority to determine membership belonged to First Nations themselves, as an element of self-government, and that federal legislation on status was an intrusion. Many women’s advocates responded that the existing rules were discriminatory, that the federal Act created them, and that equality must apply to Indigenous communities and not only to outside governments. Others sought both goals: they wanted the discriminatory provisions repealed and the right of First Nations to determine their own citizenship affirmed. Positions did not divide simply by gender; some women leaders supported the position of the national organizations, and some male leaders supported reform.

The resolution was Bill C-31, enacted on June 28, 1985, which amended the Indian Act to bring it into line with Section 15 of the Charter, whose equality guarantee came into force on April 17, 1985, three years after the rest of the Charter. The law repealed the provision under which women lost status by marriage, ended enfranchisement, and created a process for the reinstatement of people who had lost status and for the registration of certain descendants. Well over one hundred thousand people regained or acquired status in the following years. The law also allowed bands to take control of their own membership codes under conditions set by federal law, and many bands did so. But the reform produced new controversies. It created categories of status that distinguished among descendants of registered Indians and set limits on how far status could pass to later generations. Some bands objected that the federal government had imposed an influx of new members without sufficient land and funding, while some reinstated women found that their communities did not welcome them. Subsequent litigation and later amendments, treated in later chapters, continued to address gender discrimination in the registration rules.

Students should distinguish federal Indian status from First Nations citizenship. Status is a legal category created by the Indian Act and determines eligibility for certain federal benefits and treaty entitlements. Citizenship, or membership, in a First Nation is a political relationship governed, in principle, by the nation’s own laws and traditions, although for most bands the Indian Act and its membership provisions constrained those choices. The debate over Bill C-31 therefore touched the core of the self-government question: who decides who belongs to an Indigenous nation? Chapter 131 considers the related history of child welfare, and Chapter 144 examines contemporary questions of enrollment, descent, and citizenship.

Division IV

The Struggle to Define Indigenous Self-Government

Section 117.13

The First Ministers’ Conference of 1983 and the Constitutional Amendments

The Constitution Act, 1982 itself provided for continued negotiation. Section 37 required the prime minister to convene, within a year of the Act’s coming into force, a constitutional conference with an agenda item on constitutional matters directly affecting Aboriginal peoples, “including the identification and definition” of their rights to be included in the Constitution. The prime minister was to invite representatives of the Aboriginal peoples and of the governments of the Yukon and Northwest Territories. This was the first time that Indigenous representatives were guaranteed a place at a first ministers’ table, and it created a venue for the questions that Section 35 had left open.

The conference met in Ottawa on March 15 and 16, 1983. Participants included the prime minister, the provincial premiers, territorial leaders, and representatives of the Assembly of First Nations, the Native Council of Canada, and the Inuit Committee on National Issues. The Native Women’s Association of Canada sought a separate place at the table and did not obtain one, an exclusion that its leaders protested and that became a precedent for later disputes about representation. Indigenous leaders hoped to secure agreement on self-government, but governments agreed instead on a more limited package of amendments.

Under the agreement reached in 1983, the federal government and the provinces agreed to amend the Constitution to add several provisions. Section 35(3) confirmed that rights acquired through land-claims agreements were treaty rights protected by Section 35. Section 35(4) guaranteed Aboriginal and treaty rights equally to male and female persons. Section 25 of the Charter was expanded to protect rights under land-claims agreements. A new Section 35.1 committed the federal and provincial governments to the principle that, before any amendment of Section 91(24) of the 1867 Act, Section 25 of the Charter, or Part II of the 1982 Act, a constitutional conference would be convened that included representatives of Aboriginal peoples invited by the prime minister to participate in discussions on the item. Section 37.1 scheduled further conferences, in 1985 and 1987, to continue to identify and define Aboriginal rights. The amendments required resolutions of Parliament and of provincial legislatures; they were completed in 1984 and formally proclaimed on June 21, 1984.

The dating deserves emphasis. Agreement on the amendments was reached in March 1983, but they were not part of the Constitution until proclamation in June 1984. Section 35.1 and Section 35(3) and (4) were therefore not part of the original 1982 wording. Students should also understand what Section 35.1 did and did not do. It created a commitment to a conference and an invitation to participate in discussions. It did not give Indigenous peoples a veto over amendments affecting them, and it was phrased as a constitutional commitment to a principle of participation rather than an express Indigenous consent requirement. Its legal implications should not be reduced to a categorical claim that constitutional commitments can never be judicially considered. A right to be consulted differs from a right to consent. Indigenous leaders valued the commitment because it institutionalized their presence, but they also noted that it left decisions to the governments that held the amending power.

The 1983 conference thus produced limited but real gains: guarantees for modern-treaty rights, an explicit equality clause, and a framework of future conferences. It failed to produce what many Indigenous leaders most wanted, a constitutional statement that Indigenous peoples possessed an inherent right of self-government. The next several years were devoted to that question.

Section 117.14

The Penner Report of 1983 and the Vision of Indigenous Government

Meanwhile the House of Commons had established a Special Committee on Indian Self-Government in December 1982, chaired by Liberal member of Parliament Keith Penner, who represented a northern Ontario riding. The committee travelled across the country, held hearings in First Nations communities and in Ottawa, and received submissions from chiefs, councils, elders, women’s groups, and legal experts. Its report, Indian Self-Government in Canada, tabled in October 1983 and known as the Penner Report, was unusually receptive to Indigenous views, and it produced a vision of Indigenous governance that became a point of reference for years.

The report’s core recommendation was that First Nations should be recognized as a distinct order of government within the Canadian federation, with its own jurisdiction, and that this recognition should be entrenched in the Constitution. It argued that self-government should be based on the right of First Nations to govern themselves and not on delegated administrative powers, and that the Indian Act’s regime of ministerial supervision should be replaced. The committee recommended that Parliament pass legislation to establish a new relationship, that First Nations governments hold jurisdiction over matters such as education, child welfare, health, and local administration, and that they have a fiscal basis that included transfer payments and access to resources. It called for greater First Nations control over land, a reorientation of the Department of Indian Affairs toward a supportive role, and the creation of institutions, including a system of tribunals, to help implement the changes. It also recognized that governments would need institutional capacity and training to exercise their new responsibilities, an acknowledgement that governance was not only a matter of legal declaration.

The relationship among constitutional recognition, legislation, jurisdiction, and resources was thus central. Recognition without funds would be hollow, legislation without constitutional foundation would be revocable, and jurisdiction without capacity would be difficult to exercise. The report recognized that First Nations differed in size, location, and aspiration, and it did not propose a single uniform model. Critics noted that it said little about how to resolve overlap with provincial jurisdiction or how to treat First Nations with very small populations. Some Indigenous leaders felt that it stopped short of acknowledging sovereignty and used the language of Indian First Nations governments within the Canadian system, while others regarded it as a historic step.

The federal response was cautious. In 1984, just before the federal election that brought Brian Mulroney’s Progressive Conservatives to power, the Trudeau government introduced a bill on Indian self-government that died when Parliament was dissolved. The Cree-Naskapi (of Quebec) Act of 1984 implemented the self-government provisions of the northeastern Quebec agreements, and the Sechelt Indian Band Self-Government Act of 1986 gave one British Columbia band delegated powers modelled on municipal government, a legislative approach that some saw as progress and others criticized as a form of delegation. The Penner Report’s recommendations were not implemented in full, but its language of a distinct order of government became part of the political vocabulary and influenced later constitutional proposals, the Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples, and federal policy.

A comparison with the United States clarifies the distinctive features of the Canadian debate. American tribal governments had been recognized in federal law and by the Supreme Court as “domestic dependent nations” with inherent powers, and in 1975 Congress had enacted legislation promoting tribal self-determination, which Chapter 114 examines. In Canada, by contrast, there was no comparable line of constitutional or statutory doctrine, and the question was whether a constitutional order built on the federal–provincial division of powers could acknowledge a third order of government. That structural difference helps explain why the Canadian debate centred so insistently on constitutional text.

Historical Vocabulary
Inherent Right of Self-Government and Delegated Authority

Definition. The “inherent right of self-government” is the claim that Indigenous peoples possess authority to govern themselves that arises from their own existence as peoples and their own legal and political traditions, not from a grant by the Crown or Parliament. “Delegated authority” refers to governing powers that exist because a federal or provincial legislature has conferred them, and that the legislature can modify.

Historical context. Band councils under the Indian Act exercised delegated powers. Indigenous leaders and the Penner Report argued for recognition of First Nations governments as a distinct order, an inherent right. Federal governments were willing to negotiate self-government agreements, but for years hesitated to concede that the right was inherent and constitutionally protected. The 1995 federal policy accepted that the right was inherent and existing under Section 35, but framed its exercise as a matter for negotiated agreements within the Canadian constitutional framework.

Why it matters. The difference between inherent and delegated authority determines who can alter a government’s powers and where its legitimacy lies.

Potential misunderstanding. “Inherent” does not mean unlimited or independent of the Canadian state; participants in the debate disagreed about how far, and in what relation to federal and provincial law, an inherent right could be exercised.

Section 117.15

The Constitutional Conferences of 1984, 1985, and 1987

Three further first ministers’ conferences on Aboriginal constitutional matters followed the 1983 meeting: in March 1984, April 1985, and March 1987. The governments that took part included the federal government under Pierre Trudeau, then, from September 1984, Brian Mulroney; the provincial and territorial leaders; and representatives of the Assembly of First Nations, the Native Council of Canada, the Inuit Committee on National Issues, and, after 1983, the Métis National Council. The Native Council of Canada and the Métis National Council had separate positions, reflecting the Métis and non-status constituencies’ differing legal circumstances, and Inuit leaders emphasized the importance of their land-claims negotiations, which Chapter 116 describes.

The central issue was whether the Constitution should expressly recognize an inherent right of Aboriginal self-government and, if so, in what terms. Indigenous leaders presented draft amendments to entrench the right in the Constitution, to affirm that Aboriginal governments would have jurisdiction over specified matters, and to ensure financial arrangements. Federal officials were willing in principle to support some form of constitutional recognition, especially under Mulroney, who favoured a more conciliatory approach. But the provinces, especially those with large resource economies such as British Columbia, Alberta, and Saskatchewan, hesitated, concerned about the effects on provincial jurisdiction, natural resources, and the powers of provincial legislatures. They sought assurance that self-government would be exercised only after negotiation of agreements, and that its extent would be defined before it was entrenched. Several premiers argued that they could not agree to a general right whose content was undefined.

Indigenous participants responded on several levels. Many were unwilling to accept that self-government depended on provincial approval of agreements, since they regarded the right as inherent. Others, particularly Inuit and Métis leaders, were willing to consider more specific wording. The Quebec government’s position had also shifted: in 1985 the National Assembly adopted a motion recognizing the existence of Aboriginal nations in Quebec and affirming certain rights, although Quebec at this point was not a signatory of the 1982 Constitution. Governments differed over whether self-government should be recognized as a right to be defined later or as a right whose definition had to precede entrenchment, over how it would relate to federal and provincial laws, and over whether its exercise required enabling agreements. Proposals for an amendment that would entrench the right while contemplating negotiations, a model sometimes described as “contingent” recognition, failed to gain the necessary support.

The 1987 conference was the last of the series required by Section 37.1. The federal government proposed a constitutional amendment on self-government, but a sufficient number of provinces did not agree, and the conference ended on March 27, 1987 without a general agreement. The constitutional provisions requiring the conferences expired by their own terms, and the process that had begun in 1983 concluded without agreement on a definition. Indigenous leaders, some of whom had invested heavily in the process, expressed disappointment, and some blamed provincial intransigence while others criticized the strategy of seeking agreement from governments with the power to refuse.

Historians interpret the failure in several ways. Some emphasize structural obstacles: the amending formula required the agreement of many governments, each with its own interests. Others emphasize the difficulty of reconciling Indigenous claims to inherent sovereignty with a federal system in which sovereignty was divided between two orders of government. Still others stress the limits of the negotiating process itself, in which Indigenous participants could present proposals but could not vote. Whatever the weighting, the outcome left Indigenous leaders to reconsider their strategy and set the stage for the controversies of the next five years. The failure also demonstrates the value of distinguishing recognition from definition: the principle that Aboriginal and treaty rights existed was already secured by Section 35, but the further step of defining them proved impossible by consensus.

Section 117.16

Representation, Political Authority, and Disagreement within the Constitutional Movement

One of the most difficult problems in the constitutional process was the question of who could speak for Indigenous peoples. Governments dealt with a small number of national organizations, each of which claimed to represent a constituency: the Assembly of First Nations for status Indians and their governments, the Native Council of Canada for non-status Indians and Métis, the Métis National Council for Métis of the western provinces, and the Inuit organizations for Inuit. This structure made negotiation manageable, but it also excluded many voices and prompted challenges.

Elected First Nations chiefs argued that, as leaders chosen by their communities, they were the legitimate representatives of First Nations governments. Treaty organizations insisted that treaty nations could speak for themselves and that no national body could negotiate on matters affecting treaty relationships without their consent. Hereditary and traditional authorities, including leaders of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy and of Northwest Coast nations with hereditary chiefs, questioned the legitimacy of the Indian Act band-council system and of organizations that depended on it. Indigenous women’s organizations argued that the male-dominated assemblies of chiefs did not represent their interests, especially on equality, family law, and membership. The Native Women’s Association of Canada’s exclusion from the conferences became the basis of litigation in the early 1990s. Urban Indigenous communities, increasingly numerous after decades of migration, argued that they too had interests that reserve-based governments did not necessarily represent, a subject Chapter 140 considers more fully. Inuit organizations emphasized their regional land-claims agreements. Métis representatives argued that they were a distinct people and not a subset of First Nations.

These disputes complicated negotiations even when participants agreed that constitutional protection was necessary. Governments could, and sometimes did, use disagreement among Indigenous representatives to justify delay, and Indigenous leaders sometimes accused each other of undermining the common cause. Questions of accountability arose: to whom were national organizations answerable, and how could chiefs claim to speak for individuals living off reserve? The matter also raised the issue of gender: could a constitutional order that guaranteed equality accept the authority of governments whose membership rules or leadership structures women regarded as discriminatory?

For historians, the significance of these disputes lies in what they show about Indigenous political diversity. Indigenous constitutional politics was not the work of a single movement, and the leaders who appear in negotiations represented partial constituencies. Treating them as a unified bloc would obscure the real disagreements and the genuine efforts at coalition that characterized the period. It also reminds students that representation is itself a political and constitutional question, one that continued to shape Indigenous governance and Crown–Indigenous relations in later decades.

Multiple Perspectives

Who Should Shape Canada’s Constitution?

From the perspective of First Nations treaty leaders on the Prairies: Treaties were agreements between nations and the Crown, and constitutional change should protect their implementation and require the consent of treaty nations. A constitution that left rights undefined risked narrowing them, and provinces had no place in treaty relationships.
From the perspective of First Nations without historic treaties, especially in British Columbia: Aboriginal title had never been surrendered and needed recognition and a path to negotiation. Provincial governments had refused to acknowledge the title, so a constitution that treated treaties as the main source of rights risked leaving them out.
From the perspective of Indigenous women and their organizations: The Constitution should guarantee equality for Indigenous women and not leave them dependent on the decisions of male-dominated governments, while also supporting the survival of Indigenous communities. The exclusion of women’s organizations from the conference table weakened the legitimacy of the process.
From the perspective of Inuit and Métis organizations: Inuit and Métis were Aboriginal peoples with distinct histories and had to be recognized as such. Inuit emphasized land-claims agreements and regional government; Métis, particularly in the western provinces, sought recognition of a distinct land base and political status.
From the perspective of federal officials: Entrenchment of rights was acceptable in principle, but undefined rights created legal uncertainty, and any recognition of self-government needed to be reconciled with the federal framework, the Charter, and the interests of the provinces. Negotiated agreements were preferable to open-ended litigation.
From the perspective of provincial governments: Provinces had jurisdiction over lands, resources, education, and other matters, and could not commit to an undefined right of self-government that might alter that jurisdiction. They needed clarity before agreeing to constitutional change, and provincial legislatures, which had to ratify amendments, were answerable to their own electorates.

Division V

Constitutional Crisis, Self-Government, and Historical Consequences

Section 117.17

The Meech Lake Accord and Elijah Harper’s Constitutional Stand

While the Aboriginal conferences were running, a second constitutional project was taking shape. Quebec had not signed the 1982 Constitution, and after the election of Robert Bourassa’s Liberal government in 1985 Quebec set out conditions for accepting it. In April 1987 Prime Minister Mulroney and the ten premiers reached an agreement in principle at Meech Lake, Quebec, and finalized the text in June. The Meech Lake Accord recognized Quebec as a distinct society within Canada, gave provinces a role in appointments to the Senate and the Supreme Court, adjusted federal spending power and immigration arrangements, and revised the amending formula in ways that required unanimity for certain matters, including the creation of new provinces. Because it amended the Constitution, it required approval by Parliament and every provincial legislature within three years of the first approval, which Quebec gave on June 23, 1987, so that the deadline was June 23, 1990.

Indigenous leaders objected on grounds of both process and substance. The Accord had been negotiated by eleven governments in private, immediately after the failure of the Aboriginal conferences, and did not address Aboriginal self-government or the other items on the Aboriginal agenda. Leaders noted that it recognized the distinctiveness of Quebec and of Canada’s linguistic duality without comparable recognition of Aboriginal peoples. They also warned that the unanimity requirement for creating new provinces could affect the constitutional future of the northern territories, where large Indigenous populations lived. Indigenous opposition was neither uniform nor the only opposition: critics in Western Canada, Newfoundland, and Manitoba, women’s groups, and many others raised their own objections. Yet the exclusion of Indigenous peoples from a constitutional process that purported to complete the unfinished business of 1982, while their own constitutional agenda stalled, became a powerful argument.

In Manitoba, the Accord’s ratification faced difficulty. The legislature had to approve it, and the government of Premier Gary Filmon, a Progressive Conservative minority, had committed to bring it forward after a first ministers’ meeting in early June 1990 produced a last-minute agreement among the first ministers. Under the legislature’s rules, bringing the resolution forward without the normal two days’ notice required the unanimous consent of the members. Elijah Harper, a New Democratic Party member for Rupertsland and a Treaty Indian who had been elected in 1981 as the first status Indian member of the Manitoba legislature, refused consent on June 12 and repeated his refusal on following days, in each case holding an eagle feather, a symbol of spiritual authority and honour in many Indigenous traditions. He explained that Indigenous peoples had not been included in the process and that the Accord did not address their constitutional concerns. The procedural delay meant that the legislature could not complete hearings and a vote before the deadline. Harper’s actions were widely broadcast, and gave a human face to a technical process.

The failure of the Accord cannot be attributed to Harper alone. Newfoundland’s government, led by Premier Clyde Wells, had rescinded that province’s earlier approval in April 1990 and, on June 22, 1990, declined to put the Accord to a vote in its legislature after the first ministers’ negotiations failed to meet his objections. Opposition existed in other provinces and among parts of the public. Historians therefore describe Harper’s refusal as one important cause among several. Its symbolic significance, however, was great. It demonstrated that Indigenous people could use the procedures of Canadian legislatures to affect national outcomes, and it made the exclusion of Indigenous peoples from constitutional negotiations an issue that political leaders could no longer ignore. Later that summer, the confrontation at Kanehsatà:ke, near Oka, Quebec, further focused national attention on Indigenous grievances and on the unresolved relationship between Indigenous peoples and the state; that crisis belongs to other chapters, but it formed part of the political atmosphere in which governments decided to establish a royal commission and to include Indigenous leaders in the next constitutional round.

The Meech episode transformed public discussion. Before 1990 Indigenous constitutional claims were often treated as a specialized matter for ministers and lawyers. Afterward, successive governments could not plausibly negotiate a constitutional settlement without Indigenous participation. At the same time, the episode left some Canadians resentful and complicated Indigenous leaders’ relations with provincial governments and with parts of the public, a legacy that surfaced in the 1992 referendum.

Section 117.18

The Charlottetown Accord of 1992 and the Proposed Inherent Right of Self-Government

After the collapse of Meech Lake, the federal government sought a new constitutional settlement. A national consultation, a joint parliamentary committee, and a series of multilateral meetings in 1991 and 1992 culminated in a “Canada Round” in which the prime minister, the premiers, territorial leaders, and, for the first time in a general constitutional negotiation, the leaders of four national Aboriginal organizations took part: the Assembly of First Nations under national chief Ovide Mercredi, the Inuit Tapirisat of Canada, the Native Council of Canada, and the Métis National Council. Their presence reflected both the Meech experience and the effectiveness of Indigenous advocacy. It was a major departure from 1867 and 1982, when Indigenous peoples had not been at the table.

The consensus report agreed at Charlottetown on August 28, 1992, and elaborated in a legal text in October, included a wide range of constitutional changes: recognition of Quebec as a distinct society, Senate reform, changes to the division of powers, and a package of Aboriginal provisions. Among the Aboriginal proposals was a “Canada Clause” describing Aboriginal peoples as the first peoples to govern the land and affirming that their governments constituted one of three orders of government in Canada. The accord proposed constitutional recognition that Aboriginal peoples had an inherent right of self-government within Canada, to be exercised within the Canadian federation, and subject to a framework of negotiations. It provided that the courts would not be able to enforce the right for a period of five years, so as to give time for negotiation; it addressed the application of the Charter to Aboriginal governments in a manner that preserved Section 25; it provided for Aboriginal participation in future constitutional discussions; and it was accompanied by a political accord with the Métis Nation concerning a land base and a framework for negotiations. Each of these terms was contested.

Indigenous reactions were divided. National chief Mercredi and the Assembly of First Nations endorsed the package after consultation with chiefs, arguing that it offered the first express constitutional recognition of an inherent right and a basis on which to negotiate. Inuit and Métis leaders also supported it. But there were substantial objections. Some chiefs and Indigenous communities believed that the right was defined too narrowly, that it remained subject to negotiation with governments, and that accepting it would require Indigenous nations to acknowledge Canadian sovereignty. Some treaty organizations feared that the self-government provisions might weaken treaty implementation. The Native Women’s Association of Canada opposed the proposal, in part because Aboriginal governments’ relationship to the Charter was uncertain and because women’s organizations had been denied a separate place at the table; it argued that Indigenous women’s rights needed protection against abuse by Indigenous governments as well as by others, and it had sought judicial relief on the ground that the funding and participation arrangements violated its freedom of expression and equality. The Supreme Court of Canada, in 1994, concluded that the government had not violated the Charter by funding the four national organizations and not the women’s group. Opposition also came from outside Indigenous communities, including from those who feared that recognition of an Aboriginal order of government would alter the federal structure or who objected to other parts of the package.

The package went to a referendum on October 26, 1992. Nationally, about 54 percent of voters rejected it, and about 45 percent voted in favour; majorities in several provinces, including Quebec and parts of the West, voted no, and the result differed from region to region. Opponents voted for a variety of reasons. Some thought the package gave too much to Quebec, others too little; some objected to Senate reform, others to the process or to the Aboriginal provisions; and many were dissatisfied with the political leadership. Surveys and historical studies indicate that the Aboriginal provisions were not the single decisive factor, and historians warn against attributing the result to any one provision. Indigenous voters were themselves divided, and the votes of those living on reserves are difficult to summarize.

Because the proposals failed in the referendum, none of the Charlottetown amendments took effect. This outcome did not alter Section 35. The recognition secured in 1982, the amendments of 1984, and the Constitution’s existing guarantees remained in force. What failed was an attempt to define and extend Indigenous constitutional status through a further amendment. The Charlottetown experience showed that constitutional reform on a massive scale, bundling issues of great sensitivity for many Canadians, was extremely difficult; it also left Indigenous leaders with a choice between further constitutional efforts and other strategies of negotiation and litigation. In the years that followed, most chose the latter.

Section 117.19

The Inherent Right Policy of 1995 and the Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples

Two institutions responded to the constitutional impasse. The first was the Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples (RCAP), established by the Mulroney government in August 1991. Co-chaired by Georges Erasmus, a former national chief of the Assembly of First Nations, and René Dussault, a judge of the Quebec Court of Appeal, the commission had a broad mandate to examine the relationship between Aboriginal peoples, the government of Canada, and Canadian society. It held public hearings in communities across the country, commissioned research, and heard from Indigenous elders, women, youth, and leaders. In November 1996 it released a report in five volumes with some four hundred forty recommendations.

The report presented a historical analysis of the relationship, from early cooperation through the era of displacement to the contemporary period, and proposed a long-term restructuring. It argued that the relationship should be rebuilt on principles of recognition, respect, sharing, and responsibility, and that Aboriginal nations, rather than small bands under the Indian Act, should be the basis of self-government. It estimated that dozens of historically based Aboriginal nations could form the units of a renewed order. The commission took the view that Section 35 already included an inherent right of self-government, and recommended that the federal government acknowledge it, that Parliament replace the Indian Act with new legislation, that a new Royal Proclamation affirm the principles of the relationship, that a treaty process and tribunal be established to implement treaties and negotiate new agreements, that Aboriginal land bases be substantially expanded, and that Aboriginal governments be financed adequately. It also proposed ideas such as an Aboriginal parliament and a twenty-year program of change. The federal government responded in 1998 with an action plan and statement of reconciliation, but most of the commission’s structural recommendations were not implemented, and the report’s effect has been debated since.

The second response was federal policy. After its election in 1993, the Liberal government under Jean Chrétien, who had been the minister behind the 1969 White Paper, committed to implementing the inherent right of self-government without waiting for a constitutional amendment. In August 1995 the government announced its Inherent Right Policy. The policy recognized the inherent right of self-government as an existing Aboriginal right within the meaning of Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982. It stated that the right would be implemented through negotiated agreements with Aboriginal groups, in the context of the Canadian constitutional framework, and that the Charter would apply to Aboriginal governments. It specified matters, such as the identity and internal affairs of a group, over which Aboriginal governments could exercise authority through agreements, while setting out areas, including defence and national interest matters, in which federal law would remain paramount, and it said the right did not include a right of ownership or control of lands except where specified. It indicated that agreements could be protected as treaties in some cases, but that this was not automatic. The policy thus involved recognizing a right while directing its exercise into a negotiation process whose terms were set by government.

The distinction between federal policy recognition and a successful constitutional amendment is crucial. The 1995 policy did not amend the Constitution. It asserted the federal government’s interpretation that Section 35 already protected the inherent right, an interpretation that courts had not definitively adopted, and it committed the government to negotiate. A policy can be changed by a later government, whereas a constitutional amendment cannot. For many Indigenous leaders, the policy was a welcome step that reflected years of pressure and made negotiation possible; for others, it was a limited concession that left control of the process with the Crown and ignored the nation-to-nation dimension that treaty nations asserted.

Historical Debate

Did Section 35 Transform Indigenous–Crown Relations?

The question: Did the entrenchment of Aboriginal and treaty rights in 1982 fundamentally change the relationship between Indigenous nations and the Canadian state, or did it recognize a status quo while leaving the major questions unresolved? Historians, legal scholars, and Indigenous leaders have offered several answers.

Interpretation A — Constitutional recognition as a major Indigenous achievement: Some scholars and leaders argue that Section 35 marked a decisive break with the assimilationist premises of the White Paper. It placed Indigenous rights beyond the reach of ordinary legislation, obliged governments to justify infringement, gave courts a constitutional basis for protecting rights, and established a legal foundation on which the later development of fiduciary and consultation doctrines could build.
Interpretation B — Recognition as an incomplete framework: Others contend that Section 35 recognized rights without defining them, left self-government unresolved, and left control of the content of rights to governments and courts. On this view, the word “existing,” the absence of Indigenous consent to the Crown’s sovereignty, and the reliance on litigation placed the burden on Indigenous nations to prove rights, so that the transformation was more symbolic than structural.
Interpretation C — Entrenchment as a foundation for continuing negotiation: A third approach treats Section 35 as neither an end point nor a failure but as a framework within which political and legal negotiation continued. It emphasizes that the constitutional text created a vocabulary, institutions, and expectations that shaped modern treaties, self-government agreements, and consultation practice, even as it left the ultimate outcomes open.

Evidence: Participants draw on the constitutional text, the records of the conferences, the language of the 1995 policy and the Royal Commission, and court decisions after 1982, including Sparrow. They disagree about how to weigh the legal protections created against the political and financial obstacles to implementation.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and scale more than basic facts. Historians agree on the dates and texts; they disagree about the significance of constitutional recognition when the content of rights was contested, and about whether the failure to define self-government should be read as a weakness of the text or as an open door.

Why it matters: Distinguishing recognition, implementation, and enforcement clarifies the debate. Recognition occurred in 1982. Implementation, such as negotiating agreements and changing policy, proceeded unevenly. Enforcement, through courts and political pressure, depended on Indigenous nations’ resources and litigation capacity. A reader’s judgment of transformation depends on which of these dimensions is emphasized.

Student task: Construct the strongest case for each interpretation using evidence from this chapter, then identify what additional evidence, such as the outcomes of specific negotiated agreements or court decisions, would help decide among them.

Section 117.20

The Historical Legacy of First Nations Constitutional Politics

Between the White Paper of 1969 and the Royal Commission report of 1996, First Nations and other Indigenous peoples moved from the margins of Canadian constitutional life to a recognized place in it. In 1969 Indigenous peoples faced a government proposal to dismantle their legal status without their consent. By 1982 the Constitution acknowledged existing Aboriginal and treaty rights and named Indian, Inuit, and Métis peoples as Aboriginal peoples of Canada. By 1992 Indigenous leaders sat as participants in national constitutional negotiations, and by 1995 the federal government accepted, as policy, that an inherent right of self-government was an existing right under Section 35. These were substantial changes, achieved through organization, legal argument, public mobilization, and negotiation.

The achievements were concrete. Section 35 gave Indigenous rights constitutional status, shielded them from ordinary legislative extinction, and provided a foundation for litigation that produced doctrines of justification, fiduciary obligation, and consultation. The provisions on land-claims agreements recognized modern treaties as constitutional instruments. Indigenous political organizations gained experience and recognition as participants in national debate. The principle that constitutional change affecting Aboriginal peoples should involve their representatives, expressed in Section 35.1, became part of Canadian constitutional practice. And the political cost of excluding Indigenous peoples from constitutional negotiations had been made clear.

The unresolved questions were equally substantial. Self-government remained undefined in the constitutional text, and its implementation depended on negotiation. Treaty implementation continued to produce disagreement about what the treaties promised and who should perform those promises. Jurisdiction between federal, provincial, and Indigenous governments remained uncertain, and fiscal authority, the question of how Indigenous governments would be financed, remained largely dependent on federal decisions. The participation of Indigenous peoples in constitutional reform was a commitment to consult, not a veto. Equality and community authority over membership continued to produce tension. And land rights, though recognized in principle, depended on lengthy negotiations and litigation that Chapters 118 and 119 consider.

Section 35 also did not eliminate the underlying conflict between Crown sovereignty and Indigenous claims to inherent authority. The Canadian state’s assertion that it held sovereignty, and many Indigenous nations’ assertion that they retained their own, were not reconciled by constitutional text. That tension continued to animate the politics of recognition in later decades, including debates over reconciliation, treaty relations, and the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, examined in Chapter 143. The failure of Meech Lake and Charlottetown did not repeal Section 35, but it showed the difficulty of amending the Constitution to settle these questions all at once.

In sum, the movement established a new legal and political foundation for Indigenous nationhood within Canada while leaving large questions to subsequent negotiations, legislation, and judicial interpretation. Chapter 118 turns to how courts interpreted these provisions, and Chapter 139 to the institutions of contemporary Indigenous governments that operate within and alongside the constitutional framework established in this period.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Federal Administration to Constitutional Recognition

What Changed

  • Aboriginal and treaty rights, once vulnerable to ordinary legislation and ministerial policy, received constitutional protection
  • The term “Aboriginal peoples of Canada” entered the Constitution and included Indian, Inuit, and Métis peoples
  • Indigenous organizations became recognized participants in constitutional conferences, with a constitutional commitment to consultation on certain amendments
  • Federal policy moved from the White Paper’s assimilationist proposals to acceptance, in 1995, that an inherent right of self-government exists within Section 35
  • Modern land-claims agreements gained constitutional status as treaties

What Remained Unresolved or Continuous

  • Parliament’s jurisdiction under Section 91(24), the Indian Act, and provincial control of lands and resources continued to structure daily governance
  • The content of self-government, the scope of jurisdiction, and fiscal arrangements remained for negotiation
  • Indigenous assertions of inherent authority and Crown assertions of sovereignty were not reconciled
  • Disagreements among Indigenous peoples over representation, membership, and strategy persisted
  • Treaty implementation continued to depend on contested understandings of the agreements

When and how: Change was uneven and contested, concentrated in 1969–1971, 1980–1984, and 1992–1996, with long periods of negotiation between. Who experienced change most directly: national organizations and treaty and title litigants experienced the legal shift most directly, while many communities experienced continuity in federal administration. Why the pattern matters: It shows that constitutional recognition altered the legal foundation of the relationship without by itself transforming daily governance.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the transformation of First Nations demands for recognition, treaty enforcement, and self-government into a major constitutional struggle in Canada between 1969 and 1996. Its central argument is that the constitutional struggles of these decades secured recognition of existing Aboriginal and treaty rights within Canada’s constitutional order but left unresolved fundamental questions about Indigenous nationhood, self-government, treaty implementation, and the distribution of political authority.

The constitutional background was a framework in which Indigenous nations had not participated. The Royal Proclamation of 1763, historical treaties, and the Constitution Act, 1867, with its assignment of jurisdiction over “Indians, and Lands reserved for the Indians” to Parliament, formed the inherited structure, while the Indian Act supplied the daily administrative regime. The legal categories were uneven: numbered treaties covered large areas, but most of British Columbia and much of the North and Quebec were not covered by land-cession treaties. Crown sovereignty, as asserted in Canadian law, and Indigenous claims to inherent authority were two distinct claims that had never been reconciled.

The White Paper of 1969 precipitated the modern constitutional movement. By proposing to end the legal distinctiveness of Indian status, repeal the Indian Act, and transfer responsibilities to the provinces, it made explicit the threat that federal policy could be rewritten without Indigenous consent. The Red Paper of 1970 answered with the concept of Citizens Plus, defending treaties and distinct political status as compatible with full citizenship. The government withdrew the White Paper in 1971, but the episode convinced many leaders that only constitutional protection would be secure.

The 1970s saw the growth of national and regional organizations, the formation of the Native Council of Canada, Inuit Tapirisat, and the Native Women’s Association of Canada, and, in 1982, the restructuring of the National Indian Brotherhood as the Assembly of First Nations. The Calder decision of 1973 recognized Aboriginal title as a legal concept, though the court divided on extinguishment and the Nisga’a appeal was dismissed, and it prompted a federal comprehensive claims policy. Treaty nations, nations without treaties, northern peoples, Haudenosaunee communities, and Atlantic nations pursued different priorities, showing that Indigenous constitutional politics involved negotiation within as well as outside Indigenous movements.

Canada’s patriation project offered the opening. The Constitution Express and other campaigns, testimony before the Special Joint Committee, and lobbying in Canada and Britain pressed for inclusion of Aboriginal rights. The November 1981 compromise omitted the provision, which was restored, with the word “existing,” after protest. The Constitution Act, 1982, proclaimed on April 17, 1982, recognized and affirmed existing Aboriginal and treaty rights in Section 35, placed them outside the Charter’s limits and the notwithstanding clause, and defined Aboriginal peoples as including Indian, Inuit, and Métis peoples. Constitutional recognition did not create Indigenous rights; it acknowledged rights that Indigenous nations and, later, courts held to exist independently.

The meaning of “existing Aboriginal and treaty rights” remained uncertain. Recognition was distinct from determination of content, and the early judicial decisions, including Guerin and Sparrow, indicated the developing legal significance of Section 35 without resolving its scope. Indigenous women’s organizations pursued equality and challenged discriminatory provisions of the Indian Act, which contributed to Section 28 of the Charter, Section 35(4), and Bill C-31 in 1985. The legislation prompted debate between individual equality and First Nations authority over membership, and it demonstrated the distinction between federal status and First Nations citizenship.

The 1983 First Ministers’ Conference produced amendments, agreed in 1983 and proclaimed in June 1984, adding Section 35(3) and (4) and Section 35.1 and scheduling further conferences. These amendments should be dated separately from the original 1982 wording. The Penner Report of 1983 recommended recognition of First Nations governments as a distinct order of government, but its recommendations were not fully implemented. The conferences of 1984, 1985, and 1987 failed to agree on an express definition of self-government, because of disagreement over the nature of the right, its relation to federal and provincial jurisdiction, and the role of negotiation.

The disputes over representation, involving elected chiefs, treaty organizations, traditional authorities, women’s organizations, Inuit, Métis, and urban communities, complicated negotiations. In 1990 the Meech Lake Accord, negotiated without Indigenous participation, failed to be ratified; Elijah Harper’s procedural refusal in Manitoba, together with Newfoundland’s decision and other opposition, prevented its adoption. The episode made Indigenous exclusion a prominent issue. The Charlottetown Accord of 1992, negotiated with the participation of four national Aboriginal organizations, proposed recognition of an inherent right of self-government, but it was rejected by about 54 percent of voters in the October 1992 referendum. Its failure did not repeal Section 35.

In 1995 the federal government adopted the Inherent Right Policy, recognizing the right as existing under Section 35 and directing its implementation into negotiations, and in 1996 the Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples issued a report proposing long-term restructuring of the relationship. Neither policy nor report constituted constitutional amendment. The period established a new legal and political foundation for Indigenous nationhood, including constitutional protection of rights, recognized Indigenous organizations, and the principle of Indigenous participation in constitutional discussion, but left self-government, treaty implementation, jurisdiction, land, and fiscal authority to later negotiation, legislation, and judicial interpretation.

Students should take from this chapter the understanding that constitutional recognition was the product of sustained Indigenous organization, that it was achieved within a framework that Indigenous peoples did not design, and that its significance depends on how recognition, implementation, and enforcement are distinguished. The chapters that follow examine how courts interpreted these provisions, how land and water rights were pursued, and how Indigenous governments exercised authority in practice.

A royal declaration of October 7, 1763 setting out Crown policy on Indigenous lands and the procedure for cessions; named in Section 25 of the Charter.
Section 91(24)
The provision of the Constitution Act, 1867 giving Parliament jurisdiction over “Indians, and Lands reserved for the Indians.”
Indian Act
Federal legislation, first consolidated in 1876, governing status, bands, and reserves, and long used to administer First Nations communities.
Status Indian
A person registered as an Indian under the Indian Act; a legal category distinct from First Nations citizenship or membership.
Non-status Indian
A person who identifies as Indian or First Nations but who is not registered under the Indian Act, often because of loss of status or exclusion from registration.
Band
A legal category under the Indian Act for a group of Indians whose reserve land or funds are held by the Crown, and the basis of elected band councils.
Treaty rights
Rights arising from treaties between Indigenous nations and the Crown; Section 35(3) confirms that these include rights under modern land-claims agreements.
Aboriginal rights
Rights held by Indigenous peoples by virtue of their prior occupation and distinctive societies, which may exist independently of treaties.
Aboriginal title
A legal interest in land arising from Indigenous occupation, recognized as a legal concept in the Calder decision and developed in later cases.
Comprehensive claim
A claim based on unextinguished Aboriginal title in areas not covered by treaty, addressed through federal policy announced in 1973.
Specific claim
A claim concerning alleged breaches of treaties, the Indian Act, or other legal obligations.
White Paper
The 1969 federal policy statement proposing to end the distinct legal status of Indians and repeal the Indian Act; withdrawn in 1971.
Citizens Plus
A phrase from the Hawthorn Report taken up in the 1970 Red Paper to describe full citizenship combined with rights arising from treaties and historical relationships.
Patriation
The transfer to Canada of the power to amend its constitution, completed with the Constitution Act, 1982.
Amending formula
The set of rules in Part V of the Constitution Act, 1982 governing constitutional amendment, which gave Indigenous peoples no formal veto.
Notwithstanding clause
Section 33 of the Charter, permitting legislatures to override certain Charter rights; it does not apply to Section 35.
Section 35
The provision of the Constitution Act, 1982 recognizing and affirming existing Aboriginal and treaty rights; subsections (3) and (4) were added by amendments proclaimed in 1984.
Section 25
The Charter provision ensuring that Charter guarantees do not abrogate or derogate from Aboriginal, treaty, or other rights of Aboriginal peoples.
Section 35.1
An addition proclaimed in 1984 committing governments to convene a constitutional conference including Aboriginal representatives before certain amendments; not a veto.
First Ministers’ Conference
A meeting of the prime minister and provincial premiers, with Indigenous and territorial representatives invited to those on Aboriginal constitutional matters.
Inherent right of self-government
The claim that Indigenous peoples hold governing authority arising from their own existence and traditions, as distinct from authority delegated by legislatures.
Delegated authority
Governing powers that exist by grant of Parliament or a legislature and may be altered by them, as with Indian Act band councils.
Justiciability
The capacity of a right to be enforced by courts; Charlottetown proposed a temporary limit on judicial enforcement of the self-government right.
Fiduciary duty
An obligation of loyalty and care; recognized in Guerin (1984) as part of the Crown’s relationship with Indigenous peoples in specified contexts.
Distinct society
The designation for Quebec proposed in the Meech Lake and Charlottetown accords, which Indigenous leaders contrasted with the absence of comparable recognition for Aboriginal peoples.
Crown sovereignty
The sovereignty asserted by the Crown, and later by Canadian governments, over territory, a claim Indigenous nations contested or qualified by asserting continuing inherent authority.
Constitution Express
The 1980–1981 campaign, organized largely by the Union of British Columbia Indian Chiefs, to press Indigenous claims during patriation.
Bill C-31
The 1985 amendment to the Indian Act that ended loss of status through marriage, reinstated certain people, and allowed bands to adopt membership codes.
Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples
A commission created in 1991 whose 1996 report proposed a long-term restructuring of the relationship between Aboriginal peoples and Canada.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 117 matters because it shows how a political community without a seat at the constitutional table made itself a participant in constitutional change. The movement did not begin with an invitation. It began with a policy proposal that threatened to dissolve the legal basis of the relationship between First Nations and the Crown, and it developed through organization, argument, and the skilful use of an opportunity that arose from another political project. That pattern, of Indigenous leaders turning an external initiative into a vehicle for their own claims, recurs throughout the textbook, from the diplomacy of the seventeenth century to the sovereignty movements of the late twentieth.

The chapter also reveals the difference between recognition and the full realization of nationhood. Section 35 gave Indigenous rights constitutional standing and created a framework for litigation and negotiation. But the central questions, what those rights contain, how self-government is exercised, and how Indigenous and Canadian authority relate, remained open. The experience shows that constitutional text is one stage in a longer process, and that legal recognition can coexist with continuing disputes over implementation and control.

Later developments built directly on this period. Courts developed doctrines under Section 35 that Chapter 118 examines. Modern treaties and self-government agreements, land-claims negotiations, and the development of consultation obligations all took place within the framework established in these years. The unresolved issues of jurisdiction, fiscal authority, and treaty implementation continued to shape the work of Indigenous governments, which Chapter 139 describes, and the international recognition of Indigenous rights, which Chapter 143 treats, provided an additional frame for claims that the Canadian Constitution had not settled.

Finally, the chapter reminds students that Indigenous constitutional politics was plural. Treaty nations, nations without treaties, women’s organizations, Inuit, Métis, traditional authorities, and urban communities pursued different aims and sometimes disagreed. Understanding the history of Indigenous nationhood in Canada requires attention to that plurality as well as to the common experience of exclusion and the common achievement of constitutional recognition. The story continues in the courts, in negotiating rooms, and in Indigenous communities themselves.
