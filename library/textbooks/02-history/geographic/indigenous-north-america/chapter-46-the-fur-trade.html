The Fur Trade

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

The North American fur trade emerged through the interaction of Indigenous economic systems and European demand. Indigenous nations exercised substantial commercial and diplomatic power, but expanding competition, unequal colonial relationships, environmental pressures, and changing material dependencies gradually transformed the conditions under which that power operated.

In the summer, canoes laden with bundled skins arrived at places such as Tadoussac, Fort Orange, and, after 1668, the shores of James Bay. What the traders carried had been produced over months: animals located by hunters who knew their habits, skins scraped and softened by the people who prepared them, and loads paddled across waters whose rapids, portages, and meeting places were controlled by particular communities. What they received in return, kettles, cloth, tools, and beads, would be used, reworked, and given new meaning in Indigenous households. This chapter studies that exchange and the system that grew around it.

The fur trade connected Indigenous North America to European markets in ways that transformed economic relationships, political power, household production, and the environment on both sides of the Atlantic. European demand, especially for beaver fur used in felt hats, gave the trade its commercial motive. But Indigenous nations controlled production, transport, and access to territory, and European merchants could not operate without Indigenous partners. The chapter’s governing argument is that Indigenous nations exercised substantial commercial and diplomatic power, but that expanding competition, unequal colonial relationships, environmental pressures, and changing material dependencies gradually altered the conditions under which that power operated.

The chapter explores six connected problems: Indigenous commercial authority; the production and exchange of commodities; competition among French, Dutch, and English traders; the essential economic roles of women and households; ecological transformation; and the political consequences of incorporating Indigenous economies into Atlantic commercial networks. Its main treatment runs from the late sixteenth century to about 1701, with brief earlier background and limited forward references.

The chapter has boundaries with its neighbors. Chapter 45 explained how Indigenous nations shaped French alliances and diplomacy; this chapter concentrates on production, exchange, commercial institutions, labor, markets, and ecological change. Chapter 47 examines Haudenosaunee political strategy and the wars of the seventeenth century, and this chapter refers to those subjects only where needed. Métis ethnogenesis is the subject of Chapter 52, and the broader history of English colonization is treated in Chapter 48.

The evidence includes merchants’ and missionaries’ accounts, Dutch colonial records, the 1670 Hudson’s Bay Company charter, trading-post records, archaeology, and Indigenous historical knowledge. Each has limits, and the chapter pays attention to whose perspective each source represents. Numbers are used sparingly and only where they can be grounded, because the sources do not support confident estimates of prices, volumes, or animal populations for most of this period.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the fur trade is treated as a system of Indigenous and European exchange rather than a European enterprise alone.
  2. Describe the Indigenous exchange networks and ecological knowledge on which the trade was built.
  3. Analyze how European demand, especially for beaver felt, interacted with Indigenous production.
  4. Compare the French, Dutch, and English commercial systems and their relationships with Indigenous partners.
  5. Explain the roles of intermediaries such as the Innu, Algonquin, Wendat, Mahican, Mohawk, and Cree.
  6. Assess the economic and decision-making roles of Indigenous women and households.
  7. Distinguish among gift-giving, bargaining, barter, and credit in seventeenth-century exchange.
  8. Evaluate the ecological effects of trapping without assuming uniform depletion.
  9. Interpret primary sources, including the Hudson’s Bay Company charter, with attention to perspective and limits.
  10. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of Indigenous commercial power and autonomy.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. In what ways did Indigenous nations hold commercial power in the seventeenth-century fur trade, and what limited it?
  2. How did European demand change Indigenous production without creating Indigenous expertise or trade networks?
  3. Why did French, Dutch, and English trading systems differ?
  4. What roles did women play, and why are they essential to explaining how the trade functioned?
  5. How did the trade alter the environment, and how uniform were those changes?
  6. Why does the distinction between a legal monopoly and practical control matter?
  7. How should historians weigh merchant records against archaeology and Indigenous historical knowledge?
  8. Did the fur trade expand or undermine Indigenous autonomy, and how does the answer depend on time, place, and perspective?

Historical Context

By the late sixteenth century, Indigenous peoples across the northeastern woodlands, Great Lakes, and subarctic had long-standing economies suited to their environments and connected by exchange networks, as Chapter 36 described. European fishermen and whalers visited northern coasts, and Indigenous communities obtained metal goods from them. Europe, meanwhile, had a growing market for felt hats, and its own supplies of beaver had been reduced.

The permanent colonies of the early seventeenth century, French at the Saint Lawrence, Dutch on the Hudson, and later English in the Northeast and on Hudson Bay, were each tied to the trade in different ways, and each entered regions where Indigenous nations had their own political relationships. Epidemics, discussed in Chapter 40, and warfare, discussed in Chapter 47, affected the same regions, and the trade cannot be understood apart from them.

Students should also be wary of two errors. The first treats Indigenous peoples as passive suppliers whose economies were created or reorganized by Europeans. The second assumes that participation in the trade necessarily meant rapid dependency or loss. The evidence supports neither. This chapter instead traces how Indigenous economic agency operated, how it was challenged, and how it changed.

Division I · Sections 46.1–46.4

Indigenous Commerce and the Foundations of the Fur Trade

Section 46.1 · Division I — Indigenous Commerce and the Foundations of the Fur Trade

The Fur Trade as a Problem in Indigenous and Atlantic History

Older accounts of the North American fur trade usually begin with European actors: explorers who “opened” interior waterways, companies that held royal monopolies, and traders who pushed into the continent’s forests. In that version Indigenous people appear as hunters who supplied the pelts, receive manufactured goods in return, and gradually become dependent on the merchants who bought from them. This chapter starts from a different premise. The fur trade was an exchange system in which Indigenous nations controlled production, transport, access to territory, and the social relationships on which exchange depended, and in which European companies were often the dependent party, especially in the seventeenth century.

The chapter therefore treats the fur trade as a connected system rather than a single industry. Its parts included Indigenous households that prepared hides and grew food, hunters who understood animal behavior across seasons, canoe-borne trading parties that moved goods across hundreds of kilometers, European merchants who raised capital and sold furs in Atlantic markets, and hatmakers and consumers in Europe whose preferences created demand. Each part operated under different incentives. A Wendat or Cree trader pursuing a political alliance, a Dutch merchant seeking a return on investment, and a Parisian hatter seeking felting material were all part of one chain, but they were not pursuing the same goals.

The evidence is uneven and must be read critically. Much of the seventeenth-century documentary record was produced by European merchants, officials, and missionaries who were interested in prices, supplies, and conversions, not in explaining how Indigenous economies worked. Company records survive more fully for later periods than for the early decades. Archaeology, which recovers kettles, beads, cloth seals, and trade-gun parts from Indigenous sites, and Indigenous historical knowledge, which preserves a different account of why and how people traded, help correct the bias of the written record. Later evidence, especially from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, should not be used without qualification to describe the seventeenth century.

The scholarly conversation about the fur trade has itself changed. Harold Innis’s The Fur Trade in Canada (1930) presented the trade as a “staple” industry whose demands shaped Canadian economic geography, and it tended to treat Indigenous peoples as suppliers integrated into a European-directed system. From the 1970s, historians such as Arthur J. Ray and Bruce Trigger, drawing on company records, missionary accounts, and archaeology, argued that Indigenous peoples were active economic decision-makers who bargained, selected goods, and manipulated rivalries. Later work, including Richard White’s study of the Great Lakes and recent scholarship by Indigenous and non-Indigenous historians on kinship and diplomacy, has stressed that exchange was embedded in political and social relationships that merchants’ ledgers rarely record. Section 46.34 returns to these interpretations.

The principal bodies of evidence for the period are specific and unequal. For the Saint Lawrence and Great Lakes they include Samuel de Champlain’s narratives, Gabriel Sagard’s account of the Wendat country, and the annual Jesuit Relations, now available in the Thwaites edition. For the Hudson Valley they include Dutch West India Company correspondence, notarial and court records of Fort Orange and Beverwijck, and published descriptions such as Adriaen van der Donck’s. For Hudson Bay they include the 1670 charter, Company committee minutes, and the fragmentary post journals that survive from the later seventeenth century. Each was written by Europeans for European purposes, and each reaches the historian with gaps. This chapter therefore uses archaeological findings, ethnographic evidence from later periods (marked as such), and Indigenous historical knowledge as checks on what the written sources emphasize and omit.

Historical Vocabulary

Peltry, Exchange, Commodity, and Intermediary

Peltry refers to animal skins with the fur intact, or the trade in them. Exchange is a broader term than trade or market: it includes barter, gift-giving, reciprocal obligation, and negotiated sale, and historians use it to avoid assuming that all transactions were commercial in the modern sense. A commodity is a good produced or acquired for exchange; a beaver skin was a commodity in Atlantic markets, though it could also be clothing, a gift, or a symbol of alliance in Indigenous contexts. An intermediary is a person or community that stands between producers and buyers, profiting from or controlling the flow of goods.

Potential misunderstanding: the terms profit, price, and market are useful, but they should not be assumed to describe Indigenous motives. Where evidence shows Indigenous traders pursuing advantage, comparing offers, or manipulating supply, the chapter says so; where it shows them pursuing alliance or status, it says that too.

Section 46.2

Indigenous Trade Networks before Sustained European Exchange

Exchange among Indigenous societies long predated the beaver trade, a subject surveyed in Chapter 36 and only recalled here. Peoples with different environments exchanged what each could produce well. Farming communities in the Great Lakes and Saint Lawrence regions, such as the Wendat, traded maize, tobacco, and fishing nets to hunting peoples of the north, who in turn provided dried meat, furs, and hides. Copper, shell, flint, and other materials moved over long distances through relationships maintained across generations. These flows were organized through kinship, alliance, and recurring meetings, and often took place during seasonal gatherings.

It is useful to distinguish commercial exchange from diplomatic gift-giving, although the two were often intertwined. A gift confirmed a relationship and created expectations of reciprocity; a trade transaction involved a negotiated equivalence between goods. Many societies did both at the same meeting, and a poor gift could damage a relationship that a good price could not repair. Seventeenth-century Europeans often failed to recognize this distinction and were frequently surprised when Indigenous partners judged them by their generosity and their conduct as relatives or allies, not only by the goods they offered.

This background matters for the argument of the chapter. When European merchants arrived, they entered an existing economic landscape that had specialized producers, established routes, recognized intermediaries, and norms of obligation. Indigenous peoples did not need Europeans to teach them how to trade. What Europeans offered were new commodities, notably metal, cloth, and later firearms, and a new and expanding market for goods already produced, particularly skins. The task for historians is to explain how these new elements were absorbed into older systems and how they changed those systems over time.

Documentary evidence for pre-contact exchange comes mostly from early seventeenth-century observers describing arrangements that were already established. Champlain and Sagard described the Wendat as farmers and traders whose towns lay on routes connecting them with Algonquian-speaking neighbors to the north and east. In 1636, Jesuit Jean de Brébeuf reported that particular Wendat families held rights to conduct specific lines of trade, with children sharing the rights of their parents. Historians including Bruce Trigger have used this testimony to explain recognized family trading privileges, which should not be mistaken for a uniform monopoly over every Wendat exchange. The Wendat exchanged maize, tobacco, and fishing nets for dried fish, meat, and skins. Such arrangements are best understood as institutions that ensured steady supply to partners who lived in very different ecological zones.

Material evidence confirms that goods traveled far. Native copper from the Lake Superior region, marine shell from the Atlantic and Gulf coasts, and distinctive stones appear in archaeological sites hundreds of kilometers from their sources, as earlier chapters showed. Shell beads, the ancestors of the later wampum trade, were also circulating before sustained European contact, though in smaller quantities and with strong ceremonial associations. These materials moved partly through exchange, partly through gift-giving and alliance, and partly through marriage and ritual obligations, and the sources do not allow a clean division among them.

The value of this background is that it explains why European goods spread so quickly and unevenly. Metal kettles and axes obtained on the Atlantic coast could reach interior communities through existing channels long before any European visited them. It also explains why Indigenous partners judged Europeans by standards they applied to one another, such as reliability, generosity, and willingness to enter into reciprocal obligations.

Section 46.3

Fur-Bearing Animals, Ecological Knowledge, and Indigenous Resource Management

The animals at the center of the trade included the beaver, otter, marten, fox, lynx, mink, muskrat, and others, with beaver the most commercially important in the seventeenth century because of the qualities of its underfur. Each species lived in different habitats and was pursued in different seasons. Beaver colonies were located in streams, ponds, and wetlands; marten favored mature northern forests; muskrat lived in marshes. Pelts were also taken in conditions that affected their quality, since winter fur is generally denser than summer fur. Hunting successfully required detailed knowledge of the behavior, location, and seasonal condition of animals.

Indigenous communities held that knowledge as a body of practice, passed down through families and applied within systems of territorial and customary rights. Hunting territories were recognized by kin groups in some regions, particularly among northern Algonquian-speaking peoples, though the antiquity and form of these arrangements are debated and varied from place to place. Some communities limited harvests, left breeding animals, or rotated areas, while others did not, and historians should not assume that a single conservation ethic governed all Indigenous peoples. The point is more modest and more defensible: hunters relied on accumulated local knowledge, and that knowledge, not European presence, made large-scale production possible.

Hunters also had to balance furs against food. Moose, deer, caribou, fish, and waterfowl remained essential to subsistence, and a household that devoted too much time to trapping could threaten its own food supply. When market demand grew, the decision whether to shift labor toward furs was a choice made by households weighing risk. Later sections show how those choices were shaped by prices, by the availability of trade goods, by alliances, and by the changing condition of local animal populations.

Different animals demanded different knowledge. Beaver might be hunted at their lodges in winter, taken at breathing holes beneath ice, or trapped near dams, and each method required understanding of lodge construction, water conditions, and animal behavior. Marten and lynx were taken in forest country, otter along rivers, and muskrat in marshes in spring. Moose, deer, and caribou, whose hides were also traded, required other techniques. Winter hunting required snowshoes, toboggans, and the labor of other family members to move meat and skins, so a successful hunt was always a collective achievement.

The question of whether Indigenous peoples regulated harvests for conservation is contested, and the evidence is stronger for some regions than others. In the early twentieth century, anthropologist Frank Speck described family hunting territories among Innu and other Algonquian-speaking peoples; Eleanor Leacock later argued that the specific territorial system Speck characterized as ancient had developed or hardened under the pressures of the fur trade. Subsequent scholars have questioned both generalizations, stressing regional variations and the limits of retrospective ethnography. Later ethnographic sources report customs such as leaving some animals in a lodge so that colonies could recover, but it is uncertain how widely those customs applied in the seventeenth century. A cautious position is that hunters had detailed knowledge and often had customary rules about access and sharing, that these varied between and within societies, and that market demand tested them in ways that differed from place to place.

Section 46.4

European Demand and the Atlantic Market for North American Furs

European demand for beaver was driven chiefly by the felt hat. Felt is made by matting fine fibers, and the barbed underfur of the beaver produces a dense, water-resistant felt that was prized for hats in many parts of Europe. As fashions favored beaver-felt hats, hatmakers needed a regular supply of pelts, and Europe’s own beaver populations had been heavily reduced by the sixteenth century. Merchants who could obtain pelts from North America therefore had a profitable outlet, even though the market was subject to changes in fashion, trade restrictions, wars, and competition from other sources of fur.

Not all beaver skins were equally valuable to hatmakers. Skins that Indigenous people had worn as robes for a season had lost their coarse guard hairs and had become softened with grease and use, which made them more suitable for felting. European buyers came to distinguish this worn “coat” beaver from unworn skins. The trade therefore depended on Indigenous labor in dressing and wearing skins, not merely on killing animals, a point developed in Section 46.11.

European consumers’ preferences and Indigenous producers’ objectives were different. A hatter in Europe valued pelts as raw material. An Indigenous trader might value the same pelts as a means of acquiring kettles, as an instrument for maintaining an alliance, or as a way to obtain goods that enhanced a family’s status. These goals sometimes aligned, which sustained the trade, and sometimes conflicted, for example when merchants wanted greater volume while Indigenous partners preferred to limit their dependence on a single buyer or to hold supplies back. The market gave Europeans reason to expand the trade, but it did not give them the power to control Indigenous decisions about production.

The structure of the European market affected the Indigenous trade. Beaver was one of several furs, but it was valued above all for felting, and merchants judged skins by their suitability for hatmaking. Prices in European markets responded to supply, shipping, fashion, and war, and merchants’ profits depended on the cost of goods sent to America and the price of furs sold in Europe, not on any fixed relationship with Indigenous prices. Indigenous traders could not see these fluctuations directly, but they learned of them indirectly through changing terms at posts, shortages of goods, and the behavior of traders. Crown policies, such as French taxes on beaver and later restrictions on supply, shaped the market in ways that Indigenous suppliers experienced as changes in what merchants would accept.

Before North American beaver reached Europe in volume, supplies had come from Scandinavia and Russia, and local European beaver had largely been exhausted, so American supplies replaced rather than merely supplemented existing sources. This helps explain why merchants invested in distant trading posts despite the risks. It also means that European demand was not a fixed quantity pulling on Indigenous producers; it was a changing set of opportunities that Indigenous decision-makers could take up, ignore, or exploit as suited their own interests.

Section 46.5 · Division II — The Formation of Colonial Trading Networks

Coastal Exchange and the Early North Atlantic Fur Trade

Before permanent colonies were firmly established in northeastern North America, exchange took place at the margins of the European fishery. Basque, Breton, Norman, Portuguese, and English fishing and whaling crews worked the Gulf of St. Lawrence, the Strait of Belle Isle, and the Atlantic coast from the sixteenth century. Archaeological work at sites such as Red Bay in Labrador documents the scale of Basque whaling operations. Indigenous communities, notably Mi’kmaq and Innu peoples, visited these places and traded for metal goods, and linguists have documented contact languages, including a Basque-Algonquian pidgin, that arose from these exchanges.

Jacques Cartier’s 1534 voyage provides a well-known early description of such trade. According to his account, when his ships approached the Chaleur Bay region, Indigenous people signaled their wish to trade by holding furs on poles, and exchange took place on the shore. The episode is valuable, but it should be read with caution: it survives in Cartier’s narrative, which was written for a European audience, and it shows that Indigenous partners already expected to trade and understood the terms in which the transaction could occur. Early exchange was seasonal and opportunistic, not a regular market with permanent institutions.

In this period, metal goods circulated beyond the places where Europeans were present. Items obtained on the coast moved through Indigenous networks into the interior, so that communities that had not yet seen a European might already possess European kettles or axes. This diffusion shows the importance of Indigenous intermediaries and anticipates a theme that recurs throughout the chapter: the effects of European commerce were often felt before Europeans themselves arrived.

The Basque presence deserves careful attention because it shows that Indigenous–European exchange began as a seasonal maritime encounter. At Red Bay in Labrador, archaeologists have documented shore stations used for rendering whale oil in the sixteenth century, with associated buildings, cooperages, and cemeteries. Indigenous peoples visited the region, and the existence of a Basque-based pidgin used with Algonquian-speaking peoples indicates sustained communication. Mi’kmaq in particular acquired European boats, often called shallops, and used them in their own travels along the coast, which demonstrates that Indigenous peoples took what was useful and adapted it.

By the time Champlain arrived in 1603, the situation on the upper Saint Lawrence had changed. The Iroquoian communities of Stadacona and Hochelaga, which Cartier had visited in the 1530s, were no longer present, and the river was dominated by Algonquian-speaking peoples. The reasons for the disappearance of the Saint Lawrence Iroquoians, which may include warfare, disease, migration, and the shifting politics of trade, remain debated, and no single explanation has won agreement. What is clear is that the commercial geography that European traders encountered in the early seventeenth century had been reshaped by events in the previous generation, only part of which was recorded.

Early exchange was also organized by French merchants seeking royal monopolies. In 1603 Pierre Dugua de Mons received a grant covering Acadia and the Saint Lawrence on condition that he establish settlement, an example of the French pattern in which exclusive trade rights were exchanged for colonizing obligations. The monopolies did not prevent other ships from trading, and the mobility of Indigenous traders, who could go from one anchorage to another, made exclusivity difficult to enforce.

Section 46.6

Tadoussac, Quebec, and the French Commercial Corridor

Tadoussac, at the mouth of the Saguenay River where it meets the Saint Lawrence, was an Indigenous meeting place before it became a French trading post. Innu and other peoples gathered there for seasonal exchange, and French merchants established a post in 1600. Samuel de Champlain founded a settlement at Quebec in 1608, further upriver, in a location that gave French traders access to Indigenous networks leading toward the Ottawa River and the Great Lakes. The French settlement depended on the trade, and the trade depended on Indigenous partners who arrived each year with canoes loaded with furs.

French commercial organization was shaped by royal grants of monopoly. Merchants obtained exclusive trading rights in return for promises to support colonization, and in 1627 the Company of One Hundred Associates received a monopoly over the colony’s fur trade, which it held until the Crown took more direct control in the 1660s. Section 46.21 examines this regulatory structure. The essential point here is that these formal arrangements described French rights against other Europeans; they did not give the Crown authority over Indigenous traders, who could carry their furs to other buyers.

The economic structure of the corridor should be distinguished from the political alliances discussed in Chapter 45. Alliances provided the framework of trust and obligation within which trade could occur, but the movement of goods followed its own logistics: which rivers were used, who paddled, who controlled portages, and where prices were set. A river such as the Saint Lawrence was less a highway than a series of negotiated passages, each controlled by communities with their own interests.

Tadoussac illustrates how a French commercial site was grafted onto an Indigenous one. In 1603 Champlain witnessed a gathering there at which Innu, Algonquin, and Etchemin allies celebrated a victory, an event that shows the place’s political as well as commercial importance. The post’s location at the Saguenay’s mouth gave access to a route running north into the interior, which the Innu and neighboring peoples controlled. French merchants had to deal with these communities on terms the communities could accept.

Quebec’s founding in 1608 and the later posts at Trois-Rivières (1634) and Montreal (1642) extended French reach upriver, but they did not replace Indigenous carriers. From the 1650s, Indigenous trading fleets, notably those of the Odawa, arrived at Montreal each summer, and the market became an annual fair in which French officials, merchants, and missionaries participated under conditions set partly by their Indigenous visitors. The French also experienced setbacks: English forces under the Kirke brothers captured Quebec in 1629, and it was returned to France in 1632, which interrupted the trade and left Indigenous partners to find alternatives.

Company structures changed over time. After the Company of One Hundred Associates struggled to fulfill its settlement promises, it transferred trading privileges in 1645 to the Company of Habitants, a body of colonists, which was itself overtaken by royal government in 1663. These changes shifted who in the colony profited from the trade, but they did not change the dependence of every French institution on Indigenous suppliers.

Section 46.7

Innu, Algonquin, Nipissing, and Wendat Commercial Intermediaries

Indigenous intermediaries stood between the sources of furs and the French merchants at the coast. Innu traders were well positioned at Tadoussac. Algonquin communities along the Ottawa River, notably the Kichesipirini on Allumette Island, were reported by early French observers to regulate passage on that route, and the Nipissing, living between the Ottawa River and Lake Huron, connected Great Lakes communities to the east. The Wendat, whose towns lay south of Georgian Bay, were agricultural specialists who also maintained an extensive trading network with northern and western peoples, trading corn for furs and other goods.

These intermediaries held commercial advantages for reasons that had little to do with Europeans. They knew routes, spoke the languages of several peoples, held kin ties, and had established relationships of trust. A French trader who tried to bypass them had to find his own way through unfamiliar country, which was costly and dangerous. Intermediaries could also profit from their position by buying at one price and selling at another, a practice that annoyed European merchants but was a reasonable use of a commercial advantage. They sometimes limited direct contact between Europeans and the interior in order to preserve their position, as Chapter 45 explains in diplomatic terms.

The system was not stable. Intermediary positions depended on political and demographic conditions, and when those conditions changed, the pattern of trade changed with them. The dispersal of Wendat communities around 1649–1650, examined in Chapter 45 and placed within the wider wars in Chapter 47, disrupted a principal intermediary network on the Great Lakes route. Other peoples, notably the Odawa, subsequently carried furs to the French settlements, which illustrates how Indigenous commercial roles shifted in response to events that were not primarily commercial.

The Kichesipirini Algonquin on Allumette Island in the Ottawa River illustrate how a community could build a commercial position out of geography. Champlain met their leader Tessouat in 1613, and he and later observers described the community as exacting payments or gifts from travelers passing on the river. The position was advantageous because the Ottawa River was the principal water route from the Saint Lawrence to the Great Lakes. Control of it did not require European goods, but European goods made control more valuable.

The Wendat used a different foundation: agricultural surplus, dense settlement, and long-established relations with northern peoples. Their traders carried French goods north and west and returned with furs, and Jesuit sources report that the Wendat discouraged French travel beyond their own country, which suggests that they understood the value of their position. Nipissing traders occupied a similar role between the Wendat and peoples farther north. For the French, such intermediaries were both indispensable and frustrating: they supplied furs but limited French information and bargaining power.

The intermediary system was therefore a source of Indigenous commercial power, but it was also vulnerable. When the Wendat were dispersed around 1649–1650, events treated in Chapters 45 and 47, their trading role was filled by other peoples, including the Odawa, who made use of existing relationships and the routes that the Wendat had used. French officials adjusted their approach, but they did not remove intermediaries. They tried to bypass them by sending Frenchmen into the interior, a policy that fed the growth of unlicensed trade discussed in Section 46.21.

Section 46.8

Dutch New Netherland, Fort Orange, and the Hudson Valley Trade

The Dutch enterprise in the Hudson Valley developed through the Dutch West India Company and its predecessors. Traders visited the river from the early seventeenth century, and the company established Fort Orange, near present-day Albany, in 1624. A merchant community developed beside it, which became the town of Beverwijck in the early 1650s. The location lay beyond the reach of tidal navigation at the head of the river, where trading parties from the interior could reach it by land and water. Dutch traders did not obtain furs simply by building a fort; they relied on Mahican and Mohawk communities, and on Indigenous trading routes that extended into the interior.

The trade created competition among Indigenous communities as well as among Europeans. Mahicans had been established trading partners in the region, and the Mohawk, whose homeland lay further up the Mohawk Valley, became increasingly important suppliers and buyers. The conflict between the two peoples in the 1620s, which gave the Mohawk a stronger position at Fort Orange, is discussed in connection with Chapter 47. The Dutch West India Company’s fur-trade monopoly was relaxed through measures adopted in 1638–1639 that permitted broader private participation under continuing regulation and duties. Competition among Dutch merchants consequently offered Indigenous sellers additional opportunities to negotiate prices and terms.

The Hudson Valley market also became a source of European goods that other Indigenous peoples wanted, including firearms, whose sale at Fort Orange was prohibited by Dutch authorities at times but occurred nonetheless. The relationship between the Dutch and their Indigenous partners cannot be reduced to simple commercial exchange: it included diplomacy, ceremonies, and mutual obligations. But the commercial structure shaped the political one, as access to Fort Orange became a source of Mohawk influence over their neighbors.

Dutch traders first visited the Hudson in the 1610s. A trading post, Fort Nassau, was built on an island near the site of Albany in 1614 and was abandoned after flooding. Fort Orange, established in 1624, was staffed by company employees, and its early population included Walloon settlers. After 1630 the patroonship of Rensselaerswijck, created by the merchant Kiliaen van Rensselaer, surrounded the fort, and conflicts between the patroon’s agents and company officials over control of the trade reveal how contested the commercial space was even among the Dutch.

A significant source for Indigenous commerce is the journal kept by Harmen Meyndertsz van den Bogaert, who traveled in 1634–1635 from Fort Orange into Mohawk and Oneida country. It describes villages, hospitality, and the exchange of goods, and it demonstrates that the Dutch were travelers dependent on Indigenous hosts. It also shows that the movement of goods in the interior was in Indigenous hands. Dutch traders in general waited for Indigenous parties to bring furs to the fort. Records of the court of Fort Orange and Beverwijck, which survive for the 1650s and 1660s, document disputes among Dutch traders, regulations on selling goods to Indigenous people, and the commercial culture of the town.

The Hudson Valley was not the only Dutch outlet. The Swedes established a post on the Delaware in 1638 and competed for furs from the Susquehannock, and the English in New England bought wampum and furs from Dutch traders. This competition shaped how Indigenous traders chose among markets. The Dutch company’s relaxation of its monopoly in 1638–1639, which allowed private merchants to trade subject to duties and shipping rules, increased the number of buyers at Fort Orange and gave Indigenous sellers more leverage, though it also drew in traders whose conduct company officials sometimes deplored.

Section 46.9

Wampum, Coastal Producers, and Inland Markets

Wampum consists of beads made from marine shells, notably the white and purple parts of the quahog and the whelk, produced by communities on the shores of Long Island Sound and nearby coasts. Before the Dutch arrived, wampum beads were already used in Indigenous diplomacy and ritual: strings and belts served to mark agreements, condolence, and alliances. The Haudenosaunee, the Wendat, and other peoples in the interior valued the beads for these purposes, and their significance was spiritual and political as well as material.

Dutch and English colonists in the 1620s and 1630s discovered that Indigenous peoples in the interior wanted wampum and that coastal communities could produce it. Dutch traders began to use wampum to buy furs, and settlers also used it as a medium of exchange in colonial markets. The Pequot and Narragansett, among other coastal peoples, held positions in the production and distribution of wampum, and the Pequot are often cited as controlling much of the supply in the early 1630s, though the extent of their control is debated. Demand from colonial buyers expanded production and changed the relationships among coastal communities, particularly as European metal tools made bead manufacture faster.

It would be misleading to describe wampum as a universal Indigenous currency. Its ceremonial role was distinct from its use as a medium of exchange in colonial markets, and its value in colonial settings was determined in part by colonial regulation and by fluctuations in supply. Colonial authorities set and adjusted its value in their markets, while Indigenous communities continued to treat belts as records and agreements. The case of wampum shows how an object could occupy different roles in different settings, and how European demand could alter economic relations among Indigenous peoples without replacing Indigenous understandings of what the object meant.

Wampum was made by cutting, drilling, and polishing shells, most prominently the purple and white parts of the quahog and the white columella of the whelk. Before metal drills, the work was slow. Steel awls and drills, obtained from Europeans, increased the rate of production, and as demand grew, communities along Long Island Sound and the south coast of New England specialized in making beads. Plymouth colonists learned about wampum’s value in the Dutch trade after a Dutch envoy, Isaac de Rasieres, visited in 1627, and the English began buying it to trade for furs in the interior.

The Pequot’s position in the 1620s and 1630s is attested in colonial sources as one of influence over coastal producers and tribute, not simple monopoly, and the Narragansett and other peoples also participated. The Pequot War of 1636–1637, treated in Chapter 48, was driven by several causes, including competition over trade, land, and diplomacy. Colonial governments regulated wampum, with Massachusetts recognizing it as legal tender for small payments in 1637 and withdrawing that status in 1661, and Dutch officials attempted to fix its value in New Netherland. These regulations indicate that its value in colonial markets rested partly on law and partly on Indigenous demand.

For the Haudenosaunee and other interior peoples, wampum belts and strings carried messages in diplomacy and marked the seriousness of agreements. The same objects could therefore hold meaning in two different registers: ceremonial and political in Indigenous councils, and monetary in colonial markets. Overproduction, counterfeit beads, and changing exchange rates reduced its market value in the later seventeenth century, but the ceremonial role continued.

Evidence Study

Wampum, Trade Goods, and Indigenous Material Culture

Archaeologists recover shell beads, kettle fragments, glass beads, and cloth seals from Indigenous sites in the Northeast and Great Lakes. Wampum beads can be identified by their material and manufacture, and changes in drilling methods, from stone tools to metal awls, can sometimes be seen in the beads themselves. Kettle fragments cut into ornaments show that Indigenous craftspeople reworked metal to existing designs. Burial contexts show which goods were valued enough to accompany the dead.

What this evidence can establish: that goods reached particular places and were used in particular ways. What it cannot establish: the price paid, the identity of the trader, or what an object meant to its owner without additional evidence, such as oral tradition or written accounts.

Hunting, Trapping, and the Seasonal Production of Furs

Furs were produced within seasonal rounds that also served other needs. Winter pelts were generally of higher quality, and winter trapping and hunting took advantage of snow cover for tracking and transporting animals. Beaver could be taken in various ways: by breaking into lodges, by taking animals at breathing holes in the ice, by trapping, and, after the arrival of European metal goods, with steel traps in later periods. Dogs, snowshoes, and toboggans were all part of the equipment, and each required skill and labor to make and maintain.

Hunting practices varied among communities. Northern Algonquian-speaking peoples of the subarctic forest relied heavily on small family hunting groups moving across territories during winter, while communities in more southern, agricultural regions combined farming with seasonal hunts. Market demand could change established schedules: hunters might spend more of the winter on beaver and less on other pursuits, or travel farther to find undisturbed colonies. Such shifts carried risk, especially where food supplies were uncertain, and decisions about how much to commit to the trade involved household and community judgment.

The activity was thus not a simple extension of an existing pattern. Trapping for the market intensified some tasks, changed the timing of others, and altered the allocation of labor within households. It also changed who owned the product: furs taken for exchange had to be divided in ways that satisfied kin obligations as well as commercial aims. Such adjustments differed between communities, and the sources rarely describe them in detail.

The best-known early description of a seasonal hunting round is Paul Le Jeune’s account of the winter of 1633–1634, which he spent traveling with an Innu hunting band. His narrative describes moving camp, hunting moose and beaver, periods of hunger, and the negotiations within the band over where to go. It is a missionary’s report, written to demonstrate the hardships of mission work and the need for French support, and it must be read with those purposes in mind. But it records that winter hunting depended on decisions about food and risk, not merely on market incentives.

Market demand could change the balance. Where beaver were abundant and traders offered goods the band wanted, families could choose to prioritize them. Where hunting beaver reduced time for moose or fishing, a band risked shortages. Because so much depended on weather and the movements of game, households diversified their efforts, and a decision to hunt for the trade was always conditioned by fear of famine. The uneven documentation means that historians reconstruct these choices from fragments, such as Le Jeune’s account, and from later ethnographic descriptions that must be applied carefully.

This is an illustrative reconstruction, drawing on regional evidence and on later sources; it does not describe a single documented community. In many northern and woodland communities, the year was divided into periods of hunting, fishing, gathering, and, where agriculture was practiced, planting and harvest. Late autumn and winter often saw families dispersed to hunting territories; spring brought gathering, maple sugaring in some regions, and the preparation of furs for transport; summer brought gatherings at trading places, where goods were exchanged, alliances renewed, and marriages arranged.

The introduction of market demand did not create this cycle, but it added a new purpose to part of it and changed the intensity of some activities. Households had to decide how to balance fur production against food, and the work of preparing skins, repairing canoes, and provisioning travelers fell across the community.

Section 46.11

Preparing Pelts: Labor, Quality, and Commercial Value

A beaver skin had to be prepared before it was useful to a merchant. After skinning, the pelt was stretched on a frame, scraped to remove fat and flesh, dried, and in many cases softened and dressed with animal fats, brains, and smoke. The labor of preparing hides was considerable and fell largely on women in many Indigenous societies, as Section 46.16 discusses. The commercial value of a pelt thus reflected skilled labor as well as the work of the hunter.

The distinction between dressed and undressed skins helps explain the trade’s structure. Hatmakers valued skins that had been worn, since wear removed the coarse outer hairs and left the soft underfur that felted well. Indigenous households that wore beaver robes for a season thereby increased the value of the product. European buyers learned to prefer worn skins and to pay more for them, and later records distinguish between several grades. In effect, Indigenous clothing production was integrated into the Atlantic hat industry.

Preparation also affected transport. Dressed and dried skins were lighter and less perishable than raw ones, and bundling them for travel required care. Poorly prepared pelts could spoil on the journey or be rejected by buyers, who inspected goods closely. The system therefore rewarded those with skill and discouraged hasty production, and it gave producers a reason to take pride and care in their work.

The process of preparing a skin was labor-intensive. After removal of the animal’s skin, the hide was stretched on a frame, the flesh side scraped to remove fat and tissue, and the skin dried. Women often softened it by working it with animal fats or brains and smoking it. Beaver robes were then worn, usually with the fur side toward the body, and over a season the long guard hairs fell away from friction and the leather became supple. The French distinguished such worn skins from unworn ones, and English traders at Hudson Bay later used the terms “coat beaver” and “parchment beaver,” the latter for dry, unworn skins.

This practice had three effects. First, it meant that furs entering the Atlantic trade had already been transformed by Indigenous labor and use, so that a significant part of the value added in North America was created in Indigenous households. Second, it limited how fast production could expand: a skin could not simply be produced on demand. Third, it gave Indigenous consumers a reason to value robes of fur for their own use, since a robe worn for a season could be traded afterward at a better price. Merchants, who needed what hatmakers wanted, had to adapt to Indigenous clothing practices. Whether Indigenous people also deliberately adjusted the way they wore or prepared skins in response to merchants’ stated preferences is plausible but difficult to document for the seventeenth century.

Other hides followed similar patterns. Moose and deer skins, dressed as leather, were used for moccasins, leggings, and bags, and were traded within Indigenous networks and to Europeans, particularly where beaver had become scarcer. Skill in dressing a skin determined its quality, and communities developed reputations for particular products.

Section 46.12

Canoes, Portages, Provisions, and Long-Distance Transport

Transport linked producers to markets and was among the most demanding elements of the system. The birchbark canoe, light enough to carry across portages and strong enough to carry heavy loads, was developed by Algonquian-speaking and neighboring peoples and was adopted by the French as the principal vehicle for inland trade. Building and repairing canoes required specialized materials and knowledge, including bark, spruce root, and resin, and the work was usually shared among men and women. Wooden dugouts and sea-going vessels served other routes, but the bark canoe was central to the northern trade.

Journeys across the interior required provisioning. Travelers needed food, and the communities along the routes were often their suppliers. Portages, where canoes and cargo had to be carried overland, were points of vulnerability and control; communities living at rapids or river junctions could exact payments or hospitality from passing parties. Guides who knew the waterways were indispensable, and French and later English traders depended on Indigenous guides and paddlers, since the geography could not be mastered without local knowledge. Travel was also seasonal: rivers froze, and spring thaws determined when parties could depart.

Transport costs shaped both prices and political relationships. The farther furs had to travel, the more the final price reflected labor and risk, and those who controlled the intermediate links could negotiate for a share. This is why changes in routes, such as the redirection of northern trade toward Hudson Bay discussed in Section 46.25, could alter bargaining positions across a wide region.

The bark canoe’s advantages were weight and repairability. It could be lifted by one or two people at portages, drew little water, and could be mended with bark, spruce root, and resin gathered along the way. French observers adopted it quickly. Champlain traveled in Indigenous canoes, and Jesuits and traders followed his example. French carpenters could not easily replicate the craft, and the canoes used in the western trade were largely built by Indigenous craftspeople, often women and men working together.

Chokepoints mattered. On the Ottawa River, the Long Sault and the Chaudière falls required portages, and Allumette Island stood at a central point of the route. At Sault Ste. Marie, Indigenous fishing communities gathered at the rapids. At such places, travelers needed permission, local help, and often food. The geography created many opportunities for local peoples to demand gifts or payment, and for Europeans who wished to avoid paying, there were few alternatives. When warfare made these routes unsafe, as occurred on the Ottawa in the 1640s and later, the flow of furs slowed, and merchants in Quebec and Montreal felt it in their stores.

On Hudson Bay, canoe travel followed rivers draining into James Bay and the western bay, with seasonal rhythms determined by ice. Journeys inland and back could take weeks, and the route’s difficulty favored the use of intermediaries who knew the country. The costs of transport were thus carried primarily by Indigenous traders, a fact that shaped the Hudson’s Bay Company’s strategy of waiting at the coast.

Section 46.13

Exchange Rates, Gifts, Bargaining, and Commercial Negotiation

Exchanges followed norms that European observers described but often misunderstood. Trading sessions usually opened with speeches, the exchange of gifts, and ceremonial smoking or feasting, and the actual bargaining followed. Prices were negotiated, not simply imposed, and Indigenous traders compared offers, rejected goods they considered poor, and sometimes withdrew to trade elsewhere. Documented rates exist for certain times and places, but they varied with supply, quality, competition, and relationships, and this chapter does not present a single standard price list because the sources do not support one.

Valuation was relational. A buyer who honored obligations, gave generous presents, and behaved as a good partner could expect better treatment than one who did not. European merchants who tried to impose fixed prices found that Indigenous partners responded by changing suppliers or by raising demands for gifts. The line between gift and payment was blurred, and what a merchant regarded as a cost of doing business could be understood by his partners as the maintenance of an alliance.

Bargaining power depended on the availability of alternatives. When several buyers competed, as in the Hudson Valley after the opening of the Dutch trade or later when the French and English competed for the northern trade, Indigenous sellers could secure better terms. When only one buyer was accessible, sellers had less leverage, although they could still refuse to trade or reduce their supply.

Observers described a ritual structure in trade meetings that included feasts, speeches, the giving of presents, and the smoking of tobacco. The French term for the feasts, tabagie, appears frequently in the Jesuit and other sources. A trader who failed to participate properly risked offending his partners. The structure enabled each side to assess the other’s intentions and to reaffirm obligations, and it often preceded the actual exchange of goods, which was negotiated separately and sometimes over several days.

Evidence that Indigenous traders compared offers is abundant, though almost always filtered through European complaints. Colonial officials in New France and New Netherland reported that Indigenous traders knew what was offered at different posts and demanded matching terms, and French authorities in the later seventeenth century repeatedly noted that Indigenous traders could obtain goods more cheaply at Albany than at Montreal. These reports must be read as statements of European worry as well as evidence of Indigenous knowledge. They nonetheless confirm that bargaining involved information and alternatives.

Prices were not stable. Terms changed with the season, with the number of buyers present, and with the quality of what was on offer. A trader with a large surplus might accept less, while a shortage of goods at a post enabled Indigenous traders to demand more. The sources do not support a single schedule of prices for the whole period, and any figure given in popular accounts should be traced to a specific document, place, and year.

Section 46.14

Trading Posts, Merchant Accounts, Monopolies, and Credit

European merchants tried to organize trade through posts, ledgers, and regulations. Posts such as Fort Orange, Tadoussac, and later the Hudson Bay factories gave merchants a fixed location, storage, and some security, and they enabled the keeping of accounts. Merchants measured goods by standard units, counted skins, and recorded exchanges, and companies attempted to monitor their agents to limit private trade and theft. Such bookkeeping provides some of our best evidence for the trade but also reflects the commercial concerns of the people who kept it.

Monopolies, credit, and debt require careful treatment for the seventeenth century. Chartered companies sought exclusive rights, but as earlier sections show, those rights governed relations among Europeans rather than Indigenous traders. Credit existed: merchants sometimes advanced goods to trading partners in expectation of later payment, and Indigenous traders sometimes extended credit to Europeans in the form of gifts and supplies. But the elaborate system of advances and recorded debts associated with the eighteenth- and nineteenth-century trade should not be projected backward onto the early seventeenth century, when exchanges were more often made at the moment of meeting.

Merchants’ efforts to control transactions were limited by their dependence. They could not compel Indigenous partners to trade, and when they tried to restrict access to certain goods or to dictate prices, they often failed. The distinction between a company’s legal claim to a monopoly and its practical ability to enforce it is among the main themes of Division V.

Posts varied in function. Fort Orange was a fortified warehouse and residence for company employees in which trade with Indigenous visitors was conducted, and a civilian commercial community, Beverwijck, grew around it. French posts such as Tadoussac and Quebec had magazines in which goods were stored and from which they were distributed, and merchants’ agents recorded transactions. The first Hudson’s Bay Company post, Charles Fort at Rupert River, was followed by others at Moose, Albany, and later Nelson, with accounts sent back to a committee in London. These records provide evidence for goods and quantities, but the survival of early post journals is partial.

The Company’s organization in London was a joint-stock enterprise governed by a committee that gave instructions to captains and post managers. Its servants were forbidden to trade privately, but private trade occurred in all three systems, and enforcement was difficult. Dutch company employees, French agents, and English servants all had incentives to trade for themselves when opportunities arose. Historians have also noted that merchants’ complaints about private trade indicate how much commercial activity escaped official record.

Regarding credit, historians such as Ray have shown that by the eighteenth century the Hudson’s Bay Company used advances of goods to hunters who would repay with furs, with debts recorded in company books. There is little evidence that such an institutionalized system existed in the early seventeenth century. For the earlier period, the most defensible description is of exchanges negotiated at meetings, with limited and varying credit and with merchants unable to compel repayment.

Historical Vocabulary

Trade Monopoly, Credit, Price, and Bargaining

A monopoly was an exclusive legal right to trade, granted by a government to a company or individual. Credit is the advance of goods or money against future payment. Price is the rate at which one good is exchanged for another, and in this period it was often negotiated rather than fixed. Bargaining refers to the process of negotiating terms.

Potential misunderstanding: a legal monopoly over Europeans did not mean a company could set terms for Indigenous partners, who could take their goods elsewhere. And credit in the seventeenth century was not the later institutionalized debt system.

Section 46.15

Trade Goods and the Transformation of Indigenous Consumption

The goods Europeans offered included iron and steel tools, copper and brass kettles, woven cloth, glass beads, mirrors, tobacco, alcohol, and firearms. Indigenous buyers evaluated these goods by their utility, quality, and appeal. Metal kettles were durable, did not break when heated directly, and could be cut up and reworked into ornaments and tools; metal axes and knives sharpened work that had previously been done with stone and bone. Cloth was lighter than many hides, accepted dye readily, and could be tailored into garments. Beads were incorporated into existing traditions of decoration that used shell, quills, and other materials.

Indigenous consumers were active and discriminating. Traders requested specific colors, patterns, and sizes, rejected inferior goods, and affected what merchants supplied. Archaeological evidence confirms this: sites yield kettles that were cut into pieces and made into new objects, as well as lead cloth seals from textile bales, which show how important imported cloth became in Indigenous markets. Imported goods were adapted to existing aesthetic and ceremonial preferences, which means that they did not simply replace older material culture but entered into it. Kettles, for instance, were sometimes placed in graves as valued possessions.

The transformation of consumption occurred unevenly. Some communities acquired many European goods early, others few; some goods supplemented existing tools, while others replaced them. The effects on skills, such as stone-tool production or pottery making, varied, and any assumption that imported goods immediately produced dependency oversimplifies the evidence. Section 46.30 returns to the question of when reliance became a vulnerability.

Metal goods spread because they met needs. Brass and copper kettles were prized because they could be placed on a fire and carried; many were later cut up and the metal reworked into projectile points, tubular beads, bracelets, and tinkling ornaments, as archaeological finds at sites in the Northeast show. Iron axes and knives made woodworking and butchering faster. Fishhooks, awls, and needles were small items with large practical value. Glass beads of various colors and shapes appear in graves and village sites, and archaeologists have used their types to help date sites.

Firearms and alcohol were different cases. Guns were sought for hunting and war, but they required powder, lead, and flints, which Indigenous communities could not produce, and the early weapons were slow and unreliable compared with bows in some situations. The sale of brandy grew in the middle decades of the century and became controversial. Jesuit missionaries opposed it, Bishop François de Laval threatened excommunication of those who sold liquor to Indigenous people in 1660, and a royal inquiry in Quebec in 1678 heard divided testimony. Some Indigenous leaders complained of the violence that drinking produced, while others bought liquor, and French merchants argued that without it Indigenous traders would go to the English. The episode shows that consumption was contested within Indigenous communities as well as between them and Europeans.

The archaeological record shows that in some Northeast sites, chipped-stone tools declined as metal increased, but pottery and other traditional products continued for decades. Consumption did not simply replace one tradition with another. It added choices, and the choices individuals made reflected status, need, and taste.

For the precise wording, consult the Thwaites edition of the Jesuit Relations, volume VI, pages 296–299.

Analysis: The passage suggests that Indigenous people saw the exchange in terms of what the beaver provided and, with humor, that they were aware of European goods’ importance. It also shows Jesuit interest in Indigenous attitudes toward the trade.

Limitations: It is a missionary’s retelling, translated and shaped for a European audience, and the humor may be partly lost. It cannot be used to claim that all Indigenous people held the same view.

Perspective: French Jesuit; the Innu speaker is filtered through the missionary. Missing are women, and traders from other communities.

Division IV · Sections 46.16–46.20

Women, Households, Kinship, and Economic Labor

Section 46.16 · Division IV — Women, Households, Kinship, and Economic Labor

Indigenous Women and the Processing of Fur and Hides

In many Indigenous societies of the Northeast, Great Lakes, and subarctic, women were responsible for processing skins: scraping, stretching, softening, smoking, and sewing. The evidence for this comes from European observers, from ethnographic accounts, and from Indigenous oral history, and it must be used with regional care, because the division of labor was not identical everywhere and varied by community, season, and circumstance. Where documented, women’s work in hide preparation was essential to the value of what was traded.

Women also made decisions about the use and distribution of the products of their labor. In some communities they controlled the household’s stores of hides and food, determined what was given in feasts and gifts, and held authority over agricultural produce. Such authority did not mean equal political power in every setting, but it gave women a role in the economic life of their communities that European accounts, written by men with different expectations, tended to obscure. Women’s work was a precondition of the trade, not a supplement to it.

The expansion of the fur trade increased the demand for women’s labor in some places. More skins required more dressing, and prepared robes, moccasins, and clothing were themselves traded. Historians disagree about whether this change enhanced or reduced women’s autonomy, and the answer likely varied. The sources suggest that women gained influence where their skills were in demand and where kinship systems gave them authority, but also that heavier workloads placed burdens on them, as Section 46.20 considers.

Evidence for women’s work comes from the same European observers who recorded trade, and it carries their assumptions. Sagard and the Jesuits described Wendat women as cultivators who controlled fields and food stores; Le Jeune described Innu women who dressed skins, set up and moved camps, and prepared food while men hunted. Among the Haudenosaunee, women’s role in agriculture and kinship gave clan matrons political influence, a subject treated in Chapter 26. Evidence from the subarctic, where women made snowshoe netting, processed hides, and often participated in trapping small animals, derives largely from later sources and should be applied to the seventeenth century with care.

Women’s labor affected value at specific points. A well-dressed skin brought better terms, and the garments, moccasins, and bags women made were themselves objects of exchange. Women also managed the distribution of stored foods and goods within households and, in some societies, between kin groups. These responsibilities meant that men could not acquire trade goods without women’s work, and that women’s agreement was often required for the use of a household’s resources.

Whether women also traded directly with Europeans in this period is less well documented, though later sources from posts show women trading country provisions and crafted goods. For the seventeenth century, the safest statement is that women were decisive in production and often influential in consumption and distribution, while the sources rarely record their own accounts.

Section 46.17

Cloth, Sewing, Dress, and Women’s Decisions as Consumers

Imported cloth, needles, awls, and beads were taken up by Indigenous women and incorporated into clothing and decoration. Woolen cloth, such as the duffel cloth that Dutch and later English traders supplied, was cut and sewn into blankets, leggings, and mantles; metal needles and awls simplified sewing; and glass beads were used alongside quills, shell, and paint. The result was a new material culture that drew on both older techniques and new materials, and that expressed identity and status through style.

Because women made and often owned clothing and household textiles, their preferences affected the demand for particular goods. Traders reported that Indigenous buyers asked for specific colors, widths, and qualities of cloth, and merchants adjusted their stock accordingly. The historical record rarely names individual women as consumers, but the pattern of demand, together with ethnographic evidence about who made clothing, indicates that their judgment mattered. Skill in sewing and decoration conferred standing, and a family’s appearance carried social meaning.

These developments show that Indigenous people did not adopt European goods passively. They selected, altered, and combined them with local traditions. A bead worked into a pattern of quillwork or a cloth edged with ribbon belonged to a continuing aesthetic tradition even though its materials came from across the Atlantic.

Cloth entered Indigenous economies in several forms. Dutch traders supplied duffel, a coarse woolen fabric, and English and French merchants offered other woolens. Indigenous people adopted the mantle, often called a matchcoat in English sources, which was made from a length of cloth worn over the shoulders. Cloth was prized because it was warm, relatively light, quick to work, and took bright dyes. It did not replace dressed skins, which remained useful, but it provided an alternative that could be obtained through trade without the labor of dressing.

Women’s craftsmanship transformed imported materials. Glass beads, ribbons, and thread were incorporated into quillwork, painted designs, and embroidery in patterns that expressed community and personal identity. Metal needles and awls simplified sewing. Because decoration communicated status, kinship, and spiritual protection, the choice of what to buy and how to use it was a form of social decision. Indigenous traders’ demands for particular colors, as recorded in merchants’ complaints, therefore reflect aesthetic and social judgments, many of which were likely made by or in consultation with women who would work the cloth.

European officials also used clothing in diplomacy. Gifts of coats and cloth to leaders were part of alliance-making and were intended to signal rank. Indigenous leaders accepted such gifts within their own understanding of reciprocity, and the circulation of cloth thus connected household consumption to politics.

Section 46.18

Food Production, Provisioning, and the Survival of the Trading System

The fur trade rested on food. Trading parties, canoe crews, and the Europeans stationed at posts all had to eat, and in the seventeenth century most of that food came from Indigenous producers. The Wendat and other agricultural peoples supplied maize, beans, and squash, which were also carried on long journeys as dried or parched corn. Northern peoples supplied dried meat, fish, and, in the Great Lakes region, wild rice. Preserved foods, such as pemmican in later periods in the interior, made long-distance travel possible, though the evidence for pemmican belongs largely to later centuries and should not be projected back.

This dependence meant the trade was more than an exchange of pelts for manufactured goods. French settlements along the Saint Lawrence were often short of food, and inland travelers depended on Indigenous hospitality and sale of provisions. The Hudson’s Bay Company posts later relied on Indigenous hunters for geese, fish, and game. Those who produced food, often women in agricultural communities, thereby held a commercial and political advantage. The survival of European posts depended on Indigenous decisions to provision them.

Food also entered the social fabric of exchange. Feasts opened and closed negotiations, and the quality of hospitality was noticed. A host who could provide abundantly demonstrated resources and a capacity to sustain allies. In this sense provisioning was both an economic activity and a diplomatic one, and the success of European merchants depended on their ability to take part in it.

The Wendat illustrate the importance of agricultural surplus. Sagard and the Jesuits describe extensive cornfields around Wendat towns, with maize stored in large quantities, and report that the Wendat traded corn to northern neighbors and sold or gave it to the French. Women did most of the work of cultivating and storing it, which meant that the agricultural basis of Wendat commerce rested on women’s labor. The French settlement at Quebec, which was repeatedly short of food and nearly starved after the English capture in 1629, depended on Indigenous supplies as well as shipments from France.

In the Hudson Valley, Van den Bogaert’s journal records his being fed and housed in Mohawk and Oneida villages, indicating that Indigenous hospitality made Dutch travel possible. In the Great Lakes, communities that harvested wild rice, fished, and cultivated gardens supplied travelers, though written evidence for these arrangements becomes fuller after the period of this chapter. At Hudson Bay, the early posts depended largely on provisions shipped from England but supplemented them by hunting and by purchases from Indigenous hunters, and supply difficulties and scurvy troubled them. Later records stress the importance of Indigenous goose and fish provisioning, a pattern that should not be assumed for the 1670s without evidence.

Provisioning gave Indigenous suppliers influence beyond price. A community that declined to feed a trading party or garrison could cause it great hardship, and a community that supplied it generously gained claims to reciprocity. The trade was thus supported by a food economy in which women’s agricultural and preservation skills were central, a fact that the focus on pelts in merchants’ records often hides.

Everyday Life and Social History

Women’s Work in a Trading Community

In a Wendat town, a Mohawk village, or a Cree and Anishinaabe seasonal camp, women’s labor supported the trade at every point: they planted and harvested crops in agricultural communities, prepared skins, sewed clothing, repaired canoes with spruce root and resin, and managed stores of food and goods. Evidence for these roles comes from European observers, ethnographic sources, and oral history, and practices differed by place.

These tasks sustained the trade directly. Corn grown by women fed traveling parties; skins dressed by women were worth more to buyers; goods that women wanted influenced what traders carried. Treating women’s work as a background to the male-dominated trade misreads how the system functioned.

Section 46.19

Marriage, Kinship, and the Social Foundations of Exchange

Trade in Indigenous societies was embedded in social relationships. Kinship, whether by blood, marriage, or adoption, created obligations that made exchange possible and that provided trust over long distances. Indigenous communities extended these practices to Europeans, offering adoption, ritual kinship, and, in some cases, marriage, which gave outsiders a recognized place in a community and bound them to its norms. A trader who was adopted became a relative with duties as well as privileges.

Unions between Indigenous women and European traders occurred in many places and took various forms. They could secure trade relationships, provide traders with language and cultural expertise, and give Indigenous families access to European goods. They also created children who grew up between communities. The experiences of the partners varied: some relationships were lasting and recognized by both communities, and others were brief or exploitative. This chapter does not trace the later development of distinct Métis communities, which belongs to Chapter 52. For the seventeenth century, the point is that personal relationships were not incidental to commerce but an important means of conducting it.

Kinship also structured relationships among Indigenous peoples. Marriage alliances, shared clan membership, and reciprocal obligations affected who traded with whom and on what terms. The economic and political dimensions of these relationships cannot be separated, and European merchants who understood this had an advantage over those who did not.

Both Indigenous and European actors used kinship to build commercial relationships. Champlain’s practice of placing young Frenchmen, such as Étienne Brûlé in 1610, with Indigenous hosts to learn languages and customs created persons who could interpret and mediate, though also persons whose loyalties were complicated. From the Indigenous viewpoint, hosting a young man bound his family to the visitor’s people. The practice shows a mutual understanding that trust required personal ties.

Kinship terms entered diplomacy. French governors were addressed by the Haudenosaunee and others as Onontio, and Dutch and later English governors of New York were called Corlaer, after the Dutch official Arent van Curler. The terms framed political relationships in family language and carried expectations of care and protection. Chapters 45 and 56 treat them in detail. For commerce, they meant that a trader who considered himself a supplier was often regarded by Indigenous partners as a kinsman, with obligations to provide help in time of need.

Intermarriage and cohabitation varied. Indigenous families sometimes arranged unions to secure advantageous relationships, and women who married traders could acquire influence as interpreters and mediators. Other relationships were short-lived or coercive. The documentation for the early decades at Hudson Bay is thin, and the fuller story of family formation there belongs to later periods; this chapter does not project it back.

Section 46.20

Household Economies, Seasonal Labor, and Unequal Burdens

Participation in the trade altered the organization of household work. Trapping, preparing skins, transporting goods, growing food, caring for children, and maintaining social obligations all required labor, and the distribution of that labor between men and women, and among households, changed in response to market demand. Where men spent more time trapping or traveling, women often took on more agricultural, processing, and household work. Where agriculture and trapping seasons overlapped, difficult choices followed.

The benefits were distributed unevenly. Households with skilled trappers, large networks of kin, or positions at strategic points could gain more than others, and leaders who controlled access to trade goods could use them to reward followers. Imported goods could enhance prestige, but they could also create tension within communities. At the same time, the trade provided tools that eased labor in some ways, such as metal kettles and knives, even as it added new demands in others.

Historians should avoid equating commercial participation with either prosperity or dependency. Some communities used the trade to strengthen their political and economic position; others found their labor reorganized in ways that increased risk. The evidence for individual households is thin, and conclusions must be drawn cautiously from regional patterns.

The household was the unit in which these demands were balanced. Trapping took men away for long periods; women remained responsible for children, camp management, and food processing; and where agriculture was practiced, planting and harvest set limits on travel. When men’s absence increased, women’s work in the village or camp increased, and Jesuit and other observers recorded the heavy burdens that women bore in carrying loads and building shelters. The introduction of kettles and iron tools could reduce some labor, but expanded skin processing and trade preparation added others.

Archaeology offers one indication of inequality. Burials at some Northeast sites contain unusually large quantities of European goods, suggesting that wealth and prestige were unevenly distributed, though interpretations vary, and burial goods express ritual choices as well as possessions. Leaders and kin groups who controlled access to trade goods could gain authority by redistributing them, while others had fewer opportunities. Epidemic disease, which struck many communities in this period (Chapter 40), reduced the labor available and could magnify these pressures, complicating attempts to attribute outcomes to trade alone.

Historical Debate

Opportunity, Coercion, and Changing Economic Power

The question: Did participation in the fur trade primarily expand Indigenous peoples’ options and power in the seventeenth century, or did it set in motion unequal relationships that limited them?

Interpretation A — Opportunity and partnership: Scholars in the tradition of work on Indigenous participation emphasize that Indigenous peoples acted as skilled traders, selected goods that suited them, and used competition among Europeans to their advantage. On this view the trade extended Indigenous economic and political reach, at least for a time.
Interpretation B — Unequal incorporation: Other scholars stress that the trade linked Indigenous communities to markets they could not control, exposed them to disease, intensified competition and warfare, and over time reduced their options as animal populations declined and colonial settlement grew.

Evidence: Company and missionary records, archaeological evidence of consumption, and Indigenous oral history. Points of disagreement: mainly interpretation and chronology, since the evidence varies by region and decade, and the two views are not mutually exclusive.

Why it matters: The answer affects whether Indigenous history in this period is told as one of agency, of loss, or, more accurately, of both.

Division V · Sections 46.21–46.26

European Commercial Competition and the Hudson Bay System

Section 46.21 · Division V — European Commercial Competition and the Hudson Bay System

French Trading Companies, Monopolies, and Colonial Regulation

The French Crown repeatedly granted trading monopolies to companies in exchange for obligations to settle colonists and support missions. The Company of One Hundred Associates, chartered in 1627, held rights over the trade of New France until the 1660s, and subsequent arrangements gave the Crown more direct authority. The French also taxed beaver and tried to regulate who could trade in the interior, including through a system of licenses, called congés, introduced in 1681, which allowed a limited number of authorized trading expeditions.

Legal claims to control were difficult to enforce. The interior was vast, Indigenous traders could choose their buyers, and many Frenchmen traded without licenses, a practice that earned them the name coureurs de bois. Officials in the colony and in France disagreed about whether to allow or restrict inland trade, with some arguing that the trade should be concentrated at Montreal and Quebec and others that it should go to the interior to meet Indigenous partners. Indigenous traders benefited from this uncertainty.

By the mid-1690s, an oversupply of beaver relative to European demand led the Crown to curtail the trade; in May 1696 it revoked the congés and ordered most interior posts closed, a policy difficult to enforce amid Indigenous alliances, merchant interests, and continued unauthorized trade. The episode shows that colonial regulation responded to European market conditions and to the limits of French power in the interior, and that Indigenous partners could be affected by decisions taken in distant capitals.

Royal policy tied the trade to state revenue and to the colony’s survival. The Company of One Hundred Associates, created under Cardinal Richelieu, fell far short of its settlement obligations and was disrupted by the English capture of Quebec in 1629. In 1645 trading privileges passed to the Company of Habitants, a body of colonists, and after 1663 the Crown governed directly. Under Jean-Baptiste Colbert the Crown established the Domaine d’Occident, a system of taxes on colonial goods including beaver, which gave the royal treasury a stake in the trade’s volume.

Regulation was complicated by policy disagreements. Governor Frontenac and his supporters favored interior expansion, including the establishment of Fort Frontenac on Lake Ontario in 1673, as a way to secure trade and Indigenous alliances. Missionaries and some officials preferred to keep the trade concentrated in the colony’s settlements, where Indigenous visitors could be supervised and where spirits could be controlled. The congé system introduced in 1681 allowed a limited number of licensed expeditions to the interior each year, but unlicensed traders continued to operate, and the Crown’s efforts to restrain them had limited success.

The 1696 revocation had consequences beyond the colony’s warehouses. Indigenous allies in the Great Lakes, who traded and fought alongside the French, objected strongly to the closing of posts and the loss of supplies, and within a few years the policy was reversed, most visibly through the establishment of Detroit in 1701. The episode shows the gap between metropolitan decisions based on European markets and conditions in Indigenous territory, where the French could not abandon allies without cost.

Section 46.22

English Competition and the Transformation of the Hudson Valley Market

In 1664 an English fleet took New Netherland; the adjacent Dutch settlement of Beverwijck was renamed Albany, while Fort Orange came to be known as Fort Albany. The Dutch regained the colony briefly in 1673 and lost it again under the settlement of 1674. The change of European ruler did not abruptly end the commercial relationships centered at Albany. Many Dutch traders remained, the town retained its character, and Indigenous partners, among them the Mohawk, continued to trade there. English authorities sought to preserve the trade as a source of revenue and as a basis for relationships with neighboring Indigenous peoples.

For Indigenous partners the key question was whether the new regime would supply the goods they wanted at acceptable terms and maintain relationships of trust. They continued to evaluate the offerings of different buyers, including those of the French to the north, and the existence of competing markets strengthened their position. The diplomatic arrangements that developed in the following decades, discussed in Chapter 56, grew in part out of this commercial context.

The English conquest also tied the Hudson Valley market to the wider English Atlantic economy, introducing different goods and merchants and different regulatory ideas. Chapter 48 considers the broader colonial history of the English Atlantic colonies; here the emphasis is on the continuity of commercial institutions beneath political change.

After the English conquest, Fort Orange was renamed Fort Albany, and the town of Beverwijck became Albany. The Dutch population remained, and traders in Albany continued to hold the commercial networks developed in the Dutch period. English governors such as Richard Nicolls and later Edmund Andros sought to secure the loyalty of the Mohawk and other Haudenosaunee, in part through gifts and the maintenance of fair dealing at Albany, an effort whose diplomatic dimensions are treated in Chapter 56.

Albany’s merchants sought to protect their position. In 1686, under Governor Thomas Dongan, the town received a charter that confirmed privileges for its traders in the Indigenous trade, which gave local merchants an advantage over others in the province. The arrangement shows how commercial institutions persisted and changed under new rule. It also affected Indigenous partners, who dealt with a relatively concentrated group of merchants rather than a free market.

The Albany market competed with Montreal. French officials complained throughout the later seventeenth century that English goods, especially woolen cloth, were cheaper and that Indigenous traders took furs to Albany as a result. Albany traders, for their part, were interested in obtaining furs from the Great Lakes and drew on Haudenosaunee carriers. This competition, which touched French fears of the loss of their western allies, ranged beyond commerce into diplomacy and war, topics treated in Chapter 47.

Section 46.23

The Hudson’s Bay Company and the Royal Charter of 1670

The Hudson’s Bay Company was formed after an English voyage in 1668 and 1669 on the ship Nonsuch returned from James Bay with furs. Médard Chouart des Groseilliers sailed on that voyage, and the party built a post, Charles Fort, at the Rupert River. Investors, including members of the court and Prince Rupert, a cousin of King Charles II, obtained a royal charter on May 2, 1670, creating the Governor and Company of Adventurers of England trading into Hudson’s Bay. The charter granted the company exclusive trading rights and proprietary claims over the lands draining into Hudson Bay, which came to be called Rupert’s Land.

The charter is important and must be read carefully. It represents what the English Crown claimed to grant to an English company; it did not reflect the consent of the Indigenous peoples who lived in the region, who were neither consulted nor mentioned as parties. The grant covered an enormous territory in which the Cree, Assiniboine, Ojibwe, Dene, and other peoples lived and in which the company had at first only a few posts on the coast. The legal claim and the practical reality differed greatly, and the legal significance of the charter was debated in the centuries that followed.

In its early decades the company depended on coastal posts, on ships arriving from England each summer, and on Indigenous traders who brought furs to the bay. It had to compete with French traders operating from the Saint Lawrence and the Great Lakes, and in the 1680s and 1690s the two sides fought for control of posts on the bay. The company’s formal monopoly gave it advantages against English rivals, but not against French or Indigenous alternatives.

The charter’s preamble and clauses reveal the English Crown’s assumptions. The document presents the enterprise as a search for a northwest passage as well as a trading venture, grants the Company exclusive rights to trade in lands whose waters flow into Hudson Bay, and authorizes it to hold those lands as proprietors, to govern, to build forts, and to make laws. The territory was defined by a geographical formula, not by knowledge of its inhabitants or boundaries, and it was limited to places not already possessed by another Christian prince. The charter thus defined rights among Europeans.

The first governor was Prince Rupert, a cousin of the king, and the investors included courtiers and merchants. In the first years the Company established Charles Fort on the Rupert River, then posts at Moose and Albany in the 1670s, and, in the 1680s, a post at the Nelson River. The French reacted by establishing their own Compagnie du Nord in 1682 and, in 1686, by sending an overland expedition under the Chevalier de Troyes, with Pierre Le Moyne d’Iberville, that captured several bay posts. The competition continued until the end of the century and was only partly settled by European treaties.

The charter’s legacy is long. The Company’s claim to Rupert’s Land remained the foundation of its position until it surrendered the territory to the Crown in 1869–1870 for transfer to Canada, an event that occurred without treaties with most of the people living there, a subject examined in Chapters 82 and 83. For the seventeenth century, the point is that the charter reflected the Crown’s ambitions but was not the effective authority of the region, where Cree and other peoples determined who could travel, trade, and settle.

Section 46.24

Cree Commercial Power and the Hudson and James Bay Trading Posts

The Cree, whose homelands extended around James Bay and across the boreal forest to the west, were among the most important partners of the Hudson’s Bay Company in its first decades. Cree communities traveled to the bay to trade and acted as intermediaries who carried goods inland to peoples who did not come to the coast. Later company records describe some Cree groups living near the posts and supplying them with food and furs; this later pattern should not be projected without qualification onto the 1670s and 1680s, but it points to the economic roles Cree communities developed.

Other peoples, including the Assiniboine, took part in a trading network that linked the bay with the interior. Intermediaries bought goods at the posts and exchanged them with distant peoples, often at a profit and with attention to the preferences of their customers. They also provided information about the interior and control of access to it, which made them important to the company’s understanding of the country. The company’s officers were aware of their dependence, and records show that they tried to maintain good relations with Indigenous leaders.

The Cree also shaped the terms of trade. They evaluated the quality and price of goods, expressed their wants, and traveled between competing markets when it suited them. A company that disappointed its trading partners could expect reduced supplies of furs, and posts that depended on Indigenous suppliers for food could find themselves in difficulty if relations soured. For these reasons the early history of the bay is a history of Cree commercial power.

The Cree, who spoke dialects of a language in the Algonquian family, lived across a wide area from James Bay west toward the plains. Those living near the bay and its rivers, now often called Moose Cree and Swampy Cree, became the Company’s earliest partners. In the early years, they carried furs to the posts by canoe after the spring thaw, obtained goods, and carried some of those goods inland to trade with groups that did not travel to the coast. This activity made the Cree intermediaries between the Company and a larger interior network.

The Company’s officers quickly learned that they depended on Indigenous help. Post journals and correspondence, as they survive, record the need to keep Indigenous leaders satisfied with gifts and trade, to understand preferences for goods, and to rely on Indigenous hunters for food. The Assiniboine, a Siouan-speaking people who lived farther west, also traveled to the bay and traded with the Cree. In 1690–1692, the Company servant Henry Kelsey traveled inland from York Fort accompanied by Assiniboine and Cree guides and reported on the country and peoples he met. His journey depended entirely on Indigenous knowledge and hospitality, and it illustrates the Company’s lack of independent access to the interior.

Chapter 34 treats the subarctic world of the Cree in broader terms. For this chapter, the important point is that the Company’s trade at the bay was an extension of existing Cree commercial practices, within which the Cree retained significant influence for decades. The Company’s monopoly meant little to Cree traders who could, in principle, carry furs to French merchants or to other peoples.

Section 46.25

Radisson, Des Groseilliers, and the Redirection of Northern Trade

Pierre-Esprit Radisson and Médard Chouart des Groseilliers were French traders whose careers show the interaction of Indigenous knowledge and European enterprise. They traveled in the Great Lakes region around 1659 and 1660 and returned to Montreal with a large cargo of furs obtained from Indigenous partners, after which colonial authorities penalized them for trading without proper permission. Dissatisfied, they sought backing elsewhere and eventually took their proposal for a route to the northern fur country by way of Hudson Bay to English investors.

Their idea was not simply a European discovery. Indigenous peoples had long known the northern waterways and the possibility of reaching the bay, and their information, as relayed to Europeans, drew attention to the area. Indigenous traders who carried furs to the bay, and Indigenous guides who knew the country, made the Hudson Bay trade possible. The pair’s role was to connect that knowledge to English capital, which they did in part because they were frustrated with the French system.

The redirection of northern trade toward Hudson Bay changed the geography of competition. Furs that had traveled by canoe to the Saint Lawrence could now be delivered to the bay, where ships arrived directly from England. This created choices for Indigenous suppliers and for the peoples who acted as intermediaries, and it placed French and English merchants in competition for the same sources.

Radisson’s life itself shows the interpenetration of societies. Captured by Mohawk in 1651 as a young man and adopted into a family, he later escaped and returned to New France, where he developed languages and relationships useful to trading ventures. In 1659–1660, he and his brother-in-law Groseilliers traveled to the western Great Lakes and returned to Montreal with a large load of furs in company with Indigenous traders. Colonial officials, however, penalized them for trading without proper authorization. This episode shows the tension between the colony’s regulatory ambitions and the opportunities available to entrepreneurs working with Indigenous partners.

The pair’s later reputation depends on Radisson’s own narratives, which survive in manuscripts published in the nineteenth century. Their chronology and details are disputed by scholars, and they should be treated as sources written to advance the author’s interests. They do indicate that Radisson and Groseilliers learned from Indigenous peoples of a northern sea and its access to the fur country, but exactly who told them what, and when, cannot be established with certainty from his account alone.

After failing to gain French backing, they found support in England. In 1668, the ships Nonsuch, with Groseilliers, and Eaglet, with Radisson, sailed for Hudson Bay; the Eaglet turned back, while the Nonsuch reached James Bay and returned in 1669 with furs. The success convinced investors to form the Company. Radisson and Groseilliers later moved between French and English service, which shows how mobile commercial talents were, and also how dependent their value was on the knowledge and connections they had acquired among Indigenous peoples.

Section 46.26

Inland Traders versus Coastal Posts: Comparing Commercial Systems

The French and English commercial systems in the north developed different strategies. The French system was oriented toward the interior: traders and missionaries traveled inland, Indigenous allies brought furs to Montreal, and the colony depended on long canoe routes and on the labor of canoemen. Costs of transport were high, and the system required complex management, but it kept the French in contact with Indigenous peoples across a wide area and tied the colony to a network of alliances.

The Hudson’s Bay Company’s early strategy was to establish posts on the coast and wait for Indigenous traders to come to them. Ships brought goods from England and carried furs back, and company employees remained at the posts. This approach reduced the company’s transport burden, since Indigenous traders carried furs inland-to-coast, but it left the company dependent on the willingness of Indigenous peoples to make the journey and on the strength of intermediaries. It also meant the company had less direct knowledge of the interior.

Indigenous choices linked the two systems. Traders could carry furs to the bay, to the Saint Lawrence, or in some cases to Albany, depending on the goods offered, the distances involved, relations with intermediaries, and political circumstances. The competition among buyers gave Indigenous sellers leverage, and each system adjusted in response, whether by lowering prices, offering better goods, or, in later periods, building inland posts. The formal monopoly of the Hudson’s Bay Company never removed these alternatives.

The comparison can be extended to include the Albany system. The French built a chain of dependencies in which Indigenous traders delivered furs to Montreal, with extensive support from Indigenous intermediaries and a heavy reliance on licensed and unlicensed French traders. Albany combined a relatively compact market town with long-distance connections through the Haudenosaunee. The Hudson’s Bay Company, by contrast, relied on ships from England to the bay and on Indigenous carriers to the coast. Each had its own cost structure, risks, and relationships with Indigenous suppliers.

The choices open to Indigenous traders depended on distance and geography. For the Cree, the bay was relatively close, and the Company offered goods they valued. For Great Lakes peoples, Montreal and Albany were possible destinations, and Detroit, after 1701, would become another. Indigenous traders weighed price, quality, reliability, safety of travel, and political relationships in deciding where to go. A dispute with one buyer, or the prospect of a military threat, could shift their destination. The French competitor’s response, noted above in the Compagnie du Nord, illustrates that these shifts affected European enterprise.

The Company’s coastal strategy had limits that became evident later. As competition intensified, the Company would eventually build inland posts, a development beyond the scope of this chapter. For the period to 1701, the central observation is that three different commercial systems all depended on Indigenous labor and decisions, and that Indigenous peoples had alternatives, which explains why European monopolies on paper never gave Europeans control over Indigenous sellers.

Section 46.27 · Division VI — Environmental Change, Political Power, and Material Transformation

The Beaver as an Ecological Engineer

Beavers modify their surroundings by building dams, lodges, and canals. Dams slow streams, create ponds, and raise local water tables, and the resulting wetlands support many other species, including fish, waterfowl, amphibians, and plants. When beavers abandon a site, the ponds may drain and leave meadows that later grow into forest. Beaver activity thus shapes landscapes and has long been recognized as a major influence on stream environments.

Removing beavers can therefore change more than the number of one commercial species. Where trapping eliminated colonies, dams were no longer maintained, ponds could dry out, and habitats that other animals and people used could disappear or change. Historians and ecologists use records, landscape studies, and sediment evidence to reconstruct these changes, though the scale of beaver-driven landscape change in North America before the trade and the effects of its removal remain subjects of research and some uncertainty.

Indigenous peoples knew beaver ecology intimately and used its habitats for hunting, fishing, and gathering. The ponds and meadows that beavers created were part of the landscape in which Indigenous communities lived. Changes in beaver populations thus affected resources beyond the pelt trade, although the sources seldom describe such effects in the voices of those who experienced them.

Ecologists describe the beaver as a keystone or ecosystem-engineering species because its dams change hydrology, vegetation, and habitat over large areas. A dam slows water, traps sediment, raises the water table, and creates ponds that persist for years. These ponds support fish, amphibians, waterfowl, and aquatic plants, and when a pond silts up or is abandoned, the resulting beaver meadow supports grasses and other plants that attract grazing animals. A stream with many beaver complexes can store water that would otherwise run off quickly.

The magnitude of beaver populations before the fur trade is uncertain. Estimates in the scientific literature vary enormously because they depend on assumptions about habitat and density, and no census exists. Historians therefore should not cite a single figure as established. What can be said is that beaver were widespread across the continent’s forested regions and that their ponds and meadows were common features of many landscapes. William Cronon’s study of colonial New England noted that English settlers made use of abandoned beaver meadows for hay, which indicates that earlier beaver activity had shaped the land they settled.

Where trapping removed beaver, dams decayed, ponds drained, and wetland habitat diminished. Pollen and sediment records, together with historical descriptions, give some information about these changes, but results vary by region, and the long-term effects depended on whether beaver later returned. For Indigenous communities, who hunted moose and waterfowl, fished, and gathered plants in beaver-modified landscapes, the loss of beaver could alter local resources in ways that went beyond the loss of a fur-bearing animal.

Historical Vocabulary

Beaver Ecology, Depletion, and Sustainability

Beaver ecology refers to the relationship between beavers and their environment, including their dams and ponds. Depletion means a decline in a local population caused by harvest, and it is not the same as extinction. Sustainability is the capacity of a harvest to continue without exhausting the resource.

Potential misunderstanding: sustainability is a modern concept; applying it to seventeenth-century Indigenous or European practices requires evidence of how people actually managed harvests. Neither Indigenous peoples nor Europeans can be assumed to have acted uniformly.

Section 46.28

Intensive Trapping, Local Depletion, and Changing Hunting Territories

Trapping pressure was not uniform. Regions closest to major trading posts and with high demand experienced earlier and heavier harvests, while distant areas remained little affected for decades. In parts of the Northeast, beaver had become scarce in some districts by the mid-seventeenth century according to contemporary reports, and traders and hunters looked to areas farther west and north. William Cronon’s study of New England ecology is a classic account of how colonial markets altered local environments, though its evidence concerns a particular region.

The movement of production toward new areas had political consequences. As beaver became scarce in some territories, communities had to travel farther, to negotiate access to the hunting grounds of others, or to dispute those grounds. This is one of several pressures, along with epidemics, alliance politics, and competition for intermediary positions, that contributed to conflict, a subject developed in Chapter 47. Historians disagree about how much depletion by itself drove those conflicts.

Claims of uniform and inevitable depletion should be avoided. Beaver populations responded to trapping intensity, habitat, disease, and local conditions, and in some regions numbers recovered after pressure eased. The more defensible conclusion is that trapping produced local and regional declines at different times and rates, and that Indigenous and European actors responded to these changes in ways that affected the geography of the trade.

Contemporary sources show regional unevenness. In the lower Hudson Valley and parts of New England, traders and officials remarked on scarcity within a few decades of sustained trading, while in the Great Lakes and far northern forests beaver remained abundant for much longer. Dutch records from the mid-seventeenth century mention declining beaver near Fort Orange, and sources suggest that the Mohawk increasingly relied on furs obtained from other peoples, though the extent of this shift is debated. These regional differences meant that the economic position of a community depended on local ecology as well as on its political relations.

As local supplies thinned, hunters had several options. They could travel farther, negotiate access to the territories of neighbors, trade for furs from others, or shift to other animals such as marten, otter, or moose. They also could dispute territories. Historians continue to argue about how central the depletion of local beaver was to the conflicts of the mid-century, a question treated in Chapter 47. The more persuasive accounts treat depletion as one pressure among several, along with epidemics, the need for firearms and goods, alliance structures, and mourning-war practices.

The harvest’s pattern also reflected Indigenous decisions. Where communities controlled access to their territories and could exclude outsiders, they could manage harvests, and where their control broke down, they could not. The evidence suggests that no single ecological outcome applied everywhere. In some regions beaver numbers recovered when hunting pressure moved elsewhere, while in others the loss was long-lasting. The task for historians is to identify these differences and avoid sweeping statements about uniform collapse.

Section 46.29

Firearms, Military Resources, and the Economic Foundations of Warfare

Access to firearms, powder, lead, and metal weapons could affect military and political relationships. European authorities varied in their policies: some tried to restrict the sale of guns to Indigenous people, some limited them to allies or converts, and others sold freely when competition made restriction impractical. Enforcement was inconsistent, and Indigenous peoples acquired guns from several sources, including Dutch, French, English, and Swedish traders. Firearms also required maintenance, ammunition, and skill, and a gun was not an automatic military advantage.

Trade shaped war indirectly as well. Metal tools and weapons, access to markets, and the revenue from furs enabled communities to equip warriors and to support alliances. But warfare in this period had multiple causes, including mourning obligations, political rivalries, disease losses, and disputes over territory, and economic competition cannot be isolated from them. Chapter 47 treats Haudenosaunee campaigns and the conflicts often called the Beaver Wars, and this section does not narrate them.

The key point is that commercial relations and military capacity were connected. Control of trade routes and sources of arms affected the balance among Indigenous peoples and between them and Europeans, and conflict could in turn disrupt trade, as the dispersal of the Wendat showed.

European governments’ gun policies varied and changed. French authorities in the early seventeenth century sought to restrict firearms to Indigenous allies and, in some periods, to Christian converts, in part because missionaries argued that it would encourage conversion. Dutch officials at times prohibited sales to Indigenous people, but private traders and the competition among merchants made enforcement unreliable. By the middle of the century, the Mohawk and other peoples had acquired guns in substantial quantities, and the Wendat and Algonquin allies of the French had as well. In the Delaware and Chesapeake regions, other European traders supplied the Susquehannock. No Indigenous people depended on a single supplier.

Archaeology shows the diffusion of firearms through gun parts, lead shot, and flints at Indigenous sites, and the changing balance of such finds helps date sites and trace the spread. Guns were used for hunting and war, and their effect on warfare was complex. Early weapons were heavy and slow to load, but they were powerful at close range and had a psychological effect, and they also required a continuing supply of powder and lead. Control of supply routes could therefore translate into military advantage, and sharing arms with allies could strengthen alliances.

The connection to the fur trade was therefore twofold. Furs were the means of buying weapons and goods, and competition for access to furs could contribute to conflict. But it would be a mistake to reduce the wars of the century to a struggle for beaver. Conflicts involved revenge and mourning obligations, political rivalries, the pressures of disease, and the search for captives, as Chapters 47 and 53 explain. This section’s task is only to outline the economic foundations that supported military capacity.

Section 46.30

Commercial Opportunity, Adaptation, and Growing Dependencies

Trade offered clear advantages. Indigenous communities acquired durable tools, cloth, and other goods that they valued, enhanced their prestige and influence, and secured diplomatic relationships. Many used the trade to strengthen their position, and the evidence of selection and adaptation shows that they did so deliberately.

It also created vulnerabilities. Reliance on imported metal tools and weapons made communities dependent on supplies of those goods and, where traditional technologies were abandoned, on the willingness of merchants to trade. Growing involvement in the market exposed communities to shifts in European prices and to wars that disrupted supply. Credit relationships, which became more important in later periods, increased exposure further. Depletion of animals and competition for hunting territory could limit alternatives.

The assumption that imported goods immediately produced dependency is not supported. Indigenous peoples continued to make and use older tools for generations, and many goods were integrated without displacing earlier ones. Dependency, where it developed, was gradual, uneven, and shaped by circumstances such as proximity to posts, the availability of competing buyers, and the condition of local resources. The seventeenth century was largely a period in which Indigenous communities had considerable room to maneuver.

The evidence of adaptation is strong. Indigenous communities used metal to make traditional objects with greater efficiency, and cloth and beads were absorbed into long-standing aesthetic practices. Leaders used imported goods to reward followers and strengthen alliances, and families that acquired goods gained prestige. In the Great Lakes and subarctic, trade opened possibilities for new commercial roles, as the Odawa and Cree show. These developments suggest that the trade, for a considerable time, enlarged Indigenous choice.

Vulnerabilities developed in several ways. Communities that stopped making certain tools relied on supplies of replacement goods, including powder and lead for guns. Wars between European powers, such as the Anglo-Dutch wars of the 1650s, 1660s, and 1670s, could interrupt shipping and the availability of goods. Warfare among Indigenous peoples could close routes and cut off supplies. Local depletion of beaver forced communities to seek new sources. In later periods, the growth of credit increased dependence further, but for the seventeenth century the evidence for debt is limited.

Historians who have emphasized dependency have often relied on later evidence or on models from other regions, while those who emphasize adaptation sometimes understate the cumulative effects of disease, depletion, and colonial growth. The most defensible reading is that dependency developed unevenly, was greater for communities near the main colonial settlements, and remained partial and reversible for many interior peoples through the century.

Section 46.31

Indigenous Diplomacy, Market Competition, and Political Leverage

Indigenous leaders used commercial relationships to pursue political aims. They could grant or withhold access to routes, trade selectively with rival Europeans, form alliances that included trading privileges, and refuse to supply a post that had angered them. Because European settlements depended on Indigenous goods and goodwill, such actions could have significant effects, and European officials tried to anticipate and respond to them. The full alliance history of the French is the subject of Chapter 45, and the Covenant Chain relationships in the Northeast are treated in Chapter 56.

Competition among Europeans enhanced this leverage. A community able to take furs to French, Dutch, or English buyers could play one against another, and Indigenous diplomats often understood European rivalries well. They also used the trade to maintain relations with other Indigenous peoples, for example by serving as intermediaries and sharing the profits through kinship networks.

Leverage was not unlimited. Epidemics, wars, and the growth of European populations eventually reduced Indigenous options in many places. But in the seventeenth century, control over supplies, routes, and relationships gave many Indigenous nations real bargaining power, and they exercised it consciously.

The Haudenosaunee provide examples of how commercial position supported diplomatic goals. Their location in the Mohawk Valley made them important to the Dutch and English at Albany, and they sought to ensure that their goods and their access to Albany were not circumvented. They also sought access to French trade and to beaver-rich territories, an interest that was one factor in their diplomacy with the French and with Indigenous neighbors, as Chapters 45 and 47 describe. The pursuit of peace and access to markets often went together.

In the Great Lakes, the Odawa and other peoples used their positions to resist French efforts to close the trade or to dictate terms. When the French moved to restrict interior trade in the 1690s, Indigenous allies pressed for the continuation of posts and for supplies, and their pressure contributed to the reversal of policy. The Cree and their neighbors at Hudson Bay had parallel leverage. Where several European buyers competed, Indigenous leaders could play them against one another. Where one dominated, they could still withhold furs or travel elsewhere.

These examples show that Indigenous leaders understood the connections among trade, alliance, and security. They also understood Europeans’ rivalries, and used them. The limits of leverage were set by the dangers of war, the loss of population to epidemic disease, and the growth of colonial settlement, which eventually undercut the bases of their bargaining power, a process traced in later chapters.

Section 46.32

The Wider Social and Environmental Costs of Commercial Expansion

The expansion of the trade brought costs alongside benefits. Competition for routes and hunting territories could lead to conflict among Indigenous communities, and European alcohol, introduced as a trade good, caused social problems that Indigenous leaders and missionaries both recognized and tried to address, although they differed in their analyses. Epidemic disease, discussed in Chapter 40, often traveled along trade routes, and gatherings at trading places could spread infection. Ecological disturbance accompanied trapping, as earlier sections showed.

The trade was also entangled with colonial expansion. The settlements that merchants established became centers of European population and, later, of land demands, and Indigenous partners sometimes found that the commercial relationships that had empowered them also brought neighbors who wanted their land. Missionaries accompanied the trade in some regions, and religious change followed, a subject treated in Chapter 54.

Sweeping claims that the fur trade destroyed Indigenous society are not supported by the evidence. Indigenous communities adapted, remained politically active, and in many regions maintained and extended their influence for generations. The documented costs were real, including disease, conflict, ecological change, and inequality, but they occurred unevenly and alongside continued Indigenous initiative.

Alcohol was one of the best-documented social costs. Missionaries, some Indigenous leaders, and some colonial officials attributed violence and disorder to liquor, while merchants argued that restriction would drive trade to competitors. Disputes over the question in New France in 1660 and 1678 illustrate the stakes. Indigenous responses were not uniform: some communities attempted to ban the trade in liquor at gatherings, while others pursued it. The conflict reveals the divergent interests within communities as well as between them and Europeans.

Disease followed the trade routes. Gatherings at trading places and the movement of canoe parties offered opportunities for infection, as Chapter 40 explains. The impact on communities was demographic and political, since the loss of leaders and elders disrupted the transmission of knowledge and the functioning of councils. Missionary activity, discussed in Chapter 54, accompanied the trade in many places and introduced religious change that also affected communities unevenly.

The trade also contributed indirectly to land loss. Trading posts became centers of colonial settlement, and the growth of European populations in the Saint Lawrence and Hudson valleys increased pressure on land, while the English expanded in the Atlantic colonies, a subject treated in Chapter 48. Yet the sources do not support the claim that the trade alone destroyed Indigenous societies. The documented costs were substantial, but they were interwoven with epidemic disease, warfare, and colonial policy, and Indigenous communities continued to adapt and to exercise influence.

Division VII · Sections 46.33–46.35

The Fur Trade as a Historical System

Section 46.33 · Division VII — The Fur Trade as a Historical System

North American Fur-Trade Systems at the Turn of the Eighteenth Century

By about 1700 several interconnected trading systems operated. The Saint Lawrence and Great Lakes system, centered on Montreal, drew on Indigenous suppliers across a wide area, including the Odawa, Wendat, and other Great Lakes peoples, and was strained by an oversupply of beaver in France and by French efforts to restrict the trade. The Hudson Valley system, centered on Albany, remained an important market for the Mohawk and other Haudenosaunee peoples. The Hudson Bay system, by now established with several posts, depended on the Cree and their trading partners.

These systems competed for Indigenous suppliers, and Indigenous traders continued to choose among them. In 1701 the Great Peace of Montreal ended a long period of conflict between the French, their Indigenous allies, and the Haudenosaunee, a settlement treated in Chapter 45 and not retold here. The founding of Detroit in the same year, as a French post on the Great Lakes, reflected French efforts to secure the interior trade.

The condition of these markets shows continuity and change. Indigenous control over supply and exchange remained strong in the interior, while European competition, depleted beaver in older regions, and expanding settlement were creating new pressures. The system that emerged was one in which Indigenous nations remained indispensable partners but operated within growing imperial rivalries, a pattern that later chapters, including Chapters 55 and 57, follow.

A regional comparison clarifies the system around 1700. At Montreal, Indigenous traders from the Great Lakes, especially Odawa and others, brought furs in large flotillas, while French authorities struggled with oversupply and sought to regulate licensing and posts. Albany remained a market in which Mohawk and other Haudenosaunee traders played central roles and in which English cloth and other goods attracted traders from beyond the Haudenosaunee. In the Great Lakes, Anishinaabe and other peoples, discussed in Chapter 27, were both producers and intermediaries who dealt with several buyers. On Hudson Bay, the Cree and their partners carried furs to coastal posts, supplying a company whose position remained dependent on them.

In 1701 the French agreed to the Great Peace of Montreal with the Haudenosaunee and numerous Indigenous allies, and representatives of the Five Nations executed the July 19, 1701 Albany instrument often called the Nanfan Deed, which purported to surrender extensive claims to beaver-hunting territories to the English Crown while expecting continued hunting access and royal protection. The instrument's interpretation, legal effect, and implications for other Indigenous nations remain contested and are examined in Chapter 56. These agreements reflected the interplay of trade, territory, and diplomacy. They did not remove Indigenous bargaining power; each side continued to court Indigenous allies and suppliers.

By 1700 the system had become more complex than a century earlier. European companies, colonial governments, and Indigenous nations all had stakes in several interlocking markets. The pressures that would narrow Indigenous options, such as epidemics, growing colonial populations, and imperial war, were already visible, but the system as a whole still depended on Indigenous decisions.

Section 46.34

Interpreting Indigenous Commercial Power: Sources, Historiography, and Historical Significance

Historians have interpreted the fur trade in several ways. Some emphasize partnership and mutual advantage, noting that Indigenous peoples and Europeans each needed the other. Others stress Indigenous opportunity and the skill with which Indigenous traders pursued their interests, as in the work of Arthur Ray on Indigenous participation in the trade of the subarctic. Others highlight colonial extraction and the ways in which the trade integrated Indigenous peoples into a capitalist Atlantic economy on unequal terms. Environmental historians, including William Cronon, stress ecological transformation. Richard White’s The Middle Ground examines the Great Lakes as a region where neither side could dominate and where accommodation shaped interaction, and Sylvia Van Kirk’s Many Tender Ties brought attention to women’s roles, though chiefly for a later period.

The sources shape the interpretations. Merchant accounts and company records document prices and goods but filter Indigenous action through European concerns. Missionary accounts describe Indigenous society but with religious purposes. Archaeology reveals consumption and material culture but cannot explain intentions. Indigenous historical knowledge preserves accounts of trade and relationships that do not appear in European records, and it should be weighed alongside other evidence. The interpretation changes depending on whose interests are taken as the starting point: from a merchant’s ledger the trade appears as commerce; from an Indigenous community’s perspective it may appear as one element of diplomacy, kinship, and subsistence.

The historical significance of the trade is that it connected Indigenous North America to Atlantic markets while Indigenous nations still held substantial power, and that it altered economies, ecologies, and political relationships in ways that shaped later history. The chapters that follow, on the wars of the seventeenth century, the English colonies, the Métis, and the eighteenth-century struggles among empires, build on this foundation.

Several lines of interpretation can be traced to scholars’ choices of evidence and questions. Innis and later staples scholars asked how the trade shaped the economic development of the colonies and later Canada, and thus began with European structures. Ray, Trigger, and others began with Indigenous societies and asked how they used the trade. Calvin Martin’s Keepers of the Game (1978) proposed that epidemics disrupted the spiritual relationship between hunters and animals and led them to overhunt, an argument that stimulated discussion but was criticized by other scholars, including contributors to a volume edited by Shepard Krech III (1981), who argued that it relied on thin evidence and mischaracterized Indigenous motivations. The debate remains a reminder of how interpretation depends on assumptions about Indigenous thought and about the weight of evidence.

Environmental historians such as William Cronon have brought attention to the transformation of landscapes and the connections between markets and ecologies. Economic historians have examined the Hudson’s Bay Company’s organization and its pricing practices, and some have argued that Indigenous traders behaved as rational economic actors responding to prices. Scholars of kinship and diplomacy, including Indigenous historians, have emphasized that exchange was a political and social act, and that the ledger is an incomplete guide. Each approach illuminates a part of the whole.

The use of sources also affects interpretation. Company and merchant records document goods and transactions, missionary writing offers description and bias, archaeology reveals what people used and buried, and Indigenous historical knowledge preserves the memory of relationships and purposes. A history that relies on one type alone is likely to emphasize particular themes. The interpretive approach adopted here is to use each source type to test the others, to mark where later evidence is being used, and to avoid conclusions that no source type could support. The history of the fur trade is, in this sense, a history of how knowledge about the trade was produced as well as how the trade operated.

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

The sections that follow provide the chapter summary, key terms, review questions, and research extension. Before continuing, students should be able to explain how Indigenous nations, European merchants, households, and ecosystems were each part of the fur-trade system, and why the system’s balance of power changed gradually and unevenly.

Division VII has already supplied the chapter’s historical debate and timeline study, in Section 46.34, as part of its assessment of sources and interpretation.

Chapter Summary

The fur trade developed from the interaction of Indigenous economic systems and European demand. Indigenous exchange networks, ecological knowledge, and production systems existed before European merchants arrived, and the trade redirected and intensified them.

European demand, especially for beaver felt, gave the trade its market. But Indigenous producers had different objectives, and European buyers could not control production, transport, or access to territory.

Colonial trading networks formed along the Saint Lawrence, the Hudson, and Hudson Bay. Indigenous intermediaries including the Innu, Algonquin, Nipissing, Wendat, Mahican, Mohawk, and Cree held advantages by controlling routes and relationships.

Production involved hunting, dressing skins, transport, and provisioning, and it depended on women’s labor and decisions as well as men’s. Exchange operated through bargaining, gifts, and kinship, with prices negotiated rather than fixed.

European merchants tried to organize trade through posts, accounts, and monopolies, but legal claims did not equal practical control. Indigenous buyers were active consumers who selected and adapted goods.

Competition among French, Dutch, and English traders increased Indigenous leverage. The HBC charter of 1670 asserted English claims over Rupert’s Land without Indigenous consent, and the company’s early operations depended on Cree networks.

The trade altered ecologies unevenly, produced local depletion, and contributed to political and military pressures, though war had multiple causes. Dependency developed gradually and cannot be assumed from the arrival of trade goods.

Historians interpret the trade as partnership, opportunity, extraction, or ecological transformation. The best-supported view is that Indigenous power was substantial and that its conditions changed over time and varied by place.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows that the fur trade was not simply a European enterprise imposed on Indigenous North America. It was an interconnected economic system in which Indigenous nations held substantial productive and commercial leverage, and in which European merchants and governments depended on Indigenous labor, knowledge, and goodwill.

It also shows why the chapter’s theme is the changing conditions of Indigenous power. Competition among Europeans enhanced Indigenous leverage; epidemics, warfare, ecological pressures, expanding colonial settlement, and growing reliance on imported goods gradually narrowed it. These processes unfolded unevenly and differently in the Saint Lawrence, the Hudson Valley, the Great Lakes, and Hudson Bay.

The chapter links forward to the wars of Chapter 47, the English colonies of Chapter 48, the Métis of Chapter 52, and the imperial struggles of Chapters 55 through 57. It also links back to the exchange systems of Chapter 36 and the alliances of Chapter 45.

Understanding the fur trade as a system of Indigenous and European exchange is essential to the rest of the textbook. It explains how Indigenous nations remained powerful political actors in an age of expanding empires, and why the transformations of this period were consequential without being inevitable.
