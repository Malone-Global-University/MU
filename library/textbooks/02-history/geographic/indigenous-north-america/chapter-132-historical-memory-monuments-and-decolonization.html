Historical Memory, Monuments, and Decolonization

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

In 2020, a statue of Christopher Columbus disappeared from the Paseo de la Reforma in Mexico City. The vacant place did not remain politically empty. Feminist activists erected an antimonument in 2021; an Indigenous woman's sculptural likeness was installed nearby in 2023. Officials, activists, and local communities disagreed about which histories a prominent roundabout should commemorate. The sequence captures a question that appears across North America: a public monument may preserve evidence of an earlier period's priorities, yet its position in shared space also grants an honor that later generations may refuse to endorse.

Public memory is created through decisions about what to record, preserve, teach, name, and display. A historical society chooses what to acquire; a school curriculum selects events; a park agency establishes interpretive trails; a city names a street; a community gathers annually to remember ancestors. These choices affect the histories residents encounter without asking to encounter them. Bronze and stone may seem permanent, but their meaning depends on changing institutions and audiences. It is therefore necessary to distinguish historical events themselves from later stories about those events and from still later controversies over how those stories should appear in public.

Indigenous nations entered this history with established traditions of remembrance. Oral narratives, genealogies, ceremonial obligations, records of diplomacy, and place-based knowledge preserved political relationships across generations. Such traditions existed before European colonization and survived subsequent upheavals. They were not simply reactions to museums or monuments established by settler governments. Yet colonial chronicles, schools, official maps, museums, and national commemorations frequently represented Indigenous societies as obstacles to settlement or as peoples whose historical importance ended with European arrival. The distance between those public narratives and Indigenous community memory became a source of enduring conflict.

This chapter examines how such representations were constructed and challenged. It considers the production of colonial historical narratives; the treatment of Indigenous evidence in archives; the commissioning and removal of monuments; changes in museums and national historic sites; the restoration or alteration of geographic names; and efforts to commemorate residential schools, massacres, treaties, and Indigenous service. It also studies Indigenous-owned institutions and the practical difference between being consulted about an exhibit and having authority to decide what it says.

The primary geographic emphasis is the United States and Canada, whose legal and commemorative institutions differ substantially. Cases in Mexico and Greenland demonstrate that debates over colonial history have wider continental dimensions, but the chapter does not assume that each state has the same constitutional structure or experience of colonization. It recognizes that Indigenous nations and families are internally diverse, that commemorative positions can differ within communities, and that monuments associated with ethnic or religious identity may hold meanings distinct from the events in a historical figure's life.

The evidence includes monument inscriptions and dedication records; archival finding aids; official statutes, government decisions, and heritage policies; newspapers and museum catalogues; documentary scholarship; and oral histories and statements from Indigenous communities. No single type of evidence is infallible. Official records may conceal coercion or omission, while retrospective testimony requires attention to the speaker's context and its mode of transmission. An effective historical interpretation identifies which claims can be corroborated, what remains uncertain, and whose perspective a source leaves outside the frame.

The residential and boarding school investigations of Chapters 127 and 128, the repatriation questions of Chapter 125, and the child welfare history of Chapter 131 provide necessary context but are not repeated in detail. Chapter 132 focuses instead on how histories such as those become visible or invisible in public institutions. Its argument is that changing a statue or name can matter, but the more consequential historical question is who has the authority and resources to interpret the past in ways that endure. The chapter closes Part XXII, Justice, Memory, and Reconciliation, and prepares for Chapter 133's examination of Indigenous environmental knowledge.

Historical Context

The first historical context is continental Indigenous continuity. Before colonization, and throughout the centuries that followed, nations maintained their own systems of remembering territorial relationships, political agreements, ancestors, and social change. Earlier volumes of this textbook explain Indigenous diplomacy, warfare, migration, economy, and intellectual life as histories in their own right. Chapter 132 asks why these histories frequently entered public institutions built by colonial governments only in restricted or distorted forms.

The second context is the expansion of settler-state authority. European colonization transformed landholding, maps, names, institutions, and schooling. Governments and associated organizations produced records to justify legal claims and administrative policies. As modern states consolidated, they developed archives, historical societies, school curricula, museums, preservation laws, and national monuments. These institutions gave public visibility to selected accounts of conquest, treaty making, settlement, missions, and national founding. Their commemorative functions became especially influential in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

The third context is the sustained history of Indigenous challenge. Oral traditions, Indigenous-authored texts, political petitions, treaty-rights arguments, community ceremonies, and local research contested settler-centered public memory long before the appearance of modern debates about statues. In the twentieth century, Indigenous political movements created additional institutions and audiences for those histories, while scholarly revisions of frontier and imperial narratives enlarged the range of evidence historians considered. Such changes did not simply replace one authoritative story with another; they made competing claims and previously excluded records more visible.

The fourth context is the changing relationship between heritage administration and sovereignty. A national museum may welcome Indigenous curators while retaining legal ownership of collections. A park may invite tribal advisers while controlling final signage. A community-owned heritage center may maintain direct authority but face funding and conservation challenges. In Canada, reconciliation policy interacts with First Nations, Inuit, and Métis institutions; in the United States, tribal governments operate within federal and state heritage systems. These differences explain why symbolic recognition and institutional power must be studied separately.

Finally, public memory is not simply a modern controversy. It unfolds through layered chronologies: the event remembered; the date when a particular monument, map label, or exhibition first represented it; and the later date when people demanded a change. The chapter will consistently distinguish these three levels. They permit historians to examine contemporary conflict without confusing a statue's removal with the historical event that originally inspired its erection, or treating a recent government decision as the beginning of Indigenous remembrance.

Division I — Historical Memory and the Construction of Colonial Narratives

Section 132.1

Indigenous Historical Memory and the Transmission of the Past

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Memory and Public History

Definition: Historical memory is how a community remembers its past; public history is the presentation of the past to general audiences through museums, sites, monuments, and ceremonies.

Historical context: Both are shaped by institutions and power.

Why it matters: They explain why contests over the past are also contests over authority.

Potential misunderstanding: Memory is not the same as history as a scholarly discipline, nor is it false by nature.

Indigenous nations did not wait for outsiders to document their pasts. Historical knowledge was carried in oral narratives, genealogies, songs, ceremonies, wampum, winter counts, carved and painted houses, place names, and the specialized memory of designated knowledge keepers. These systems differ sharply among nations: a Haudenosaunee condolence council, a Lakota winter count, a Tlingit clan house narrative, and an Inuit account of a family's travels are distinct intellectual traditions with their own rules about who may speak and when.

Place is central to many of these traditions. Narratives attached to rivers, mountains, villages, and burial grounds anchor memory in landscape, so that a place name can function as an archive. Knowledge is also frequently governed by protocol: some accounts belong to particular families, clans, or ceremonial societies and are not meant for public circulation. Historians therefore treat Indigenous historical traditions as sources requiring both critical reading and respect for community authority, as Chapter 1 argued.

The variety of these records matters. A Lakota winter count conventionally uses an image or remembered event to identify a year within a sequence; its meaning depends on a knowledgeable interpreter and a community's understanding of that year's significance. Haudenosaunee wampum belts can preserve political relationships and ceremonial obligations, but their significance cannot be inferred simply by counting beads or treating them as alphabetic writing. For some Northwest Coast peoples, histories of houses, clans, origins, and territorial relationships are maintained through named hereditary responsibilities, performances, and visual forms. These are not interchangeable technologies of memory. Each connects historical knowledge to particular persons, occasions, languages, and places.

Historical continuity does not imply that Indigenous accounts were immune to change or disagreement. Kin groups could preserve different versions of an event, negotiations could redefine relationships, and people could interpret older knowledge in response to war, migration, or dispossession. Historians ask when an account was recorded, how it was transmitted, whom its speaker represents, and whether independent sources corroborate particular events. They also distinguish a community's obligation to preserve restricted knowledge from an outside researcher's desire to publish it. The scholarly challenge is to undertake rigorous comparison without claiming ownership of knowledge that communities understand as governed by their own protocols.

The colonial archive frequently obscures this ongoing intellectual work. An official might write that a treaty extinguished a territorial claim, while an Indigenous nation's transmitted account describes a continuing agreement to share or protect land. Such divergence may arise from translation, deliberate misunderstanding, incompatible concepts of jurisdiction, or later changes in law. Reconstructing the encounter requires records of the agreement, Indigenous interpretations, diplomatic practices, and subsequent conduct. The dispute is not resolved simply by choosing a document over an oral account: both must be evaluated for the questions each can answer and the purposes for which each was preserved.

Family teaching makes historical memory an everyday institution. Stories associated with a river crossing or a seasonal gathering teach young people the location of ancestral activities, the obligations attached to a place, and the conduct expected of relations. Communities remember loss and violence, but also diplomacy, agriculture, craftsmanship, intellectual achievement, and humor. That breadth is vital to the chapter's argument. Indigenous remembrance did not arise only as a response to monuments erected by settlers; it long sustained distinct political and cultural worlds, even when public institutions outside those communities refused to recognize their authority.

Section 132.2

Colonial Histories and the Construction of North American Memory

European and settler governments produced their own public histories: royal chronicles, missionary reports, maps that named and claimed territory, official histories, school texts, and ceremonies celebrating exploration, discovery, and settlement. These narratives served political purposes. Claims of discovery implied that lands were previously unclaimed, and accounts of progress framed Indigenous nations either as obstacles to expansion or as peoples belonging to a vanishing past. Neither framing matched the continuing political existence of Indigenous nations treated in earlier chapters.

Colonial narratives were not uniform. Spanish, French, British, Mexican, American, and Canadian traditions emphasized different themes, from missionary salvation to commercial partnership to manifest destiny, and each changed over time. What they shared was authorship by the colonizing society and a tendency to place Indigenous people at the margins of the national story.

Cause and Effect

Colonial Historical Narratives and Indigenous Exclusion from Commemoration

Long-term causes
Colonial territorial claims and the unequal distribution of power over schools, printing, archives, maps, and civic institutions. Indigenous accounts remained active but were commonly excluded from official historical narration.
Short-term causes
Nineteenth-century urban boosterism, anniversary campaigns, national expansion, and requests by influential donors to honor particular explorers, missionaries, or colonial leaders.
Immediate trigger or context
A documented fundraising effort or civic decision to commission a statue, name a place, dedicate a park, or authorize a curriculum that selects the commemorated person and approves the message.
Event or transformation
Public institutions install the monument or promulgate the name, circulate an inscription or account, and normalize the chosen narrative through ceremonies and education.
Immediate effects
A selected colonial figure receives public honor while Indigenous losses, treaties, political institutions, or rival interpretations often remain absent from the display.
Long-term consequences
Repeated public use makes the narrative seem inherited and uncontested; later organizing may demand reinterpretation, renaming, additional memorials, or new governing arrangements.

Historical complexity: A particular commission may have multiple motives, including ethnic pride or religious devotion, as well as exclusionary effects. The historian must document the chronology and institutional mechanism rather than presuming every patron intended the same outcome. Counter-memory and Indigenous critique may exist throughout the process, not only after the statue is installed.

The phrase 'discovery' illustrates how public history can transform a legal and political claim into familiar language. Europeans arriving in places with established peoples, governments, and trade networks could describe the territory as newly discovered from the standpoint of their own institutions. Over time, maps, textbooks, and commemorations repeated that observer-centered perspective as if it described a universal condition. Particular colonial ventures differed in their purposes—missionization, trade, settlement, military occupation, or territorial administration—but their documentary practices frequently strengthened European claims to represent the history of the continent.

In the nineteenth-century United States, stories of western expansion often turned a series of military campaigns, treaty negotiations, coerced removals, and Indigenous political strategies into a single narrative of inevitable frontier progress. Frederick Jackson Turner's 1893 frontier thesis became influential within American historical writing, although historians later questioned its treatment of land, violence, race, and continuing Indigenous sovereignty. Canada's national stories often gave greater prominence to Crown treaty making, exploration, and settlement while obscuring Indigenous understandings of those agreements and the coercive effects of colonial administration. Mexican narratives could celebrate precolonial civilizations as national ancestors while marginalizing living Indigenous communities and their political demands. Such contrasts show why a continental history cannot treat all settler-state narratives as identical.

These historical narratives gained authority through repetition across institutions. School readers selected a limited set of heroes and events; official maps normalized territorial names; monuments translated political choices into seemingly permanent civic landscapes. Museums arranged artifacts into sequences that implied Indigenous peoples belonged to an earlier developmental stage. Historical holidays gave familiar rhythms to stories of discovery or state founding. None of these media operated independently. They reinforced one another, allowing an interpretive framework to become common sense long after the original reasons for its adoption had faded from memory.

A useful example of the mechanism is a monument commission. Civic patrons first identify a figure whose life can stand for the community's desired origins. They raise money, obtain a site, approve an inscription, and dedicate a statue through speeches and ceremonies. Newspapers report the occasion, schools repeat the approved story, and successive generations encounter the monument as inherited heritage. Indigenous exclusion may occur at every stage without a single order demanding it: the selectors, archives, artistic conventions, and budgets already privilege one narrative. Later campaigns for reinterpretation therefore address not just a statue but the institutional chain that made the statue authoritative.

Section 132.3

Archives, Historical Institutions, and the Authority to Interpret History

Historical societies, government archives, universities, and museums decide which records are kept, catalogued, and made accessible. Colonial archives preserve treaties, correspondence, military reports, and census records, but usually through the eyes of officials; Indigenous testimony was often unrecorded, mistranslated, or dismissed. This is archival bias: the record is uneven because of who held the pen and who controlled the repository.

Historians do not resolve conflicts between written and oral sources by presuming either infallible. Written reports can reflect propaganda or ignorance, and oral accounts can compress time or serve community purposes, yet each can be tested against other evidence. Where the two disagree, the disagreement itself becomes evidence about perspective.

An archive is not a transparent storage place for everything that happened. Its holdings result from decisions about which records officials created, what institutions retained, how archivists arranged them, and what restrictions govern access. Treaty documents, land surveys, police reports, and missionary correspondence can preserve invaluable detail about specific encounters, but their categories often reflect the administrative needs of colonial governments. A census might count individuals according to legal status rather than a nation's own understanding of belonging; a military report might record the losses of one army in detail while barely identifying Indigenous civilians.

Finding aids and cataloguing terms can reproduce those inequalities. Materials were sometimes grouped under broad, inaccurate labels that concealed the names of communities or the identities of individual knowledge keepers. Researchers seeking an Indigenous-language document might have to search under a collector's name or a colonial designation. Changing an exhibition label is therefore only one part of reform. Re-description, community review of catalogues, corrected geographic metadata, and improved access to copies of documents can change what questions future students are able to ask.

There are also conflicts between openness and responsibility. Publicly funded archives ordinarily seek broad scholarly access, but some recordings concern ceremonies, burials, family histories, or knowledge that communities restrict according to their own law and custom. Sensitive information can cause harm when circulated without context or consent. Indigenous-directed archival policies may specify which community members can review a recording and who may authorize its reproduction. Negotiation is required because the institution holding a legal title to a document may not be the community that supplied the knowledge it contains.

A disciplined historian makes the evidentiary process visible. The date on a document is not always the date of the event it describes; a translation can introduce concepts absent from the source language; a retrospective interview may preserve testimony unavailable in any written record while also requiring contextual interpretation. Comparison among archives, oral histories, material evidence, landscape knowledge, and records of subsequent actions helps test claims. The result may confirm one account, establish that more than one event has been conflated, or leave a genuine unresolved question. Scholarly honesty requires identifying which result the evidence supports.

Section 132.4

Indigenous Historians, Community Memory, and Historical Reinterpretation

Indigenous-authored history is much older than the modern academy. Nineteenth-century writers, including Indigenous journalists, ministers, and political leaders, published accounts of their nations, and twentieth-century community historians and elders continued recording oral history. From the late twentieth century, Indigenous scholars entered universities in growing numbers, and tribal historic preservation offices, language programs, and community oral-history projects multiplied.

Their work has changed interpretations of treaty making, removal, and dispossession by foregrounding Indigenous political goals, legal understandings, and continuity. It treats elders, educators, and community researchers as historical actors and authorities, not merely informants.

Indigenous historical interpretation has named authors and long-standing written traditions. William Apess, a Pequot writer and minister, challenged the celebratory accounts of colonial warfare in his 1836 'Eulogy on King Philip,' reconsidering the life of the Wampanoag leader Metacom. Sarah Winnemucca's 'Life Among the Piutes' (1883) recounted Northern Paiute experience and criticized American policy through the perspective of a person directly involved in the political events described. These works were not merely reactions to outsiders; they interpreted sovereignty, ethics, violence, and the evidence of Indigenous experience for contemporary audiences.

Other writers expanded that record in different genres. George Copway, an Anishinaabe author, published accounts of his life and people during the nineteenth century. Zitkala-Sa recorded experiences of schooling and cultural conflict while pursuing literature and political organizing. Such authors occupied complicated positions, sometimes writing for non-Indigenous readers through formats shaped by publishing markets. Their histories must be situated in those circumstances without reducing them to passive witnesses. Their interventions show that Indigenous challenges to settler historical authority were not invented by university departments in the late twentieth century.

During the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, community historians, treaty researchers, legal advocates, and university scholars increasingly questioned the assumptions embedded in official archives. Vine Deloria Jr. combined historical argument and institutional criticism in works beginning in the 1960s; historians such as Jennifer Nez Denetdale and Ned Blackhawk have reconstructed Indigenous political experiences through multiple archives and methodological frameworks. In Canada, researchers have explored the histories of First Nations, Inuit, and Métis communities in ways that foreground their own political relationships and intellectual traditions. Their scholarship does not represent one unified Indigenous interpretation: authors disagree about chronology, methods, strategies, and the emphasis particular events deserve.

Community historical offices make this work durable. A tribal historic preservation program may document ancestral sites, advise on archaeological projects, maintain place-name knowledge, and train younger researchers. A local history committee may pair photographs with testimony from descendants who can identify people erased by the original captions. Partnerships with universities can provide preservation equipment or training, but they require decisions about intellectual authority, return of copies, and who approves public release. The consequence for historical method is profound: communities once described only as subjects of outside research increasingly act as authors, custodians, and institutional partners.

Section 132.5

Columbus, Discovery Narratives, and the Commemoration of 1492

Columbus commemorations developed over centuries. In the United States, the 1892 quadricentennial and later Italian American advocacy tied Columbus to immigrant belonging and civic pride, and Congress established an annual presidential proclamation framework in 1934; Columbus Day became a legal federal holiday under legislation enacted in 1968 and effective in 1971. Latin American countries developed their own observances of 12 October, with different names and meanings. The figure thus carried national, ethnic, and religious significance quite apart from the history of 1492.

Indigenous critics argued that discovery-centered narratives erased peoples already present and sanctioned conquest. The 1992 quincentenary prompted wide public debate and alternative commemorations. South Dakota observed Native American Day in place of Columbus Day in 1990, and Berkeley, California adopted Indigenous Peoples' Day in 1992. These disputes involved both interpretation of the past and competing claims to civic belonging, and they should be distinguished from disagreement about documented facts.

Commemorating Columbus tells us as much about the generations that honored him as about the voyages of the late fifteenth century. The 1892 quadricentennial in the United States appeared amid arguments about national growth, citizenship, immigration, and the country's place in the world. For many Italian Americans, public identification with Columbus offered an answer to exclusion and prejudice: his elevation made an immigrant community's contributions more visible in an American national story. A historical treatment must recognize that social context even while explaining why Indigenous peoples rejected a celebration that used European arrival as the beginning of continental history.

Federal recognition accumulated in stages rather than through a single 1937 law. Congress approved an annual presidential proclamation arrangement in 1934, while later legislation in 1968 designated Columbus Day as a legal federal holiday, first observed under the new Monday schedule in 1971. Municipal observances and community celebrations followed additional local histories. In Latin America, October 12 was marked under names and ideologies that varied across nations and changed over time, including ideas of Hispanic heritage, race, national origins, and later Indigenous resistance. There is no single continental Columbus Day tradition.

The 1992 quincentenary made competing interpretations especially visible. Indigenous organizations and scholars emphasized the existing societies of the Americas, the violence of conquest, and the enduring consequences of colonization. Others defended ceremonies as recognition of maritime exploration or immigrant heritage. South Dakota's recognition of Native American Day in 1990 arose in its own reconciliation politics; Berkeley's 1992 Indigenous Peoples' Day drew on local organizing and the symbolic pressure of the anniversary. The chronology matters because Indigenous alternatives were built through specific political campaigns rather than appearing spontaneously during a nationwide change of opinion.

As debates developed, some governments proclaimed Indigenous Peoples' Day while Columbus Day remained recognized elsewhere or at another jurisdictional level. In 2021 a U.S. president issued an Indigenous Peoples' Day proclamation, but that act did not repeal the statutory federal Columbus Day holiday. Students should distinguish presidential proclamations, local ordinances, state holidays, and federal statutes. The interpretive question is also narrower than whether Columbus crossed the Atlantic: it asks whom civic institutions choose to celebrate, what the celebration omits, and how communities negotiate multiple histories within shared public space.

Section 132.6

Colonial Founders, Missionaries, Military Leaders, and Public Monuments

Monuments to missionaries, colonial officials, settlers, and soldiers were mostly erected long after the lives they honor, often in periods of nation building, anniversary celebration, or racial and regional politics. Who sponsored a monument, when, and why tells historians about the commemorating generation as much as about the commemorated person. Honor is a judgment, not documentation.

Cases show the range. Statues of the Franciscan missionary Junípero Serra celebrated California's Spanish heritage, while many Indigenous Californians remember the mission system as forced labor and demographic catastrophe (Chapter 70). Statues of Don Juan de Oñate in New Mexico honored a founder of Spanish colonization, while Pueblo people recalled the violence of the 1598 conquest at Acoma. In each case, a single monument carried more than one historical memory.

Historical Vocabulary
Commemoration and Monument

Definition: Commemoration is the public act of remembering people or events; a monument is a lasting physical form such as a statue, plaque, or building that embodies it.

Historical context: Monuments reflect the values of the people who built them and usually say little about the full historical record.

Why it matters: Distinguishing commemoration from documentation explains why a monument can be debated without anyone disputing that its subject lived.

Potential misunderstanding: A monument is not a neutral historical source about its subject; it is evidence about the later society that erected it.

Monuments carry the evidence of their commissioning moment. An inscription can identify donors, elected officials, civic organizations, or religious patrons; a dedication program can reveal the intended audience; a design may borrow from earlier national symbols. Historians ask whether a monument was erected soon after a person's lifetime or generations later, what local conflict preceded it, and which community had access to the decision. A statue commissioned during a jubilee or anniversary is often as valuable a source about the anniversary's politics as it is about the commemorated individual's biography.

California's Junípero Serra monuments emerged from reverence for the Spanish mission period within Catholic and regional heritage traditions. Indigenous criticism directs attention to forced labor, confinement, disease, and restrictions experienced by Native Californians within the mission system. These are historical subjects that must be documented independently of a modern statue dispute; the monument neither proves benevolence nor disproves abuses. For some defenders, Serra represents missionary devotion and architectural heritage; for critics, public honor minimizes coercion. The question before civic authorities is whether particular forms of honor remain appropriate, not whether archives concerning the missions should survive.

New Mexico's Juan de Oñate monuments illustrate a different regional setting. Oñate led a Spanish colonial expedition and directed harsh punishment after resistance at Acoma in 1598–1599, events central to Pueblo historical memory. Public depictions of him as an imperial founder, presented in stone or bronze, therefore intersect with Indigenous remembrance of invasion and violence. The arguments intensified around anniversaries and public installations; during the 2020 protests, statues at Albuquerque and in Rio Arriba County were removed. Explain separately the seventeenth-century history of colonial violence, the later decision to honor Oñate, and the modern public controversy.

In Canada, statues of Edward Cornwallis and John A. Macdonald similarly reflected subsequent periods of civic memorialization. The documented bounty against Mi'kmaq people under Cornwallis's colonial administration and the role of Macdonald's government in policies affecting First Nations were central to campaigns for reconsideration. Yet Halifax's 2018 removal of Cornwallis and Victoria's 2018 removal of Macdonald occurred through particular municipal decisions, while the toppling of Montreal's Macdonald statue in 2020 followed a different process. An evidence-based chapter must distinguish formal votes, protective storage, direct action, and reinstallation debates instead of treating every monument as the same kind of case.

Section 132.7

Remembering Massacres, Forced Removal, and Indigenous Resistance

Massacres and removals were long described in official language that minimized Indigenous suffering or celebrated military victory. Survivors and descendants preserved different accounts. At Sand Creek, Colorado, where Colorado militia attacked a Cheyenne and Arapaho camp in 1864, descendants pressed for acknowledgment, and Congress established the Sand Creek Massacre National Historic Site, with the National Park Service working in consultation with associated tribes. At Wounded Knee, South Dakota, commemorative rides and gatherings, beginning around the 1990 centennial, honor the Lakota killed in 1890.

Commemoration of removal likewise developed through the Trail of Tears National Historic Trail and tribal-led remembrance (Chapter 66). The military histories belong to earlier chapters; the point here is how survivor testimony and descendant organizing altered public understanding.

The commemoration of the Sand Creek Massacre was shaped by both descendants and an unusually detailed record of controversy dating to the event itself. The November 1864 attack by Colorado territorial troops against Cheyenne and Arapaho people was condemned in investigations soon afterward, yet public narratives differed for generations. Descendant communities maintained knowledge of the attack and the landscape where it occurred even as displacement complicated access to that place. Historical recovery required comparing testimony, military reports, later maps, and oral knowledge with physical geography.

Congress authorized a Sand Creek Massacre National Historic Site in 2000, following work directed by a 1998 study act involving consultation with Cheyenne and Arapaho tribal governments. The site formally opened in April 2007. The effort to identify the location was consequential: an official designation needed boundaries and documentary support, while descendant communities understood an extensive landscape of flight, loss, and mourning. Memorialization thus demanded negotiation between federal preservation procedures and Indigenous accounts of place; it could not be accomplished simply by installing a plaque.

The Little Bighorn battlefield offers a further transformation of interpretation. Earlier commemorations focused heavily on George Armstrong Custer and the Seventh Cavalry. Congress renamed the site Little Bighorn Battlefield National Monument in 1991, and an Indian Memorial dedicated in 2003 recognized Indigenous participation and the defense of homelands. These changes did not alter the date or basic outcome of the 1876 battle. They altered whose losses, motives, and experiences visitors could encounter in the commemorative landscape. Student analysis should compare the positions of the memorials, their inscriptions, and the institutions responsible for them.

At Wounded Knee, Lakota commemorative practices and memorial activities connect the 1890 killing of Lakota people to later family, political, and spiritual remembrance. Trail of Tears remembrance likewise takes place through historic-route interpretation and ceremonies sustained by Cherokee and other descendant communities. These examples should not be reduced to the decisions of national agencies. Indigenous remembrance was already present before federal designation and can continue outside government control. The historical significance lies in the meeting—sometimes collaborative and sometimes contentious—between long-standing community memory and the state's new willingness to acknowledge it in public institutions.

Section 132.8

Contested Statues and the Politics of Monument Removal

Statue controversies have long histories, but they intensified in the 2010s and 2020. In Canada, the Halifax city council moved in 2018 to remove a statue of Edward Cornwallis, whose 1749 bounty on Mi'kmaq people is documented. Victoria removed its statue of Sir John A. Macdonald in 2018, and in 2020 protesters toppled a Macdonald statue in Montreal. In 2021 the Egerton Ryerson statue in Toronto was toppled. In the United States, statues of Serra were toppled in San Francisco in 2020, and in June 2020 authorities took down Oñate statues in Albuquerque and Rio Arriba County, New Mexico.

Removing a statue from a place of public honor does not necessarily destroy historical evidence; original monuments should be documented and, where feasible, preserved, because they are artifacts of the periods that commissioned them. Disagreements about commemoration concern values and public space, not established facts. Constituencies differed: some wanted contextual signs, others removal, others retention as heritage.

Opposing campaigns often draw on different conceptions of what a monument does. Defenders may regard it as a record of civic origins, ethnic or religious heritage, or an achievement they believe should remain visible even alongside criticism. Indigenous critics may argue that the same statue grants public honor to a figure associated with coercion, dispossession, or violence and thereby asks descendants to treat that honor as a condition of belonging. Preservation specialists may focus on the integrity of public collections and the possibilities of contextual exhibition. None of these perspectives should be invented as quotations; historians should ground them in the published statements of actual participants in individual cases.

The words 'erase history' and 'preserve history' can obscure the difference between recording a person and honoring that person. Removing a statue from a town square need not remove the documents, scholarship, photographs, or even the statue itself. Conversely, destroying an original monument can eliminate material evidence of the later period that commissioned it. Relocation to a museum preserves an object while changing its social function; an interpretive plaque retains public honor but can challenge the original inscription; a replacement monument introduces a different civic narrative. The case-specific record should establish what actually happened to the object and whether future public access remained possible.

The political process is an additional source of historical evidence. Minutes of a city council meeting, the votes of a heritage commission, the testimony of a tribal government, and the terms of a municipal storage contract can reveal who was able to influence the outcome. The cost of installation or maintenance may have once been borne by patrons different from those funding removal or reinterpretation. Public opinion within Indigenous communities may also vary and should not be presumed uniform. A serious history explains these institutions and choices while distinguishing documented historical responsibility from the different normative judgments that citizens make about contemporary honor.

Division III — Museums, Archives, and the Transformation of Public History

Section 132.9

Museums and the Historical Representation of Indigenous Peoples

Museums collected Indigenous belongings through excavation, purchase, and sometimes coercion, often under nineteenth- and twentieth-century anthropological theories that ranked societies. Exhibition traditions arranged objects by type or supposed stage of development and displayed living nations in dioramas of the past, reinforcing the idea that Indigenous people had vanished. Ethnographic collecting and world's fairs shaped this public image.

Challenges grew from the 1970s. In Canada the 1992 Task Force on Museums and First Peoples called for partnership, and in the United States the National Museum of the American Indian opened in Washington in 2004 with Native curatorial participation. Repatriation law is treated in Chapter 125; this section concerns representation.

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century museums often presented Indigenous peoples primarily through ethnographic categories. Collectors amassed baskets, clothing, carvings, ceremonial objects, photographs, and ancestral remains; labels could identify a culture but omit the maker, original owner, circumstances of acquisition, or continuing life of the community. Exhibitions sometimes placed Native objects beside materials classified as remnants of earlier human development. Visitors might encounter elaborate displays of Indigenous craftsmanship without learning that the artists' descendants continued to live, work, teach, and govern in the present. The appearance of scientific objectivity could therefore reinforce a misleading chronological message.

Collecting histories were not uniform. Some items were purchased from willing sellers, some were commissioned, and others were acquired under coercive or deeply unequal circumstances. Objects moved through private collectors, military institutions, missionaries, dealers, archaeological excavations, and government agencies. Catalogues usually preserve the names of collectors more reliably than those of Indigenous creators. The historical task is to establish provenance—the sequence of custody and transactions—where evidence permits and to recognize where records do not disclose how an item entered a museum. The legal questions of repatriation belong primarily to Chapter 125; this chapter asks how collections shaped public representations of living peoples.

International exhibitions and world's fairs intensified the problem. Display organizers could construct 'traditional' villages or invite performers to represent entire societies, sometimes highlighting an imagined past while ignoring contemporary political struggles. Visitors then carried such images into newspapers, postcards, schoolbooks, and later cinema. Museum practice was not only a reflection of prevailing ideas; it helped produce them. Reconsidering those exhibitions requires examining curatorial instructions, publicity, visitor reactions, and surviving Indigenous commentary, not merely judging photographs by modern standards.

The 1992 Canadian Task Force report 'Turning the Page: Forging New Partnerships between Museums and First Peoples' advocated new relationships between cultural institutions and Indigenous communities. In the United States, the National Museum of the American Indian, established by federal legislation in 1989 and opened on the National Mall in 2004, made the inclusion of Native voices a prominent part of institutional practice. These developments were neither universal nor final. A museum can consult Indigenous people on an exhibition yet retain unilateral control of funding or labels. The history is a process of uneven institutional reform rather than a single transition from colonial display to fully shared authority.

Section 132.10

Indigenous Museums and Community-Controlled Interpretation

Tribal museums and First Nations and Inuit cultural centers show that Indigenous communities can hold institutional authority over interpretation. Examples include the Mashantucket Pequot Museum (opened 1998), the Haida Heritage Centre at Kay Llnagaay, and the Inuit Heritage Trust, created in connection with the Nunavut land claim. Such institutions decide what is displayed, how collections are cared for, and how stories are told, often alongside language and education programs.

Collaborative exhibits in conventional museums vary. Genuine collaboration requires shared decision-making and resources, not consultation alone; where final authority stays with the museum, communities may be heard but not empowered.

Indigenous-directed museums make a different institutional proposition: the nation or community concerned should be able to determine which aspects of its own history are interpreted publicly, in what language, and with which educational purposes. The Mashantucket Pequot Museum and Research Center in Connecticut, opened in 1998, presents Pequot history through the work of a tribally connected institution. Its exhibitions can connect archaeology and colonial records with community continuity rather than treating the Pequot people as the conclusion of a colonial-war narrative. The museum's ownership, staffing, collections policies, and relation to tribal government are therefore part of its historical importance.

On Haida Gwaii, the Haida Heritage Centre at Ḵay Llnagaay houses museum and teaching spaces, including a carving shed and canoe-related facilities. Its public opening began in 2007, followed by a grand opening in 2008. The site demonstrates how a cultural institution can join exhibition to the teaching of skills, language, artistic practice, and historical responsibility. Visitors encounter not simply an assemblage of objects but an active community's capacity to interpret and sustain its own practices. The distinction matters because museum authority can be exercised through the daily work of carvers, teachers, archivists, and caretakers as much as through a formal board.

Inuit heritage organizations face distinctive circumstances across Nunavut and other Arctic regions. Collections were historically dispersed among southern museums, scientific institutions, and private holdings, while communities sought access to ancestral objects and documentation. The Inuit Heritage Trust, arising from the Nunavut land-claims framework, illustrates how heritage authority can be connected to larger political agreements and institutions. Exhibits in the Arctic may also address climate, travel, family history, and regional knowledge, but the chapter should focus on who chooses the historical narrative rather than repeating the ecological knowledge assigned to Chapter 133.

Community control is not a guarantee of an uncontroversial interpretation. Elders, youth, artists, elected leaders, and families can disagree over access, display, or the proper account of a historical event. A museum must still maintain collections, sustain staffing, manage security and conservation, and decide how to serve visitors from outside the community. Collaborative exhibitions in national museums create additional choices: do communities approve the final label, choose the curators, control images, or merely provide consultation? Evaluating the specific arrangement avoids both dismissing collaboration and assuming that an invitation to participate equals actual decision-making authority.

Section 132.11

Archives, Oral History, Digital Collections, and Cultural Authority

Many nations now collect photographs, family histories, and oral testimony, sometimes recovering materials held in outside archives. Questions of access are complex: some knowledge is culturally restricted, some records contain private or sacred information, and institutional ownership may conflict with community authority. Protocols and tools such as the Mukurtu content-management platform let communities set culturally appropriate access conditions.

Digital archives expand access but do not resolve authority. Detailed questions of data sovereignty and technology belong to Chapter 145.

Archival reform often begins with a deceptively simple task: identifying what an outside institution holds. A photograph may carry only a collector's surname and an inaccurate label; a recording may have been catalogued under a colonial language designation; a land-use account may be buried in the correspondence of a distant official. Community researchers compare these materials with names, families, and locations known through oral history. Reidentification can restore an artist's name, correct a place, establish a family's connection to a picture, or reveal that an official report described only one side of a negotiation.

Digital technologies can help reconnect materials dispersed across continents, but digitization alone is not repatriation of authority. An archive that scans a photograph and posts it publicly without consulting the people shown may reproduce older assumptions about institutional ownership. Other records contain sensitive testimony, restricted ceremonial knowledge, or information about burial places. Ethical choices require attention to who originally provided the material, what permissions accompanied it, what current community protocols require, and whether the risk of exposure differs among users. Public access may be valuable, but it is not the only legitimate objective of preservation.

Mukurtu CMS offers a concrete example of a technical response. The platform, stewarded by the Center for Digital Scholarship and Curation at Washington State University, permits administrators to define communities and cultural protocols that govern access to particular digital heritage items. A community might determine that some historical photographs can be seen by everyone while recordings associated with specific families or cultural responsibilities are accessible only to a defined group. This illustrates a governance choice implemented through software design, not proof that all Indigenous nations share the same access rules. The chapter's purpose is to explain that relationship between historical authority and institutions; Chapter 145 will study data sovereignty more systematically.

Researchers must also preserve the possibility of criticism. When community stewardship restricts access, historians should describe the limitation instead of pretending to have examined sources they cannot see. When governments invoke privacy to withhold records of past official wrongdoing, transparency may be central to accountability. The difficult historical question is which institution has legitimate authority over each kind of record and what responsibilities accompany that authority. Solutions can include copies returned to communities, agreed descriptions, differentiated access, shared cataloguing, and sustained funding for local archives. These practical arrangements show that the politics of memory extends far beyond the visible controversy of a statue.

Section 132.12

Historical Parks, Heritage Sites, and the Interpretation of Indigenous Landscapes

National and provincial parks and historic sites shape public understanding by choosing which events to mark and how signs, tours, and ranger talks tell them. For decades many sites interpreted Indigenous history as background to settler heroes. Reforms include incorporating oral tradition and place knowledge, tribal consultation, and co-management; disputes involve interpretive wording, access to sacred places, and who staffs and funds sites.

Locations such as Sand Creek, Little Bighorn, and the Trail of Tears trail illustrate how revised interpretation and added Indigenous memorials changed what visitors learn, although practices differ widely by site and agency.

A historic site turns a location into an argument about the past. Its boundary selects a landscape, its entrance establishes a route, and its signs decide which visitors and actors appear in the narrative. A battlefield marker that identifies military units but not displaced civilian communities creates a different experience from a trail emphasizing families' flight and later survival. Historical interpretation therefore involves geography as well as words. A visitor's movement through the place can reinforce or challenge the story presented in brochures and guides.

The federal management of Little Bighorn illustrates institutional change over time. Earlier memorials honored Seventh Cavalry soldiers, with the site long carrying the name Custer Battlefield. The 1991 congressional renaming and the dedication of an Indian Memorial in 2003 expanded the commemorative focus. The revised landscape did not require erasing the earlier markers; it could make their partiality apparent and bring additional participants into view. Students should compare who received physical markers first, who gained recognition later, and what changes in administration enabled the addition.

Sand Creek presents an important but different administrative problem. Its landscape was studied under federal legislation with the participation of Cheyenne and Arapaho governments and descendants. Identifying a massacre location through testimony, geographic reconstruction, and land transactions did not eliminate debates about boundaries, access, or the most appropriate form of remembrance. National historic-site status can protect land and create educational programs, yet the federal government remains a legal manager with obligations and limits that differ from those of tribal governments. Joint planning may represent meaningful progress without amounting to full Indigenous control.

Sacred and historic landscapes also raise questions that a conventional visitor-centered model cannot answer. Greater visitation may expose sensitive sites to damage; a trail may cross land used for ceremonies; an interpretive reconstruction can flatten differences between neighboring nations. Agencies can involve tribal representatives in research, signage, preservation, and staff training, but the terms of each agreement determine whether Indigenous expertise is advisory or authoritative. The historian should explain the institutional arrangement rather than adopting the promotional language of a park brochure. Because the same site can be a public destination and a living place of responsibility, its historical significance cannot be reduced to tourist attendance.

Section 132.13

Geographic Names, Colonial Cartography, and Indigenous Renaming Movements

Colonial maps replaced Indigenous place names with names honoring explorers, officials, and rulers, or translated them in distorted forms, producing a landscape that appeared newly discovered. Indigenous names often described features, events, and relationships, and many persisted in community use.

Renaming has several forms: restoring Indigenous names, as when the mountain officially called Mount McKinley became Denali in federal use in 2015 (a federal decision reversed in 2025), and removing derogatory terms. In 2021 Interior Secretary Deb Haaland declared a derogatory term used against Indigenous women unsuitable for federal geographic names, and in 2022 the Board on Geographic Names approved replacement names for 643 federal geographic features. Many involved tribal consultation, and critics and supporters disagreed over process and local choices.

Place names preserve several layers of history at once. Indigenous names may describe a river's behavior, identify a significant event, mark kinship responsibilities, or locate a route. Colonizing governments often applied honorific names to the same landscape, creating maps that represented exploration and imperial authority. Some Indigenous names survived in adapted spellings or translations, while others continued primarily through community speech. Studying the relationship among names requires distinguishing the language of origin, the official spelling, the authority that approves maps, and the different forms of actual local use.

The mountain long known in Koyukon Athabascan as Denali is an especially revealing case. Alaska officially adopted Denali for state purposes in 1975; the federal government changed the mountain's official name from Mount McKinley to Denali in 2015. Executive Order 14172 in January 2025 directed federal restoration of the name Mount McKinley, and the Department of the Interior implemented that federal naming policy. The surrounding Denali National Park and Preserve retained its name. These reversals show that an official map label is a historically contingent government decision and does not determine which names Indigenous communities use.

Other renaming initiatives concern explicitly derogatory language rather than which historical person should be honored. In November 2021 Secretary of the Interior Deb Haaland directed the removal of a derogatory term historically used against Indigenous women from federal geographic naming. In September 2022 the U.S. Board on Geographic Names approved replacement names for 643 geographic features, following proposals, consultation, and review. A federal decision does not automatically settle usage by every private publisher, local government, or community. Nor can the number of renamed features demonstrate that all affected nations supported every individual replacement.

Comparative naming demonstrates why careful geography matters. Inuit place names occur across Arctic regions divided by the boundaries of Canada, Greenland, and Alaska, while Anishinaabe and O'odham homelands cross modern international lines. A name on a border map may reflect a government survey more than the movement of people through the region. Some communities favor official recognition of Indigenous names; others prefer dual naming, corrections of spelling, or continued local use without state standardization. In all cases the question is not merely which word appears on a sign but who can legitimate a landscape's history through public language.

Section 132.14

Holidays, Civic Ceremonies, and Public Commemoration

Holidays transmit interpretations of the past through repetition. Columbus Day, Indigenous Peoples' Day, Native American Day, Canada's June 21 observance (proclaimed National Aboriginal Day in 1996 and renamed National Indigenous Peoples Day in 2017), and commemorations such as Orange Shirt Day, observed on 30 September since 2013, each reflect specific political histories. Decisions are made at different levels: local councils, states, provinces, and national governments often differ, and a change in one does not alter the others.

In 2021 President Joe Biden issued the first presidential proclamation of Indigenous Peoples' Day, while the federal holiday remained Columbus Day by statute. Such changes show that official recognition follows broader political developments rather than a single shift.

Commemorative calendars are institutions of repetition. Each year a holiday asks schools, media organizations, municipal governments, and families to revisit a particular account of the past. The meaning of that account can change even when the legal name of the holiday remains the same. In the United States, Columbus Day became linked to immigrant pride, patriotic civic ceremony, and narratives of European discovery; in different periods it also became a subject of criticism from Indigenous activists. Local Indigenous Peoples' Day observances therefore arose in a layered commemorative landscape rather than replacing one uniform national celebration.

The chronology of legal decisions is crucial. Federal presidential proclamations concerning Columbus Day were encouraged by Congress in 1934, while the holiday acquired its modern federal statutory position under legislation adopted in 1968 and effective in 1971. South Dakota's 1990 Native American Day was part of a state reconciliation initiative, while Berkeley's Indigenous Peoples' Day in 1992 responded to the Columbus quincentenary and local organizing. In 2021 President Biden issued a presidential proclamation for Indigenous Peoples' Day, which did not repeal the federal Columbus Day statute. A student who confuses a proclamation with a statutory change will misunderstand how commemorative authority is distributed.

Canada has different commemorative histories. June 21 was proclaimed National Aboriginal Day in 1996; the federal government announced the name National Indigenous Peoples Day in 2017. Orange Shirt Day emerged through survivor-led remembrance in 2013 and became a significant public observance before Canada established the National Day for Truth and Reconciliation as a federal statutory holiday in 2021. The latter falls on September 30, but its status and effects differ among federal, provincial, territorial, and private employers. The coexistence of community-led traditions and official law matters: governments can recognize a day without determining every meaning attached to it.

Ceremonies also include language, location, and participants. A public event that invites an Indigenous representative to speak from a prescribed script differs from one whose purpose and format are designed by the relevant nation. Events may combine mourning, celebration, political demands, or educational programs, and they need not fit neatly into the categories of festival and protest. Historians should examine the programs, organizers, speeches, and audience responses of specific commemorations. That evidence reveals whether a ceremony reinforces an older national mythology or creates room for forms of historical knowledge previously excluded from official civic life.

A public memorial should be understandable to visitors, but its design can affect people whose relatives attended institutions associated with violence and loss. Section 132.16

Regional and Continental Differences in Decolonizing Public History

Approaches differ across jurisdictions. In the United States, heritage law, the National Park Service, and tribal historic preservation offices shape practice. In Canada, the Commission and provincial and territorial institutions guide it. In Mexico, Indigenous peoples were long incorporated into national narratives of mestizo identity; in October 2020 Mexico City removed its Columbus statue from the Paseo de la Reforma; feminist activists installed an antimonument in the roundabout in September 2021, and the sculpture La Joven de Amajac was unveiled nearby in July 2023. In Greenland, where Inuit are the majority, debate surrounds the statue of Hans Egede in Nuuk, which was defaced in 2020 as protesters objected to its colonial symbolism.

Because constitutional arrangements, languages, and demographics differ, techniques cannot simply be transferred. Memory also crosses borders: Inuit, Anishinaabe, and O'odham histories do not stop at modern frontiers.

The United States, Canada, Mexico, and Greenland share histories of colonization, but their institutions for public history are not equivalent. U.S. tribal governments interact with federal and state heritage law, national parks, local monument commissions, and institutions such as the Smithsonian. Canada's commemorative policy is influenced by constitutional and treaty relationships, the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, provincial heritage agencies, and First Nations, Inuit, and Métis political organizations. In Mexico, state narratives have often honored precolonial civilizations as part of national identity while sometimes obscuring the political demands of living Indigenous communities. Greenland's Inuit-majority society and its relationship with Denmark produce still another political setting.

Mexico City's former Columbus roundabout gives an especially useful case study because several constituencies contested the same space. Authorities removed the Columbus statue in October 2020. In September 2021 feminist collectives established the 'Glorieta de las Mujeres que Luchan' and installed an antimonument as a memorial to women facing violence. Meanwhile, city officials proposed the image of an Indigenous woman as a replacement for the colonial figure. A replica of the sculpture known as 'La Joven de Amajac' was installed alongside the contested roundabout in July 2023. Presenting the Indigenous sculpture as having replaced Columbus in 2021 would erase the intervening feminist memorial and flatten a dispute involving Indigenous recognition, women's rights, and control of shared civic space.

In Nuuk, Greenland, a statue of the missionary Hans Egede became the subject of protest and defacement in 2020, as some residents challenged its place in the city's commemorative landscape. A subsequent local vote supported keeping the statue. That outcome does not demonstrate uniform approval of colonial history or the absence of criticism; it establishes that the debate reached a different public decision from those in Halifax or Victoria. Political control of a historic object depends on local institutions and public preferences, and the presence of a largely Inuit population does not mean residents share one interpretation of every colonial figure.

The examples also warn against treating decolonization as a standardized checklist. Restoring place names, creating an Indigenous museum, replacing a statue, and changing a school curriculum operate through different offices and affect different publics. A symbolic victory might be followed by funding constraints; a public vote might preserve a statue while inviting additional interpretation; an Indigenous-controlled institution may emerge from a treaty arrangement rather than a protest. The appropriate comparative question is what authority each arrangement redistributes, how its history was documented, and which people gain or lose opportunities to tell their own histories. A continental approach remains alert to Arctic, Great Lakes, and southern borderland networks that extend across modern states.

Section 132.17

Indigenous Memorials, Commemorative Landscapes, and Community Remembrance

Indigenous communities have long built their own memorials: ceremonial gatherings at massacre sites, markers for treaties and leaders, memorial rides, veterans' commemorations, and cemetery care. The National Native American Veterans Memorial at the National Museum of the American Indian opened on November 11, 2020, and was formally dedicated on November 11, 2022. Annual remembrances connect generations through family narratives and place.

Local historical projects, ceremonies, and sacred landscapes often matter more to community memory than national monuments. They show remembrance as living practice, not only as response to colonial monuments.

Everyday Life and Social History

Community Remembrance in Daily Life

Memory is made in kitchens, ceremonies, language classes, and drives across a reservation or reserve. Families pass on accounts of ancestors; elders correct errors in school texts; young people join memorial runs and rides; tribal offices record stories; and local historical societies sometimes include or exclude Indigenous accounts. Written sources rarely capture these practices, so historians rely on oral history, ethnography, and community-authored records.

Indigenous memorial landscapes cannot be understood only as reactions to commemorations established by settlers. Families and communities have maintained places of remembrance through gatherings, burial care, storytelling, dances, prayers, and annual journeys. Some memorial practices are public; others are governed by ceremonial protocols that outsiders should not attempt to reproduce. The very persistence of such practices under conditions of displacement is historical evidence of collective continuity. Memorialization may therefore be rooted in a place recognized by community members long before it appears on a tourist map.

At Wounded Knee and Sand Creek, descendants' visits, ceremonies, and efforts to preserve accurate histories have shaped the meaning of those places. Memorial rides and walks can place bodily movement across a landscape at the center of remembrance. Family members may trace an ancestor's route or restore a name to an official list of those affected. These acts are forms of historical interpretation because they connect an event's documentary record to inherited responsibilities and to the geography in which it occurred. Their educational significance should be explained without depicting every ceremony as a public performance for non-Indigenous spectators.

Veterans' remembrance offers an additional field of Indigenous historical authorship. The National Native American Veterans Memorial at the Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian opened on November 11, 2020. Its formal procession and dedication took place on November 11, 2022, after pandemic disruption. Designed by Harvey Pratt, a Cheyenne and Arapaho veteran and artist, the memorial recognized Native military service across eras and branches of the U.S. armed forces. The two dates must be distinguished: public opening and ceremonial dedication are separate events, each documented by the institution.

The memorial's meaning cannot be reduced to simple endorsement of all U.S. military policies. Indigenous service members and their descendants have described service in terms of individual choice, family tradition, public duty, and relationships to sovereignty, while the history of U.S. expansion includes military conflict against Indigenous nations. A mature historical treatment allows those histories to coexist. Community memorials to Indigenous leaders, veterans, treaty negotiators, and ancestors frequently sustain memories that state monuments neglected. The purpose of this section is to show the constructive work of Indigenous commemoration, not simply to catalogue successful demands for removal of colonial statues.

What Should Societies Do with Monuments to Colonial Figures?

The question: Should such monuments be kept, reinterpreted, moved, or removed?

Preserve and contextualize: Monuments are historical artifacts; removal can narrow public discussion, and interpretive signs let the public see both the honor and its critique.
Remove or relocate: Public honor in civic space endorses the figure; museums can preserve objects without honoring them, and Indigenous people should not pass monuments to those who harmed their ancestors.

Evidence: Records of who erected each monument, local votes, and documented actions of the figure.

Points of disagreement: Mostly values and the purpose of public space, not chronology; real disagreement also exists over how to interpret certain figures.

Why it matters: Decisions signal who has authority to shape public memory.

Student task: Choose one case and evaluate each approach against the table above.

A monument is both an artifact and a public statement. Its physical materials, design, dedication records, and later alterations provide evidence about the people who created and maintained it. Its placement in a square or on government property can also convey honor. Distinguishing these functions permits a more precise debate. Preserving an object may be compatible with ending its civic honor if it is relocated and interpreted; leaving an object in place with additional explanatory signs may preserve an earlier commemorative hierarchy even while contesting its original message. Each choice is historically and institutionally specific.

Consider a municipal statue under dispute. Before a decision is reached, the city may need to establish legal ownership, the applicability of heritage protection, the views of the Indigenous nation whose history is implicated, the financial cost of removal or conservation, and the status of any donated funds or site agreements. A museum might accept the statue but lack room to display it. A plan for reinterpretation might be welcomed by some groups and rejected by others because the original figure would still dominate the space. These practical issues are not distractions from historical memory: they explain how political judgments become durable public arrangements.

Evidence from monument disputes should be organized in chronological layers. First reconstruct the events in the life of the commemorated person. Second study the much later commissioning of the monument, including sponsors and dedication rhetoric. Third examine the critiques or defenses leading to its reconsideration, and finally document what the responsible authority actually decided. A claim that 'history was erased' can then be tested against the preservation of records and artifacts; a claim that a statue 'proved' a historical wrongdoing can be tested against independent evidence. This method distinguishes factual research from differences in contemporary values and helps avoid equating all disagreements with uncertainty about the past.

The choices are not necessarily binary or permanent. An institution may initially cover a statue, commission interpretation, relocate it, or revise a plan after further consultation. Communities sometimes propose new memorials that do not replace an older figure one-for-one but change the meaning of the whole site. In Mexico City, for example, a feminist antimonument and a later Indigenous sculpture complicated the idea that public space must contain a single mutually accepted national symbol. Students should evaluate which arrangement answers the historical concern, whose decision is recognized as legitimate, and what record is preserved for future research. No universal formula can settle these questions across every jurisdiction.

Section 132.19

Indigenous Authority and the Institutions of Historical Representation

Indigenous influence over public history ranges from symbolic recognition to enforceable authority. Mechanisms include tribal ownership of museums, governance seats at public institutions, co-management agreements for historic sites, tribal historic preservation offices, heritage designation, consultation requirements, and public funding for community-controlled interpretation. Consultation alone does not give decision power.

Institutional control matters to sovereignty because the power to define, preserve, and publicly interpret history is part of self-government.

Historical Vocabulary
Decolonization

Definition: The processes by which Indigenous nations and others challenge colonial structures of power, knowledge, and representation.

Historical context: The term came from the end of formal empires and now also describes Indigenous efforts within settler states, including museum, naming, and memorial reform.

Why it matters: It focuses attention on authority as well as symbols.

Potential misunderstanding: Decolonization is not only removing statues; scholars also debate whether the term fits the settler context.

The distinction between participation and authority is central to the institutional history of public memory. A tribal government may be invited to comment on a proposed exhibition while a museum board retains the final vote. At another institution, an Indigenous governing body may own the collection, employ the curator, approve access to materials, and set policy for interpretation. Both can be described loosely as collaboration, but they distribute power differently. Historians should examine the terms of agreements, the control of budgets and collections, and the procedures for resolving disagreement before deciding what kind of relationship exists.

Tribal historic preservation offices in the United States provide one example of institutional capacity. They can maintain records of cultural places, advise governments on development projects, and participate in heritage processes affecting tribal history. Their responsibilities depend on law, resources, and relationships with federal or state agencies; they do not have unlimited jurisdiction over every site connected to a nation. In Canada, First Nations governments, Inuit organizations, and Métis institutions negotiate differing roles in museums and heritage management. Community-owned facilities may offer stronger direct control over interpretation but require predictable operating funding, trained personnel, and long-term conservation plans.

A co-management agreement must also be examined as an actual governing instrument. The term can encompass an advisory committee, shared planning, delegated administrative tasks, or joint decision-making, and those alternatives are not equivalent. At a historic site, the relevant questions include who approves new signs, whose testimony establishes the meaning of particular places, who can restrict access to sensitive locations, and how conflicts with public visitation requirements are handled. Equitable governance also requires that community representatives have the time, support, and resources to participate rather than being asked for unpaid advice at the end of a design process.

The institutional transformation of public history has educational effects. A community archive can train young researchers; an Indigenous museum can connect historical interpretation with language teaching and arts; a historic-site partnership can bring descendant testimony into visitor programs. These achievements are neither purely symbolic nor automatically sufficient for political sovereignty as a whole. They operate within larger contests over land, law, education, and fiscal resources. The chapter's analytical responsibility is to explain how control over the public account of the past can become one concrete expression of self-government, while recognizing that a plaque or consultation meeting alone does not transfer jurisdiction.

Section 132.20

Historical Memory, Decolonization, and Indigenous Sovereignty

Indigenous nations challenged narratives that denied their continuing presence, created institutions to hold their histories, and changed public space, names, holidays, and museums. Achievements are real but uneven, and conflicts persist over funding, authority, access, and the meaning of reconciliation. Decolonizing public memory concerns not only physical monuments but who has the power to interpret the past. The next chapter turns to Indigenous environmental knowledge.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Colonial Control to Indigenous-Directed Public History

What changed: Names, holidays, exhibitions, site interpretation, and in some cases governance of institutions, mostly since the 1970s and unevenly across jurisdictions.

What remained continuous: Indigenous oral and community traditions; settler-controlled archives and funding in many places.

Pattern: Gradual, contested change, experienced most directly by Indigenous nations whose histories were misrepresented, and least by institutions that kept control. It matters because it shows that Indigenous historical authority is old, not newly invented.

The history reconstructed in this chapter begins long before recent controversies about statues. Indigenous nations maintained accounts of their pasts through relationships, places, ceremonies, records, and intellectual traditions. Colonization created new institutions that could claim authority to define continental history: government archives, museums, school systems, historical societies, national parks, and civic monuments. These institutions did not simply preserve evidence. They selected, arranged, and repeated narratives that often presented colonization as discovery or progress and Indigenous peoples as marginal to the present. Understanding that process reveals how a public interpretation can acquire institutional power without necessarily reflecting the full evidentiary record.

Resistance to those narratives likewise has a long history. Indigenous authors, political leaders, elders, community researchers, and later museum professionals and historians challenged the assumptions of official accounts. Some produced written histories directed at non-Indigenous audiences; others sustained records within families and nations; many did both. The expansion of community museums, heritage offices, documentary projects, and co-curated exhibitions broadened the ways these histories could be presented publicly. Their significance lies not in a claim that one new narrative replaces every old one, but in the increased ability of communities previously excluded from the decision to exercise historical and institutional authority.

The monument cases show the difference between historical evidence and public honor. The events of conquest, treaty making, violence, and resistance must be investigated on their own terms. A statue erected later tells a second history about the society that chose to celebrate a particular actor. The contemporary dispute tells a third history about public responsibility, political representation, and the institutions controlling civic space. Those histories are related but not identical. Removing a statue may end public honor without destroying historical knowledge, while preserving an original artifact may protect evidence of the earlier commemorative order. Accurate analysis depends on keeping these distinctions visible.

The continental comparison adds caution. United States tribal nations operate within federal and state legal structures unlike those of First Nations, Inuit, and Métis in Canada. Mexico's national historical imagery and Greenland's debates over Danish colonial symbols arise from still other institutional settings. The same phrase, 'decolonization of public memory,' therefore describes different historical projects. Renaming a mountain, writing a new museum label, creating a national holiday, and negotiating management of a massacre site redistribute authority through distinct processes. The relevant evidence includes statutes, administrative decisions, oral accounts, community practices, and the consequences of implementation.

At the conclusion of Part XXII, the chapter shows that justice, memory, and reconciliation are not merely retrospective concerns. They affect which institutions students encounter, which names appear on maps, whose words appear on signs, and whether living Indigenous peoples can author the public account of their own histories. No monument decision can resolve every conflict over sovereignty, and no single account can substitute for careful evidence. Yet the continuing transformation of historical representation demonstrates that Indigenous nations are participants in making the continent's present as well as preserving its past. Chapter 133 turns to Indigenous environmental knowledge, another field in which historical authority and contemporary governance intersect.

Chapter Summary

Indigenous historical memory long predates modern North American public-history institutions. Nations preserved knowledge through named places, oral traditions, diplomatic records, ceremonies, family histories, and dedicated knowledge keepers. These traditions varied widely, and their meaning often depended on rules of transmission within particular communities. Treating the written colonial archive as the sole valid form of history obscures those institutions and the evidence they preserve.

Colonial governments and later settler societies produced maps, chronicles, school histories, museums, and commemorations that represented expansion as discovery or national progress. Their narratives differed by empire and modern state, but they often marginalized living Indigenous political communities. Historical societies, publishers, civic patrons, and public officials multiplied such interpretations through repeated public display. The resulting account could seem neutral even though it reflected decisions about which people and events deserved public recognition.

Archives provide essential evidence but also reveal institutional inequalities. Officials recorded subjects relevant to administration, and cataloguing systems preserved collectors’ names more consistently than those of Indigenous creators. Historians must evaluate provenance, translation, the original purpose of records, and the perspectives not recorded. Oral histories require critical contextualization as well; comparison rather than automatic preference is the proper historical method.

Indigenous historians actively challenged the dominant record. Writers including William Apess and Sarah Winnemucca presented alternative accounts of colonial warfare and government policy during the nineteenth century. Later Indigenous scholars, elders, community researchers, and political advocates developed further historical interpretations. Museums, historic-preservation offices, and local archives have made some of this historical authority institutionally durable, although they also face disagreements over access and adequate resources.

Monuments are not neutral summaries of the individuals they honor. Their design, patrons, funding, and installation are historical evidence of the period in which they were erected. Columbus commemorations, statues of missionaries and colonial leaders, and military memorials often communicated particular ideas about national origins and territorial expansion. Modern disputes over Cornwallis, Macdonald, Ryerson, Serra, and Oñate involve documented past events, the later act of public honor, and contemporary decisions over shared civic space.

Commemorating sites of Indigenous suffering and resistance has changed the interpretive landscape. The establishment of Sand Creek Massacre National Historic Site and the renaming and addition of the Indian Memorial at Little Bighorn show that descendant testimony and organizing can alter federally administered places. Indigenous remembrance existed before those official acts; designation brought new forms of protection and visibility while leaving continuing questions about authority, access, and management.

Museums have shifted unevenly from collecting and exhibiting Indigenous life primarily as ethnographic material toward including or supporting Indigenous-directed interpretation. The Mashantucket Pequot Museum, the Haida Heritage Centre, and the National Museum of the American Indian illustrate different institutional forms. Their histories reveal that a voice in an exhibition is not identical to authority over the museum, its budget, collections, or public access rules. Digital systems such as Mukurtu make the institutional dimensions of cultural protocols particularly visible.

Geographic naming and holidays show how historical power operates through routine government decisions. The federal designation of Denali in 2015, restoration of Mount McKinley in 2025, and continued name Denali for the national park demonstrate that names can be reversed and differ among institutions. The 2022 replacement of 643 derogatory federal geographic names followed a different process. Indigenous Peoples’ Day, Native American Day, and Canadian commemorative observances similarly vary by law and level of government.

Canada’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission treated heritage and public memory as institutional responsibilities in Calls to Action 79–83. The 2021 federal holiday fulfilled an important component but did not by itself resolve all recommendations concerning national frameworks, capital-city monuments, and arts funding. In Mexico City, a feminist antimonument and a later Indigenous sculpture changed the former Columbus roundabout in stages, while Greenland residents voted to retain the Hans Egede statue after protests. These outcomes emphasize the diversity of commemorative settings.

The chapter’s concluding argument is that disputes over public memory are disputes over more than symbols. They involve authority to select evidence, manage places, determine public interpretation, and preserve knowledge for future generations. Indigenous nationhood does not depend upon the approval of museums or governments, yet decisions within those institutions can either reinforce colonial silences or recognize continuing Indigenous historical presence. Explaining how these decisions were made is part of writing a history of sovereignty, justice, and reconciliation.

Chapter Reflection

Historical memory is not an afterthought to political history. It provides some of the names, places, symbols, records, and habits by which a society understands its origins and obligations. When public institutions recognize only the histories of conquerors or settlers, their choices become part of the political world experienced by Indigenous peoples. The analysis of these choices requires the same care as the study of a treaty or statute: attention to actors, dates, evidence, institutions, and consequences.

Indigenous nations maintained histories before colonization and continued to transmit them through periods in which colonial institutions asserted the right to define the continent. Their writers, elders, researchers, museums, and heritage programs show that historical interpretation has been an active exercise of agency rather than merely the recovery of something lost. The diversity of those practices also warns against presenting Indigenous peoples as a single voice or treating an outsider’s institutional recognition as the foundation of their authority.

Public controversies over statues and names are valuable historical evidence, but they are not the whole story. A removal can alter public honor; a preserved artifact can illuminate the commemorative priorities of an earlier generation; a new memorial can bring other experiences into civic life. The deeper issue is whether people affected by a historical representation have authority over how it is created, revised, and sustained. That institutional question links historical memory to sovereignty, even though symbolic recognition alone cannot resolve land, law, and resource conflicts.

The close of Part XXII leaves a methodological lesson for the remainder of the textbook. Justice and reconciliation require accurate reconstruction of what occurred and candor about whose perspectives were marginalized, while continuing Indigenous governance requires institutions capable of acting in the present. Chapter 133 will apply the same respect for Indigenous knowledge and historical agency to environmental history. In that next field, as in public memory, the question is not only what outsiders have recorded, but how Indigenous societies have understood, managed, and transmitted knowledge across generations.
