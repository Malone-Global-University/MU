Chapter Opening

On February 18, 2017, representatives of Columbia Plateau peoples reburied an ancestor in Washington State, in a private ceremony at a location they chose not to disclose. The man they called the Ancient One had been found in July 1996, and he had spent more than two decades in federal custody and in laboratories, courtrooms, and congressional debates before returning to the earth.

The Ancient One, widely known in the scientific literature as Kennewick Man, was discovered along the Columbia River at Kennewick, Washington, and dated to roughly nine thousand years ago. His reburial in 2017 followed a long contest in which the Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation, the Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakama Nation, the Nez Perce Tribe, the Confederated Tribes of the Colville Reservation, and the Wanapum Band asserted responsibility for him, while a group of anthropologists sued for the right to study him, and federal agencies and courts decided who counted as an authorized descendant, which evidence mattered, and which law applied. The reburial did not settle every question. It did show how a single set of remains could place before the American public a deeper question about who has the authority to decide what happens to the dead.

That question is the subject of this chapter. Repatriation is often described as the return of objects and human remains from museums to the nations from which they were taken. That description is accurate as far as it goes, but it leaves out the larger issue. For well over a century, museums, universities, government agencies, and private collectors acquired Indigenous ancestors, funerary belongings, sacred objects, and inherited communal property, and they claimed the authority to classify, study, display, store, trade, and interpret them. Repatriation movements challenged that authority. They did not merely ask for objects to be handed back; they asked that Indigenous nations be recognized as the rightful decision-makers about their own ancestors and cultural inheritance.

The chapter's central subject is the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act of 1990, usually called NAGPRA, enacted on November 16, 1990. It was a landmark statute, but it was also one result of earlier Indigenous demands, such as the Zuni campaign for the return of the Ahayu:da, the Larsen Bay campaign in Alaska, and the Omaha, Pawnee, and other efforts of the 1970s and 1980s, and of the separate National Museum of the American Indian Act of 1989 that governed the Smithsonian Institution. NAGPRA is also a specifically United States federal law. It does not govern Canada, Mexico, or Greenland, where repatriation has followed different legal paths, and the chapter treats those histories under their own frameworks rather than as variations on an American model.

The historical evidence for this subject is unusually varied. It includes the statutes and legislative records of the United States, Canada, Mexico, and Denmark and Greenland; federal regulations and the published notices that museums and agencies must file; court opinions; museum accession records, which tell us how items entered collections but rarely tell us what the people who made or cared for them thought; reports of national task forces and professional ethics codes; and the testimony, oral histories, and legal arguments of Indigenous nations and their cultural officials. Each source has limits. Museum records were generally created by collectors and curators, not by the communities concerned, and federal data on repatriation are only as complete as the inventories that institutions chose or were able to produce. The chapter therefore asks throughout whose record a document is, and whose knowledge it excludes.

The history moves through five connected transformations: the collecting that placed Indigenous ancestors and belongings in institutional hands; Indigenous demands for their return; legal recognition of those demands, most visibly in 1989 and 1990; a contested and uneven period of implementation, including the revised federal regulations that took effect on January 12, 2024; and the gradual reconstruction of relationships between cultural institutions and Indigenous nations. Neither a story of triumph nor one of mere conflict between religion and science would be accurate. Many ancestors and sacred belongings have returned home, and many remain in collections; many museums have changed their practices, and many resisted; and a number of nations have built their own museums, archives, and heritage offices to care for what comes back.

The chapter keeps its boundaries with neighboring chapters. Archaeological and oral historical methods are introduced in Chapter 1 and are discussed here only as they bear on affiliation claims. The broader fight over sacred landscapes belongs to Chapter 119, language recovery to Chapter 123, tribal colleges to Chapter 124, cultural production to Chapter 126, and the investigation of residential and boarding schools to Chapters 127 and 128. The question this chapter pursues is a more specific one than any of those: once Indigenous ancestors and sacred belongings had passed under the control of outside institutions, who had the authority to decide what happened to them, and how did that authority change?

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Indigenous nations have understood ancestral remains, ceremonial belongings, and inherited cultural property in ways that do not match Western categories of personal property and museum ownership.
  2. Trace the routes by which Indigenous ancestors and cultural materials entered museum, university, and government collections, and distinguish among theft, coercion, unequal exchange, and documented agreement.
  3. Analyze how anthropology, racial science, and preservation arguments shaped institutional treatment of Indigenous remains and belongings.
  4. Describe the early repatriation movement, including the Zuni campaign for the Ahayu:da and the National Museum of the American Indian Act of 1989, as precedents for federal law.
  5. Explain the structure of NAGPRA, including its categories of protected material, its priority rules, and the duties it places on federal agencies and museums.
  6. Distinguish NAGPRA's rules for existing collections from its rules for burials excavated or discovered on federal and tribal lands.
  7. Evaluate how oral tradition, kinship history, geography, archaeology, and archival records contribute to determinations of cultural affiliation.
  8. Interpret the Ancient One dispute by separating archaeological findings, judicial decisions, legislative action, and Indigenous claims.
  9. Assess the implementation of NAGPRA and the significance of the 2024 federal regulations.
  10. Compare the repatriation frameworks of the United States, Canada, Mexico, and Greenland, avoiding the assumption that NAGPRA operates beyond the United States.
  11. Evaluate the role of Indigenous museums, cultural centers, and heritage departments in stewardship, and distinguish possession from authority over care and interpretation.
  12. Connect repatriation to international Indigenous cultural rights, including Articles 11 and 12 of the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples.

Historical Context

By the late twentieth century, Indigenous nations across North America had experienced centuries of dispossession examined in earlier volumes: conquest, removal, treaty breaking, reservation and reserve systems, assimilation policy, and the legal and administrative regimes of the United States, Canada, Mexico, and Denmark. Collecting was entangled with these histories. Ancestral burial grounds were often disturbed in the course of settlement, road building, farming, and military operations. Ceremonial life was suppressed by law in some places, which left religious objects vulnerable to confiscation or to sale. Poverty and the loss of land and resources sometimes led individuals and communities to sell belongings. Scientific and museum institutions grew during these same centuries, and they were often funded by governments and wealthy patrons who treated Indigenous cultures as disappearing and treated collecting as a form of rescue.

Indigenous peoples did not, however, remain passive. Many communities hid ceremonial belongings, protected burial places, and objected to excavation when they could. In the second half of the twentieth century, the political context shifted. Indigenous activism, the civil rights era, religious freedom claims, the growth of tribal governments and legal organizations, and new Indigenous scholars and museum professionals gave repatriation claims political force. Changes in the law, including the American Indian Religious Freedom Act of 1978, the Archaeological Resources Protection Act of 1979, and a growing number of state burial laws, supplied some of the tools that claimants and legislators later used.

Existing law was nonetheless poorly suited to the problem. Before 1989 and 1990, American law largely treated ancestral remains found on public land as federal property or as archaeological resources, and treated museum holdings as the property of the institutions that had acquired them. Canada had no national statute directed at repatriation. Mexico had a strong national law vesting archaeological monuments in the nation, which protected antiquities from export and private ownership but did not necessarily give Indigenous communities authority over them. In Greenland, the relevant history was one of Danish collections formed during a colonial relationship and a political transformation after Home Rule in 1979. Because these systems were so different, the chapter treats each in its own context.

A final piece of context is the history of the vocabulary. Museums have historically spoken of specimens, collections, accessions, and holdings; Indigenous nations speak of ancestors, relatives, belongings, and responsibilities. The words matter because they carry competing claims about what these things are. This chapter generally uses "ancestors" and "ancestral remains" for human remains and "belongings" or "cultural items" for objects, while using the statutory terms where a law defines them.

Section 125.1

Indigenous Ancestors, Sacred Objects, and Cultural Responsibilities

Repatriation claims make sense only against the background of how Indigenous nations understood the dead and the sacred. There is no single Indigenous view, and this textbook has insisted since Chapter 1 that North America contained hundreds of distinct peoples. Yet many nations share a set of ideas that differ sharply from the assumptions of nineteenth-century collecting. Ancestors are frequently regarded as continuing members of a community, with whom the living have ongoing obligations. A burial place may be understood as a place where relationships are held, not as an archaeological deposit. Some ceremonial belongings are understood as living beings or as persons, some require care by particular societies, clans, or religious leaders, and some are meant to be kept out of public view.

Differences among nations are as important as similarities. Some communities bury their dead and expect them to remain undisturbed. Others have distinct practices for treating particular categories of dead, or for objects that are meant to be removed from use. The Zuni, whose experience is discussed in Section 125.4, place the Ahayu:da, twin war-god figures, at outdoor shrines and allow them to weather and return to the earth, so that a figure in a museum is, from the Zuni perspective, out of its proper place and unable to fulfill its responsibilities. Among the Tlingit, clan-owned objects known as at.óow, including crest hats, robes, and house posts, embody ancestral histories and are held by clans, not individuals. For the Kwakwaka'wakw, regalia, names, and the privileges of the potlatch are inherited rights, and displays of them are part of law as well as ceremony. The Omaha Sacred Pole, Umon'hon'ti, was understood as a living ancestor of the nation, and its custodianship was a communal responsibility.

These examples point to a legal difference. Western property law, as applied by museums, generally assumed that an object could be owned by an individual or institution and could be sold or given away by the person who held it. Many Indigenous legal traditions, by contrast, treated certain belongings as inalienable collective property: a person might care for or hold such an item, but could not sell it, because it belonged to a clan, society, or nation and to generations not yet born. This difference mattered historically, because an individual's sale or gift of such an item, which a museum might treat as a valid purchase, could be regarded by the community as no transfer at all. The later legal idea of "cultural patrimony," discussed in Section 125.6, was an attempt to write this principle into American law.

Historical Vocabulary
Repatriation, Restitution, and Cultural Patrimony

Definition. Repatriation refers to the return of ancestral remains or cultural items to the people or nation from which they came. Restitution refers more broadly to restoring what was wrongfully taken, and in some legal systems includes the restoration of rights or legal ownership as well as physical return. Cultural patrimony refers to cultural property held collectively across generations, which no single person has the right to sell or transfer.

Historical context. The words come from different traditions. "Repatriation" derives from the return of persons to their homeland and entered museum policy in the late twentieth century. "Restitution" is more common in international and civil-law settings, including discussions of Mexican and European heritage law. "Cultural patrimony" appears in both American statute and international heritage law, though its meaning differs between them.

Why it matters. The three terms point to different remedies. Physical return may happen without any change in legal authority, and legal restoration may happen without physical return, as when an item stays in a museum under a nation's direction. Indigenous collective responsibility is the idea that connects them.

Potential misunderstanding. Many people assume repatriation means returning every Indigenous object. In law it is far narrower, and in Indigenous thought it is often broader than possession.

The responsibilities Indigenous nations asserted over ancestors and sacred belongings did not end when those items were removed. Many communities recorded the removals in memory and oral tradition, and many continued to ask for return across generations, even when no government or museum would receive the request. The historical record of these requests is much thinner than the record of collecting, because requests were often made orally, locally, or in forums that did not generate archives. Historians must therefore read museum archives critically for the traces of community objection that they do contain.

Section 125.2

Colonial Collecting and the Removal of Indigenous Cultural Heritage

Indigenous ancestors and belongings entered collections by many routes, and no single account explains them all. A responsible history distinguishes among theft, coercion, unequal exchange, and agreements that the parties understood differently, and it also recognizes that for many items the surviving record is too thin to say which occurred.

Some of the most plain cases involved removal of human remains and burial goods without the consent of the community. Excavation of burial mounds, cemeteries, and rock shelters by antiquarians and archaeologists was widespread from the nineteenth century. Military and medical personnel also collected. In 1868, the United States Army Medical Museum's leadership asked army surgeons to send skulls and other remains of Native people, and collectors took remains from battlefields, burial platforms, and graves. Some were collected soon after violent conflict, which raised the further injury of taking the remains of people killed in war. Because many of these acquisitions were undocumented or poorly documented, institutions often cannot tell now whose ancestors they hold.

Other acquisitions took place under coercion by law and administration. In Canada, amendments to the Indian Act in 1884 and later years restricted the potlatch and related ceremonies, discussed in Chapter 85. After a large potlatch held by Dan Cranmer at Alert Bay in 1921, which led to prosecutions, Indian agents and officials took ceremonial regalia in 1922 and sent it to national and other museums. Missionaries and agents elsewhere also urged or required communities to give up ceremonial objects as a sign of conversion or compliance, and many items were then offered to collectors or institutions.

Many acquisitions took the form of purchases. Collectors, including museum-sponsored expeditions such as the work of Johan Adrian Jacobsen for the Berlin ethnological museum on the Northwest Coast in the early 1880s, bought large quantities of material from Indigenous owners. Some sellers were authorized to sell or made items specifically for trade, and Indigenous intermediaries such as the Kwakwaka'wakw ethnographer George Hunt worked with outside scholars as collaborators and brokers. Other sales involved people who were destitute, individuals with no authority to sell communal property, or purchasers who knew or should have known the item was sacred. The fact of payment therefore does not settle the question of legitimacy. For this reason Indigenous nations have often evaluated the circumstances of each transfer rather than accepting a general rule that sales were or were not valid.

Collecting was also a global enterprise. Ancestors and belongings were carried far from their homelands to institutions in Europe as well as in the United States and Canada. Greenlandic materials accumulated in Copenhagen during the period of Danish rule, and Mexican antiquities and feathered works were taken to European collections from the sixteenth century onward. The movements of material across national borders, discussed in Sections 125.15, 125.16, and 125.19, mean that repatriation often requires negotiation among governments and institutions, not only between an Indigenous nation and a local museum.

Section 125.3

Museums, Anthropology, and the Institutionalization of Indigenous Collections

During the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, collecting moved from private cabinets into permanent institutions. Museums, universities, and government agencies built large anthropological and archaeological collections, created curatorial staffs, and developed methods of cataloguing, display, and study. The Smithsonian Institution, the Bureau of American Ethnology founded in 1879, the Peabody Museum at Harvard, the Field Museum in Chicago, the American Museum of Natural History, the Heye museum in New York, and many state and university museums became repositories of Indigenous belongings and human remains. The Army Medical Museum's collection of crania was transferred to the Smithsonian, which became one of the largest holders of Native human remains in the United States.

Scientific ideas shaped what institutions collected and how they used it. Nineteenth-century physicians such as Samuel George Morton measured skulls to rank human groups, and later anthropologists, including Aleš Hrdlička at the Smithsonian, continued to collect remains for physical anthropology. These racial theories are now rejected, but at the time they gave collecting a scientific rationale and encouraged the acquisition of large numbers of skeletons. At the same time, the doctrine of "salvage anthropology" assumed that Indigenous cultures were vanishing, which made collecting seem an urgent duty and made Indigenous objections seem less important.

Institutions also asserted that they were preserving heritage for the public. Many curators in fact documented and conserved material, and some collaborated closely with Indigenous knowledgeable people. Exhibitions made Indigenous peoples the subject of public education, but usually from a perspective that treated them as part of the past, and they displayed human remains and sacred objects with little regard for religious restriction. Federal law reinforced this system. The Antiquities Act of 1906 and the Archaeological Resources Protection Act of 1979 regulated excavation and protection of archaeological resources on federal land, usually through federally administered permits; these laws did not themselves supply a general repatriation process, and Indigenous nations were not initially central decision-makers.

The scale of the change after 1990 becomes clearer when one follows an accession record through its institutional life. A museum registrar might have recorded an item under a collector’s name, an estimated tribal attribution, and a purchase date; a descendant community might instead know it by a ceremonial relationship, a family history, or the place from which it was removed. Those are not necessarily competing explanations of the same kind. A register can establish physical custody, but it cannot alone establish that an individual collector had authority to alienate communal property or that the museum acquired a morally uncontested right to display it. This gap between administrative documentation and social authority became especially consequential when the law required museums to look back at collections assembled under very different assumptions.

It would be a mistake to treat every institution or person involved as identical. Some collectors worked with community consent, some museums had Indigenous employees and consultants, and some individual anthropologists defended Indigenous religious and cultural interests. Yet the structural pattern was clear: the authority to define, study, and keep Indigenous ancestors and belongings rested with institutions and their professionals, and the nations concerned possessed very limited effective repatriation remedies and little recognized decision-making authority. That structure is what later repatriation law was intended to change.

Section 125.4

Indigenous Resistance and the Early Repatriation Movement

Indigenous demands for return predate any statute. They became politically effective in the decades after the Second World War, as Indigenous activism broadened and as tribal governments and Native legal organizations gained capacity. The demands took several forms: protests against the display and storage of ancestors, requests to museums for return of sacred objects, litigation, state legislation, and negotiation with professional associations.

One of the best known early burial cases arose in Glenwood, Iowa, in 1971, when excavators at a highway project separated the remains of a white woman, who was reburied, from those of Native people, which were sent to a laboratory. Maria Pearson, a Yankton Dakota woman, publicly protested the unequal treatment. Her campaign contributed to the Iowa Burials Protection Act of 1976, which extended protection to Native burials. In 1989, Nebraska passed a law covering remains in state institutions after Pawnee leaders and their attorney Walter Echo-Hawk pressed for the return of Pawnee ancestors held by the Nebraska State Historical Society. Dickson Mounds in Illinois, where Native burials were displayed in situ, drew sustained protest, and the exhibit closed in the early 1990s. In Alaska, the Larsen Bay Tribal Council on Kodiak Island sought return of Alutiiq ancestors excavated in the 1930s by Aleš Hrdlička. After a dispute with the Smithsonian that was among the best known in the field, the Institution agreed to return the remains of more than 750 individuals in 1991.

These campaigns developed organizational methods that later repatriation officers would recognize: tribal resolutions establishing a collective position, careful research into accession registers, testimony from ceremonial authorities, comparison of institutional descriptions with community knowledge, and persistent meetings with curators or government lawyers. The Zuni campaign illustrates the importance of distinguishing a figure’s physical removal from any valid transfer of ownership. Negotiations beginning in the late 1970s eventually produced the Smithsonian’s return of two Ahayu:da in 1987; later returns from other collections followed. The dated Smithsonian case history is more useful than an unsupported cumulative count. A different challenge appeared in burial disputes: Native communities objected not only to display but to the routine assumption that archaeological study could proceed without consulting descendants or culturally responsible nations. The two kinds of campaign converged on a demand for enforceable recognition of living communities.

The passage from particular cases to national legislation also required coalitions. Native legal advocates, tribal governments, religious leaders, museum professionals, and sympathetic members of Congress did not all agree on ownership, scientific study, or the proper procedure for contested collections. What they shared, at least sufficiently to legislate, was the recognition that ordinary museum discretion could not be the only remedy. The Smithsonian legislation in 1989 and NAGPRA in 1990 created different institutional obligations because the Smithsonian’s federal structure differed from that of museums receiving federal financial assistance. Their distinction was an outcome of legislative compromise, not evidence that one set of ancestors deserved less protection.

Case Study 1

The Zuni Ahayu:da Returns

Participants. Zuni religious leaders and the Zuni Tribal Council; museums and private collectors in the United States; later, the staff and committees who handled each return.

Objectives. To retrieve the Ahayu:da, which the Zuni understood as sacred protectors placed on outdoor shrines to weather, and to reestablish the shrines.

Evidence. Zuni religious knowledge and law, which held that the figures belonged to the community as a whole and could not be sold by any individual; and museum records showing that many figures had been taken from shrines, sometimes by collectors, and sold or donated.

Institutional decisions. Beginning in the late 1970s, the Zuni Tribal Council pressed museums for return. Museums, at first hesitant, gradually agreed to returns in many cases, partly because the Zuni argument that the figures were communal property cast doubt on any individual's right to have sold them. Dozens of figures were returned and, in keeping with Zuni belief, placed on shrines to decay rather than conserved.

Consequences. The Zuni campaign provided a working model of communal inalienable property, showed that museums could return sacred objects without institutional collapse, and influenced the later statutory categories of sacred objects and cultural patrimony.

Documentary context: Smithsonian Archives, “The Return of the Ahayu:da”, describing the 1987 return of two figures.

Professional and legal developments reinforced these demands. The American Indian Religious Freedom Act of 1978 declared federal policy to protect Native religious practices, though it lacked enforcement tools. In 1989, the World Archaeological Congress adopted the Vermillion Accord on human remains, which affirmed respect for the wishes of the dead and of the communities concerned. A national panel on museum and Native American relations, with museum and Native participants, issued a report in 1990 recommending dialogue and return. Museums such as the Peabody at Harvard returned the Omaha Sacred Pole in 1989, one of a number of early returns negotiated directly.

Case Study 2

The National Museum of the American Indian Act, 1989

Participants. Senator Daniel Inouye and the Senate Select Committee on Indian Affairs; Indigenous leaders and organizations; the Smithsonian Institution and its Secretary; the Museum of the American Indian–Heye Foundation in New York.

Objectives. To create a national museum of Native cultures within the Smithsonian and to address Native demands for the return of remains and objects in Smithsonian collections.

Evidence. Smithsonian inventories showing large holdings of Native human remains, and testimony from Native nations and scholars.

Decisions. The Act, signed on November 28, 1989, established the National Museum of the American Indian, brought in the Heye collection, and required the Smithsonian to inventory Native human remains and funerary objects and to return them to lineal descendants and culturally affiliated tribes on request. Amendments in 1996 extended the repatriation provisions to sacred objects and cultural patrimony.

Consequences. The Act produced the first federal repatriation regime and served as a precedent for NAGPRA a year later. Because it applies only to the Smithsonian, it must be distinguished from NAGPRA, which covers other federal agencies and federally funded institutions.

Original statute: National Museum of the American Indian Act, Public Law 101-185, 103 Stat. 1336 (1989). The Smithsonian-specific process should not be confused with the coverage of NAGPRA.

Cause and Effect

Why Federal Repatriation Law Emerged in 1989–1990

Long-term causes

Generations of collecting and the unequal legal status of Native and non-Native burials; Indigenous religious and legal traditions that never accepted the transfer of ancestors and communal belongings; and the growth of Native political organization.

Short-term causes

Public protests such as Glenwood; state burial laws; precedents such as the Zuni and Larsen Bay returns; and debate within museums and professional associations over ethics.

Immediate context

Congressional hearings and negotiations among Native organizations, museums, and scientists in 1989 and 1990, and the 1989 Smithsonian legislation.

Effects

Statutory recognition of Native authority over human remains and certain objects, new duties for institutions, and criminal penalties for trafficking in some remains and items.

Historical complexity: The law was a negotiated compromise, not an inevitable result. It reflects Indigenous pressure, the openness of some museum and scientific leaders, and the political skill of sponsors, and it left many questions of scope and enforcement unresolved.

Division IINAGPRA and the Transformation of Federal Law
Section 125.5

The Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act of 1990

NAGPRA was the product of negotiation. After the Smithsonian bill of 1989, Native organizations such as the National Congress of American Indians and the Native American Rights Fund, museum associations, and scientific and archaeological societies worked with the Senate Select Committee on Indian Affairs, led by Senator Daniel Inouye, and with members of the House, including Representative Morris Udall, to find language acceptable to the main parties. The panel report of 1990 and the Larsen Bay and Zuni experiences had shown that return was practicable, while museum and scientific leaders were concerned about the loss of collections and research. The bill that emerged was a compromise. It was passed by Congress in the autumn of 1990 and signed by President George H. W. Bush on November 16, 1990.

The statute had several purposes. It recognized Native rights in the remains of their ancestors and in certain cultural items; it established procedures by which federally funded museums and federal agencies would identify their holdings and return qualifying items to descendants and affiliated nations; it protected Native burials and certain cultural items on federal and tribal lands; and it criminalized trafficking in Native human remains and in cultural items taken in violation of the Act. It extended these protections to Native Hawaiian organizations as well as federally recognized tribes, including Alaska Native villages. It created a seven-member Review Committee, with members nominated by Native peoples and traditional religious leaders, by museum and scientific organizations, and by agreement of the other members, to monitor implementation and help resolve disputes. It authorized grants to help tribes and museums carry out the work, and it provided for civil penalties against museums that failed to comply.

NAGPRA is limited in significant ways, and these limits are essential to understanding it. It applies to federal agencies and to institutions, including state and local government bodies and universities, that have possession or control of Native items and receive federal funds. It does not require private collectors to return items, though its trafficking provisions apply more widely to remains and to certain items taken from federal or tribal land. It does not cover the Smithsonian, which has its own statute. It does not apply outside the United States, and the groups that may make claims are those it names: lineal descendants, federally recognized tribes, Alaska Native villages, and Native Hawaiian organizations. Nations without federal recognition, and Indigenous peoples of Canada or Mexico, are not claimants under the statute, though some may appear as cooperating parties or through other arrangements.

The statute also made a political statement. By recognizing Native authority in federal law, Congress rejected the earlier assumption that the institutions that held ancestors were the proper judges of their fate. Congress also located repatriation within federal Indian law and the government-to-government relationship, rather than within general property or museum law, which is why the statute speaks of tribes and consultation. At the same time, it incorporated scientific interests: it allowed a short delay in return where an item was indispensable to a specific study of major benefit to the United States, and it conditioned return on findings of cultural affiliation by a preponderance of the evidence.

Brief excerpt and paraphrase: The statute defines cultural affiliation as a "relationship of shared group identity" that can be reasonably traced between a present-day tribe or Native Hawaiian organization and an identifiable earlier group. In paraphrase, it creates an order of priority for ownership of remains and items found on federal or tribal land, giving first priority to lineal descendants, then to the tribe on whose land the item was found, then to the tribe with the closest cultural affiliation, and in some cases to a tribe aboriginally connected to the area. It requires museums and agencies to prepare summaries and inventories and to consult with affected nations.

Analysis: The statute's structure shows its priorities. Lineal descent and tribal authority come first; institutions are required to act, not merely to consider requests; and the categories of protected material are defined by law and are not left to curatorial judgment. Its reliance on a cultural-affiliation standard created a bridge between scientific and Indigenous evidence, but also opened the later disputes discussed in Sections 125.9 to 125.12.

Limitations: The text alone does not show how the law worked in practice. Implementation depended on inventories, agency practice, regulations, and court decisions that produced different results in different settings. The statute also reflects compromise, and some of its language, such as the definition of "Native American," proved contested.

Perspective: The statute speaks in the voice of Congress. Native nations, museums, and scientists each shaped it, but none authored it. The perspectives of Indigenous peoples outside federal recognition and of those outside the United States are not represented.

Section 125.6

Human Remains, Funerary Objects, Sacred Objects, and Cultural Patrimony

NAGPRA protects four defined categories, which the statute groups together as cultural items. Understanding the categories is essential, because a belonging is repatriable under NAGPRA only if it fits one of them. The statute is not a general law requiring the return of every Indigenous artifact.

The first category is human remains of Native Americans. The second is funerary objects, items that were placed with a body at the time of death or later as part of a death rite. The statute divides these into associated funerary objects, where the remains are in the possession or control of a museum or agency, and unassociated funerary objects, where the remains are not in the institution's possession but the item can be related to a specific individual or burial site known to the institution. The third category is sacred objects, defined as specific ceremonial objects needed by traditional Native American religious leaders for the practice of traditional religions by present-day adherents. This definition ties the claim to current religious use, which some critics have regarded as narrow, because it requires showing present need and can exclude objects whose ceremonial importance has been interrupted by suppression. The fourth category is objects of cultural patrimony: items having ongoing historical, traditional, or cultural importance central to the group or culture itself, rather than property owned by an individual, and which therefore could not be alienated, appropriated, or conveyed by any individual.

Cultural patrimony is the category that most directly embodies the principle of inalienable communal property developed in Zuni practice and in other nations' law. If an item is cultural patrimony, a seller who had no right to alienate it could not pass good title to a museum, and the nation can claim it even where the museum can show a purchase. This is a significant legal innovation, because it shifts the question from the museum's chain of purchase to the nation's law.

The categories also have boundaries. A great deal of Indigenous material, including everyday tools, items sold by makers for trade, art made for sale, and objects with no funerary, sacred, or patrimonial status, falls outside them. Nations may regard many of these items as important, and may seek to negotiate for their return or access, but NAGPRA does not require return. Items of uncertain status create difficult cases, since the nation may hold knowledge that the museum lacks, and the statute leaves the determination to a consultation process and, if disputed, to the Review Committee, the agencies, and the courts.

To qualify under the statute, the claimant must be an eligible party, the item must be in the control of an institution covered by the Act, and the claimant must establish either lineal descent or cultural affiliation or, for objects, the relevant sacred or patrimonial character. Congress built in a scientific-study exception allowing a limited delay, and it included a requirement that institutions with a right of possession under the Act, who can show they acquired an item with the voluntary consent of a person with authority to alienate it, may keep it. The result is a statute that favors Native claimants in several respects but that requires evidence and process, and these features explain much of the dispute described in the following sections.

Section 125.7

Inventories, Consultation, and Cultural Affiliation

NAGPRA's work in existing collections follows a sequence. Federal agencies and museums were first required to prepare summaries of unassociated funerary objects, sacred objects, and objects of cultural patrimony, due within three years of enactment, and detailed item-by-item inventories of human remains and associated funerary objects, due within five years. They were required to consult with tribes and Native Hawaiian organizations, to notify those with a likely connection, and to publish notices in the Federal Register of inventories completed and of intended repatriations. After notice and a waiting period, if no competing claim emerged, the institution transferred the remains or items to the claimant.

The historical deadlines and the contemporary workflow must be kept distinct. The original summaries and inventories were attempts to expose the scale and identity of holdings that many institutions had never systematically examined in consultation with descendants. Summaries were not interchangeable with inventories: the former addressed specified cultural-item categories at a broader level, while the latter catalogued ancestors and associated funerary objects with itemized determinations. Even when an institution met a filing deadline, a summary could omit information important to claimants, and an inventory based solely on old accession labels could repeat collectors’ errors. Consultation could disclose that a label naming one people actually concealed a different community’s association, or that a documented purchase concerned an item that was never individually saleable.

The critical step was determining cultural affiliation. The statute defines it as a relationship of shared group identity that can be reasonably traced historically or prehistorically between a present-day tribe or organization and an identifiable earlier group, and it directs that affiliation be established by a preponderance of the evidence from sources including geography, kinship, biology, archaeology, linguistics, folklore, oral tradition, and historical evidence. Institutions often interpreted this standard strictly and relied on their own records. Many items, especially older remains lacking documentation, were designated "culturally unidentifiable," a category not found in the statute itself that the regulations of 2010 later addressed. This designation left many ancestors in storage, and the number of individuals in this category was large.

Under the revised process, cultural affiliation turns on a reasonable connection supported by the available evidence; institutions are not licensed to demand an uninterrupted documentary genealogy across centuries before considering Indigenous traditional knowledge. Geographic location and acquisition history can support a connection, as can oral traditions, biological evidence, archaeological findings, or other relevant information. This does not mean every claimant automatically prevails: competing claims require attention to stronger relationships and to the rules governing priority. It does mean that missing museum paperwork cannot be treated as proof that no connection exists. The National Park Service’s cultural-affiliation guidance is particularly helpful in distinguishing a historical test of relationship from the institution’s former ability to certify its own collection as indefinitely unaffiliated.

The 2024 regulations changed the process significantly. They directed museums and agencies to defer to the Indigenous knowledge of lineal descendants, tribes, and Native Hawaiian organizations when determining affiliation, set standards for consultation, replaced the culturally unidentifiable category with a duty to seek affiliation through consultation with nations connected by geography, and required institutions to update inventories on a schedule that runs into the later 2020s. These changes are discussed more fully in Section 125.12. The earlier requirements were in place for more than three decades, and the experience of that period explains what the revisions sought to correct.

Everyday Life and Social History

The Work of Return

Repatriation is carried out by people, usually in small offices with limited budgets. Within tribal governments, tribal historic preservation officers, cultural resource staff, elders, and religious leaders receive notices, travel to examine collections, speak with relatives and neighbors, and decide how to respond. Within museums, collections managers search catalogs and storerooms, consult files, and write reports. For many Indigenous participants the work is painful, since examining the remains of ancestors or sacred objects may carry ceremonial risk, and communities often develop protocols for how people may handle or view them. Return itself usually involves ceremony: transport, receiving, cleansing, and reburial or return to ritual use, in forms specific to each nation and frequently closed to outsiders.

The surviving written record of this work is largely institutional, consisting of notices, inventories, and agency reports. Everyday experience is documented mainly through Indigenous testimony, oral history, and the reports of staff, and historians must treat the paperwork as the visible surface of a much larger body of labor.

Section 125.8

Burial Protection, Archaeological Discovery, and Federal Responsibilities

NAGPRA contains a second set of rules that apply to burials and cultural items that are excavated or discovered on federal or tribal lands, as distinct from items already in museums. These provisions respond to a different historical problem: the continuing disturbance of Native graves by construction, erosion, looting, and research. They have not received the public attention given to museum return, but they structure much of the day-to-day work of land-managing agencies such as the National Park Service, the Bureau of Land Management, the Forest Service, and the Army Corps of Engineers.

The statute distinguishes intentional excavation from inadvertent discovery. Intentional excavation of Native human remains or cultural items on federal or tribal land requires a permit under the Archaeological Resources Protection Act and prior consultation with the relevant tribe or, on tribal land, the tribe's consent. Inadvertent discoveries, such as those found during construction or erosion, require the person who finds them to stop activity in the area and notify the responsible agency, with a waiting period during which the agency consults the tribe. For ownership of what is found, the statute applies an order of priority beginning with lineal descendants, then the tribe on whose land the item was found, then the tribe with the closest cultural affiliation, and in some circumstances a tribe aboriginally connected to the area.

The provisions also have jurisdictional limits. They apply only to federal and tribal lands and not to private or most state lands, where state burial laws, many of which were adopted after the 1970s, apply and differ widely. Remains discovered on federal land are thus subject to a different legal regime from remains found a short distance away on private property. NAGPRA also created criminal penalties for trafficking in Native human remains and in cultural items excavated from federal or tribal land, and Congress later strengthened enforcement through the STOP Act, passed in 2022, which addressed the export of items obtained in violation of the law.

The relation between these provisions and sacred places should be kept distinct. NAGPRA protects burials and defined cultural items and does not give tribes a general right to protect sacred landscapes, which is a subject of Chapter 119. It also does not resolve questions of land ownership. Even so, the burial provisions have been among the most direct means by which tribes have asserted a role over federal land management, and many tribal governments maintain procedures, agreements, and staff to monitor excavation and respond to discoveries. As with museum collections, the promise of the law has depended on the resources and cooperation of agencies and on the consistent consultation of tribes.

Division IIIRepatriation in Practice: Disputes, Evidence, and Reform
Section 125.9

Indigenous Knowledge and the Evidence of Cultural Affiliation

The statute's list of evidence for affiliation invites a question that has proven central to repatriation: how should different kinds of knowledge be weighed? Oral tradition, kinship histories, place names, ceremonial knowledge, geography, archaeological findings, linguistic reconstruction, biological and genetic data, and archival records can all bear on whether a present-day nation is related to an earlier group. These sources do not always agree, and each reflects a different way of knowing. The framework in Chapter 1, which treats oral tradition as a legitimate but interpretively demanding source, applies directly here.

Historically, institutions tended to give priority to the kinds of evidence they produced and trusted: catalog records, excavation reports, and measurements. These records are valuable, but they were created by collectors and scientists and often say little about the communities concerned. Early determinations frequently asked tribes to meet a standard of proof expressed in the institution's terms, for example by requiring a continuous archaeological sequence or documentary record linking a historic nation to a much older site. Indigenous nations pointed out that this standard was often impossible to meet, because the written and excavated record is partial and because displacement, epidemic disease, and removal had disrupted the documentation of their history. The tendency to privilege institutional records over community knowledge has been among the most frequently criticized features of the early implementation of NAGPRA.

Different forms of evidence also answer different questions. Archaeological evidence can show patterns of material culture across time and space but cannot, on its own, show language, political identity, or descent; genetic evidence can show biological relationships among populations, but ancestry is not identical with cultural affiliation or political identity; and oral tradition can preserve knowledge of migration, place, and kinship, along with the responsibilities attached to particular places and objects. A responsible determination weighs these sources together, recognizes uncertainty, and does not treat any one as automatically decisive. The statutory standard of a preponderance of the evidence means that the claim must be more likely than not, not proven beyond doubt.

Genetic evidence can illuminate ancestry but presents a further distinction between biological relatedness and the legal or cultural relationships asserted under NAGPRA. A genetic sample might identify affinities among populations without establishing the ceremonial custodian of a specific belonging or the modern government best placed to receive it. Conversely, the absence of recoverable DNA does not cancel oral-history or geographic evidence. The 2024 framework explicitly places Indigenous traditional knowledge within the evidentiary process, rather than treating it merely as supplementary testimony. Historians should therefore ask not whether one kind of evidence defeats all the others, but what question each source can answer, who generated it, and whether the institution’s chosen standard of proof has excluded information relevant to the statutory decision.

Historical Vocabulary
Cultural Affiliation and Indigenous Knowledge

Definition. Cultural affiliation is the legal term for a relationship of shared group identity that can be reasonably traced between a present-day tribe or Native Hawaiian organization and an earlier group. Indigenous knowledge, as the term is used in the 2024 regulations and in scholarship, refers to knowledge held by Indigenous peoples about their histories, places, relationships, and responsibilities, transmitted through oral tradition, practice, and community institutions.

Historical context. The statute named oral tradition and folklore among the types of evidence for affiliation, but institutions long treated them as secondary. The revised regulations require agencies and museums to defer to Indigenous knowledge in affiliation determinations, a significant change in the hierarchy of evidence.

Why it matters. The weight given to Indigenous knowledge influences which claims succeed and who decides when sources conflict.

Potential misunderstanding. Affiliation is a legal and cultural relationship, not a statement of genetic purity or of unbroken cultural sameness. Nations changed over time, as did every society, and shared group identity does not require that nothing changed.

The experience of repatriation has led some scholars and communities to argue that Indigenous knowledge should be treated not as a supplement to science but as a parallel system of expertise, and that institutions should share authority over interpretation. Others, including some archaeologists, emphasize that scientific evidence provides tools for testing claims that no community can fully check by itself. This disagreement, which this chapter takes up as a historical debate in Section 125.11, does not divide neatly between Indigenous and non-Indigenous people, since many Native archaeologists and anthropologists work within both traditions.

Section 125.10

Competing Claims, Descendant Communities, and Cultural Authority

Disagreement arises not only between nations and museums but among Indigenous claimants themselves. Several recurring situations produce competing claims. Two or more nations may claim the same remains or sites because their historical territories overlapped, because they share ancestors, or because earlier migrations, wars, removals, and treaties moved peoples across the landscape. Lineal descendants, such as families, may differ with tribal governments about what should be done. Native Hawaiian organizations and other bodies may represent different constituencies in the same island or district. Remains or items with unclear provenance, lacking information about where they came from, may be claimable by many nations or by none that can be demonstrated.

The resulting choices are often more subtle than a single competition for exclusive ownership. A burial place may lie within territory now governed by one nation but have a longer history linking it to several peoples. A community displaced through removal may have responsibility to ancestors far from its present jurisdiction. An artifact may have been acquired from one person but be governed by a ceremonial society whose members belong to more than one political community. In each case the museum’s desire for one claimant and one mailing address can distort a more complex history of belonging. Claimants may prefer joint consultation, sequential responsibilities, restricted public identification, or an agreement not to disclose the resting place. Such arrangements require enough time and trust for the nations concerned to negotiate on their own terms.

The statute and regulations provide some tools. Lineal descendants have the highest priority for remains where they can be identified, and the Act envisions that if multiple tribes claim, the institution may retain the items until the claimants agree or a court decides. Many disputes in practice are resolved by agreement: nations negotiate joint claims, divide responsibilities, or allow one nation to act on behalf of others. The Review Committee may also facilitate resolution. In many cases, as in the Ancient One dispute, several nations form a coalition so that a single entity can represent their shared interest.

The rules nevertheless require decisions. Known lineal descendants may have priority for certain categories; for other cases, the applicable regulation distinguishes cultural affiliation, location, acquisition history, and claims supported by a stronger relationship. Museums and federal agencies should document consultation and must not use disagreement as a pretext to abandon legal duties. The 2024 rule on determining cultural affiliation outlines how additional evidence may be presented and evaluated. A review committee can offer recommendations or facilitate discussion, but it cannot replace every governmental or judicial decision. Many successful resolutions emerge from nations agreeing on a common claim, identifying a lead representative, or jointly specifying the treatment of remains. Those methods preserve Indigenous agency more effectively than forcing each nation into litigation against all others.

Competing claims show that repatriation is not the application of a simple rule but a continuing political and cultural process. They also show how historical dispossession continues to matter. A nation that was removed from its homeland may find itself far from the burial places of its ancestors, with limited legal standing under the law on the lands where they are found. Others hold cultural responsibility for ancestors who lived in an area long before the current boundaries, according to their own knowledge. The existence of disagreement, however, does not erase the cultural responsibilities involved. Many nations have emphasized that they do not seek to prevail over other Indigenous peoples but to carry out obligations to ancestors in a manner consistent with their own law, and that a disagreement among nations is a reason for more consultation, not for returning authority to institutions.

A Disputed Collection: How Participants Understand the Same Claim

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing positions documented in the repatriation literature. They are not quotations from any named individual.

From the perspective of a tribal cultural official: The ancestors are relatives to whom the nation owes care. The claim rests on the nation's own knowledge of its history and law, and delay by an institution prolongs a continuing injury. Evidence is welcome when it supports the community's knowledge, but the community does not need outside proof to know who its ancestors are.
From the perspective of a museum collections manager: The institution holds legal duties and public trust responsibilities, and it must follow the statute, document its decisions, and avoid returning items to the wrong claimant. Incomplete records, limited staff, and multiple claimants make the process slow, and the manager may sympathize with the claim while being unable to resolve it alone.
From the perspective of a research scientist: The remains may contain information about human history, health, and migration that cannot be obtained otherwise, and once reburied they are lost to study. Some scientists accept that tribes should control decisions, while others argue that their research serves a wider public interest and should be weighed.
From the perspective of a competing claimant nation: A claim by another nation may seem to ignore the second nation's own ties to the same place or ancestors. The nation seeks recognition of its own responsibility, often through coalition and negotiation, and may regard institutional delay as favoring whoever has the most resources.
Section 125.11

The Ancient One: Kennewick Man and the Struggle over Ancestral Remains

Case Study 3

The Ancient One, 1996–2017

Participants. The Umatilla, Yakama, Nez Perce, and Colville tribes and the Wanapum Band; the United States Army Corps of Engineers; the Department of the Interior; a group of scientists who sued for study access; the federal courts; Congress; and geneticists.

Objectives. The nations sought to rebury the remains as an ancestor. The scientists sought to study them. The Corps, which controlled the land where they were found, sought to comply with the law and defend its decisions.

Evidence. Radiocarbon dating placing the remains at roughly nine thousand years old; skeletal analysis; later ancient DNA research; and Columbia Plateau oral traditions and cultural knowledge.

Decisions. The Corps' initial decision to repatriate was overturned in court; a 2004 appeals ruling held that the remains had not been shown to be "Native American" under the statute; later genetic evidence and federal reconsideration supported affiliation; and Congress directed transfer in 2016.

Consequences. The case tested the statute's definitions, raised public awareness of repatriation, influenced regulatory change, and ended with the reburial of the Ancient One in 2017.

On July 28, 1996, two young men watching hydroplane races on the Columbia River at Kennewick, Washington, found a human skull in shallow water near the shore. The county coroner and an anthropologist who examined the remains initially considered them possibly those of a European settler, because of skull features, but a radiocarbon test showed the individual to be roughly nine thousand years old. The land was managed by the Army Corps of Engineers, which took custody. The Umatilla, Yakama, Nez Perce, and Colville tribes and the Wanapum Band, whose homelands include the middle Columbia River, asserted that the remains were those of an ancestor and sought reburial under NAGPRA. Their claim rested on their long-standing connection to the region and on the oral traditions of their peoples, which describe their presence since the beginning of time.

A group of anthropologists and archaeologists sued in 1996 to prevent the transfer, arguing that the remains were exceptionally ancient and scientifically important, and that no demonstrated link tied them to any present-day tribe. The litigation, known as Bonnichsen v. United States, ran for years. In 2002 a federal district judge concluded that the Corps' decision to repatriate had not been properly supported. In 2004 the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, holding that the statutory definition of "Native American" referred to a tribe, people, or culture that is indigenous to the United States and that the evidence had not shown a relationship between the remains and a presently existing tribe. During the dispute the Corps stored the remains at the Burke Museum at the University of Washington, and its own handling, including actions to protect the discovery site, drew criticism from both sides. Scientists studied the remains under court-supervised conditions.

The legal ruling did not end the matter, and later scientific work changed the evidentiary picture. Interior Secretary Bruce Babbitt had determined in 2000 that the Ancient One was Native American and culturally affiliated with the claimant tribes, but that earlier administrative determination was rejected during the litigation; the Ninth Circuit upheld the relevant judicial ruling in 2004. In 2015, a team led by Eske Willerslev published a genome study in Nature finding that the Ancient One was genetically closer to present-day Native Americans than to other sampled populations, with a close relationship among sampled groups to the Colville. Following additional review of this research, the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers determined in April 2016 that the remains were Native American for purposes of NAGPRA. This was not a 2015 Department of the Interior cultural-affiliation determination. Congress then directed the transfer in the Water Infrastructure Improvements for the Nation Act of December 2016. The Corps transferred the remains to the Washington State Department of Archaeology and Historic Preservation on February 17, 2017, for delivery to the claimant nations; representatives of the five nations reburied the Ancient One on February 18, 2017. The dated official account of the 2016 Corps determination, the 2017 transfer announcement, and the Burke Museum chronology help separate the decisions.

The Bonnichsen litigation is especially valuable because it shows that the meaning of statutory categories can change with the evidence available to decision-makers. The government’s 2000 determination and the courts’ later rejection belong to one administrative and judicial sequence; the 2015 DNA findings and the Corps’ 2016 determination belong to another. The final return also depended on a separate act of Congress, which established the legal transfer mechanism rather than merely announcing a scientific consensus. Students can test each step against the 2004 Ninth Circuit opinion and the government’s later public notices. Keeping the sequence intact prevents a misleading narrative in which a single test settled a dispute involving evidence, administration, legislation, and Indigenous responsibilities.

Students should keep four kinds of authority distinct. Archaeological findings concern what the evidence shows about age, anatomy, and relationship. Judicial decisions determined what the statute meant and whether the agency had acted lawfully. Legislative intervention reflected Congress's judgment about outcome and was not itself a scientific or judicial finding. Indigenous claims rested on the nations' own knowledge, law, and responsibility, which they asserted before the genetic work and independently of it. The 2015 genome study supported the nations' claim in the eyes of many outside observers, but the nations did not regard their relationship to the Ancient One as dependent on that study. Some scientists, in turn, have argued that the outcome showed a loss of scientific access and that the law should balance research interests more explicitly.

Historical Debate

Who Should Have Final Authority over Ancestral Remains and Sacred Objects?

The question: When Indigenous communities and scientific or cultural institutions disagree about the treatment of ancestral remains and sacred objects, who should decide? The question combines two issues that are often confused: what the evidence shows, and who has the right to make the decision.

Interpretation A — Indigenous nations should hold final authority: Many Indigenous leaders and scholars, along with some archaeologists, argue that the remains are ancestors and sacred belongings of living nations, that the nations have a right of self-determination over their heritage, and that outside institutions acquired the items in unequal circumstances. On this view science may inform but should not override the nation's decision, and consent should be the governing principle.
Interpretation B — Scientific and public interests should carry weight: Some scientists argue that exceptionally old remains or unique material may belong to a shared human heritage, that research benefits many people including Indigenous communities, and that decisions should rest on demonstrated cultural affiliation, so that remains with no demonstrated link to a living nation are not automatically returned.
Interpretation C — Shared decision-making and negotiated protocols: Others, including many museum professionals and Indigenous archaeologists, argue that the choice between control and access is too stark, and that agreed protocols, consultation, limits on destructive testing, and collaborative research can honor both obligations in many cases.

Evidence: The positions draw on the statute and its legislative history, court opinions such as Bonnichsen, the genetic and archaeological findings of the Ancient One case, the experience of repatriations in which research continued by agreement, and museum ethics codes.

Points of disagreement: The dispute concerns interpretation of the statutory definitions, the weight of oral tradition relative to scientific evidence, the value of research, and the proper role of agencies and courts. It also concerns terminology, such as whether "affiliation" requires continuity of identity.

Why it matters: If the question is about evidence, the answer depends on the strength of the data. If it is about authority, the data matter only to the extent that law requires them. The 2024 regulations adopted a framework in which consent and deference to Indigenous knowledge carry greater weight, reflecting a decision about authority and not a new scientific finding.

Student task: Distinguish the evidentiary questions from the authority questions in the Ancient One case, and explain which position you find more persuasive and what evidence or principle would change your mind.

Section 125.12

Implementation, Institutional Resistance, and the 2024 NAGPRA Reforms

NAGPRA has produced the return of a very large number of ancestors and cultural items, but implementation has been uneven. Institutions missed or delayed deadlines for inventories. Some produced inventories that were incomplete or that classified many individuals as culturally unidentifiable. Staffing and funding were frequently inadequate, in museums and in tribal offices, which had to respond to a flow of notices with limited resources. A report by the Government Accountability Office in 2010 found that key federal agencies had not fully complied with the statute. Enforcement through civil penalties was rarely used. Public investigations in 2023 reported that institutions still held the remains of a very large number of Native individuals, on the order of one hundred thousand, and that a small number of institutions held a disproportionate share. Some institutions resisted by contesting claims or by interpreting their legal duties narrowly, though others sought to cooperate, and many changed their practices over time.

For communities seeking return, the practical costs of consultation were often as consequential as the legal standards. Museum collections might be scattered across several repositories, catalogued under obsolete names, or divided among departments that had never coordinated with one another. A tribe receiving notices could need staff fluent in historical records, cultural protocols, federal procedure, travel arrangements, and the logistics of respectful care. Those capacities rarely grew at the same rate as the number of notifications. Institutions, too, differed: some established dedicated repatriation programs and sustained consultation over many years, while others relied on short-term grants or treated requests as interruptions to normal work. These inequalities explain why a statute requiring action did not yield the same pace of return everywhere.

Reform came through regulations and legislation. In 2010 the Department of the Interior adopted a rule for the disposition of culturally unidentifiable Native American human remains. It was not an unconditional instruction to give every such remain to any requesting nation. Instead, the rule provided a process for requests, consultation, and priority determinations that took account of geographic and aboriginal-land connections even where the museum had not established cultural affiliation under the earlier procedure. That rule generated its own difficult questions about which communities should be consulted and how to treat limited records (see the March 15, 2010 Federal Register rule). Congress separately passed the STOP Act in 2022, targeting trafficking and prohibited export of covered Indigenous cultural items. The major administrative revision was announced by Secretary of the Interior Deb Haaland in December 2023 and published on December 13, 2023; it took effect on January 12, 2024 (official final rule).

The 2024 regulations changed multiple stages of the process. They place greater procedural emphasis on consultation and direct museums and federal agencies to defer to the Native American traditional knowledge of lineal descendants, Indian Tribes, and Native Hawaiian organizations, while evaluating the available evidence for cultural affiliation. Rather than continuing the old operational category of culturally unidentifiable remains as a default endpoint, they direct a renewed effort to identify affiliation, including reasonably established geographic and acquisition-history connections. They establish new inventory and notice procedures, with some inventories due by January 10, 2029, depending on prior compliance history. Most visibly, the duty-of-care requirement in 43 CFR §10.1(d)(3) requires covered museums and federal agencies to obtain free, prior, and informed consent from the appropriate lineal descendants, Indian Tribes, or Native Hawaiian organizations before exhibition of, access to, or research on human remains or cultural items. It does not impose that duty indiscriminately on every private owner or foreign museum. Major American museums reconsidered exhibits and research access when the rule took effect; institutional concerns about staffing and interpretation did not eliminate the new consent obligation. The regulatory text and the National Park Service inventory guidance allow readers to distinguish obligations from commentary.

The revised rules also changed the meaning of a museum’s duty of care while a claim remains open. Institutions must safeguard the materials and not treat the period before repatriation as unlimited permission for exhibition, digital reproduction, or scientific sampling. The practical questions are specific: Which community must be approached about an object attributed to several nations? Can an existing photograph remain publicly accessible? How should staff handle requests by researchers when consent has not been obtained? A responsible answer starts from the item’s status, the institution’s coverage under the law, and consultation with the relevant descendants or nations. These distinctions make the regulations a change in governance, not just a new timetable of paperwork.

Section 125.13

Canada: First Nations Repatriation and Museum Reform

Canada has no comprehensive national equivalent to NAGPRA, and its repatriation history follows a different course. The legal framework consists of general museum and heritage law, provincial legislation, institutional policies, treaties and agreements, and, increasingly, constitutional and reconciliation commitments discussed in Chapter 117. Change came largely through Indigenous organizing, public controversy, and negotiation between First Nations and museums, and not through a single federal statute.

One important background was the suppression of Indigenous ceremony. Restrictions on the potlatch and related practices, added to the Indian Act in the 1880s and examined in Chapter 85, led to confiscations such as the 1922 seizure of Kwakwaka'wakw regalia after the Cranmer potlatch. In the 1970s and 1980s, Kwakwaka'wakw communities negotiated with the National Museum of Man, the institution that later became the Canadian Museum of Civilization and then the Canadian Museum of History, for the return of hundreds of items, on condition that suitable facilities be created. Cultural centers at Cape Mudge and at Alert Bay, the latter operated by the U'mista Cultural Society, opened around 1979 and 1980 to receive them. This episode became a model for Canadian return and for community-controlled museums.

A second turning point was a 1988 exhibition, "The Spirit Sings," organized by the Glenbow Museum in Calgary as part of the cultural program for the Winter Olympics. The Lubicon Lake Cree, engaged in a long dispute with governments over land, called for a boycott because the exhibition was sponsored by an oil company active on lands they claimed, and because the show displayed Indigenous heritage without Indigenous direction. Some lenders withdrew, and the controversy drew national attention. In its aftermath, the Assembly of First Nations and the Canadian Museums Association created a joint task force in 1990 to recommend a new relationship between museums and Indigenous peoples. Its 1992 report, Turning the Page: Forging New Partnerships Between Museums and First Peoples, called for Indigenous involvement in museum governance and interpretation, access to collections, training and funding for Indigenous museum professionals, and the repatriation of human remains and sacred and other cultural objects in appropriate circumstances. The report is available through the Canadian Museums Association and the Assembly of First Nations, and students can read it directly as a primary document.

The task force's recommendations did not become law, but they shaped museum policies across the country. Many institutions adopted repatriation and consultation policies in the 1990s and later, and Indigenous curators and cultural officers entered museums in greater numbers. Provinces and territories took varied steps. Alberta enacted legislation in 2000 to provide for the return of sacred ceremonial objects to First Nations of the province, an unusual instance of statutory repatriation in Canada. In other places, agreements between nations and museums, and treaty and self-government negotiations, set terms for return and for the care of cultural property. The report of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission in 2015 included calls to action on museums and archives, and the Canadian Museums Association followed with a national review in 2022, Moved to Action, which assessed museum practice against the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples. Canada's own legislation to implement the Declaration, enacted in 2021, provides an additional reference point.

The 1992 Canadian task-force report, Turning the Page: Forging New Partnerships Between Museums and First Peoples, gave a public vocabulary to negotiations already underway. It addressed improved access, interpretation, the care and return of human remains and sacred objects, and the participation of First Peoples in museum decisions. Because the report was jointly sponsored by the Assembly of First Nations and the Canadian Museums Association, it also illustrates the possibility—and the difficulty—of developing shared professional commitments without an overarching statutory repatriation right. A 1992 bibliographic catalogue record identifies the original publication; this catalogue is not represented here as the complete report text.

The absence of a single statute has had consequences. The Canadian approach is flexible and has allowed Indigenous nations to negotiate terms that fit their own laws and protocols, but it depends on the willingness of institutions and on the capacity of nations to carry out negotiations, and it produces variation among provinces, institutions, and nations. Where a federal statute offers claimants a specific legal right, a negotiated system offers possibilities and uncertainties of a different kind.

Section 125.14

Canadian Repatriation Agreements and the Return of Cultural Heritage

Canadian repatriation is best understood through particular negotiations. These involve different kinds of nations, institutions, and material, from ancestral remains held in museums to ceremonial belongings and community collections, and they show a range of arrangements: permanent return, loan, shared care, and the creation of new institutions.

Case Study 4

The Canadian Museum of History's Sacred Materials Project

Participants. The Canadian Museum of History (formerly the Canadian Museum of Civilization), First Nations and other Indigenous communities and cultural officers, and museum curators and repatriation staff.

Objectives. For the museum, to respond to requests and to the 1992 task force report by developing a systematic process; for communities, to recover sacred materials and ancestors and to control their care.

Evidence. Museum acquisition records, community knowledge and protocols, and consultation with elders and cultural leaders about the status of particular items.

Decisions. The museum established its sacred-materials work in 1993 and developed policies enabling communities to request return, negotiating the terms with each nation. Returns and shared-stewardship arrangements followed.

Consequences. The project illustrates a policy-based, negotiated model suited to a country without a national statute, and it contributed to changing norms in other Canadian museums.

Institutional documentation: Canadian Museum of History, repatriation program and Sacred Materials Project.

The Canadian Museum of History is a national institution, and its approach has been influential. The museum's program, begun in 1993, developed procedures through which Indigenous communities could request the return of sacred and ceremonial materials and, in some circumstances, ancestral remains, and through which staff worked with communities to determine appropriate care. The details of any return are specific to the nation and the item, and many are not publicly described out of respect for ceremonial restrictions. This private character is itself a historical reality: the written record shows that returns occurred but often provides few particulars of what was returned or how it was received.

Other cases show the range. The Kwakwaka'wakw returns of the late twentieth century resulted in the first community-controlled cultural centers designed to house repatriated regalia. The Haida, working through a repatriation committee and the Council of the Haida Nation, have brought home hundreds of ancestors from museums in Canada, the United States, and Europe, and have created cultural institutions on Haida Gwaii to care for them. In the Prairies, Blackfoot nations have pursued the return of sacred bundles, which are understood as living and transferable only through ceremony, using both the Alberta legislation and negotiation. Nisg̱a’a leaders secured the museum’s agreement to return the House of Ni’isjoohl memorial pole in 2022; it traveled from Scotland to the Nass Valley in 2023 and was raised at the Nisg̱a’a Museum in October 2023. This overseas return shows the significance of descendant-house leadership, museum negotiations, and community protocols outside a NAGPRA-like statute (see Museums Galleries Scotland’s account).

The 2023 memorial-pole return is especially instructive because the House of Ni’isjoohl’s claim was articulated through Nisg̱a’a hereditary and house responsibilities, not merely as a national request for an attractive museum piece. The return required negotiations about who could speak for the pole, how it would be prepared for travel, how curators would surrender physical custody, and how it would be received on the Nass River. National Museums Scotland had made its transfer decision in 2022, but the journey, ceremonies, and raising took place in 2023. The historical outcome is therefore more precise than saying simply that a museum “agreed to repatriate” an artifact: the community regained the ability to care for a living part of its inherited history.

The agreements differ in important ways. Some are with federal institutions, some with provincial museums, and some with universities or private collectors. Some involve payment or exchange, which can be controversial because they may suggest that nations must purchase what was taken. Some transfer legal title, while others recognize Indigenous ownership but leave physical custody with the museum under agreed terms. Provincial jurisdiction over museums, the lack of a federal rule, and the wide variety of Indigenous governance traditions mean that a nation's route to return depends on where the item is held and on its own capacity to negotiate. Students should therefore resist the temptation to treat Canadian repatriation as a weaker version of the American model. It rests on different assumptions, including the view of many First Nations that their own laws determine how their cultural property is to be handled, and that agreements with museums are exercises of authority they already possess.

Section 125.15

Mexico: Archaeological Heritage, Indigenous Communities, and Restitution

Mexico's approach grows from a different historical foundation. Mexican law, from the early twentieth century and in the federal law of 1972, treats archaeological monuments as the property of the nation, protected by the state and not available for private ownership or export. The National Institute of Anthropology and History, founded in 1939, is the federal agency responsible for protecting, studying, and managing the country's archaeological, historical, and anthropological heritage. The state has used this framework to oppose looting and the international antiquities trade, to recover items from abroad, and to build national museums, including the National Museum of Anthropology in Mexico City. Treaties such as the 1970 cooperation agreement with the United States on the recovery of stolen archaeological and cultural property, and Mexico's participation in the UNESCO convention of 1970, support this effort.

Case Study 5

Mexico's Cultural Heritage Protection Framework

Participants. The Mexican federal government and INAH; Indigenous and Afro-Mexican communities; community museum organizers; foreign museums, auction houses, and governments.

Objectives. The state sought to protect national heritage and recover items from abroad; communities sought recognition of their authority over living cultural heritage and sacred sites.

Evidence. National heritage statutes, archaeological registers, auction and museum records, and community testimony.

Decisions. The federal law of 1972 vested archaeological monuments in the nation; a federal law enacted in January 2022 recognized Indigenous and Afro-Mexican peoples and communities as holders of their cultural heritage and set protections against appropriation.

Consequences. The framework made national return from abroad a state-led process while opening a legal space for community authority, though the relationship between national ownership and community rights remains unsettled.

The result is a system of national ownership that differs from NAGPRA in a fundamental way. NAGPRA vests authority in Native nations and lineal descendants; the Mexican framework for archaeological material vests it in the state. International return to Mexico, such as the recovery of antiquities from foreign collections or sales, therefore benefits the national patrimony, and the objects typically go to national or state institutions, not to a specific Indigenous community. An illustration is the long-running request of Mexico for the return from Vienna of a feathered headdress associated with the Aztec world, which Austrian authorities have declined to send because of its fragility, in a dispute framed as one between states. Another earlier example is the removal in 1964 of a monumental stone figure from the village of Coatlinchan to Mexico City, an action that the local community resisted and that has often been cited as showing the tension between national and local claims.

Indigenous peoples in Mexico, who account for many millions of people and speak dozens of languages, have long regarded their heritage as a living inheritance, not only as archaeological material. Constitutional reform in 2001 recognized Indigenous peoples and communities and their right to preserve their cultures, and a further reform in 2024, noted in Chapter 1, recognized them as subjects of public law. In this setting the federal cultural heritage law published January 17, 2022 is significant. It recognizes those peoples as holders of their cultural heritage, including knowledge, expressions, and symbols, and aims to prevent appropriation and commercial exploitation without their consent. Mexican communities have also built their own museums: the community museum movement in Oaxaca, beginning with the museum at Santa Ana del Valle in the 1980s, is a well-known example of communal heritage stewardship.

It is important to distinguish three things: the return of an item to Mexico, its restitution to an Indigenous community that regards it as its own, and the community's authority over how it is cared for or displayed. Mexican state recovery does not automatically accomplish the second or the third. The history of Indigenous Mexico and the northern borderlands is treated further in Chapter 141, and the contrast with NAGPRA shows that a legal regime built on national ownership can protect heritage from export while leaving questions of Indigenous authority open.

Section 125.16

Alaska, the Arctic, and Greenlandic Repatriation

The Arctic is divided among several states, and Inuit and other Indigenous peoples live across their boundaries. Repatriation there therefore involves several legal systems. In Alaska, Alaska Native villages are eligible claimants under NAGPRA and the Smithsonian statute, while the Alaska Native corporations created by the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act, discussed in Chapter 115, are business entities and not tribal governments in the same sense. The Larsen Bay return of 1991, described in Section 125.4, was an early Alaska case, and it led to the creation of the Alutiiq Museum in Kodiak in 1995 as a community institution. In Canada, Inuit heritage is addressed through the negotiated land-claims agreements examined in Chapter 116 and through museum policy. In Greenland, the context was one of colonial collecting by Denmark and a modern movement for self-government.

Case Study 6

Greenland's Utimut Process

Participants. The Danish National Museum and other Danish institutions; the Greenland Home Rule government, formed in 1979, and later Greenland's self-government; the Greenland National Museum and Archives.

Objectives. Greenland sought the return of archaeological and ethnographic material that had accumulated in Denmark, and the right to determine how its cultural heritage was held and presented. Danish institutions sought to resolve the relationship in a way that preserved scholarly cooperation.

Evidence. Museum catalogs and registers, archaeological records, and negotiated lists of material.

Decisions. From 1982 to 2001, under the process called Utimut, approximately 35,000 archaeological and ethnographic objects were transferred from Denmark to Greenland.

Consequences. The process helped build a national museum in Greenland and became an example of a long, negotiated return between institutions of a former colonial relationship.

Institutional record: Greenland National Museum and Archives, collections and Utimut.

Danish collections of Greenlandic material grew over centuries, through expeditions, missionaries, officials, and scientists. When Greenland obtained Home Rule in 1979 and took over many areas of government, cultural heritage became a political question as well as a scholarly one. The negotiations that followed led to a process that Greenlanders called Utimut, a word meaning to return. Beginning in 1982, material was moved in stages from Danish institutions to Greenlandic ones, in cooperation with museum staff on both sides. By 2001 about 35,000 objects had been transferred, a figure that includes both archaeological finds and ethnographic items. The Qilakitsoq mummies, discovered in the 1970s and studied in Denmark, were returned to Greenland and placed within Greenlandic museum stewardship; their documented display history should not be conflated with every component of the much larger Utimut program. The Self-Government Act of 2009 further recognized Greenlanders as a people with a right of self-determination under international law, and heritage has continued to be part of Greenland's relationship with Denmark.

The transfer of collections to Nuuk also posed challenges of institutional capacity: storage, conservation, research, exhibitions, staff expertise, and the public responsibility of a national museum serving communities separated by long distances. The arrangement was an important reversal of the colonial direction of collecting, but it did not mean that every object immediately returned to the village or family from which it had originally departed. It changed the location of stewardship and the terms of professional collaboration, while leaving further questions about community access and interpretation. The Greenland National Museum’s collections account and the research history of the Utimut process document the breadth of this negotiated transfer.

Utimut differs from NAGPRA in important ways. It resulted from negotiation between governments and institutions in a post-colonial relationship and not from a statute giving claimants specific legal rights. The returned material went to a national institution of a self-governing Inuit society, and not to separate descendant communities. Yet it addressed the same underlying issue of who should hold and interpret a people's cultural heritage. The Arctic also shows the importance of cross-border cases. In 1993 the American Museum of Natural History returned to Greenland the remains of four Inughuit who had been brought to New York in 1897 by the explorer Robert Peary and had died there; this case followed its own institutional and diplomatic arrangements, rather than the ordinary museum-claims procedure described in this chapter. Inuit across Alaska, Canada, and Greenland are connected through bodies such as the Inuit Circumpolar Council, founded in 1977, which treats cultural heritage as part of a broader Inuit agenda. Chapter 142 discusses Indigenous peoples and international borders more generally.

Division VMuseums, Cultural Sovereignty, and the Future of Repatriation
Section 125.17

Indigenous Museums and Community-Controlled Cultural Stewardship

Repatriation is closely tied to the growth of Indigenous institutions. Nations that receive ancestors and belongings need places and procedures for their care, and many have built them. The Makah Cultural and Research Center at Neah Bay, which opened in 1979 to house material from the Ozette archaeological site excavated in the 1970s in cooperation with the Makah Tribe, was an early model of a tribal museum that combines archaeology, community interpretation, and cultural education. The U'mista Cultural Centre and the Kwagiulth Museum in British Columbia, the Zuni A:shiwi museum, the Alutiiq Museum in Kodiak, the Mashantucket Pequot Museum, the museum at Warm Springs, and community museums in Oaxaca, among many others, show similar efforts across the continent. In 2004 the National Museum of the American Indian opened in Washington, adding a national institution with Native leadership and a policy of consultation.

Tribal governments have also developed heritage offices, historic preservation officers, archives, and cultural committees. In the United States, amendments to the National Historic Preservation Act in 1992 allowed tribes to assume the functions of State Historic Preservation Officers on tribal lands, and many tribes now maintain tribal historic preservation offices that review projects and respond to NAGPRA notices. Associations of tribal archives, libraries, and museums provide training and networks. These institutions give nations a direct role in determining what is kept, who may view it, and how it is interpreted.

Returned materials serve several functions. For ceremonial belongings, return restores their use in religious practice. For ancestors, return permits reburial under the nation's own ceremonies, which may require rigorous protocols and which are often private. For historical and cultural material, return supports research by community members, the education of younger generations, and the transmission of knowledge. Some nations place returned items in museums, some keep them in restricted storage, and some allow them to decay according to their own beliefs, as in the Zuni case. These choices demonstrate that return does not require a single model of care.

It is important to distinguish physical possession from authority over care and interpretation. A nation may hold an item without holding the resources to conserve it, and a museum may hold an item while a nation has authority over its display and use. Some nations have chosen shared arrangements that leave items in a museum under agreed conditions, because the nation lacks storage or because the museum can provide care they judge beneficial. Others insist on full custody. The common feature is that the nation decides. Indigenous museums also face real difficulties, including funding, staffing, climate-controlled storage, and the burden of managing a large volume of returns, and the capacity of nations varies widely.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Indigenous Care for Ancestors and Sacred Belongings

What Changed

  • Many ancestors and sacred belongings were removed to institutions far from their homelands, and communal care was interrupted for generations
  • Care came to be exercised through new forms: tribal museums, heritage offices, and legal procedures under federal and provincial law
  • Public and institutional views shifted from treating remains as specimens to recognizing consent and Indigenous authority, though unevenly and with continuing disagreement

What Stayed the Same

  • The responsibility of the living to ancestors and to inherited ceremonial property persisted in nations' own laws and memory
  • Many nations continued to seek the return of what was taken, across generations and political circumstances
  • Ceremony remained central to the handling of ancestors and sacred belongings

Timing and experience: Change was gradual in some respects, such as museum ethics, and abrupt in others, such as the passage of statutes. It was experienced most directly by nations whose ancestors were held in institutions, and by museum staff who had to adapt. The pattern matters because it shows that return restored interrupted responsibilities and did not create new ones.

Section 125.18

Museum Ethics, Research Consent, and the Transformation of Exhibition Practices

Museum ethics changed substantially during the period covered by this chapter, partly through law and partly through the work of professional bodies and Indigenous critics. Early codes of ethics focused on legal title, conservation, and public service. Later codes and policies, including those of the International Council of Museums and of national museum associations, came to address human remains, culturally sensitive material, and the rights of source communities. Archaeological and anthropological associations also revised their statements to give greater weight to consultation with descendant communities.

Several issues recur. The first concerns human remains: whether they should be displayed at all, and what research may be done on them. The second concerns ceremonial restrictions. Some objects may be seen only by certain people or at certain times, and public display or photographs may violate those rules. The third concerns provenance, the documented history of acquisition, and whether institutions should publish and review it. The fourth concerns destructive testing, such as taking samples for dating or DNA analysis, which cannot be reversed. The fifth concerns access and publication, including who may photograph, publish, or digitally share images of sensitive material. Practices that were once routine, such as displaying skeletal remains or reproducing images of sacred objects in catalogs, have been questioned and in many institutions discontinued.

A practical change concerns the museum record itself. Catalogues once organized around collectors and typologies increasingly require fields documenting cultural relationships, access restrictions, community-preferred names, and the history of decisions about care. These records can be corrected, but the corrections may expose uncertainty that museums previously concealed behind confident labels. Institutions also confront digital repatriation: a scanned photograph or online catalogue can help dispersed descendants recover knowledge, yet indiscriminate publication of a ceremonial image may reproduce the harm that physical return was intended to repair. A community may welcome a searchable archive for its own members while opposing unrestricted online circulation. That position is neither anti-technology nor inconsistent; it distinguishes access for a responsible community from use by anyone with an internet connection.

Historical Vocabulary
Provenance, Stewardship, and Consent

Definition. Provenance is the documented history of an object's origin and ownership. Stewardship is the responsibility to care for something on behalf of others, and in Indigenous usage often refers to responsibility owed to ancestors and future generations. Consent, in the contexts discussed here, means agreement given freely, in advance, and with adequate information by those with authority to give it.

Historical context. Museums traditionally treated provenance as a matter of establishing title. Repatriation shifted attention to who had authority to transfer an object at all, and later to who may authorize study or display.

Why it matters. The history of an object's acquisition does not by itself determine who should now decide what happens to it. A well-documented purchase may establish provenance without establishing the seller's right to sell, and an institution's possession does not establish a right to study or exhibit.

Potential misunderstanding. Stewardship in museums sometimes implies that institutions are the best caretakers. Indigenous nations may use the term to describe their own long-standing responsibilities, and the same word can carry opposed meanings.

The introduction of consent as a principle is the most significant change. The 2024 regulations require consent from the appropriate Indigenous parties before exhibition, and before research and access in covered circumstances, and many institutions had adopted similar practices earlier by policy. Collaborative research has grown, in which nations help define research questions, control the use of samples, and share the results. Some nations permit research under agreed conditions, and others prohibit it. The shift has not ended disagreement. Some scholars argue that limits on research reduce knowledge, and some museum professionals say that closing exhibits reduces public understanding of Indigenous history. Native critics reply that exhibitions of ancestors and sacred objects cannot be justified by education, and that a fuller public understanding is possible through exhibitions designed with and by Indigenous communities. The historian can record these disagreements without resolving them, and can note that the debate now takes place on terms that recognize the standing of Indigenous nations.

Consent is an ongoing relationship rather than a one-time signature. A nation may approve conservation treatment but not chemical sampling, approve restricted scholarly consultation but not publication of high-resolution images, or permit an exhibition for a defined period on condition that its interpretation remains under community review. Who speaks for the community can also matter: a tribal government may need to consult religious authorities, elders, or family custodians before agreeing to a museum proposal. Where the law applies, museums must follow its specific procedures; beyond those legal minimums, ethical practice involves transparent records, long-term communication, and willingness to reconsider inherited assumptions. Some researchers fear the loss of unrestricted access, while some Indigenous scholars argue that better-defined questions and sustained relationships can improve the quality of research. The central historical change is that institutions increasingly must explain and justify their practices to peoples they once studied without asking.

Section 125.19

International Repatriation and Indigenous Cultural Rights

Many ancestors and belongings are held outside the countries in which their nations now live, in museums in Europe and elsewhere, and returning them involves international law and diplomacy. International instruments are limited. The UNESCO convention of 1970 addresses illicit trafficking in cultural property but applies prospectively and between states, and it is not an Indigenous rights instrument. The Hague and UNIDROIT conventions address related matters. Because a national law such as NAGPRA does not apply to foreign institutions, return from abroad generally depends on negotiation, on the policies of individual museums, and on the political climate in the holding country. Examples include the return of a Lakota Ghost Dance shirt from a museum in Glasgow in 1999 and the House of Ni’isjoohl memorial pole from Scotland in 2023 (National Museums Scotland announcement). Several European countries have also adopted guidelines for dealing with collections from colonial contexts.

International returns also expose an important difference between a state claiming national patrimony and a nation or community asserting its own cultural responsibilities. A museum may be willing to return an object to the government of the country from which it was exported, yet the Indigenous community concerned may seek direct authority over its care. The distinction is visible in Mexico’s strong national protection of archaeological heritage and in Greenland’s negotiated return to a national museum; neither arrangement by itself resolves every local claim. For international researchers, therefore, documenting an object’s itinerary must be followed by a question about the proper recipient and the legitimacy of the proposed institutional arrangement, not simply by a question about which national flag will stand over the collection.

The most important international statement is the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, adopted by the General Assembly on September 13, 2007, by a vote of 144 in favor, 4 against, and 11 abstentions. The four states that opposed it, Australia, Canada, New Zealand, and the United States, each later changed their positions. Article 11 recognizes the right of Indigenous peoples to practice and revitalize their cultural traditions, and provides that states are to offer redress, including through restitution, for cultural, intellectual, religious, and spiritual property taken without free, prior, and informed consent or in violation of their laws. Article 12 recognizes the right to practice and teach spiritual and religious traditions, to maintain and have access to religious and cultural sites, and to use and control ceremonial objects, and it calls on states to seek to enable access to and repatriation of ceremonial objects and human remains through fair, transparent, and effective mechanisms developed with the peoples concerned.

Their significance lies in recognizing repatriation as a matter of rights and in specifying that mechanisms should be developed with Indigenous peoples. They have informed national legislation, such as Canada's 2021 act, and museum reviews.

Limitations: A declaration is not by itself legally binding, and its provisions depend on domestic implementation. The articles do not tell a nation how to claim an item from a particular museum.

Section 125.20

Repatriation, Historical Justice, and the Future of Indigenous Cultural Sovereignty

The chapter has traced five transformations. First, ancestors and sacred belongings entered institutional collections through a range of means, from outright removal to coerced and unequal transactions, within a framework that gave institutions authority over them. Second, Indigenous nations resisted and demanded return, through ceremony, protest, negotiation, and litigation. Third, those demands won legal recognition, first in the National Museum of the American Indian Act of 1989 and then in NAGPRA in 1990, and, in different forms, in Canada, Mexico, and Greenland. Fourth, implementation was contested and uneven, with disputes over evidence, competing claims, and institutional compliance, leading to reforms including the regulations effective in 2024. Fifth, relationships between Indigenous nations and institutions were reconstructed through new museums, new ethical standards, and the principle of consent.

The achievements are substantial. Many thousands of ancestors and very large numbers of cultural items have returned to Indigenous nations. Museums have changed their practices, Indigenous people work in museums and run their own, and international instruments recognize rights that institutions once ignored. Yet the limits are also real. Large numbers of ancestors and belongings remain in institutions, and the resources of nations and museums are unequal. NAGPRA covers a defined set of items and claimants, leaving out unrecognized nations, items outside its categories, private collections, and collections abroad. National frameworks differ, so that a nation whose homeland crosses a border may face different laws on each side. Disputes over research and public education persist.

The continental comparisons help identify what the different systems can and cannot accomplish. In the United States, a defined statutory process created enforceable obligations for covered federal agencies and museums, although exclusions and uneven compliance remain. In Canada, negotiated returns and selected provincial laws have sometimes made room for community-specific solutions but have required sustained bargaining without a general national process. Mexico’s legal tradition protects archaeological monuments as national property while recent legislation recognizes the collective cultural rights of Indigenous and Afro-Mexican communities; the distribution of authority between public heritage institutions and communities remains a historical question in its own right. Greenland’s Utimut experience demonstrates how political self-government and cooperative institutional planning can relocate major collections, even where no NAGPRA-equivalent statute controls the outcome. None of these models should be treated as the universal final stage of repatriation.

The measure of future progress will therefore not be a return count alone. Numbers can describe transfers of remains or objects, but they do not show whether culturally responsible people have the resources to receive them, whether restrictions on research and exhibition are respected, or whether collections held abroad are accessible to their source communities. A more complete history will examine both formal rules and the everyday capacity to exercise them: trained heritage staff, ceremonial knowledge, safe storage when desired, transparent provenance, accessible archives, and agreements durable across changes in museum leadership. These are the institutional conditions under which the recognition of cultural sovereignty becomes more than a declaration.

The larger historical point is the one stated at the beginning. Repatriation is not only the return of objects. It is a redistribution of authority, in which Indigenous nations are recognized as continuing political and cultural communities with the right to decide how their ancestors and inherited property are treated. That recognition connects repatriation to the themes of self-government and sovereignty that run through Volume X, including the educational authority discussed in Chapter 124. It transformed the responsibilities of museums, universities, and governments, which now must consult, document, and sometimes defer, and it altered the responsibilities of Indigenous nations, which have taken on the work of receiving, caring for, and deciding about what returns. Whether a given return is understood as justice, as repair, or as only a partial correction is a question on which communities themselves differ, and the unfinished work of repatriation continues.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that repatriation is a reconsideration of who has authority over Indigenous ancestors, sacred belongings, and cultural heritage. It is the return of objects and remains, but it is also a change in the legal and moral standing of Indigenous nations relative to museums, universities, and governments.

Indigenous nations understood ancestors, burial places, and ceremonial belongings in terms of continuing relationships and responsibilities, and many treated certain belongings as inalienable collective property. These views did not match the assumptions of museum ownership, in which items could be bought, sold, and classified as specimens.

Collecting took place through many routes. Excavation, military and medical collecting, missionary activity, legal suppression of ceremony, purchases, and expeditions all contributed, and historians must distinguish theft, coercion, unequal exchange, and agreement, recognizing that many acquisitions are poorly documented. Collecting was also international, so that ancestors and belongings were held in distant countries.

Museums and universities institutionalized collections through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, supported by racial science, salvage anthropology, and preservation claims. Law at the time gave institutions and scientists authority, and Native nations lacked standing to object, a structure that repatriation law later challenged.

Indigenous resistance took many forms, including protests like those in Glenwood and Dickson Mounds, the Larsen Bay return, and the Zuni campaign for the Ahayu:da, which rested on communal property and religious responsibility. Professional ethics and state laws shifted, and the National Museum of the American Indian Act of 1989 created the first federal repatriation framework for the Smithsonian.

NAGPRA, enacted on November 16, 1990, extended repatriation duties to federal agencies and federally funded museums, defined categories of human remains, funerary objects, sacred objects, and cultural patrimony, established priorities for claimants, and protected burials on federal and tribal lands. It is a United States statute with defined limits and does not require the return of every Indigenous artifact.

Implementation raised questions about evidence. Cultural affiliation could be shown through oral tradition, kinship, geography, archaeology, and other sources, but institutions often favored their own records. Competing claims among nations and the problem of unidentified remains showed that repatriation is a continuing political process.

The Ancient One case, from 1996 to 2017, illustrated these tensions. Archaeological findings, court decisions, genetic research, congressional action, and Indigenous claims each played different roles, and the case demonstrated the difference between questions of evidence and questions of authority.

Delays and incomplete compliance led to reforms, including the 2010 rule, the STOP Act of 2022, and the regulations effective January 12, 2024, which emphasized consultation, deference to Indigenous knowledge, and consent for exhibition, research, and access.

Canada, Mexico, and Greenland pursued different paths. Canada relied on the 1992 task force report, museum policies, provincial legislation, and negotiated returns such as those of the Canadian Museum of History. Mexico developed a state-centered system of national ownership, later supplemented by a 2022 law recognizing Indigenous and Afro-Mexican heritage rights. Greenland's Utimut process returned about 35,000 objects from Denmark between 1982 and 2001.

Indigenous museums, heritage offices, and community institutions have given nations means to care for returned ancestors and belongings, and museum ethics have shifted toward consent. International instruments, especially Articles 11 and 12 of the 2007 Declaration, recognize rights but depend on domestic implementation. The chapter concludes that repatriation has redistributed authority without fully resolving unequal resources, differing legal systems, or disputes over research.

Chapter Reflection

Repatriation matters because it shows how a question that begins with objects becomes a question about political standing. When a museum agrees to return an ancestor, it concedes that it did not have the last word. When a legislature writes consent into law, it acknowledges that Indigenous nations are not subjects of study but authorities in their own right. The chapter's histories of Zuni, Larsen Bay, the Columbia Plateau, Kwakwaka'wakw, Greenland, and Mexico show different routes to the same recognition.

The history reveals how law and knowledge intertwine. Statutes defined categories, but nations supplied the understanding that gave the categories meaning. Scientific evidence helped decide some disputes, but authority over outcomes was a political and legal question. Chapter 1 argued that oral tradition, archaeology, and documents are different but legitimate kinds of evidence; repatriation is one of the arenas in which that claim has been tested in practice.

The chapter also points toward later developments in the textbook: the investigation of boarding and residential schools in Chapters 127 and 128, the treatment of Indigenous knowledge and data in Chapter 145, and the discussion of sovereignty in Chapter 148. What persists across these topics is the question of who decides, and how Indigenous nations exercise authority over their own pasts. The answer given in this chapter is provisional, since collections remain unreturned and disagreements continue, but it is clear that the relationship between institutions and Indigenous nations has been permanently altered.
