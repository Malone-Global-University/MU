Chapter Opening

Wounded Knee is both a living Oglala Lakota community and a place that carries the memory of 1890. In February 1973 a local political crisis on the Pine Ridge Reservation drew that place into a seventy-one-day armed confrontation with the United States, and the confrontation has been argued over, inside and outside the Oglala community, ever since.

Wounded Knee lies along Wounded Knee Creek in the northern part of the Pine Ridge Reservation in southwestern South Dakota, roughly fourteen miles from the agency town of Pine Ridge. In 1973 it was a small community with a trading post, a church, a cemetery on a hill, and a scattering of homes. On the evening of February 27, a caravan of vehicles carrying Oglala residents, elders, members of the American Indian Movement (AIM), and supporters from other nations arrived in the village after a meeting at Calico Hall. By the following morning, federal marshals, agents of the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI), and Bureau of Indian Affairs (BIA) police had begun to seal the roads. The village would remain surrounded until May 8.

The site carried a history that the occupiers understood very well. On December 29, 1890, soldiers of the U.S. Seventh Cavalry killed a large number of Lakota people, most of them from Chief Big Foot's band, along the same creek, an event examined in Chapter 90. For many Lakota people the mass grave on the hill was a place of mourning before it was a symbol. Anyone who chose Wounded Knee as a stage for a political action in 1973 knew that the choice would call up that memory. But the people who came in 1973 were responding to a present crisis, and the chapter that follows takes that present seriously.

That crisis was, first of all, a dispute among Oglala people about their own government. Richard "Dick" Wilson, elected tribal chairman in 1972, was accused by opponents of favoritism, misuse of office, and the intimidation of critics. In February 1973 the Oglala Tribal Council held a hearing on impeachment charges, and Wilson remained in office. The same weeks saw anger over the killing of Wesley Bad Heart Bull, an Oglala man, in Buffalo Gap, and over the way the case was handled in nearby Custer. Members of the Oglala Sioux Civil Rights Organization (OSCRO) and a number of elders concluded that the avenues open to them had failed, and they asked AIM for assistance. That request did not represent every Oglala person. Wilson's supporters and many other residents opposed the occupation, and the people living in and around Wounded Knee did not all share a single view of it.

What began as a protest against a reservation government became a confrontation with the federal government. The occupiers demanded that the United States re-examine its treaties with Native nations, investigate the BIA, and address conditions on Pine Ridge. In March they proclaimed an "Independent Oglala Nation" and invoked the Treaty of Fort Laramie of 1868. The federal government sent marshals, FBI agents, and BIA police, supported by military equipment and advice, and set up a perimeter. There were firefights, a ceasefire, a preliminary agreement that foundered on the question of disarmament, the paralyzing wounding of a U.S. Marshal, and the deaths of two people inside the village, Frank Clearwater and Lawrence "Buddy" Lamont. A second agreement, signed on May 5, led to the surrender of weapons and the departure of the occupiers on May 8.

This chapter explains the occupation through five interacting problems: Oglala political legitimacy, Indigenous sovereignty and the meaning of the 1868 treaty, the exercise of federal power, the human consequences for residents and participants, and the contested historical memory of the event. It does not treat armed confrontation as inherently heroic, and it does not treat every allegation against federal authorities, or every allegation against the occupiers or the tribal government, as established fact. It also does not treat AIM and the Oglala people as a single political body with a single set of priorities. Chapter 110 studies AIM as a movement; this chapter studies Wounded Knee as a specific conflict that arose from Oglala politics and became national.

The evidence is unusually rich and unusually partisan. Historians draw on the records of the Oglala Tribal Council, federal court opinions, U.S. Marshals Service institutional history, contemporary newspapers and radio broadcasts, photographs held at the Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian, defense-committee archives, memoirs, and later scholarship, notably Akim D. Reinhardt's study of Oglala politics and Paul Chaat Smith and Robert Allen Warrior's history of the Indian movement. Each of these sources was created by someone with a position, and the people least often heard in the record are the ordinary residents who lived through the siege. Throughout, the chapter asks whose voice a source preserves and whose it leaves out.

The question that organizes the chapter is this: how did an Oglala struggle over political leadership and governmental accountability become a seventy-one-day national confrontation over Indigenous sovereignty, and what did the participants and their communities gain or lose?

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the 1973 occupation of Wounded Knee originated in an Oglala political crisis and not only in a conflict between AIM and the federal government.
  2. Describe the structure of the Oglala constitutional government created under the Indian Reorganization Act and its relationship to traditional leadership.
  3. Distinguish among documented proceedings, allegations, and interpretations in the controversy over Richard Wilson's administration.
  4. Trace the sequence from the Custer demonstrations and the February 1973 impeachment hearing to the decision to occupy Wounded Knee on February 27.
  5. Analyze what sovereignty meant in practice to different participants, and distinguish treaty rights, traditional legitimacy, and recognition of elected institutions.
  6. Reconstruct daily life inside the occupied village, including the work of women, elders, medics, and spiritual leaders.
  7. Explain the federal response, distinguishing civilian law enforcement from military support and identifying the legal questions later raised.
  8. Assess the role of journalism, radio, and publicity in amplifying the confrontation and sometimes obscuring local Oglala concerns.
  9. Interpret the April 5 and May 5 agreements and explain why the first failed to end the occupation.
  10. Evaluate the human costs of the siege, including the deaths of Frank Clearwater and Lawrence Lamont, while separating verified records from disputed claims.
  11. Analyze the 1974 trials, the dismissal of the Banks and Means prosecution, and the appellate ruling.
  12. Evaluate competing interpretations of the occupation's significance and its place in Oglala and Indigenous historical memory.

Historical Context

The Oglala are one of the Lakota (Teton) peoples of the Oceti Sakowin, the Seven Council Fires. Their homelands lay across the northern Plains and the Black Hills, and the Treaty of Fort Laramie of 1868, examined in Chapter 76, recognized a Great Sioux Reservation west of the Missouri River and promised protections that later federal actions eroded. The Act of 1889 divided that reservation into smaller ones, one of which became Pine Ridge. The massacre at Wounded Knee on December 29, 1890, which fell within that world of confinement and dispossession, is treated in Chapter 90. This chapter assumes the reader knows that history in outline and returns to it only where 1973 participants drew upon it.

By the 1970s Pine Ridge was a large, rural, poor reservation. Federal programs, tribal government, the BIA agency at Pine Ridge, ranching and farming leases, and wage work shaped local life, and unemployment and housing shortages were severe by national standards. The Oglala Sioux Tribe had adopted a constitution in the 1930s under the Indian Reorganization Act (Chapter 97), creating an elected council and executive. That form of government sat alongside older networks of kinship (tiyospaye), treaty-era headmen, ceremonial leaders, and community organizations, and the relationship between elected and traditional authority had been a source of tension for decades. Akim D. Reinhardt's work places the events of 1973 within that long local history, and it cautions against reading them as simply the arrival of outside activists.

The national setting was also changing. The Occupation of Alcatraz (Chapter 109), the growth of AIM (Chapter 110), and the Trail of Broken Treaties and occupation of the BIA headquarters in November 1972 (Chapter 111) had made Indigenous protest visible to a national audience and had made federal officials wary of another confrontation. The Nixon administration had publicly endorsed self-determination in principle, a policy shift taken up in Chapter 114, but day-to-day administration on reservations still relied heavily on the BIA. The Vietnam War had left many Native veterans, including some of those at Wounded Knee, with military training and political questions about the country they had served. The Watergate scandal was also building in the spring of 1973, which affected how the White House and Justice Department handled a crisis that attracted worldwide press.

Finally, the occupation raised legal problems that have no neat resolution. The United States recognizes tribal governments as dependent sovereigns with a government-to-government relationship, yet federal law enforcement, the BIA, and the courts all exercise authority on reservations. A tribal council elected under a federally approved constitution, a group of treaty-based traditional leaders, and a movement claiming the 1868 treaty as the supreme law of the land each made a claim to legitimacy, and those claims did not coincide. Untangling them is one of the main tasks of the chapter.

Section 112.1

112.1 — Wounded Knee before 1973: The Land, the 1890 Massacre, and Treaty Memory

Wounded Knee Creek runs through the rolling grassland and pine-broken ridges of the northern Pine Ridge Reservation. For the Oglala and their Lakota relatives this was country long used for hunting, gathering, ceremony, and travel between the Black Hills and the White River. The village that bears the creek's name grew up in the reservation era around a trading post, a mission church, and the homes of families whose ties to the land preceded the reservation. Because the Oglala remember place through kinship and story as much as through survey lines, "Wounded Knee" in 1973 named a community, a creek valley, and a burial ground at once.

The 1890 massacre made the place exceptional. It followed the Ghost Dance crisis and the killing of Sitting Bull, and it ended with the deaths of a large number of people from Big Foot's band, including many women and children. Estimates of the dead vary and are discussed in Chapter 90; most range from roughly 150 to well over 300. What matters for this chapter is how the event was remembered. Survivors and their descendants kept the story in family and community memory, while federal records framed it as a battle. Dee Brown's best-selling Bury My Heart at Wounded Knee (1970) then carried the name to a national readership and, for many Americans, made it shorthand for the whole of the Indian wars. That shorthand had consequences. By 1973 the name carried emotional power for Native and non-Native audiences alike, and it could be invoked by people who had little to do with Pine Ridge.

The link to the treaty was equally important. The Treaty of Fort Laramie of 1868 (Chapter 76) recognized a Great Sioux Reservation, including the Black Hills, and provided that major land cessions required the consent of three-fourths of adult male Lakota signatories, a provision that later federal acts bypassed. The 1889 division of the reservation and the 1890 massacre were stages in a longer pattern of contraction, administered through the Indian agency at Pine Ridge. For Oglala who held that the treaty remained binding, the history of the reservation was a history of promises broken by the United States. That argument did not belong to AIM alone. Treaty-minded elders and traditional leaders on Pine Ridge had made it for generations, and it formed a common ground, however uneasy, between them and younger activists.

It is important not to compress these different histories into one. The treaty was a diplomatic instrument between two parties. The 1890 massacre was a mass killing of a specific group of people. The 1973 occupation was a political action by living people with their own grievances. Participants linked the three, and the occupiers sought to make that link visible, but the connections were interpretations, not self-evident facts. A student who reads 1973 as a "second Wounded Knee" is following the language the participants themselves often used; a historian also asks what that language includes and excludes. Section 112.20 returns to the question.

The village itself was a layered place. A trading post and small museum stood beside the road, a Catholic mission church occupied a rise above the creek, and the cemetery and mass grave lay on the hill nearby. Families from the surrounding districts used the cemetery for their own dead, so the burial ground was both a memorial to 1890 and a working community cemetery. Annual remembrance, prayer, and visits to the grave were part of local life long before any press arrived. This is one reason some residents later objected to treating Wounded Knee primarily as a stage: for them it was a place where relatives were buried and where grief was a private obligation.

The memory of the massacre also moved through public institutions. Federal records, military reports, newspaper accounts, and later monuments each told it differently, and the Lakota survivors' testimony, recorded in the decades that followed, often contradicted the official narrative. By the 1960s, as Native writers and activists challenged older histories, the contrast between those narratives was itself a political resource. The 1973 occupiers drew on that long contest over who had the authority to tell what had happened, which is why the choice of site was also a claim about historical truth.

Finally, the place sat inside a reservation economy that offered few opportunities. Ranching leases, federal jobs, small businesses, and wage work off the reservation sustained families unevenly, and many households depended on federal programs administered through the agency. In that setting, control of tribal government mattered materially, because the council and chairman influenced hiring, housing, and the distribution of services. The stakes of the political dispute were therefore concrete as well as symbolic.

Historical Vocabulary

Oglala, Lakota, Sioux, and Oceti Sakowin

Definition. Oglala is the name of one of the Lakota (Teton) bands, today the Oglala Sioux Tribe of Pine Ridge. Lakota is the name used for the western division of the Oceti Sakowin ("Seven Council Fires"), whose other divisions are Dakota and Nakota. Sioux is a long-used exonym, shortened from a European rendering of a term used by neighboring peoples, and it remains in many legal and tribal names.

Historical context. The Oglala Sioux Tribe uses "Sioux" in its official name, and many Oglala people use "Lakota" or "Oglala" in everyday speech. Federal records of 1973 and much press coverage use "Sioux" or "Oglala Sioux."

Why it matters. The terms mark different levels of belonging: a band, a tribal government, a language community, and a confederation. Wounded Knee concerned all of them.

Potential misunderstanding. "Sioux" is not a single political body. Treaties, reservations, and governments divided the people into many separate nations.

Section 112.2

112.2 — Oglala Tribal Government and the Political Crisis of 1972–1973

The Oglala Sioux Tribe organized its government under the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 (Chapter 97). Its constitution created an elected tribal council with representatives from the reservation's districts, and an elected president, usually called the chairman in everyday usage. The council could pass ordinances, manage tribal funds and enterprises, and negotiate with federal and state governments, subject to the approval requirements the constitution and federal law imposed. The BIA agency superintendent retained substantial practical authority over budgets, programs, and leasing. Elected officials thus held genuine power but operated within a system in which federal review was routine.

The system never displaced older sources of authority. Families organized in extended kin groups (tiyospaye), treaty-era headmen and their descendants, and ceremonial leaders continued to command respect that did not depend on a ballot. Some Oglala saw the elected government as a legitimate evolution of Oglala self-rule. Others regarded it as an instrument of federal design that had displaced treaty-based leadership. Many held both views at different moments. The shorthand that sets "traditionals" against "progressives," or full-blood against mixed-blood, was widely used in the 1970s, but it hides as much as it reveals: people moved between positions, families were divided, and the same individual could defend the constitution and demand treaty enforcement. Reinhardt's study of Oglala politics treats the 1973 conflict as the culmination of decades of such argument rather than as a sudden break.

Richard Wilson was elected tribal chairman in 1972. He was a controversial figure from early in his term. His opponents accused him of favoring relatives and supporters in jobs and services, of bypassing council procedure, of misusing tribal funds, and of using a private force, the Guardians of the Oglala Nation, to intimidate critics. Wilson and his supporters rejected these charges. They described his opponents as disruptive, and they portrayed AIM as an outside movement that threatened order on the reservation and the authority of the elected government. They also pointed out that Wilson held office through an election. Each of these claims has to be examined against the evidence, and many of the sharpest allegations rest on testimony and recollection rather than on documents that can be independently verified.

Early in 1973 a group of council members and tribal citizens brought impeachment charges against Wilson. A council hearing was held on February 22 and 23. The proceeding ended without his removal. Opponents argued that the hearing was manipulated and that Wilson controlled the process; Wilson's defenders argued that the council had followed its procedures and reached a lawful decision. The published record of the hearing, reproduced in Reinhardt's documentary reader, allows students to read the charges and the proceeding directly. What the record can establish is the sequence, the issues raised, and the outcome. It is less able to establish the truth of every allegation, because an allegation made at a hearing is not the same as a finding.

The result mattered politically even apart from the merits. Opponents concluded that the constitutional remedy of impeachment had been closed to them. Within days, the Justice Department, anticipating unrest, sent marshals to Pine Ridge, and Wilson's headquarters was reinforced. The local dispute was already acquiring a federal dimension. The council's decision gave Wilson formal vindication and gave his critics a grievance they carried to Calico Hall.

The council's structure explains some of the disputes. The constitution divided authority between the council and the chairman, and it left room for disagreement over the reach of the chairman's powers, especially in matters such as hiring, contracts, and the use of tribal police. Disagreements of this kind were not unique to the Oglala, since many IRA governments struggled with the relationship between executive and legislative authority. What was distinctive on Pine Ridge in 1972 and 1973 was the degree to which these questions merged with accusations of violence and with the entry of outside organizations.

The federal role complicated matters further. Because BIA officials had to approve certain tribal actions and administered federal funds, a chairman who enjoyed good relations with the agency could govern more effectively than one who did not. Critics suspected that Wilson's closeness to federal officials shielded him from scrutiny, while supporters argued that cooperation with the agency was a practical necessity. The question of how much independence an IRA government actually had remained open, and it would shape the argument over sovereignty that unfolded at Wounded Knee.

It is also worth noticing what the impeachment record cannot show. A council is a political body, its members have loyalties, and the procedure that governs a hearing may favor one side. Historians who read the record therefore ask not only what was said but how the rules of the hearing shaped what could be said, who controlled the agenda, and what alternatives were available. Those questions do not have a single answer, and the answers offered by Wilson's defenders and his critics differ in ways the surviving evidence can only partly resolve.

Section 112.3

112.3 — The Oglala Sioux Civil Rights Organization and the Appeal to AIM

OSCRO emerged in 1972 as an organization of Oglala citizens who objected to the conduct of the tribal administration. Its leaders included Pedro Bissonette, who served as its director, and a number of women and elders, among them Gladys Bissonette and Ellen Moves Camp. They were not outsiders. They were Oglala people with local standing, and they framed their demands in terms of civil rights and constitutional procedure as well as treaty rights. Their opponents sometimes described them as pawns of AIM. The record points the other way for the early period: OSCRO existed before the arrival of AIM's national leaders and was the party that issued the invitation.

OSCRO's critics in the tribal government pointed to the Guardians of the Oglala Nation, formed under Wilson's authority, as an auxiliary force; opponents called its members GOONs, and the acronym stuck. Opponents charged that the Guardians harassed, beat, and threatened people who spoke against Wilson. Wilson's supporters defended them as a means of keeping order. Because many accounts of the Guardians come from partisans, historians treat specific incidents with care and rely most heavily on those corroborated by independent reports, court records, or multiple witnesses.

In late January 1973 an Oglala man, Wesley Bad Heart Bull, was killed in Buffalo Gap, off the reservation. A white man, Darld Schmitz, was arrested and charged with second-degree manslaughter, a charge that Bad Heart Bull's family and many Native people regarded as inadequate. On February 6, AIM members, local Oglala, and the victim's mother, Sarah Bad Heart Bull, went to Custer to demand a murder charge. The demonstration turned violent. Arrests followed, including Sarah Bad Heart Bull's, and a building was set on fire. The confrontation convinced many Oglala that the legal system of the surrounding region would not treat them fairly, and it linked the reservation crisis to the wider movement against racial injustice in border towns.

After the failed impeachment, OSCRO leaders and traditional elders met to decide what to do. They asked AIM to come to Pine Ridge. For AIM, whose leaders had just been through the Trail of Broken Treaties and the Custer arrests, the invitation was an opportunity to support an Oglala community in a dispute that bore on the national agenda of treaty rights. For the elders, it was a decision to turn to allies with experience of confrontation and publicity. The decision remained controversial, then and later. Some Oglala saw AIM's arrival as a rescue. Others saw it as an intrusion that would bring federal force down on the reservation.

The essential distinction for the chapter is that AIM came at the request of Oglala opponents of Wilson, and that this request must not be read as the unanimous will of the Oglala people. The reservation held a range of views, and many residents, including some who disliked Wilson, did not want an armed standoff.

The role of women in OSCRO deserves emphasis. Gladys Bissonette and Ellen Moves Camp were not auxiliary figures. They spoke at meetings, helped organize the local response to the arrests and intimidation reported in early 1973, and became public voices for the community in the weeks that followed. Their authority drew on age, family standing, and long residence on the reservation, and it gave the opposition a credibility that a purely outside movement could not have commanded. Their statements and recollections are among the most valuable evidence of how local participants understood the crisis.

The Custer events had national resonance as well. Border-town justice in South Dakota had long been a subject of complaint among Native people, who described unequal treatment in arrests, prosecutions, and sentencing. The decision to charge the man arrested in the Bad Heart Bull killing with manslaughter rather than murder was read in that light. The prosecution of demonstrators, including Sarah Bad Heart Bull, reinforced the perception that the system treated Native people as offenders even when they sought justice for a family member. Those perceptions are part of why the Custer episode, which occurred off the reservation and outside the tribal government's authority, fed directly into the Pine Ridge crisis.

Meanwhile AIM's leaders were weighing their own position. After the BIA headquarters occupation of November 1972, the organization had national notoriety and a need to show that its activism could serve Native communities. An invitation from Oglala elders, in the home country of Russell Means, offered a legitimacy that earlier actions had lacked. That is part of the context for the decision to answer the call, and it helps explain why the relationship between AIM and the local people was one of mutual need and mutual suspicion.

Section 112.4

112.4 — February 27, 1973: The Decision to Occupy Wounded Knee

On February 27, a large meeting gathered at Calico Hall. Elders, OSCRO members, AIM leaders including Russell Means, Dennis Banks, and Clyde Bellecourt, and many local people were present. The meeting, held in a community hall on the reservation, was meant to decide how to respond to Wilson's continued hold on office and to the tensions of the preceding weeks. Frank Fools Crow, whom many regarded as an authoritative ceremonial leader, was among those present. By the account of several participants, the decision to go to Wounded Knee emerged from that meeting, and it was taken with the full awareness of what the name implied.

The decision was shaped by fear as well as hope. Marshals had been sent to Pine Ridge two days earlier, and Wilson's headquarters was heavily guarded. Reports that police would block meetings or arrest leaders circulated. Means reported that he had been assaulted by Wilson's police earlier that day, though accounts of the incident vary. The group decided to move to a place that could not easily be closed off and that carried symbolic weight. Wounded Knee met both requirements.

That evening a caravan of about fifty vehicles carrying a few hundred people, with estimates varying, arrived in the village. The occupiers took control of the trading post, occupied the church, and spent the night preparing. Some residents of the village were present when the group arrived. Federal reports soon described eleven residents as hostages. Occupation leaders said the residents were free to leave and were present by their own choice, and some residents later gave accounts that differ from one another. The question of consent and coercion, and of who controlled the trading post and its stock, was one of the first factual disputes and remains unsettled in the public record.

The occupiers issued a political statement. They demanded Senate hearings on treaty violations, investigation of the BIA, scrutiny of the conditions on Pine Ridge, and removal of Wilson. They did not intend a short demonstration. They gathered weapons already present in the village and brought others, and they prepared defensive positions. Both their own stated aims and the language of their leaders suggest that they expected a standoff.

By the morning of February 28, federal marshals, FBI agents, and BIA police had established roadblocks and positions around the village. Gunfire was exchanged within a day. Federal officials, who had already been anticipating trouble, saw an armed takeover of a community and moved to contain it. The federal reaction determined the shape of what followed. Once armed agents faced armed occupiers, the question of a negotiated exit became inseparable from the question of who would give way first.

The days immediately before the occupation were marked by rumor and anticipation. Wilson had reportedly restricted public meetings, and word spread that arrests or confrontations were likely. The marshals sent to Pine Ridge on February 25 made the possibility of force concrete. In that atmosphere, participants at Calico considered several options, among them a march, a continued meeting, or a move to a symbolic location. Accounts differ on how much of the decision was settled at the meeting and how much was elaborated by the leaders afterward, which is a reminder that recollections of a decisive moment are often tidied by later memory.

Once the caravan reached the village, the early hours were disorganized. People arrived in waves, fortifications were improvised, and security assignments were made on the spot. Residents of the village found themselves in the middle of an event they had not planned. Some welcomed the occupiers and joined them. Others were alarmed or angry. Reports that some residents were detained, which the federal government described as a hostage-taking, were rejected by occupation leaders, and several residents later gave differing accounts of what they had experienced. Because these accounts came from people with very different positions, historians generally treat the question of coercion as unresolved, and they separate the clearly documented seizure of the trading post and its goods from the contested claims about confinement.

The first night also set the tone for the federal response. Officials who had hoped to avoid a confrontation now faced an armed group in a populated village. Their decision to seal the perimeter rather than to attempt immediate removal reflected both the risk to residents and the lessons of earlier encounters, and it set the pattern of a siege rather than an assault.

Long-term causes

Short-term causes

Wilson's administration and the allegations against it; the failed impeachment; the killing of Wesley Bad Heart Bull and the Custer arrests; AIM's recent national visibility.

The Calico meeting on February 27 and fear that leaders would be arrested or meetings closed.

Immediate effects

Seizure of the village; federal roadblocks; exchange of fire; transformation of a local dispute into a national event.

Section 112.5

112.5 — The Occupied Village and the Assertion of Indigenous Political Authority

Within days the occupied village had become a functioning community with its own improvised institutions. Leaders held meetings, issued statements, assigned security duties, organized kitchens and medical care, and negotiated with outside visitors. Russell Means, Dennis Banks, Carter Camp, and others served as public spokesmen and as negotiators, while Oglala elders and residents, among them Gladys Bissonette and Frank Fools Crow, provided a standing that the AIM leaders by themselves could not claim. The relationship between these groups was not fixed. AIM leaders often did the talking to reporters, and the elders sometimes constrained or redirected them. Several participants later recalled the arrangement as a partnership of unequal public visibility, in which the national spokesmen were heard far more than the local people who had invited them.

In early March the occupiers began asserting what they called the Independent Oglala Nation. The formal declaration by traditional leaders was dated March 11, 1973, a date recorded in the Pacifica radio documentary and in contemporary federal reporting; some press accounts date the emergence of the independence claim earlier in March. The March 11 declaration invoked the 1868 treaty as a basis for renewed political authority. Organizers claimed the village as the territory of an independent nation and offered symbolic "citizenship" to those who wished it. The formal document and the earlier public assertions should not be conflated. Whether this was intended as a literal assertion of separate statehood or as a strategic way of dramatizing treaty rights is itself debated. Some participants described it as a claim that the Oglala had never lawfully ceded their treaty status. Others saw it as political theater that expressed a real grievance in a form the press would carry.

Controlling a village is not the same as possessing recognized jurisdiction over it. The occupiers held the buildings and the ground within their perimeter, and they could decide who entered and who left. They could not collect taxes, enforce laws against dissenters with any legitimacy that the United States recognized, or secure the flow of food and medicine, because the roads and the airspace were controlled by their opponents. Their authority depended on the consent of the people inside and on the fact that federal officials hesitated to storm a village full of residents. That consent was real for many and contested for others, and the strain of the siege tested it daily.

The occupiers' authority also stood against two other claims. The elected tribal government under Wilson regarded the occupation as unlawful and treated the occupiers as lawbreakers on tribal land. The federal government, which recognized the tribal government as the lawful governing body of the reservation, treated the village as a place of civil disorder. In practice each of the three bodies claimed to speak for the Oglala people, and none could legitimately speak for all of them.

Inside the perimeter the occupiers developed a rough division of labor. Security leaders organized guard posts and bunkers. A communications effort handled press and visitors. Kitchen and supply work was coordinated by those who had experience with community events, many of them women, and medical volunteers established a clinic. Decision-making was a mixture of meetings and the authority of particular individuals, and participants later disagreed about how democratic it had been. Some remembered open discussion, and others recalled that a small group of leaders made the key choices.

The relationship with the tribal government was one of mutual refusal of recognition. The occupiers did not accept Wilson's authority, and Wilson regarded the occupiers as intruders. Yet both claimed to act in the name of the Oglala. That symmetry explains why efforts to find a settlement could not be reduced to a conversation between federal officials and AIM. The federal government had to decide how to treat the elected government whose authority it recognized, and any agreement with the occupiers risked appearing to undermine that government.

The experience of other Native nations also mattered. Delegations from other nations visited, including representatives of the Haudenosaunee, and their presence extended the significance of the occupation beyond Pine Ridge. They also brought their own perspectives on sovereignty and treaty rights, which were not identical to those of the Oglala or of AIM. The occupation thus became a point of contact among Indigenous political traditions, as well as a confrontation with the United States.

Section 112.6

112.6 — The Treaty of Fort Laramie, 1868, and the Question of Sovereignty

The occupiers' political demands centered on treaties. They called for federal review of the Treaty of Fort Laramie and of other treaties with Native nations, and for congressional hearings on how the United States had kept or broken them. The occupiers' argument was that the 1868 treaty remained valid, that its guarantees, including the consent provision for land cessions, had been violated, and that the Oglala therefore retained claims to territory and to a political status that the United States had not lawfully extinguished. These were not new claims. Lakota leaders had pressed them for generations, and later litigation over the Black Hills reached the Supreme Court in 1980, a story told elsewhere in this textbook.

The treaty argument raised distinct questions that were often run together in public debate. The first was historical: what the 1868 treaty promised and whether the United States had honored it. The second was political: who had the authority to enforce or renegotiate it on behalf of the Oglala people. The third was legal: how a modern court or the executive branch would treat a nineteenth-century treaty claimed against a government that had since legislated otherwise. The occupiers could make strong historical arguments. Their position on the second and third questions was weaker, because the federal government dealt with the Oglala Sioux Tribe through its constitutional government and because courts treated treaty-based claims through established legal channels.

Traditional treaty-minded leaders such as Frank Fools Crow and the Oglala chiefs saw the treaty as the foundation of their people's relationship to the United States and were open to discussing its enforcement with federal officials. The tribal government under Wilson also invoked treaty rights, but it understood them as exercised through the elected council, and it rejected the notion that a group of activists could speak for the nation. In this way "sovereignty" meant different things to different parties: for the occupiers, a restoration of treaty-based nationhood; for the council, the authority of its constitutional government; for federal officials, the government-to-government relationship with a recognized tribe. These overlapped without being identical.

The legal landscape in 1973 offered the occupiers little direct support. Courts had long held that Congress possessed broad authority over Native affairs and could modify or abrogate treaty provisions by statute, and the Black Hills had been taken by an act of Congress in 1877 after the Lakota refused to sell. Treaty claims therefore had to be pursued through the courts, through Congress, or through negotiation, each of which was slow and uncertain. The occupiers' insistence on a direct reexamination of treaties by the executive branch reflected frustration with those channels, and it also reflected a view of treaties as international agreements between nations rather than as domestic statutes.

That view had deep roots. Lakota leaders in the nineteenth century had negotiated with the United States as the representatives of a distinct nation, and the treaty texts were written in the language of agreements between sovereign parties. The twentieth-century argument that the treaties remained in force drew on this record and on legal scholarship that treated the treaties as living instruments. The federal government's position, in contrast, rested on the principle that treaty rights existed within the framework of federal law and could be limited by Congress. These two understandings did not meet, and the occupation placed them in direct confrontation.

The tribal government's position added a further layer. Because the elected council had the authority to bring legal claims on behalf of the tribe, the Oglala Sioux Tribe was the party that could pursue treaty litigation in court. The occupiers could press the moral and political case, but the formal claim belonged to the government they rejected. That tension between moral claim and legal standing recurs through the history of Native treaty activism, and it is one reason the debate over representation is central to the chapter.

Historical Debate

Who Could Speak for the Oglala People?

The question: In February and March 1973, which body or bodies had a legitimate claim to speak for the Oglala people: the elected tribal government, the local critics and traditional leaders who invited AIM, or the occupation leadership?

Interpretation A — Constitutional legitimacy. The elected council and chairman held office under a federally approved constitution and, despite the allegations against them, had been chosen by a vote and had survived an impeachment hearing. On this view the occupiers were challenging a lawful government through force.
Interpretation B — Traditional and community legitimacy. Elders, treaty-minded leaders, and OSCRO members argued that a government shaped by federal structures and alleged to have used intimidation had lost the confidence of significant parts of the community, and that treaty-based authority and the right to resist abuses outweighed constitutional form.
Interpretation C — Overlapping and incomplete claims. Some scholars argue that all of these claims had a basis, that none was complete, and that the confrontation demonstrates the unresolved relationship between constitutional government and older sources of authority on Pine Ridge.

Points of disagreement: Whether allegations against Wilson were established; whether the impeachment hearing was fair; how to weigh election against community consent; and how far a political movement from outside Pine Ridge could act for Oglala people.

Section 112.7

112.7 — Daily Life under Siege: Food, Water, Shelter, Medicine, and Families

For the people who lived inside the village the occupation was a daily problem of survival. Wounded Knee had a handful of buildings and a limited supply of food. Early March nights were bitterly cold. Residents and occupiers shared the available houses, the church, and the trading post. Sleeping arrangements were improvised, and heating depended on wood and stoves. Water and sanitation were makeshift in a place that had never been designed to hold hundreds of people. Meals were cooked communally and rationed according to the stock available, and women organized much of this work.

Supply was constrained by the federal perimeter. Roadblocks stopped vehicles. People carried food, medicine, and ammunition on foot through the lines at night, along creek bottoms and ridges, at the risk of arrest or being shot. Supporters also used small aircraft to drop supplies into the village in April. Federal authorities regarded such efforts as the movement of contraband and prosecuted some who attempted them, while many local Oglala saw the carrying of food as an act of kinship and mutual obligation. Federal restrictions on movement also affected the decision to leave. Some residents wanted to depart and were able to do so. Others who left found that they could not easily return, and the question of who could cross the lines, and under what conditions, was a source of persistent friction.

Medical care was provided by volunteer medics, including women such as Madonna Thunder Hawk and Lorelei DeCora, who treated gunshot wounds, exposure, illness, and the ordinary ailments of children and elders. Wounded people could be evacuated only by negotiation with federal authorities, which contributed to the gravity of the injuries suffered in April. Weather, mud, and cold complicated every effort. Mary Brave Bird recorded in her memoir that she gave birth during the siege, a reminder that a village under arms was also a place where children were born and families lived. Memoirs are valuable and also partial; they record one person's recollection, written later.

Children and elders lived with the sound of gunfire and with the fear of an assault. Firefights broke out at night, and the sense of being surrounded affected everyone. Some families left at the first opportunity. Others remained because the village was their home or because they believed in the cause. The experience of residents who did not support the occupation, and who found their homes occupied and their routines overturned, is among the least documented aspects of the event, and it should not be assumed that all those present shared the same view.

Sanitation and health were persistent concerns. A village built for a small population could not easily accommodate several hundred people through the end of winter and the onset of spring. Overcrowding, makeshift shelter, and the lack of reliable water increased the risk of illness, and medics worked with limited supplies. Pregnancy, chronic illness, and old age all demanded care that the village could provide only in part. Evacuating those with serious injuries or illness depended on negotiation with federal authorities, and the delays that sometimes resulted were later cited by critics of the federal handling of the siege.

Communication was another daily problem. Radios, telephones, and messengers moved information, and the village depended on contacts outside the perimeter for news and for coordination with lawyers and supporters. Federal officials limited or monitored some of this traffic. Rumors spread easily, and participants later recalled the anxiety produced by not knowing whether an assault was about to begin. The experience of living under threat for weeks, with night firefights and the constant presence of armed agents on the ridges, left a mark on those who lived through it, including children.

The treatment of residents who wished to leave or return is central to any account of the siege. Federal officials at the roadblocks controlled who could enter or leave and applied rules that residents often found arbitrary. Occupation leaders, for their part, sometimes discouraged departures or insisted that people who left register their intentions. Some departures were made quietly, and others in negotiated groups. The difficulty of reconstructing these movements from the surviving records is one reason the experience of ordinary residents remains so poorly documented.

Everyday Life and Social History

The Occupied Village as a Lived Community

The occupied village was not simply a military position. It had kitchens and a clinic, daily meetings, prayers and ceremonies, guard rotations, and a constant flow of visitors, journalists, lawyers, clergy, and delegations from other Native nations. Households shared scarce food. Women and elders organized much of the community's daily work, and young men and women stood guard.

Students should consider who was inside: Oglala residents, families from elsewhere on Pine Ridge, AIM members from many nations, veterans, and supporters from outside the reservation. Each group had different reasons for being there, different options for leaving, and different levels of risk. The sources, including photographs, interviews, and memoirs, record some of these experiences far better than others, and historians must read them with the awareness that those who spoke publicly were not necessarily those who bore the greatest burden.

Section 112.8

112.8 — Women, Elders, Spiritual Leaders, and the Occupation Community

Women were central to the occupation, and the popular memory of male spokesmen and rifles has obscured this. Ellen Moves Camp and Gladys Bissonette were among the Oglala women who helped to initiate the request for AIM's assistance, and they remained prominent in the village and in negotiations with outside visitors. Madonna Thunder Hawk and Lorelei DeCora served as medics and organizers. Other women ran kitchens, managed supplies, kept records, spoke to reporters, and took part in guard duty. Their roles built on the work women had long done in Lakota communities as heads of households and as political advocates, and they also reflected the practical needs of a community under siege.

Elders provided moral authority and a link to the history of the land. Their presence made it harder for the federal government to treat the village as only a stronghold of armed militants, and it also reminded the activists of obligations to the community. Frank Fools Crow in particular was widely respected, and his willingness to speak with federal officials and to counsel the participants affected the way negotiations developed. Not all elders agreed on strategy. Some supported the occupation wholeheartedly. Others tolerated it, worried about its consequences, or sought to move it toward a settlement.

Ceremony and prayer were part of daily life in the village. Leonard Crow Dog, a Sicangu Lakota spiritual leader, led ceremonies and counseled participants, and others prayed and observed their own traditions. Lakota religious practice is not uniform: ceremonial leaders differ in lineage, authority, and understanding, and the relationship between AIM and older religious leaders was complex. Presenting ceremony at Wounded Knee as a single, agreed tradition would flatten that diversity. The burial ground carried a particular significance. It held the dead of 1890, and for many participants its presence made the occupation an act of remembrance as well as protest. Those who died in 1973, including Buddy Lamont, were remembered within the same landscape.

The gender and age composition of the village affected the political life of the occupation. The presence of women, children, and elders constrained certain choices and gave the occupation a moral weight that purely military framing cannot capture. It also meant that the human cost of any assault would have been very high, which both sides understood. The participation of these groups should not be treated as incidental, because it shaped how the occupation was organized, how it was seen from outside, and how it ended.

The involvement of Native veterans of the Vietnam War deserves attention. Several of the people who stood guard at Wounded Knee had served in the military, and they brought skills and an understanding of the risk of armed confrontation. Lawrence Lamont, who died on April 26, was one such veteran. For these participants the experience of fighting for the United States abroad and then facing its agents at home was a source of bitter reflection, and some described their choice to take part in the occupation as an extension of a duty to protect their people.

The occupation also drew clergy and church workers, whose role was complex. Some missionaries and church officials supported the occupiers or acted as mediators, while others sided with the tribal government or with federal officials. The Catholic church in the village was occupied and became a focus of activity, and its use illustrates the intertwining of Christian and Lakota religious life in the community. Many Oglala people were Christian, Lakota traditional practitioners, or both, and the occupation brought these identities into view.

The question of who spoke for the spiritual dimension of the occupation was itself disputed. Some of the older ceremonial leaders were cautious about the use of sacred ceremonies in a political and armed context, and some questioned the authority of younger leaders to conduct them. Others saw ceremony as inseparable from resistance. Historians who write about these matters take care not to ascribe a single religious position to the occupation, and they rely on participants' own accounts of what the ceremonies meant to them.

Section 112.9

112.9 — The Federal Response: U.S. Marshals, FBI, BIA Police, and Military Support

The federal response was shaped by recent experience. The occupation of the BIA headquarters in Washington in November 1972 had embarrassed the administration, and the Justice Department was determined not to be caught unprepared again. On February 25, two days before the occupation, the department sent a force of marshals to Pine Ridge in anticipation of disorder. When the village was seized, the Justice Department took charge of the response. The U.S. Marshals Service, whose newly formed Special Operations Group (SOG) had been created in 1971 to respond to civil disturbances, deployed to the area. The Marshals Service later described the confrontation as the longest civil disorder in its history.

Three civilian forces shared the perimeter. Marshals and FBI agents, many of them transferred from across the country, set up roadblocks and fortified positions on the roads and ridges around the village. BIA police, who belonged to the agency of the Interior Department that administered the reservation, took part as well. Wilson's supporters also manned barricades at points, and the relationship between federal agents and the tribal chairman's armed supporters is one of the most criticized aspects of the response. Occupiers and their allies charged that federal forces tolerated or enabled intimidation by Wilson's allies, and that they gave Wilson political cover. Federal officials said that they were protecting the tribal government and keeping order.

Military support was substantial. The Army provided armored personnel carriers, ammunition, flares, and other equipment, along with officers who observed and advised. The Air Force and National Guard provided aerial reconnaissance and support. Accounts based on Pentagon records mention about fifteen armored personnel carriers and well over a hundred thousand rounds of ammunition. Figures vary by source, and students should treat them as approximate. The Posse Comitatus Act of 1878 generally bars the use of the Army to execute civilian laws, and the question of whether the support at Wounded Knee crossed that line became a legal issue in later trials. Federal courts did not agree. Some held that equipment loans and passive advice were permissible, while Judge Fred Nichol, as Section 112.18 explains, found unlawful military involvement in the Banks and Means case.

The Marshals Service's own historical account, which stresses the professionalism and courage of its personnel, is a useful institutional source, and it is also an institutional source, written to commemorate the agency's work. It should be read alongside civilian testimony, court findings, and journalism. Evidence of federal conduct, including the shooting of Marshal Lloyd Grimm, is considered here with the same care that is applied to claims about the occupiers.

Federal planning reflected a determination to avoid a repetition of earlier failures and an unwillingness to use maximum force. Officials in Washington debated the options, which ranged from a quick assault to a prolonged containment. The decision to contain the village rather than storm it reflected concern for the residents inside and awareness that a bloody assault at the site of the 1890 massacre would have been a political catastrophe. This restraint was real, and it coexisted with an extensive display of force and with the use of military equipment that critics found excessive.

The role of the FBI was twofold. Agents served on the perimeter and conducted investigations, gathering evidence for the prosecutions that followed. The Bureau's conduct in those investigations, including its handling of informants and witnesses, became a subject of judicial criticism in 1974. The agency's later history on Pine Ridge, including its role in the investigations that followed the 1975 shootout, makes the relationship between the FBI and the Oglala community a recurring theme in the chapters that follow.

The BIA police occupied an awkward position. They were federal employees serving a tribal community, and their loyalties were tested by the conflict. Some Oglala officers sympathized with the occupiers, and others supported the tribal government. The agency's overall posture, which aligned with federal and tribal authorities against the occupation, reinforced the belief among critics that the BIA was an instrument of the system the occupiers opposed. The tension between the BIA's administrative role and the community's expectations of it was one of the structural problems that the later policy of self-determination attempted to address.

Section 112.10

112.10 — The Geography of the Siege: Roads, Perimeters, and Supply Routes

The terrain defined the siege. Wounded Knee sits in a creek bottom, with ridges and rolling hills around it. The village could be seen from the heights, and federal positions on the high ground overlooked it, while the occupiers held bunkers and defensive positions within and around the village. The open ground between the lines was exposed to fire, and the distances were short enough that gunfire could reach buildings and people on either side.

Roads connected the village to the rest of the reservation and to the highways beyond. Control of those roads determined the flow of food, medicine, news, and people. Federal roadblocks closed vehicle access. The distance of about fourteen miles from Pine Ridge village meant that the agency town became the center of federal operations and of Wilson's tribal government, and the supply and communication lines between the two went through hostile ground. For the occupiers, the effective way to move people and goods was on foot, at night, over creek beds and hills the Oglala knew well.

The perimeter was not a clean line. Its shape changed from week to week. Periods of ceasefire allowed supplies and visitors to pass, and renewed fighting closed gaps. Federal strategy sought to isolate the village, wear down supplies and morale, and avoid an assault that would endanger civilians. The occupiers' strategy depended on keeping the press and public attention focused on the village and on the possibility of federal violence. Each side's choices were conditioned by the geography and by the information it had about the other's.

Weather compounded the problems of terrain. Late winter on the northern Plains brings snow, mud, and sudden cold, and early spring thaws turned the roads and fields into mire. Movement across open ground was difficult for both sides. The conditions affected the supply of the village, the health of its inhabitants, and the endurance of the federal personnel stationed on the ridges. Participants on both sides later described the siege as a test of endurance as much as of arms.

The perimeter also created zones of uncertainty for people who lived near the village but not in it. Families whose homes lay along the roads found their movements restricted by federal checkpoints and sometimes by the armed supporters of the tribal government. Ranchers and farmers had difficulty reaching their land. Some residents were separated from relatives on the other side of the lines. The effects of the siege thus extended beyond the village itself, into the surrounding districts, and the accounts of those who lived there are important evidence of the broader costs of the confrontation.

Air power played a role as well. Federal aircraft conducted surveillance, and supporters of the occupation used small planes to drop food and supplies into the village. The airspace over the village became contested, and the use of aircraft for both purposes illustrates the ways in which the technology of the 1970s changed the nature of a siege. A village that could be reached by road only through federal checkpoints could still be reached from the air.

Section 112.11

112.11 — Wounded Knee before the Cameras: Journalism, Radio, and Political Publicity

The occupation was a media event from its first day. Reporters and photographers arrived within hours, and the national networks, wire services, and newspapers covered it for ten weeks, as the Marshals Service later observed. AIM leaders, who had learned during Alcatraz and the BIA occupation how to use publicity, made themselves available to the press, held press conferences, and issued statements. Radio, including the independent and public stations whose recordings survive in archives, carried voices from inside the village and from the federal side. Federal officials also sought to shape the coverage, and at times they restricted press access to the village and its surroundings.

Media attention amplified the confrontation and made the demands of the occupiers visible across the world. It also had costs. It tended to center the national AIM leaders, with their rhetoric of treaty sovereignty and armed resistance, and it often left the local Oglala context in the background. The impeachment dispute, the Guardians of the Oglala Nation, and the views of residents were less photogenic than armored vehicles and rifles. Coverage also reflected the assumptions of reporters, many of whom approached the story as an episode in the "Indian wars" or as a throwback, a framing that Native participants sometimes resisted.

On March 27, 1973, at the Academy Awards in Los Angeles, Sacheen Littlefeather appeared in Marlon Brando's place and declined the Best Actor award he had won for The Godfather, referring to the treatment of Native people in film and to the events at Wounded Knee. The appearance was brief and drew mixed reactions in the hall. It carried the occupation into an audience that followed the entertainment industry rather than reservation politics. In later decades the episode was revisited: the Academy issued an apology to Littlefeather in 2022, and she died that year. The incident illustrates the way publicity could travel far beyond the village while the people inside remained confined.

Journalists and filmmakers also became participants in the story. Some carried messages. Some were detained. Their reports, combined with federal and AIM statements, make the occupation among the best-documented events in the history of Native political protest, and also one of the most affected by competing efforts to control the narrative.

The federal government's own use of publicity deserves attention. Justice Department officials gave briefings and statements that emphasized the danger posed by the occupiers, including the presence of weapons and the detention of residents, and they sought to frame the confrontation as a matter of law and order. Wilson also spoke to the press, presenting himself as the lawful head of the Oglala government under threat from outsiders. The competing narratives meant that the same events could be reported in strikingly different terms, depending on whose briefing a reporter relied on.

The occupation's leaders were adept at symbolism. They displayed flags, issued proclamations, and staged ceremonies and press conferences that conveyed the message of a nation asserting its sovereignty. The upside-down American flag, the language of treaties, and the images of Native people standing guard on a historic site made for powerful visual communication. Critics, including some Oglala people, argued that the emphasis on spectacle sometimes overshadowed the substantive political questions and that the audience for the symbols was outside the reservation more than within it.

Public opinion was divided. Polls and letters of the period show sympathy for the occupiers' treaty claims among some audiences and hostility among others, who saw the occupation as an armed seizure. Many Native people across the United States and Canada followed the events closely, and the occupation became a reference point for activism well beyond Pine Ridge. Delegations, donations, and messages of support arrived from many communities, which gave the occupation a significance among Indigenous peoples that the mainstream press only partly captured.

Section 112.12

112.12 — The First Weeks of Confrontation: Ceasefires, Visitors, and Negotiations

The first weeks were a succession of armed exchanges and pauses. Gunfire broke out in the first days and recurred in March, with both sides accusing the other of starting it. Periods of ceasefire allowed visitors, including religious representatives, lawyers, elected officials, and delegations from other Native nations, to enter the village. South Dakota's two U.S. senators visited early, accompanied by reporters. On March 13, Harlington Wood Jr. of the Justice Department entered the village without an armed escort, a step that marked the opening of direct negotiation. Later Kent Frizzell, an assistant attorney general, became the chief federal negotiator.

The parties wanted different things. Federal officials wanted the occupiers to lay down their arms, leave the village, and submit to arrest and prosecution for the crimes they had allegedly committed, and they wanted to avoid a bloody assault. The AIM leaders wanted federal commitments on treaty review, investigations of the BIA and of the Wilson administration, and protection from prosecution or at least fair treatment. OSCRO representatives and elders wanted the removal of Wilson or fair elections and an end to intimidation. Residents wanted safety and the ability to come and go. Each of these goals had a different priority and timeline, and the negotiators did not always represent the same constituencies.

Disarmament was the sharpest point of conflict. The federal side insisted on weapons being surrendered first or at the start, while the occupiers feared that surrender without guarantees would leave them open to arrest and without leverage. The occupiers' demands about treaty review also ran into the fact that the executive branch could not itself rewrite treaties or compel Congress to hold hearings. These problems were still unresolved when a marshal was shot in late March and fighting intensified, and they shaped the April agreement discussed in the next division.

The sequence of exchanges in March shaped the negotiations. Firefights tended to occur at night, and each side blamed the other for beginning them. The wounding of individuals on both sides raised the stakes and encouraged hardliners. Yet the ceasefires, however brief, allowed contacts that would not otherwise have been possible. Senators, clergy, and Justice Department officials traveled to the village, and their reports to Washington and to the press shaped understanding of the situation on the ground.

Negotiation was complicated by the number of parties. Within the federal government, the Justice Department, the Interior Department and its BIA, the FBI, the Marshals Service, and the White House all had interests, and they did not always agree. Within the occupation, AIM leaders, OSCRO members, elders, and residents had different priorities. Wilson's government was a further party that demanded to be consulted. An agreement acceptable to one group might be unacceptable to another, and the negotiators repeatedly discovered that the people with whom they had reached an understanding could not deliver the assent of all the others.

The role of intermediaries was significant. Clergy, lawyers, and representatives of other Native nations carried messages and sometimes proposed solutions. Their credibility depended on the trust of both sides, which they did not always enjoy. The difficulties they encountered show why the end of the siege required a combination of military pressure, human loss, and the intervention of respected local elders, not any single decisive negotiation.

Section 112.13

112.13 — The April 5 Preliminary Agreement and the Dispute over Disarmament

By early April, after weeks of firefights and talks, negotiators produced a written preliminary agreement. The final negotiating session took place on April 4, and the agreement was signed on April 5. In general terms it provided for the occupants to give up their weapons, for a meeting between AIM representatives and White House officials, and for the government to review the treaty issues and the matters raised by the occupiers. It did not, however, set a clear schedule for when the weapons would be surrendered relative to the White House meeting, and that omission became the center of a bitter dispute.

Federal officials, including Richard Hellstern, a deputy assistant attorney general, said that the AIM negotiators had agreed that disarmament would begin at the time of the White House meeting, when Russell Means would telephone the village and instruct the occupants to put down their arms. They said that the written text was silent on the call in order to preserve flexibility for the occupiers, and they accused AIM of reneging. Means said that he would make such a call only after meeting White House officials and judging whether they intended to act in good faith. On April 10, Minnesota Public Radio reporter Paul Gruchow, who had obtained a copy of the signed text and a recording of the last negotiating session, reported that the agreement set no timetable and that the recording supported the AIM position on the sequence, in that Means had said he understood the signing to be followed by the preliminary meeting and only afterward by discussion of how to resolve the arms question.

The argument was about sequence, but it was also about trust. For the government, disarmament first protected agents and set the terms of surrender. For the occupiers, disarmament first without evidence of federal good faith meant giving up their only leverage after more than five weeks of siege. Both positions were reasonable from the standpoint of each party's interests, and the written document did not settle between them. The agreement therefore did not end the occupation. In the following days both sides accused the other of bad faith, the promised meetings did not take place as planned, and the confrontation resumed.

The week after the April 5 agreement shows how a document can fail even when both sides sign it. Federal officials had expected a rapid sequence, with a meeting and the surrender of arms following in short order. The occupiers had expected the government to demonstrate its good faith before they gave up their weapons. When the meeting did not occur on the timetable each side had in mind, each interpreted the delay as evidence of the other's bad faith. Fighting resumed, and the government tightened its restrictions on supplies and access.

The testimony before the House subcommittee illustrates the political dimension of the dispute. Federal officials presented their case to Congress and the public, claiming that the occupiers had broken their word. The occupiers responded through the press, arguing that the government had misrepresented the agreement. Because each side addressed a different audience, the dispute over the agreement became a contest over the public understanding of who was acting in good faith. The MPR report is valuable partly because it tried to test those competing claims against a primary record.

The episode also shows how the absence of a written timetable can be strategic as well as accidental. The government's explanation that the silence on the telephone call preserved the occupiers' appearance of flexibility is itself an interpretation of the drafting, and one that the occupiers rejected. Historians cannot determine from the surviving evidence whether the omission reflected deliberate ambiguity, a failure to resolve the issue, or an honest misunderstanding. What they can say is that the omission was consequential, because it left each party free to claim that the other had broken the agreement.

Section 112.14

112.14 — The Escalation of Violence and the Deaths of Frank Clearwater and Lawrence Lamont

After the failure of the April agreement, firefights became more frequent and more dangerous. Earlier, in late March, U.S. Marshal Lloyd Grimm of Nebraska had been shot and was left permanently paralyzed. A detailed federal operational chronology places the shooting on March 26, 1973, while a later Associated Press obituary gave March 16; rather than silently resolve that discrepancy, this chapter identifies the conflicting dates. The Marshals Service records his wounding among the most serious events of the siege. Federal officials treated the shooting as evidence of the danger of the situation, and the occupiers said that shots were exchanged amid fire from both sides. An FBI agent was also wounded during the confrontation.

On April 17, Frank Clearwater, a man of Cherokee descent from North Carolina, was shot in the head while resting in the church. Accounts of his heritage vary, with some sources also identifying him as Apache. He was taken out for treatment in Rapid City and died on April 25, eight days after being shot. Reports describe him as being in his forties and as a recent arrival at Wounded Knee. Who fired the shot is not established beyond the fact that it came during exchanges of fire across the perimeter, and accounts from the two sides differ on whether the shot was an aimed or a random one.

On April 26, Lawrence "Buddy" Lamont, an Oglala man in his early thirties and a Vietnam veteran, was fatally shot during a firefight. His death carried particular weight in the Oglala community. He was a local man, his family lived on the reservation, and he was buried at Wounded Knee, in a place the occupation had made a site of mourning. Accounts attribute the fatal shot to federal gunfire. As with Clearwater, the precise circumstances are known in part through participant accounts and in part through official reports, which differ.

Ray Robinson, a civil-rights activist who went to Wounded Knee, disappeared during the occupation. In 2014, the FBI publicly concluded from its investigation that he had been killed there; his remains have not been recovered, and the identity and legal responsibility of any perpetrators have not been established by a conviction. Section 112.19 examines this distinction between an investigative conclusion and a judicially established fact. The conventional siege casualty tally records two people fatally shot in the armed exchanges—Frank Clearwater and Lawrence Lamont—without claiming that this tally comprehensively resolves Robinson’s case. Marshal Grimm and an FBI agent were among those wounded.

The deaths changed the occupation. For the families of the two dead men they were personal losses. For many elders, Lamont's death in particular persuaded them that continued armed confrontation would only bring more deaths. For federal officials, the casualties reinforced the case for a negotiated end. For the occupation's leaders, they were a source of both grief and pressure to justify the sacrifice. The weeks that followed show a movement trying to turn loss into political gain while its own supporters questioned how long the siege could continue.

The human dimension of the deaths is easy to lose in a chronology of negotiations. Frank Clearwater came to Wounded Knee from outside Pine Ridge, as part of the wider Native support for the occupation, and his death at a distance from his home and family reflected the intertribal character of the movement. His family had to cope with his shooting, the long wait for his death, and the aftermath, without the support of the community in which they lived. The circumstances of his death, in a church that had become a place of shelter, gave it a particular poignancy for those who were present.

Buddy Lamont's death was felt differently. As an Oglala man with relatives on the reservation, he belonged to the community whose political crisis had produced the occupation. His mother and family mourned him within that community, and his burial at Wounded Knee made his death part of the landscape of memory. The fact that he was a veteran added another dimension, since the nation whose uniform he had worn was the one whose agents were on the ridge. Participants later described the moment of his death as a turning point, after which the question was no longer whether the siege would end but how.

The question of responsibility remains painful. Federal reports and the accounts of occupiers differ on who fired, from where, and under what circumstances. The shooting of both men took place amid exchanges of fire in which both sides were armed, and the surviving evidence does not permit a definitive reconstruction of each incident. Students should therefore keep apart the established facts, the dates and the fact of the deaths, from the contested claims about who fired and why. The families of the dead, for their part, have continued to remember them regardless of how the legal and historical questions are resolved.

Section 112.15

112.15 — Renewed Negotiations and the May 5 Agreement

After Lamont's death, talks resumed under new pressure. Federal negotiators, led by Kent Frizzell, met with AIM leaders and Oglala elders. Lawyers for the occupiers took part, as did religious mediators and personnel of the Justice Department's Community Relations Service, an agency designed to help resolve disputes. White House officials were also involved. Frank Fools Crow and other Oglala elders had a decisive role: their authority to speak for the community and their willingness to seek a settlement gave the occupiers a way to end the standoff without appearing to surrender to the government.

The agreement signed on May 5 set out arrangements for the surrender of weapons, the processing of occupants by federal officers, criminal investigation of events at Wounded Knee, and the examination of the civil-rights complaints and treaty issues that the occupiers had raised. It also provided for a meeting between White House representatives and Oglala traditional leaders. The text distinguishes among obligations to be carried out immediately and those that depended on later actions by the government. Students should keep this distinction in view. A written commitment to hold a meeting or to consider a question is not a commitment to a particular outcome, and many of the promised steps either did not occur or did not produce the results the occupiers sought.

The May 5 agreement differed from the April 5 agreement in important ways. It was drafted after two deaths and after weeks of fighting, and both sides had stronger reasons to reach an end. The sequence of disarmament was addressed more directly, and the arrangements for the departure of the occupants and the processing of those who left were set out in greater detail. At the same time its commitments on treaty review rested on the good faith of an administration that was by then preoccupied with the Watergate scandal.

The role of Oglala elders in the settlement was decisive. After Lamont's death they were able to argue within the village for an end to the confrontation, on the grounds that the cost of continuing was too high, without appearing to betray the cause. Their standing allowed them to speak to both the occupiers and the federal negotiators, and they insisted that the safety of the people in the village be the first priority. Accounts of the final negotiations credit their influence, and the contrast with the failed April talks suggests that the presence of local authority gave the process a legitimacy that outsiders alone could not supply.

The government's position had also evolved. Officials in Washington were aware that the siege had become a political liability. The deaths, the wounding of a marshal, the criticism from Congress and the press, and the attention of the world made a prolonged stalemate unattractive. At the same time, the Watergate crisis was consuming the attention and credibility of the administration. The combination of these pressures made a settlement more desirable, and it made officials more willing to offer commitments that they might not have given earlier.

The question of amnesty or leniency was left largely unresolved. The occupiers sought assurances that they would not be prosecuted, and the government made no promise of immunity. The agreement instead provided for processing and investigation, with the prosecution of those who had committed crimes left to the ordinary process of the law. This arrangement meant that the end of the siege was also the beginning of a long legal struggle, and it explains why the next division of the chapter turns to the courts.

Section 112.16

112.16 — May 8, 1973: Disarmament, Departure, and the End of the Siege

The occupation ended on May 8. Occupants surrendered weapons, and the U.S. Marshals Service took control of the village. Participants were processed by federal officers, and many were arrested. Some Oglala residents, who were not subject to the same procedures, left more informally, and in the final nights many people walked past federal lines to go home. The leadership, including Russell Means and Dennis Banks, was subject to arrest and indictment. Federal agents entered the village, searched it, and collected weapons and other evidence. The Marshals Service describes the end as the surrender of arms and the assumption of control by its personnel.

The duration of seventy-one days is calculated by counting both the first and the last day: from February 27 to May 8 is seventy days elapsed, and seventy-one counting both dates. The Marshals Service speaks of "ten weeks," which is a rounded way of saying seventy days. The May 5 agreement, independently reproduced in the United States Marshals Museum collection, originally scheduled implementation for 7 a.m. on May 9. An operational chronology reports that the timetable was advanced on May 7, and the Marshals Service and Justice Department identify May 8 as the actual end. Students should therefore distinguish the original agreed schedule, a subsequent change, and the documented historical outcome. Documents record plans; they do not always record what happened.

The end of the armed occupation did not resolve the dispute that had caused it. Wilson remained chairman. The treaty review that the occupiers had demanded did not take place in the form they sought. Prosecutions began. The village was left damaged and its residents had to rebuild lives disrupted by ten weeks of siege. The political questions of who represented the Oglala, how treaty obligations should be addressed, and how federal law enforcement should act on reservations continued for years.

The final days were quiet compared with the weeks before. Weapons were gathered and counted. Participants made decisions about whether to remain, to leave quietly, or to submit to processing. Some occupiers slipped away before the formal surrender, which complicated the federal effort to account for everyone who had been in the village. The Marshals Service, taking charge, found a damaged village and a large quantity of weapons, and it recorded the end of the operation as the longest civil disorder in its history.

The departure itself was emotional. Elders and families returned to homes they had left or had been unable to reach for weeks. Participants from other nations dispersed to their own communities, carrying with them the experience of the occupation. The leaders faced arrest and the uncertainty of prosecution. Those who had been on the front line of the siege faced the question of what, if anything, had been gained. The answers they gave varied, and many were ambivalent.

Federal reoccupation of the village took place amid a dispute about the condition in which the occupiers had left it and the damage that had been done. Each side blamed the other, and the question of what had been destroyed by the occupiers, what by federal gunfire, and what by ten weeks of use remained disputed. The assessment of damage, and the claims for compensation that followed, were one of the first of many arguments that continued after the armed phase had ended.

Division 5

Trials, Political Aftermath, and the Historical Memory of Wounded Knee

Sections 112.17–112.20 · Local consequences, judicial proceedings, continuing division, and historical interpretation

Section 112.17

112.17 — The Immediate Aftermath: Damage, Displacement, and Unresolved Local Grievances

When the siege ended, residents of Wounded Knee and its surroundings faced the practical work of recovery. Buildings had been damaged by gunfire and by ten weeks of heavy use. The trading post, the church, and homes bore the marks of the confrontation. Property claims were filed, and accounts of the extent of damage and of who was responsible differ. Occupiers pointed to federal gunfire. Federal officials and some residents pointed to the occupiers. Some families had left during the siege and returned to find their homes changed, and others had been displaced for weeks.

The restoration of federal control did not settle the political dispute. Wilson remained in office, and the allegations of intimidation and misuse of power that had driven the opposition continued. OSCRO members and others who had supported the occupation found themselves subject to arrest and prosecution, and some reported harassment. The immediate result for many families was uncertainty: the cost of defending themselves in court, the fear of reprisals, and the strain on relationships among relatives who had taken opposite sides.

The occupation also left a mark on the village as a place. Wounded Knee had been a small community. After 1973 it was a site known around the world, visited by tourists, journalists, and pilgrims, and the people who lived there had to reconcile their daily lives with that visibility. For some, the attention brought opportunity and recognition. For others, it was an intrusion on a place of mourning and an unwanted reminder of a painful season.

The aftermath for individuals varied widely. Some participants returned to their home communities and resumed ordinary life. Others faced years of litigation, travel to distant courts, and the loss of employment. Local Oglala supporters, who had no national organization to protect them, often bore the greatest burden. The defense committee provided lawyers and funds, but the personal cost of prosecution fell on families, especially those with limited resources.

The wider reservation community also felt the aftermath. The political polarization of the siege continued in the form of suspicion and hostility among neighbors and kin. Participation in or opposition to the occupation became a marker of political identity, and families that had been divided found it difficult to reconcile. Observers have noted that many reservation conflicts of the following years drew on these divisions. The experience of a community in which political disagreement was entangled with threats of violence left a long legacy.

The federal government's own response included investigations of the conduct of individuals on both sides, as well as an inquiry into the conditions that had provoked the occupation. Some of these examinations were announced publicly, and others took place with little notice. The occupiers' demand for investigations of the BIA and the tribal government was met only in part, and the extent to which the investigations that did occur addressed the original grievances is a matter on which participants and historians still differ.

Historical Vocabulary

Occupation, Takeover, Siege, Standoff, and "Wounded Knee II"

Definition. Different terms describe the same event. Occupation is the term most used by participants and scholars. Takeover and seizure appear in federal and press language. Siege describes the federal encirclement. Standoff emphasizes the stalemate. Wounded Knee II and Second Wounded Knee link the event to 1890.

Historical context. Each term carries a judgment about who acted and what the action was. This chapter uses "occupation" and "siege" together, because the village was both held by its occupiers and surrounded by federal forces.

Why it matters. Terminology shapes interpretation: whether the event is seen as protest, rebellion, crime, or defense.

Potential misunderstanding. The numerical label "II" suggests moral equivalence with 1890, which participants and historians have debated.

Section 112.18

112.18 — The Wounded Knee Trials and the Judicial Examination of Government Conduct

Federal prosecutors brought charges against hundreds of people connected with the occupation. Most cases ended in dismissal, acquittal, or limited outcomes, and relatively few produced convictions. Advocacy sources report that only a small number of prosecutions succeeded, and some of those involved local supporters who had brought food into the village. The Wounded Knee Legal Defense/Offense Committee organized defense efforts, and its records, held by the Minnesota Historical Society, are a major archive. Separate prosecutions continued for several years, and Leonard Crow Dog was convicted in a case the Eighth Circuit reviewed in 1976.

The most prominent trial was that of Dennis Banks and Russell Means, the leaders of AIM at Wounded Knee, who were charged with offenses including conspiracy and assault. The case was moved to St. Paul, Minnesota, and was tried before Judge Fred Nichol, a federal judge from South Dakota. The trial began on January 8, 1974. The government's case ended on July 24, and the defense presented its evidence in August. The jury began deliberating on September 12. Defense lawyers, including William Kunstler, Mark Lane, and Kenneth Tilsen, made the treaty and the government's conduct central to their case, and Nichol instructed the jury that the treaty was in evidence and that possible violations could be considered in assessing the defendants' motives.

The trial exposed serious problems with the government's conduct. Nichol criticized the prosecutor and the FBI for deceiving the court about the chief government witness, Louis Moves Camp, and for withholding evidence. He also found that the military had been involved in civilian law enforcement at Wounded Knee in ways that the government had tried to conceal. Late in the deliberations, a juror became ill. The government refused to proceed with eleven jurors. On September 16, 1974, Nichol dismissed the remaining charges, citing government misconduct, including the refusal to allow the jury system to function, the improper military involvement, and the conduct of the prosecution and the FBI. In his memorandum decision he described the prosecution's conduct as offensive to traditional notions of justice. The government appealed, and in 1975 the Eighth Circuit held that the appeal was barred by the Double Jeopardy Clause, because the dismissal had terminated the trial in the defendants' favor after jeopardy had attached.

It is important not to overstate the result. The dismissal was not an acquittal, and it did not decide whether the defendants had committed the acts alleged. Nor did it establish that every action by federal officials was unlawful. Other courts reached different conclusions about the Posse Comitatus Act in other Wounded Knee prosecutions, holding that equipment and passive advice were permitted, and civil litigation by Pine Ridge residents over alleged unlawful military involvement continued for years. The legal record shows that a federal judge found serious misconduct in the prosecution of the occupation's leaders, and that the legal consequences for other defendants varied widely.

The courtroom itself became a stage on which the issues of the occupation were reargued. The defense used the trial to present the treaty history, the conditions on Pine Ridge, and the conduct of federal officials, and witnesses testified about each. Judge Nichol permitted a good deal of this evidence, and his instruction about the treaty was an unusual acknowledgment of its relevance to the defendants' motives. At the same time the judge made clear that the treaty could not be used as a legal defense to the charges, and he kept the jury's task focused on whether the defendants had committed the offenses alleged.

The government's difficulties with its principal witness drew particular attention. Nichol concluded that the prosecution and the FBI had misled the court about the witness and about the circumstances in which he had been questioned, and he criticized the withholding of material that the defense was entitled to see. The episode was widely read as evidence that the government had been willing to bend the rules to obtain convictions of the occupation's leaders. Others have argued that the failures were mistakes of a hurried and sprawling investigation rather than a deliberate scheme, and the surviving record supports a range of interpretations about intent.

The wider legacy of the trials includes civil litigation by residents of Pine Ridge, who alleged that the use of military force during the siege had violated their rights. These cases proceeded through the federal courts over many years and raised questions about the limits of military assistance to civilian authorities. Their outcomes were mixed. Together with the criminal cases, they illustrate the ways in which the legal system was used both to punish and to scrutinize the actions of the government at Wounded Knee.

Section 112.19

112.19 — Pine Ridge after the Occupation: Political Violence and Competing Assessments

Political conflict on Pine Ridge continued after the occupation. Wilson's administration remained in power, and in 1974 he was reelected in a contested election in which Russell Means was a candidate. Wilson's opponents alleged intimidation and irregularities. Wilson's supporters regarded the result as a vindication of the elected government. In October 1973, Pedro Bissonette, the OSCRO director, was killed in a confrontation with law enforcement officers, and the circumstances were disputed. Wilson lost office in the election of 1976, to Albert Trimble.

Many opponents of the tribal government died violently in these years. Advocacy sources and some scholars cite a large number of deaths, often over sixty, and describe an atmosphere of intimidation in which crimes against AIM supporters were not adequately investigated. Federal and tribal officials disputed these characterizations and pointed to the difficulties of policing a large reservation. The counts rely on varying definitions of a political killing and on incomplete investigations, and historians treat the figures as contested. What the evidence does support is a climate of fear and a pattern of unsolved cases that deepened distrust of both tribal and federal authorities.

The violence reached a different scale on June 26, 1975, when a shootout on the Jumping Bull property left two FBI agents and a young AIM supporter dead. That event and its legal aftermath, including the conviction of Leonard Peltier, belong to the wider history of AIM treated in Chapter 110 and are mentioned here only to explain the escalation.

The disappearance and probable killing of Ray Robinson show the difficulty of the evidence. Robinson, a Black civil-rights activist with experience in the wider U.S. freedom struggle, traveled to Wounded Knee and subsequently disappeared. In February 2014, newly released FBI investigative records led the Bureau to publicly conclude that Robinson had been killed during the occupation. The conclusion rested on witness accounts and investigative material, not a recovered body or a criminal conviction. Robinson’s remains have not been located, his family has continued to seek answers, and allegations naming particular individuals have not been established through a prosecution. The historically responsible distinction is therefore between the FBI’s documented conclusion that he died, the unresolved location of his remains, and still-contested allegations about who caused his death. For primary evidence and the family’s response, see the Associated Press report of February 20, 2014 and the underlying FBI investigative releases.

Interviews with Pine Ridge residents from the mid-1970s, including those preserved in broadcast archives, show a community under stress. Supporters of the tribal government described a campaign of disruption by AIM that endangered ordinary people. Opponents described intimidation and a failure of protection. Many residents, who aligned with neither faction, described a climate in which it was dangerous to speak, and they sought above all a return to ordinary life. These accounts do not agree, and a historian who relies on one set without the others will produce a distorted picture.

Investigation of the violence was uneven. Tribal police, BIA officers, and the FBI shared responsibility for crimes on the reservation, and jurisdictional divisions complicated the pursuit of cases. Many killings went unsolved or produced no prosecution. Critics have argued that the federal authorities showed less zeal in investigating crimes against AIM supporters than in prosecuting AIM members, while officials emphasized the difficulty of gathering evidence in a community afraid to cooperate. The failure of justice in these cases left lasting resentment and informs the later history of jurisdiction and law enforcement in Native communities.

The election of 1976, which removed Wilson from office, suggests that the reservation's political divisions were more fluid than the factional language of the period implies. Voters weighed a range of concerns, including the conduct of the administration, the quality of services, and the desire for stability, and the outcome did not simply reflect the preferences of AIM or its opponents. The change of leadership did not end the controversies of the decade, but it showed that the constitutional system could produce political change.

Historical Debate

Achievements and Human Costs of the Occupation

The question: Should the occupation be judged mainly by what it achieved in visibility and political mobilization, or by its costs in lives, prosecutions, and reservation division?

Interpretation A — A catalyst. The occupation made Indigenous treaty and civil-rights grievances visible worldwide, inspired new activism and cultural pride, and exposed federal misconduct through the 1974 trial.
Interpretation B — A costly failure at the local level. The occupation did not remove Wilson, did not produce treaty review, left two dead and others wounded, and was followed by a period of violence on Pine Ridge.
Interpretation C — Inseparable achievements and costs. The visibility and the suffering came from the same events and cannot be neatly weighed. Different participants experienced the result differently.

Evidence: Court records, press and broadcast reports, oral histories, tribal election results, and later federal policy changes.

Points of disagreement: Causation (how much later reform was due to the occupation), scale (the number and attribution of deaths), and interpretation (what counts as success).

Why it matters: The judgment affects how the occupation is taught, remembered, and used as a model for later protest.

Section 112.20

112.20 — Two Wounded Knees: 1890, 1973, and the Continuing Meaning of Sovereignty

By 1973 the name Wounded Knee referred to two events separated by eighty-three years. The first was a mass killing. The second was a political action that sought to claim the first as part of a continuing struggle. The connection was powerful, and it was not obvious. Descendants of the 1890 victims had their own mourning traditions, and some did not see the occupation as an appropriate way of honoring them. Others saw the occupation as precisely that. The Oglala community's memories of 1973 differ by family, political alignment, and generation.

Memory has been kept through anniversary gatherings, photographs, oral histories, memoirs by participants, and historical scholarship. The 1990 centennial of the massacre, which included commemorative rides, brought attention to 1890. In Senate Concurrent Resolution 153, agreed to on October 25, 1990, Congress expressed deep regret for the 1890 massacre and supported reconciliation with descendants and survivors; the resolution did not constitute an apology or settlement concerning the 1973 occupation. Gatherings in 2023 marked fifty years since the occupation and drew participants, descendants, and scholars. Smith and Warrior's history of the Indian movement and Reinhardt's study of Oglala politics offer interpretations that differ in emphasis: one tracing the movement from Alcatraz to Wounded Knee, the other situating the occupation in Oglala political history.

The occupation's immediate political results were mixed. The treaty hearings the occupiers sought did not occur, Wilson remained in office until 1976, and many participants faced prosecution. At the same time federal Indian policy moved toward expanded tribal administration in the years that followed, a development examined in Chapter 114, and Indigenous visibility and organizing grew. Historians disagree about how much of that change flowed from Wounded Knee and how much from other pressures. A symbolic gain in visibility should not be mistaken for governmental reform.

Sovereignty remained the central unresolved question. For the occupiers it meant treaty-based nationhood. For the elected government it meant the authority of the Oglala constitution. For federal officials it meant a government-to-government relationship. The events of 1973 did not settle among these. They did make the debate harder to avoid, and the arguments of those weeks echo in later disputes over jurisdiction and recognition (Chapter 118) and in the broader sovereignty era (Chapter 148).

Oral histories and memoirs are essential to the memory of the occupation, and they also demand care. Participants often recall the experience as a defining moment of their lives, and they emphasize the sense of solidarity and purpose. Others recall fear, exhaustion, and loss. The passage of decades shapes memory, and published recollections may be influenced by the telling and retelling that follows a famous event. Historians compare these accounts with contemporary records to identify where they converge and where they differ.

The question of who owns the memory of Wounded Knee is itself contested. AIM veterans, Oglala families, scholars, filmmakers, and visitors all make claims about its meaning. Some Oglala people resent the use of the village as a symbol by outsiders, while others welcome the attention it brings to the conditions of the reservation. The site's role as a place of mourning, a destination for visitors, and a symbol of resistance creates tensions that no single narrative resolves.

The question posed at the beginning of the chapter, how a struggle over leadership became a national confrontation over sovereignty, has no single answer. The evidence supports a view in which local political dispute, treaty memory, national activism, federal anxiety, and the dynamics of a siege combined, and in which the participants gained recognition and lost lives, security, and unity. Students who can hold these facts together, without reducing them to a story of simple triumph or simple failure, will be well prepared for the chapters on self-determination, tribal courts, and sovereignty that follow.

Chapter Summary

The 1973 occupation of Wounded Knee began as an Oglala political crisis and became a national confrontation over treaty rights, tribal government, and federal power. It began on February 27, 1973, and ended on May 8, after seventy-one days.

The crisis grew from conflict over the administration of Richard Wilson, who was elected tribal chairman in 1972. Opponents accused him of favoritism, misuse of office, and intimidation, and he and his supporters rejected the accusations. The February 1973 impeachment hearing ended without his removal, and opponents concluded that constitutional remedies had failed.

The Oglala Sioux Civil Rights Organization, led by local people including Pedro Bissonette, Gladys Bissonette, and Ellen Moves Camp, asked AIM for help. The anger over the killing of Wesley Bad Heart Bull and the Custer confrontation of February 6 strengthened the alliance. The request was not unanimous, and many Oglala opposed the occupation.

The choice of Wounded Knee tied the present dispute to the massacre of 1890 and to the Treaty of Fort Laramie of 1868. The occupiers demanded treaty review and investigations, and in March they proclaimed an Independent Oglala Nation. Sovereignty meant different things to occupiers, the elected council, and federal officials.

Inside the village, women, elders, medics, and spiritual leaders sustained a community under siege. Food, water, shelter, and medicine were scarce, and supply depended on night movement through federal lines. Residents who did not support the occupation are among the least documented participants.

The federal response involved marshals, FBI agents, and BIA police, supported by military equipment and advice. The geography of the village, the roads, and the high ground shaped both strategy and civilian experience. Press and radio coverage made the confrontation a global event, including through Sacheen Littlefeather's appearance at the Academy Awards.

Negotiations were repeatedly stalled by the question of disarmament. The April 5 agreement set no clear timetable, and each side accused the other of bad faith. A marshal was paralyzed in late March, and Frank Clearwater and Lawrence Lamont died in April. Lamont's death moved many elders toward ending the siege.

The May 5 agreement arranged the surrender of weapons and promised investigations and treaty-related meetings. The armed occupation ended on May 8, though the reproduced agreement schedules arrangements to May 9. Many promised steps were not carried out in the form the occupiers sought.

Prosecutions followed. Judge Fred Nichol dismissed the remaining charges against Dennis Banks and Russell Means on September 16, 1974, citing government misconduct, and the Eighth Circuit held in 1975 that the government's appeal was barred by double jeopardy. Other courts decided other Wounded Knee cases differently, and outcomes varied widely among defendants.

Pine Ridge remained divided. Violence, unresolved cases such as that of Ray Robinson, and contested elections marked the following years. Historians disagree about whether the occupation is best seen as a victory for visibility, an incomplete local struggle, an assertion of treaty sovereignty, or an event whose achievements and costs cannot be separated, and this chapter has asked students to weigh the evidence for each.

Chapter Reflection

Wounded Knee in 1973 matters because it shows how a local dispute over government can carry the weight of a nation's history. The people who occupied the village were responding to conditions on Pine Ridge, and they were also invoking treaties, a massacre, and a century of dispossession. Both layers were real, and neither cancels the other.

The event also reveals the limits of any single claim to legitimacy. An elected government, a body of treaty-minded elders, a movement of activists, and a federal agency each held part of the authority at issue, and none held all of it. The occupation exposed that incompleteness in a way that could not be ignored.

Its consequences ran in several directions. It made Indigenous sovereignty a subject of national debate, produced a landmark judicial finding of government misconduct, and strengthened later activism. It also cost lives, led to prosecutions, and deepened divisions on Pine Ridge that lasted for years. Those outcomes belong together in any honest account.

For this textbook the chapter is a bridge. It links the treaty history of Volume VII and the assimilation era of Volume VIII to the self-determination and sovereignty chapters that follow. It also shows why the question of who speaks for a nation remains alive, a question taken up again in later chapters on tribal government, recognition, and sovereignty.
