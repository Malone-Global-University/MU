Indigenous Peoples and the First World War

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

Indigenous men and women served in a war fought by governments that, in many cases, still restricted Indigenous political rights, land, and self-government. That contradiction is the subject of this chapter.

In August 1914, Francis Pegahmagabow, an Anishinaabe man from Parry Island, Ontario, enlisted in the Canadian Expeditionary Force. He would become one of the most decorated Indigenous soldiers of the war. In the same months, women at Six Nations of the Grand River organized a patriotic league to send supplies overseas. Later, other women from the same community petitioned the King to oppose compulsory service. These were not contradictory impulses so much as different answers to a shared question about what Indigenous nations owed to the states that claimed authority over them.

The documented scale of participation was substantial. Estimates commonly cited run to roughly 12,000 Native Americans in the United States and more than 4,000 Indigenous personnel in Canada, with incomplete counts for Métis and Inuit. These figures are uncertain because sources count enlistments, registrations, legal status categories, or completed overseas service differently, and because military records did not consistently record Indigenous identity.

This chapter covers 1914–1924, with emphasis on 1914–1919. It follows recruitment and motives, battlefield service, the home front, the politics of conscription and treaty obligation, and the postwar return. It treats Indigenous participation as neither uniform patriotism nor simple exploitation. Individuals volunteered for duty, pay, family tradition, adventure, or hope of recognition; communities debated; some opposed compulsory service on grounds of nationhood.

Evidence comes from military service files, government correspondence, petitions, newspapers, veterans' questionnaires, and later oral history and memoir. Each has limits. Service files record administrative facts, not experience; recollections recorded decades later are retrospective memory; official correspondence usually reflects the perspective of Indian agents and officers rather than Indigenous communities.

The chapter connects to its neighbors. Chapter 91 treated Indigenous economies and Chapter 93 treated citizenship law; this chapter examines their wartime intersection without repeating them. Chapter 95 turns to organized criticism of assimilation policy, to which wartime experience contributed. Chapters 101 and 102 treat the Second World War and code talking in full.

The central argument is that the war expanded Indigenous military participation, public visibility, and political expectations, but did not dissolve colonial inequality. Service did not automatically produce equality. Its most lasting political significance lay in the contrast between sacrifice and the persistence of unequal treatment.

The war also altered how Indigenous history could be documented. Soldiers appeared in military bureaucracies that standardized dates and ranks but often omitted their nations; women’s community organizations created material objects and correspondence that archives did not always preserve; and postwar veterans explained their service to collectors who chose which testimony to retain. The chapter therefore connects military action to the politics of the record itself. It examines differences between original administrative files, translated petitions, contemporary diaries, and later accounts, and it distinguishes an evidenced quotation from an editorial paraphrase. Those distinctions matter because wartime commemoration can easily flatten a complex history of decisions, sacrifices, and inequalities into a single account of loyal service.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the differing political positions of Indigenous nations toward the Crown, Canada, and the United States in 1914–1918.
  2. Distinguish enlistment, registration, conscription, and overseas service as legal and statistical categories.
  3. Trace the development of Indigenous recruitment in Canada and the United States.
  4. Analyze the range of motives behind Indigenous military service and opposition to it.
  5. Describe the experiences of Indigenous soldiers in Canadian, American, and Newfoundland forces.
  6. Evaluate the meaning and limits of military decorations.
  7. Assess the contributions of Indigenous women to the home front and military nursing.
  8. Explain the arguments against conscription advanced by First Nations and the outcome of Order-in-Council P.C. 111.
  9. Compare wartime conditions across regions, including Alaska, the Southwest, the Canadian North, and Labrador.
  10. Analyze postwar veterans' benefits and Soldier Settlement.
  11. Interpret the 1919 U.S. veterans' citizenship provision without overstating it.
  12. Evaluate whether the war advanced Indigenous political equality.

Historical Context

By 1914 most Indigenous nations in Canada and the United States lived under reserve and reservation systems, as earlier chapters showed. In Canada, the Indian Act governed the lives of status Indians through Indian agents; status Indians generally could not vote federally unless they gave up status. In the United States, legal status varied: some Native people held citizenship through allotment, treaty provisions, or other routes, while many did not, and citizenship did not necessarily mean full access to political rights in practice. Boarding and residential schools had shaped a generation of young adults, giving many English literacy and some military-relevant training, while also disrupting families.

Indigenous nations also had their own political and military traditions. Some communities, including the Haudenosaunee, understood their relationship to the Crown through long-standing treaty diplomacy rather than subjecthood. Those understandings stood in tension with state administration and are central to Section 94.15. Newfoundland, a separate dominion in these years, raised its own forces and is distinguished throughout from Canada.

Division I — Indigenous Nations and the Coming of World War

Section 94.1

Indigenous North America in 1914: Empire, Nationhood, and the Outbreak of War

Canada entered the war in August 1914 as part of the British Empire, while the United States remained neutral until April 1917. That difference in timing shaped everything that followed. Indigenous people in Canada faced a war declared by a Crown with which many nations claimed treaty relationships, administered through a state that governed them under the Indian Act. In the United States, Indigenous men entered a conflict declared by a government that had made treaties with their nations but had also pursued allotment and assimilation.

These relationships were not the same. Some Indigenous nations saw themselves as allies of the Crown, not subjects; others, in the United States, held a trust relationship with federal authorities; many individuals held a mixture of statuses. Chapter 93 treats citizenship law in full; here the point is that legal position, treaty memory, and local circumstance created different frames for the same question: whether to take part.

The chronology mattered to the way Indigenous communities understood the conflict. Britain entered the European war on 4 August 1914 after Germany violated Belgian neutrality, and the Canadian government mobilized forces as an imperial dominion without a separate declaration of war. Yet membership in the British Empire did not have the same political meaning everywhere. A farmer on an Ontario reserve might encounter the Indian Department in almost every matter concerning land or funds, while a Haudenosaunee council could insist that its treaty relationship was with the Crown as an allied nation, not as a municipal constituency. An Inuk family in Labrador lived in Newfoundland's separate political orbit. Thus a single announcement of war initiated overlapping, unequal conversations about service and obligation rather than a unified Indigenous reaction.

For communities already navigating the effects of railway expansion, allotment, reserves, and schools, the European crisis initially appeared remote. Word traveled by government agent, newspaper, church, trader, or personal letter; some settlements had regular access to daily journalism, while others received news weeks later. Recruiting centers were likewise distributed unevenly. Distance affected the ability to enlist, the expense of travel, and the visibility of recruiters. Geographic isolation did not mean indifference: families debated unfamiliar European place names in terms of recognizable questions about kinship, alliance, defense, and dependence upon the state. Any historian who speaks of an instant 'Indigenous war effort' risks imposing the timeline of national capitals upon communities that learned about mobilization through slower and more complicated channels.

In the United States, neutrality before April 1917 produced a different setting. Indigenous newspapers and educated public figures could follow events in Europe while American Indian communities continued to confront guardianship, allotment, and an uneven patchwork of citizenship. Some individuals enlisted in forces before the formal United States entry or crossed borders in search of opportunity; others experienced the war principally through rising prices and family migration. Washington's eventual mobilization brought national conscription machinery into local communities, multiplying interactions with agents, registrars, and military doctors. The new bureaucracy had to classify men whose membership in Indigenous nations might not fit a draft form's simple boxes for citizenship and nationality. Formal neutrality, therefore, did not mean an absence of Indigenous wartime experience, but it did shape the timing of legal obligations.

Military service already had distinct Indigenous histories. Alliance diplomacy, defense of homelands, armed conflict with colonial forces, participation in imperial military campaigns, and service in national armies all formed precedents. Some First Nations leaders emphasized commitments to the British Crown; others emphasized past promises that people living in peace on reserves would not be compelled to fight abroad. In the United States, experience as scouts or soldiers created possible networks of military familiarity without erasing collective memories of nineteenth-century dispossession. Neither the image of a uniformly eager warrior nor that of an entirely reluctant subject describes this range. Service in a foreign army could be interpreted as preserving one's community, improving one's prospects, meeting the expectations of a recruiter, or accepting a state authority still being disputed.

The chapter's basic methodological point follows. Governments left attestation papers, troop lists, enlistment circulars, and correspondence written mainly to administer manpower. Indigenous soldiers and families left letters, testimony, community records, later interviews, and memories often unevenly preserved. To understand the war as Indigenous history, these records must be read together: a bureaucratic entry can establish an enlistment date but cannot reveal the conversation at home; a recollection can explain motive but may compress dates or reflect later political experience. The contrast is especially important here, because wartime publicity often portrayed Indigenous service as evidence that assimilation was succeeding, even as Indigenous speakers described the same service through nationhood, family obligation, and the right to choose.

Section 94.2

First Nations Recruitment and Voluntary Enlistment in Canada, 1914–1917

Early Canadian enlistment was shaped by uncertainty. Officials were at times unsure whether and how to accept First Nations volunteers, and some men were accepted while others were discouraged. As casualties mounted and manpower demands grew, restrictions eased and recruiting became more organized by 1917. Indian agents, military officers, community leaders, and recruiting campaigns all influenced who enlisted.

Enlistment varied regionally. Communities such as Six Nations contributed many volunteers, while others, especially more remote ones, contributed fewer. Counting is difficult: attestation papers did not always record Indigenous identity consistently, Métis men often appear without any identifying mark, and Inuit service is poorly documented. The figure of more than 4,000 should be read as a documented minimum, not a precise total.

The Canadian Expeditionary Force began mobilizing during a moment of uncertainty about Indigenous recruits. Some officers welcomed volunteers and praised their fitness, while others worried about language, paperwork, race, or unfamiliarity with military discipline. There was no single practice across the recruiting system in 1914. Men from particular communities reached local recruiters, were medically examined, and sometimes moved to Valcartier or other training grounds, where national military needs increasingly outweighed earlier reluctance. Francis Pegahmagabow, associated with the Parry Island or Wasauksing community, appeared among the early volunteers. His story should not be used to imply that all First Nations recruitment was straightforward: acceptance depended on local officials, distance, timing, the kind of unit sought, and the changing demands of the war.

The severity and duration of the war altered recruitment policy. Casualties in 1915 and 1916, and the need to replace men already serving in France and Belgium, encouraged the Canadian authorities to seek recruits from additional groups. Recruitment through Indian agents could be simultaneously helpful and intrusive. Agents might circulate notices, report local responses, translate official messages, or offer arrangements for travel, but they occupied positions of substantial power over reserve residents. A young man could see an agent as an employment intermediary, an obstacle to his ambitions, or the representative of an unwanted policy. In some places chiefs or councils encouraged service; in others they warned against pressure on men whose political rights the state limited. Recruitment was not merely the delivery of national propaganda to passive recipients: families assessed, negotiated, and sometimes resisted the invitations.

The 114th Battalion, formed in 1915–1916 and associated especially with Six Nations of the Grand River, has often served as an emblem of Canadian Indigenous participation. Its recruitment involved public appeals and symbolic recognition, including an embroidered flag presented by the Six Nations Women's Patriotic League. But not every recruit served in a unit with such an identity, and the battalion did not survive as an intact infantry formation on the Western Front. Like many Canadian units, it was broken up to provide reinforcements. A history built only around famous Indigenous-branded units would therefore omit soldiers distributed throughout the Canadian Expeditionary Force. Men entered infantry battalions, service and transport organizations, forestry companies, engineering and railway formations, or other wartime assignments as manpower requirements changed.

Numerical claims require equal care. Canadian commemorative sources commonly state that more than four thousand Indigenous people served in uniform during the First World War, but the surviving category counts are incomplete. Status Indian enlistments could sometimes be tracked through Department of Indian Affairs correspondence; Métis recruits frequently appeared on forms without an identifying classification, and Inuit cases were scattered among unfamiliar jurisdictions and inconsistent record series. Researchers must distinguish formal enlistment from overseas deployment and casualties from total service. A service record may identify occupation, religion, next of kin, and birthplace while saying little about nation or ancestry. A high community enlistment rate cannot be generalized to all Canada, just as low numbers from a distant region do not prove a lack of willingness.

Regional conditions helped explain the pattern. Communities near Brantford or accessible rail centers had recruiting connections unavailable to settlements reached largely by water. Household economies also mattered: losing a young man could imperil agriculture, fishing, logging, seasonal labor, or care for relatives. In communities with very small populations, even a modest number of enlistments affected schools, ceremonial obligations, and the transmission of practical knowledge. Some volunteers understood service as an alliance obligation to the Crown; others sought wages or independence from agency supervision. The historical task is not to select one motive as 'authentic' but to explain why officials' strategic manpower needs and Indigenous families' calculations sometimes converged and sometimes came into sharp conflict.

Section 94.3

Native American Enlistment and the United States' Entry into the War, 1917

After April 1917, Native Americans entered service by volunteering in the Army and Navy and by registering under Selective Service. Many already held citizenship; others did not. It would be wrong to assume that all were noncitizens or that all volunteered. Recruitment drew from Oklahoma, the Plains, the Southwest, the Great Lakes, and other regions, and the commonly cited total of about 12,000 reflects varying definitions of service.

Local circumstances mattered: the attitude of a draft board or agent, the presence of schools that had trained young men, and the proximity of recruiting stations all shaped outcomes.

American mobilization began after Congress declared war in April 1917. The Native population was legally heterogeneous: members of some nations had obtained U.S. citizenship through treaties or allotment, others through particular statutes or marriage, and many Indigenous people were still regarded by federal authorities as noncitizens. Nevertheless, voluntary enlistment drew men from Oklahoma, the Dakotas, the Great Lakes, the Southwest, and elsewhere. Recruiting posters and wartime speakers framed military service as patriotic participation in democracy. For Indigenous listeners whose own rights remained uncertain, that language could be appealing, paradoxical, or objectionable. It is equally misleading to assume that every recruit saw himself as serving only the United States or that tribal identity vanished when a military oath was taken.

The Selective Service Act of 18 May 1917 created a highly decentralized mechanism for registering and selecting men. Local boards operated under federal supervision and had to decide liability, medical fitness, exemptions, and deferments. Original forms asked about citizenship, occupation, dependents, and other matters that could expose conflicting classifications. The first large registration on 5 June 1917 principally concerned men aged twenty-one to thirty; later registrations reached newly eligible men and, in September 1918, the broader range of eighteen to forty-five. Registration itself did not mean actual induction. Nor did all those who entered the armed forces pass through the draft: some volunteered before registration, others enlisted afterward, and some received exemptions or never served. The National Archives' registration series provides indispensable information precisely because it records men who may never have worn a uniform.

The question of noncitizen Indigenous men's liability was not uniform in practice. Statutory categories, military interpretations, and local decisions intersected with tribal political relationships. A local board could treat a registrant according to a citizenship status recorded on a form while the registrant or his community understood the matter differently. Some cases generated appeals and correspondence about exemption, federal guardianship, or special relations to Indian nations. Officials sought manpower and administrative uniformity, but Indian Affairs policy had long rested on differentiation. Students should resist converting the existence of Native draftees into a claim that every noncitizen Indigenous man was automatically liable under precisely the same legal theory. Establish the law, read the local record, and separate the stages of registration, selection, induction, and discharge.

Once accepted, Native American soldiers mostly served in the army's existing units rather than in a single nationwide Indigenous formation. Some were deployed to France with the American Expeditionary Forces; others performed military work in training, supply, transport, and support structures. Native nurses and civilian volunteers also joined wartime organizations. The National Museum of the American Indian documents extensive Red Cross activity in Indigenous communities, and women's contributions should not disappear behind a count of soldiers. Public reporting sometimes celebrated Native service through the stereotype of the 'natural warrior,' while actual veterans faced the same essential problems as other soldiers: fatigue, disease, technical training, orders, transport, weaponry, and the industrial scale of death.

The total often given as approximately twelve thousand Native Americans in military service is useful as a scale indicator, not a number to treat as an exhaustive audited roster. A museum chronology also identifies nearly twelve thousand Native men registered for military service by September 1917, a different category that must not be silently substituted for the number who served. The wartime draft's documents, national commemorations, and later researchers use different definitions and sometimes different denominators. This is especially significant for Indigenous people omitted from ethnic categories or enrolled as members of mixed communities. The chapter must tell readers what a statistic measures before asking them to interpret its significance. Without that discipline, a seemingly precise total can obscure more history than it reveals.

Documentary research trail: National Archives (U.S.), National Museum of the American Indian.

Section 94.4

Why Indigenous Men Enlisted: Duty, Opportunity, Community, and Individual Choice

No single explanation accounts for Indigenous military participation. Some men followed family military traditions or understood service as fulfilling obligations to allies. Some felt patriotism toward the Crown or the United States. Others sought wages, training, travel, or adventure, in settings where reserve and reservation economies offered few options. Some hoped that service would improve how governments treated Indigenous people. Others opposed enlistment or considered foreign wars no obligation of their nations.

Historians have only partial access to these motives. Service files rarely record reasons; letters, interviews, and later memoirs are fuller but retrospective and unevenly preserved.

A volunteer's motives were rarely recorded fully in an attestation form. Officials wanted age, address, health, family circumstances, and the information necessary to place a recruit in a unit. Later biographers, newspapers, or family members might attribute enlistment to patriotism, but such language could have served recruitment or commemoration. Indigenous men's reasons could change over time. Someone initially drawn by pay or the chance to travel might come to understand service through comradeship after training; someone who thought of military duty as a Crown alliance could become deeply critical of Indian Affairs after returning home. Historical explanation requires room for these shifts rather than treating the recruit's original decision as a single permanent motive.

The material calculation was substantial. For individuals living amid land restrictions, limited credit, seasonal employment, and agent-supervised economic activity, military wages offered an alternative to occupations available locally. Pay and allowances could assist relatives, although deductions, interruptions, and the cost of maintaining a home complicated the benefit. Training might confer technical skills or access to employment networks. Travel could be attractive or frightening, particularly when young people had previously left home only through mission schooling or seasonal labor. These considerations did not make the service decision mercenary. Economic opportunity, kin obligations, and political convictions commonly coexist in the same household, and historians cannot insist that only one counts as 'real' service.

For other men, service expressed inherited understandings of honorable conduct, protection, or public responsibility. These terms must be grounded in the speaker's nation and life, not assigned to all Indigenous societies. Haudenosaunee traditions of diplomatic alliance could lead some members to consider the Crown a legitimate partner in wartime, while other leaders cited the same relationship to reject compulsory service. A person who volunteered could identify himself as a member of his own nation as well as a soldier of a state. Army units often brought together individuals who had never previously encountered one another, making military life an unexpected site of intertribal contact, disagreement, and solidarity. These connections sometimes mattered in the postwar politics addressed in Division V.

Opposition is equally part of the record. Some families needed the labor of young adults to sustain farms, hunting territories, fisheries, and care networks. Others distrusted recruiting speeches delivered by agents whose peacetime responsibilities included enforcing unpopular restrictions. People could oppose overseas service on religious grounds or believe that a government's control over their homeland made its demand for military sacrifice unjust. A nation might honor volunteers while disputing the legal right of that government to draft anyone. Women who knitted supplies or raised money could also petition for the return of young relatives. Such positions were not necessarily inconsistent: voluntary aid and coerced participation were different political questions.

The best historical evidence pairs individual testimony with community circumstances. A service letter may reveal a soldier's stated reasons but not his parents' response; a band council minute may document collective opposition while overlooking the views of volunteers; a photograph in uniform shows a moment of participation but little about subsequent discrimination. Rather than impose a contest between loyalty and resistance, this section should treat enlistment as a decision made at the intersection of the nation, household, employer, recruiter, and state. That approach prepares students to understand why the war could both advance public recognition and deepen disputes over political rights without any historical contradiction.

Historical Vocabulary

Voluntary Enlistment

Definition: Joining an armed force by individual decision rather than compulsory induction.

Historical context: Military recruiting in Canada, the United States and Newfoundland, 1914–1918, under unequal economic and political conditions.

Why it matters: Separates choice, mixed motives and community obligations from the state’s power to conscript.

Potential misunderstanding: Voluntary does not mean free of economic pressure or of institutional persuasion.

Cause and Effect

Why Indigenous Recruitment Increased, 1914–1918

Long-term setting: Reserve and reservation economies, treaty relationships, military histories, and restrictions on political rights shaped the choices available before 1914.

Immediate pressures: Mounting casualties, Canadian manpower shortages, American entry into war, expanding local recruitment, and the promise of pay or opportunity intensified military engagement.

Individual decisions: Volunteering could express duty, desire for travel, economic need, family tradition or hope for recognition; opposing conscription did not necessarily mean opposing volunteers.

Effects: Expanded military participation, new intercommunity networks, family burdens, deaths, disability and later political arguments.

Section 94.5

Military Training, Unit Assignment, and Indigenous Soldiers in Uniform

Entering the army meant travel to training camps, medical examination, discipline, new equipment, and close contact with non-Indigenous soldiers. Language barriers affected some recruits, particularly those with limited English. Stereotypes about Indigenous soldiers as natural scouts or snipers circulated among officers and in the press; they could create opportunities but also confined men to assumed roles. Treatment varied by unit, officer, and individual.

The Canadian Expeditionary Force, American Expeditionary Forces, U.S. Navy, and Newfoundland Regiment were distinct institutions with different rules. Within them, army service offered pay, skill, and recognition, yet reproduced racial hierarchies.

The move from civilian life to military service was an institutional transition. In Canadian camps such as Valcartier and in American training establishments, recruits encountered standardized medical examinations, equipment issue, hierarchical command, close-order drill, and a demanding schedule. Civilians accustomed to seasonal work, family enterprises, or relative independence were placed into organizations where an order could determine when to eat, sleep, travel, and risk death. Training sometimes offered instruction in first aid, marksmanship, communications, transport, field engineering, or handling specialized equipment, though assignment depended on the military's assessment of available skills. A recruit's prior experience as hunter or laborer might assist with particular tasks without preparing him for poison gas, machine-gun fire, wire obstacles, or bombardment.

Language mattered at several levels. Orders had to be understood quickly and precisely, and most Canadian and American units operated in English, although some soldiers were familiar with French or Indigenous languages. A man who could communicate readily with family in his own language might struggle to understand military technical vocabulary. School attendance sometimes reduced this barrier, but it would be a mistake to praise coercive schooling merely because its graduates could read military orders. Translation, informal interpretation by comrades, repeated demonstrations, and shared labor all helped soldiers learn. Where records are silent, historians should not invent a language difficulty merely from an Indigenous identity; many recruits were multilingual and used English fluently before enlistment.

Military assignment also exposed the tension between actual ability and racial stereotypes. Newspapers and some officers represented Indigenous men as naturally suited to scouting and sniping because they supposedly possessed innate stealth or hunting instincts. This language romanticized military danger and could make particularly hazardous assignments appear inevitable. Some men had practical skills gained through hunting, trapping, forestry, or ranching and used them effectively, but soldiering demanded learned techniques and discipline, and many Indigenous soldiers served outside front-line scouting. The Canadian Expeditionary Force included Indigenous men in infantry, railway and engineering troops, the forestry corps, veterinary and transport work, and other organizations. An Indigenous soldier's career must be reconstructed from records rather than inferred from publicity about identity.

The Canadian, American, and Newfoundland forces were institutionally distinct. Canadian recruits swore to serve within imperial arrangements and often trained in Canada before departing for Britain; Americans were mobilized in 1917 into the expanding American Expeditionary Forces; Newfoundland maintained its own regiment and administrative responsibilities during this war. Paths into the Western Front thus differed by the country or dominion under which a person enlisted. The same military landscape imposed shared hazards: transport through a submarine-threatened Atlantic, damp training fields, crowded camps, and the psychological impact of losing comrades before or after entering combat. Women serving as nurses entered separate structures of professional obligation and danger, as discussed in Section 94.11.

Training records and postings offer historians a framework for comparison but not a full measure of experience. A soldier assigned to a reserve battalion may have performed essential work without visiting the front, while a man recorded as a combatant might have spent months in hospital after injury. Neither case should be flattened into a heroic photograph or summarized with an ethnicity label. A complete account examines rank, unit, skills, movement, service dates, medical experiences, and the constraints of the institution. It also recognizes that human relationships formed within units could coexist with discrimination in hiring, discipline, advancement, or publicity. The battlefield cases that follow are meaningful precisely when they are understood within these ordinary structures of modern military service.

Section 94.6

Francis Pegahmagabow, Henry Norwest, and Indigenous Service in the Canadian Corps

Francis Pegahmagabow enlisted in August 1914 and served as a scout and sniper with the Canadian Expeditionary Force. He was awarded the Military Medal and two bars, a distinction that makes his service unusually well documented. After the war he became involved in community politics and Indigenous advocacy.

Henry Norwest, a Métis man from Alberta, served as a sniper in the Canadian Corps and was decorated before his death in 1918. His story illustrates that Métis soldiers served but often cannot be identified in records that did not track them as Métis.

Decorations are evidence of recognized combat service, but they do not describe the man's inner life, and they should not turn Indigenous soldiers into romanticized warriors. Trench warfare meant artillery, exhaustion, injury, and loss for all soldiers. Pegahmagabow's postwar struggles with the Indian Department show that a decorated veteran could return to unequal treatment.

Francis Pegahmagabow's military record offers a useful lesson in distinguishing historical dates rather than treating them as errors to be hidden. Parks Canada describes his decision to enlist in August 1914, soon after the war began; his Library and Archives Canada personnel index gives a formal enlistment entry of 15 September 1914 at Valcartier, Quebec, with service number 6846 and the 1st Battalion. These dates are not necessarily mutually exclusive: a first appearance at a local recruiting point and subsequent attestation or administrative entry could differ. A responsible account reports what each source actually dates and avoids claiming to have reconstructed a complete paperwork sequence without the file itself. The record situates an Ojibwe soldier from the Georgian Bay region within the early Canadian Expeditionary Force.

Pegahmagabow served through major episodes of the Western Front, including fighting at the Second Battle of Ypres, Passchendaele, and the final campaigns of 1918. He became known for scouting and marksmanship and received the Military Medal with two bars, meaning two subsequent awards of the same decoration. These honors document military recognition, not every day of his experience or a verified tally of opponents killed. Some popular accounts reproduce exact sniper figures with little explanation of how they were recorded, and historians should separate officially documented decorations from later retrospective numerical claims. His service also included the ordinary risks of prolonged infantry war: bombardment, exhaustion, illness, and the vulnerability of units expected to hold ground during industrial fighting.

The comparison with Henry Norwest introduces different legal and cultural histories within the same Canadian Army. Norwest, born in the Fort Saskatchewan region of Alberta and described by biographers as Cree-Métis, worked in livestock occupations before military service. His military career included an early 1915 enlistment under another name, discharge, and re-enlistment in September of that year; the second period placed him with the 50th Battalion. Norwest became celebrated as a sniper and received the Military Medal and a bar, but died near Amiens on 18 August 1918. His status as a Métis soldier underscores how ethnic classification was inconsistently recorded in recruitment totals. His biography also demonstrates that military careers could involve interrupted service, discipline, and reassignment rather than a straight march from heroic enlistment to battlefield fame.

The two men's experiences should be compared through evidence instead of being made examples of a single stereotyped 'Indigenous fighting style.' Practical familiarity with terrain, observation, and hunting could be relevant to soldiering, as for many non-Indigenous country-born recruits, but the highly technical Western Front required understanding maps, signaling, protective equipment, artillery schedules, and the actions of fellow soldiers. Sniping was not an ethnic vocation; it was a military assignment requiring patience and skill under lethal conditions. Public praise for Indigenous soldiers could coexist with imperial writers' assumption that they were naturally martial and thus belonged in the most dangerous roles. The stories of Pegahmagabow and Norwest are better understood as professional military lives shaped both by individual action and by an unequal society's expectations.

After the war, Pegahmagabow became involved in leadership and political advocacy at his home community. Military decorations did not exempt him from the supervision of the Indian Department or resolve conflicts concerning local autonomy. His later career is important not because wartime service made him suddenly political, but because it reveals how a decorated veteran continued to inhabit a political relationship that the military institution had not transformed. Norwest's death illustrates the other outcome: for family and community, the most immediate legacy of service was irreversible loss. When historians pair such biographies, they should consider differences in survival, legal classification, family circumstances, and access to public remembrance rather than measure each man's significance by awards or martial statistics alone.

Section 94.7

Native American Soldiers in the American Expeditionary Forces

Native American soldiers served in infantry, communications, logistics, and other roles in American operations in France. Joseph Oklahombi, a Choctaw soldier, was recognized for his actions during the Meuse-Argonne Offensive. In 1918, Choctaw soldiers also used their language for field telephone communications, an episode introduced here and developed fully in Chapter 102. The emphasis should remain on soldiers' military work, not on Indigenous identity as their only defining feature.

Native American soldiers in the American Expeditionary Forces entered a military landscape shaped by a late American arrival and rapid mobilization. Following the declaration of war in April 1917, the United States raised and trained a mass army that would fight alongside but not simply merge into the British and French forces. Transportation across the Atlantic, training behind the lines, supply organization, and integration into larger offensives all demanded time. Indigenous soldiers served as infantrymen, cooks, engineers, drivers, telephonists, and in other roles required by this expanding institution. Most did not serve within exclusively Indigenous military units, and their experiences depended more immediately on particular regiments and commanders than on an aggregate racial category used by later commemorative accounts.

The Meuse-Argonne fighting of 1918 provides a way to examine Native American participation without reducing it to a single celebrated episode. The offensive was a large Allied operation against entrenched German defenses in northeastern France, with difficult terrain, heavy artillery, machine guns, gas, and severe logistical demands. Many American formations had only recently entered combat, and coordination under fire was imperfect. Within this setting, Choctaw soldier Joseph Oklahombi of Oklahoma served with the 36th Division. Official and state historical accounts connect his remarkable actions during October operations near Saint-Étienne with the capture of prisoners and later decorations. A lesson should specify the documented date, unit, and honor where known and should not reproduce embellished retellings as an eyewitness transcript. The larger story also includes the comrades whose collective actions made individual recognition possible.

Choctaw-language military communications developed within the same operational setting. American officers sought to protect messages carried by field telephones from German interception. Soldiers who spoke Choctaw helped transmit information in a language unfamiliar to enemy listeners, sometimes using improvised equivalents for military terms. This was not simply a matter of speaking a secret tongue: military communication required agreement about meanings, prompt relay, and coordination between telephonists and commanders. National Archives material identifies Choctaw speakers serving in the 142nd Infantry Regiment, while accounts of Oklahombi place him in the 141st. Students should distinguish the documented communications program from claims that every decorated Choctaw soldier personally operated every part of it. Chapter 102 will treat the broader history and technical development of Indigenous code talking.

The wartime use of Indigenous languages reveals a profound contradiction. Federal Indian policy had long discouraged or punished Indigenous-language use in schooling, presenting English as indispensable to progress. Yet army officials recognized that languages sustained by families and communities possessed practical strategic value. This observation should not be romanticized into a claim that military use undid the harms of suppression. It demonstrates instead that a language maintained outside state approval could become important when the state faced an urgent need. The history also challenges the assumption that Indigenous contribution consisted only of physical prowess: soldiers supplied linguistic skill, judgment, technical improvisation, and familiarity with collective communication networks.

Published accounts praise Indigenous bravery and claim that approximately twelve thousand Native Americans served during the war. Those summaries illuminate public recognition but cannot tell us the fate of every soldier. Some men returned with wounds, some died, and others came back to families that had suffered economic losses during their absence. Many remained barred from voting in their states or still lacked federal citizenship before 1924. The best historical reconstruction connects military records to a family's life after return, rather than allowing the photograph of a decorated uniform to substitute for the rest of a biography. Oklahombi's service belongs in the mainstream of American military history, but it also exposes the unequal political order within which Native American soldiers served.

Section 94.8

John Shiwak, Inuit Service, and Indigenous Soldiers from Newfoundland and Labrador

John Shiwak, an Inuk hunter and trapper from Labrador, enlisted in the Newfoundland Regiment in July 1915 and served as a scout and sniper. He was killed at the Battle of Cambrai in November 1917. Newfoundland was a separate dominion, not part of Canada, so his service belongs to a different political and military context. Records of Indigenous service from Newfoundland and Labrador are incomplete, and the total number of participants remains uncertain.

Comparing Shiwak, Pegahmagabow, and Oklahombi shows that Indigenous soldiers served under three different establishments, each with distinct rules, records, and postwar systems.

John Shiwak's story requires particular geographic and constitutional care. He was an Inuk hunter and trapper from Labrador, a northern coast linked commercially and politically with the Dominion of Newfoundland during the First World War. Newfoundland maintained a separate government and raised its own military forces. Labrador communities were not simply remote subdivisions of the Canadian provinces, and a soldier who enlisted in the Newfoundland Regiment should not be silently counted as having joined the Canadian Expeditionary Force. The distinction matters for recruitment, records, military routes, and subsequent remembrance. It also reminds students that a map of 'Indigenous Canada' drawn with present-day boundaries can obscure an important historical jurisdiction.

The Newfoundland Veterans Affairs biography records that Shiwak enlisted in July 1915, declared his occupation as trapper, and named his mother Sarah as next of kin. It identifies his service number, 1735. Such details give a glimpse of the bureaucratic passage from community life into an army without explaining every private motive. He traveled from the Labrador coast to St. John's and then by rail and sea toward larger overseas training operations. The distances and transport changes are historically significant: a man accustomed to hunting routes and seasonal coastal travel entered a system in which orders, timetables, and ships determined his movement across an ocean. The journey was not a picturesque prelude but an essential part of mobilization.

The Newfoundland Regiment had already experienced devastating losses during the war, including at Beaumont-Hamel in 1916, and its continuing operations required replacements. Shiwak was associated with scouting and marksmanship, skills that drew contemporary attention. But a battlefield assignment cannot be inferred simply from his identity as a hunter. Patrols required cooperation, discipline, timing, attention to wire and shell craters, and willingness to act under enemy observation. The Western Front transformed the meaning of terrain: a landscape of familiar trees, fields, and roads could become a maze of shattered positions, shell holes, and artificial boundaries. Shiwak died in November 1917 during operations associated with the Battle of Cambrai, leaving a named life within the much larger loss sustained by the regiment.

His biography also exposes archival limits. Service documents may survive in military collections while Inuit-language descriptions of family life or local remembrance remain in different forms of community memory. A record showing a next of kin does not disclose the family's thoughts on the war; an obituary may reflect a newspaper's desire to celebrate a brave soldier rather than the perspectives of everyone in Labrador. Researchers should distinguish the relatively well-established outline of Shiwak's service from claims about his interior motives. They should also avoid using his story as proof that Inuit people participated in the war at the same rate as better-documented First Nations communities. The population categories and the surviving sources do not support that inference.

Comparison with Pegahmagabow, Norwest, and Oklahombi reveals both shared experience and political diversity. All entered industrial military organizations and faced severe danger, but they came from different nations and legal orders, joined different forces, and returned to different possibilities—or did not return at all. The ocean crossings of the Newfoundland Regiment and the Canadian Expeditionary Force could overlap physically without erasing their separate governments. Shiwak's history belongs to the continental scope of this series precisely because it cannot be accommodated by a simplified binary of Canada and the United States. His service and death must be understood as a Labrador Inuit life intersecting with Newfoundland, the British Empire, and war in Europe.

Section 94.9

Indigenous Women's Patriotic Organizations and Wartime Community Mobilization

The Six Nations Women's Patriotic League, founded in October 1914, raised funds, produced clothing and knitted goods, and sent supplies to military relief efforts. Women used existing community networks, church and social institutions, and leadership traditions to organize. This work created opportunities for public leadership even though women faced both gender and colonial restrictions. The same community's women later opposed conscription, showing that support for soldiers and opposition to compulsory service could coexist.

The Six Nations Women's Patriotic League emerged in October 1914, early in the war, and offers a concrete example of Indigenous women's collective leadership. Members mobilized existing relationships, organizational skills, and local resources to support soldiers. Their activities included knitting socks, preparing quilts and other material supplies, gathering food, raising funds, and coordinating with wider relief organizations. The public language of wartime charity could make these activities appear secondary to combat, but armies depended on vast systems of material support, and families depended on organizations that kept contact with serving relatives. The League thus belonged to the war's social and institutional infrastructure, not merely its sentimental background.

Women at Six Nations had longstanding traditions of political influence, public consultation, family responsibility, and collective organization. They did not suddenly become civic actors because the Crown needed their sewing. Wartime conditions created additional public forums and new expectations, however, and women adapted familiar skills of coordination to the distribution of supplies across distance. Their work required collecting money or materials, deciding priorities, identifying recipients, negotiating shipping, and communicating with military and civilian officials. An embroidered battalion flag and other objects associated with the organization can be studied as material expressions of community attachment, although they cannot alone reveal the full extent of women's activities or the different opinions that existed among them.

Patriotic work did not guarantee equal recognition. Published studies recount instances in which donations prepared by Six Nations women encountered racial prejudice, including anxieties expressed about the supposed health risk of donated socks. The example captures the simultaneous expectation of Indigenous contribution and the persistence of discriminatory assumptions. Women could be asked to sacrifice labor, money, and time for a national campaign while being treated as suspect participants in the very networks they were supporting. Such incidents should be contextualized rather than allowed to stand for every encounter, but they reveal why the distinction between wartime praise and equality remained acute even away from the battlefield.

The League's members were not necessarily identical in political orientation to women who petitioned the Crown over conscription. Some supported individual volunteers while rejecting the state claim to compel younger men into foreign service. This was not an illogical shift from patriotism to disloyalty; the right to assist a relative and the right to determine obligations under treaties were different questions. Women were therefore visible as both suppliers and political authors. The 1917 petition discussed later invokes wampum agreements and the Crown relationship, demonstrating that historical diplomacy continued to inform public argument. Not every woman at Six Nations signed every petition, and a chapter should not speak of a single unified women's opinion without evidence.

Women's home-front labor also extended far beyond one famous organization. Across Canada and the United States, women sustained farming, fishing, households, trade, and care while men were absent. Some joined the Red Cross or local funds; others sewed, organized benefit events, translated letters, or provided assistance to bereaved families. Much of this work went unrecorded because public memorials favored military rosters and battlefield decorations. Its historical importance can nevertheless be reconstructed through community newsletters, agency correspondence, organizational records, photographs, and family histories. Taking Indigenous women seriously as wartime actors changes the scale of explanation: the war was not simply an overseas military undertaking but also a prolonged reorganization of labor and political responsibility in Indigenous communities.

Section 94.10

Indigenous Agriculture, Industry, and Resource Production during the War

Wartime demand raised prices for grain, livestock, fish, and timber and created wage opportunities. Governments also encouraged or directed production on reserves and reservations, and in Canada reserve lands were sometimes used or leased for wartime agriculture. Some Indigenous producers benefited from higher prices; others experienced pressure and coercion. Labour shortages, transport limits, and administrative control affected outcomes. Chapter 91 treats Indigenous economies broadly; this section concerns their wartime pressures.

Wartime production reached Indigenous communities through the prices of grain, livestock, timber, fish, and other commodities, but also through government regulation and the demand for workers. Farmers who could sell into expanding markets sometimes benefited from high prices, while communities already lacking equipment, secure water, or access to transport could not take full advantage of the opportunity. In some areas, Indigenous households continued mixing subsistence production with commercial sales and wage labor. Chapter 91 explained the long history of these mixed economies; the present section examines how the extraordinary needs of 1914–1918 changed their terms. A sudden demand for food or materials did not automatically make production profitable for the people supplying it.

The military's need for horses, timber, transport facilities, and food tied reservation and reserve economies to events far away. Railroad connections and purchasing contracts could determine which producers gained access to markets. Men leaving for military training also removed labor from farms and other household enterprises during critical seasons. Women, elders, younger relatives, and hired workers adapted by reallocating tasks; some farms reduced production, while others took advantage of high prices. A letter from an agent reporting increased acreage does not necessarily reveal how much labor the household contributed or whether earnings remained under community control. Wartime economic records must therefore be read alongside family and administrative documents rather than treated as a simple success metric.

Government policy shaped opportunities. In Canada, Department of Indian Affairs officials sometimes promoted greater production from reserves while continuing to regulate the sale or leasing of lands and resources. Crown and private claims could be described as national necessity even when Indigenous communities faced long-term losses. The same state that praised men in uniform maintained controls over the land and economic resources of their relatives. In the United States, war demand intersected with allotment, leasing, and federal oversight of trust resources. Local arrangements varied, and blanket assertions that every wartime lease was coerced or that every producer profited are unsupported. The analytical task is to identify who made the decision, who controlled the income, and whether the transaction preserved the community's future choices.

Industrial work also created new routes into wage employment. Indigenous people worked in fishing, lumbering, transport, manufacturing, and seasonal agriculture as labor needs shifted. Labor conditions were shaped by region, race, gender, and skill. A higher wage in a distant workplace could be offset by travel costs, discrimination, or the difficulty of maintaining ties to home. Some workers moved back and forth seasonally, making war work one element in a broader economic strategy rather than a permanent departure from community life. Such mobility also complicated administrative statistics: a resident absent at census time might be counted as unemployed or missing by an official who did not understand the household's seasonal organization.

The economic consequences continued after 1918. Commodity prices shifted, veterans returned seeking livelihoods, and land became central to governmental schemes for resettlement. The Soldier Settlement measures discussed in Section 94.18 brought sharply unequal access to loans and acreage, in some cases facilitating transfer of reserve land to non-Indigenous settlers. Wartime economic history therefore cannot end at the armistice. It must follow the movement of labor, capital, and land across the war's boundaries while keeping separate the different powers held by Indigenous producers, commercial purchasers, military authorities, and Indian departments. Those comparisons reveal the difference between participation in an expanding national economy and equitable control over its rewards.

Section 94.11

Edith Anderson Monture and Indigenous Women in Military Nursing

Edith Anderson Monture, from Six Nations, trained as a nurse in New York and served with the U.S. military medical establishment at Base Hospital 23 in Vittel, France, where she treated wounded soldiers. She trained outside Canada partly because opportunities for Indigenous women in Canadian nursing were limited, and her career crossed national and gender boundaries. Her later recollections are valuable but retrospective and must be distinguished from surviving service documentation.

Charlotte Edith Anderson, later known as Edith Anderson Monture, was born at Six Nations of the Grand River in 1890. Her career illustrates how the war intersected with barriers faced by Indigenous women seeking professional training. Interested in nursing, she encountered limited opportunities in Canada and completed her training at the New Rochelle School of Nursing in New York State, qualifying in 1914. She worked as a nurse in the United States before entering wartime service in 1917. This sequence matters: she did not enter the medical profession merely because the war opened a temporary occupation to women. She had already undertaken professional education and work, crossing an international border in pursuit of training constrained by Canadian racial exclusions.

After the United States entered the war, Anderson joined the American Red Cross Nursing Service and traveled to France with a nursing unit associated with the American military medical system. She served principally at Base Hospital No. 23 in Vittel, where a former resort hotel was adapted for the care of sick and wounded soldiers. The conversion of civilian buildings into hospitals was part of a larger wartime medical network that depended on nurses, orderlies, surgeons, transport workers, sanitation systems, and the movement of patients from the front. Medical staff treated injuries from artillery, bullets, gas, disease, and exhausting conditions in the trenches. Nurses had professional responsibilities that were highly technical and emotionally demanding, and their labor should not be described simply as comforting soldiers.

Anderson's surviving testimony and diary provide ways to reconstruct the human dimensions of nursing. Parks Canada reports that she recorded details of hospital work, recreation, training, and grief, including the death of a young patient. These personal records differ from an official posting entry: they reveal what she noticed and how she understood events, while still reflecting choices about what to record and preserve. A later interview brings a second temporal layer. Historians should identify which statements were made during the war and which came from retrospective recollection, then avoid weaving the two into an imaginary minute-by-minute hospital shift. The chapter's Everyday Life feature can ask students to compare records instead of supplying invented dialogue or private thoughts.

Monture's service also complicates categories of nation and citizenship. A Mohawk woman from Six Nations, trained in the United States and employed in an American medical formation in Europe, crossed boundaries that national commemorations often treat as rigid. Canada's Military Voters Act of 1917 created a wartime franchise for eligible military personnel, including qualifying women serving with allied forces. Her military-related opportunity to vote therefore belonged to an exceptional wartime legal arrangement, not a general recognition of suffrage for all status First Nations women. Many others remained excluded under the normal Canadian federal rules. The incident underscores how an individual's professional position could create a temporary political right without transforming the legal status of her community.

The medical story requires attention to its limits as well as its possibilities. Monture's experience was remarkable and well documented, but it should not be treated as representative of every Indigenous woman during the war. Most never crossed the Atlantic; their labor took place in homes, farms, schools, hospitals, fundraising societies, or seasonal workplaces closer to home. By using her story as a case rather than a universal example, the chapter can show the common questions shared across different lives: who could train for professional work, who recognized that qualification, what kinds of service were counted as patriotic, and what happened when an Indigenous woman returned to a society still organized by racial and gender restrictions.

Section 94.12

Families, Correspondence, Casualties, and the Human Cost of War

Separation affected parents, spouses, children, and elders. Families depended on correspondence, military pay and separation allowances where available, and casualty notices that could arrive weeks after events. Maintaining farms, fishing, trapping, and households without men placed burdens on women and elders. Deaths and disabilities left lasting losses. The 1918 influenza pandemic then struck communities already facing health and economic inequality, compounding wartime grief; its impact varied widely by community. Where documented letters or family accounts survive they should be used, and no correspondence should be reconstructed by imagination.

War disrupted Indigenous families long before formal casualty notices arrived. A recruit's departure altered the work that remained to be done: animals needed feeding, crops tending, fish processed, fuel gathered, children cared for, and elders assisted. In communities already constrained by land policies and limited access to capital, a household often had little room for the sudden absence of an able-bodied member. Military pay could help, but allowances depended on legal or administrative eligibility and might not match the needs of an extended family. Women and older relatives frequently assumed additional responsibilities. This labor was essential to the war effort even when it did not appear in the administrative categories of enlistment or military service.

Letters connected soldiers and households but did so imperfectly. Military censorship, distance, mail delays, and the soldier's own reluctance to distress relatives shaped what could be communicated. A letter might describe weather, food, or friends while concealing fear or illness. Indigenous-language correspondence could carry meanings inaccessible to censors or later archivists, while letters written in English might reflect school experience and the expectation of official reading. Many families received news through intermediaries because addresses changed or postal delivery was unreliable. Correspondence is valuable precisely because of these constraints: it shows an ongoing relationship strained by wartime institutions rather than transparent access to everything a soldier felt.

Casualty records are another partial archive. A military list can establish the date and officially recorded cause of death, while a community remembrance may locate that loss in kinship, family history, and collective obligation. The death of Henry Norwest or John Shiwak affected more than the institution that listed him as killed in action. It deprived families of a relative with a particular economic and social role, and it changed what could be passed to future generations. Injured survivors might need long-term assistance, experience uncertain employment prospects, or depend on relatives for care. Where an official term such as 'shell shock' appears, it must be interpreted in its historical medical context rather than simply equated with every modern psychological diagnosis.

The influenza pandemic of 1918–1919 compounded these hardships. Its timing overlapped demobilization and the movement of troops, but the severity of outbreaks varied greatly by place and cannot be inferred solely from the war's global chronology. Housing conditions, access to medical treatment, local transportation routes, and existing inequalities affected vulnerability. Indigenous communities in different regions experienced catastrophic losses or other patterns of illness and disruption. A scholarly treatment needs community-specific evidence before assigning numbers or naming particular causes, especially where surviving records were produced by outsiders. The point is not that every pandemic death was a direct war casualty, but that households were asked to absorb multiple crises in a short period.

Recovering family experience requires combining different source types. Pension applications, agency letters, a soldier's record, local newspapers, community remembrance, oral histories, and household economic accounts may each illuminate one aspect of a person's return or loss. They may also contradict one another on dates, names, or family relationships. Historians should resist converting uncertainty into a polished fictional scene. The absence of surviving letters from a mother does not mean she was silent or unaffected; it means the archive preserves one part of a larger social life. A history attentive to that absence can still analyze work, separation, grief, and the ongoing responsibilities that families sustained during and after the war.

Section 94.13

Canadian Conscription and First Nations Opposition, 1917–1918

Canada's Military Service Act of 1917 introduced conscription and prompted objections from First Nations. Communities and Indian agents sent petitions and correspondence arguing from treaty obligations, political status, and the absence of federal voting rights, and distinguishing voluntary enlistment from compulsory service. On January 17, 1918, Order-in-Council P.C. 111 exempted status Indians from compulsory combatant service. This was not an unconditional exemption from every military-related obligation; noncombatant duties could remain.

Conscription became a major Canadian political crisis as overseas losses increased and voluntary recruitment proved insufficient to sustain an expanding army. The Military Service Act of 1917 authorized compulsory service under conditions set by the federal government. For First Nations living under the Indian Act, however, the state had never settled the moral and political question of whether a person denied full electoral rights could be compelled to fight in Europe. The draft legislation initially did not adequately account for this distinctive legal situation. Its implementation drew protests from communities and correspondence from Indian agents concerned about the policy's implications. The controversy revealed that the government could not treat Indigenous people merely as an undifferentiated pool of military manpower while simultaneously maintaining a special system of administrative control.

Different communities challenged the act through different arguments. Some invoked historical treaty assurances or the relationship with the Crown; others pointed to the absence of ordinary political rights or the agricultural needs of their households. Officials such as Duncan Campbell Scott initially resisted a general exemption, but continued pressure forced reconsideration. The administrative record thus shows not a benevolent exemption spontaneously offered by the state, but a policy revision emerging from public and community objection. The proper comparison is not between a patriotic government and unpatriotic critics. Many people who opposed compulsory service had already supported volunteers, donated goods, or lost relatives at the front. They objected to compulsion and the political assumptions it carried.

On 17 January 1918 the federal cabinet adopted Order-in-Council P.C. 111. Government historical accounts describe it as exempting status Indians from compulsory combatant overseas service while leaving possible noncombatant obligations within Canada and facilitating certain deferments for work in industry or agriculture. The distinction is significant. An exemption limited the coercive reach of conscription but did not remove all governmental claims on Indigenous labor, and it did not settle disputes over citizenship, treaties, or self-government. The language and administration of the order must be handled carefully because summaries sometimes call it a blanket exemption when the documented qualifications were more complex. Students should compare official commentary with the order's legal purpose instead of relying on slogans alone.

The Six Nations women's petition of 1917 provides an especially revealing window into these debates. A contemporary document reproduced or described in scholarship invokes the Two Row and Covenant Chain wampum relationships and the claim that the Crown lacked authority to compel Six Nations men into foreign war. Research also identifies a specific request concerning younger sons who had entered service, demonstrating that the petition was rooted in immediate family circumstances as well as political doctrine. Its creators were not simply repeating abstract sovereignty language: they used historically meaningful objects and collective memory to make a concrete appeal. The chapter should report that specificity and explain that an edited reproduction must be distinguished from the full archival original in Library and Archives Canada's RG 10 collections.

Conscription also shows the limits of comparison. The unresolved exemptions of Inuit and Métis people were not debated in the same way or reflected in the same legal categories as those of status First Nations. Records may be silent because officials did not classify individuals consistently, because communities were distant from draft operations, or because no equivalent formal petition survived. None of these possibilities justifies an assertion that all Indigenous peoples in Canada received identical treatment. What can be said confidently is that First Nations objections compelled a significant amendment to wartime administration and that the outcome exposed a profound contradiction between collective treaty claims, individual military obligations, and a federal government still restricting Indigenous political participation.

Section 94.14

United States Selective Service, Military Obligations, and Indigenous Legal Status

Under American wartime legislation, citizens and some noncitizens were required to register, and Native American men were subject to registration and the draft in varying ways depending on citizenship and residence. Federal rules and the practices of local draft boards and recruiters did not always match. The contradiction was that compulsory obligations applied to people who in many cases lacked equal political rights. This builds on Chapter 93's legal framework.

The United States used a different legal and administrative mechanism. The Selective Service Act of 18 May 1917 created registration and draft machinery overseen by the Provost Marshal General but administered through state, territorial, district, and local boards. The first registration applied to men aged twenty-one through thirty; subsequent registrations extended the range. Draft cards collected information about nationality, citizenship, employment, dependents, physical characteristics, and previous military service. The questions could be difficult for men whose political membership existed simultaneously in an Indigenous nation and in an American legal category. An official might ask whether a registrant was a citizen, an alien, or a person with special status when the law itself had produced inconsistent answers for generations.

Registration and liability to induction must be distinguished. National Archives material explains that millions of men registered, but many registrants never served. Conversely, some Native American men volunteered before a given registration or entered military service through separate recruiting procedures. The original draft statute defined a narrower category of men liable to be drafted than the broad population compelled to register, and the law was amended during the war. Local boards had to decide exemptions or deferments, interpret instructions, and maintain records. Native American citizenship status could affect those decisions, but federal practice was not simply a single automatic rule applied without local discretion. Where a particular tribal community protested or negotiated, a historian should cite that case rather than infer a universal reaction.

The draft system highlighted an institutional paradox. Federal policy had treated many Indigenous people as wards under special supervision, and state election laws denied practical voting rights to many who were citizens. Yet the mobilizing government appealed to a national language of sacrifice and democracy. Some men accepted this framing and sought to serve, while others challenged a compulsory obligation imposed without corresponding representation. The situation cannot be reduced to the claim that every Native soldier was drafted against his will: voluntary service was substantial, and individual motives varied. Nor does it follow that the existence of the draft proved all Indigenous people possessed equal citizenship. The legal position of the registrant, the administrative practice of the board, and the personal choice of the serviceman must each be investigated separately.

Military mobilization also affected communities through industries and households. Draft exemptions might depend on agricultural production, dependent relatives, or vital war work, matters shaped by land access and the availability of local employers. A man with dependents could seek relief while relatives continued to perform the labor necessary to maintain the household. Draft registration therefore provides an unexpected source for Indigenous economic history: it can disclose occupations, residences, and family connections otherwise missing from government reports. The documents still require careful interpretation, as a recorded occupation may represent seasonal work or the registrant's response to a bureaucratic question rather than a complete account of livelihood.

Comparison with Canada's P.C. 111 underscores the danger of applying one country's terms to another. Canada developed a specific political exemption in response to First Nations protest; the U.S. system wrestled with citizenship, registration, and local draft practices within a different constitutional structure. Indigenous communities in border regions could encounter both states and their distinct rules. The war did not homogenize North America into a common military jurisdiction. Instead it expanded the occasions on which Indigenous individuals had to confront state classifications of personhood, loyalty, labor, and legal obligation—a central theme already developed in Chapter 93 and now made concrete through the mechanics of wartime administration.

Section 94.15

Indigenous Nations, Treaty Obligations, and Competing Definitions of Loyalty

Loyalty could be defined in different ways. For some Haudenosaunee, treaty relationships with the Crown had been alliances between nations, not grants of authority to conscript. For volunteers, service was consistent with national values or obligations. Support for soldiers and opposition to compulsion were compatible: a community could aid its sons and daughters overseas and still reject the state's claim to compel them.

Loyalty during the First World War could be understood through more than one political language. Canadian ministers often described the conflict as a defense of the Empire, while First Nations leaders sometimes understood their relation to the Crown through treaties of alliance rather than through ordinary subjection to the Canadian government. For some individuals, military service expressed a continuing promise to stand beside allies; for others, the same treaty history placed a limit on the Crown's power to demand compulsory overseas combat. The point was not simply whether people felt affection for Britain or Canada. It was whether the state could convert an alliance into an unrestricted command over another nation's members.

The 1917 appeal associated with Six Nations women demonstrates how the past could become immediate political evidence. Wampum belts, including those associated with Two Row and Covenant Chain diplomacy, carried understandings of separate political relationships. Petitioners invoked these relationships when addressing King George V, arguing against compelled participation in foreign wars. Their argument also arose from concern for specific young relatives whose military service raised questions about age, authorization, and family authority. The document should therefore be read both as a diplomatic claim and as an intervention in actual family lives. Its meaning cannot be reduced to a modern slogan or to the assumption that every Haudenosaunee person rejected service.

Soldiers who volunteered could likewise understand their choice in terms of political belonging rather than assimilation. Some believed that defense of an ally or service to relatives required participation; others hoped that performance in uniform would change how outsiders regarded their nations. A volunteer might accept army discipline without conceding the full legitimacy of Indian Department control over his homeland. An Indigenous woman could send food or supplies to men serving overseas while criticizing the government's attempt to conscript others. The apparent contradiction dissolves when one separates the question of an individual's voluntary commitment from a collective dispute over the authority to impose it.

Different Indigenous leaders also disagreed about how to express opposition. A council might send a resolution to Ottawa, a group of women might appeal directly to the monarch, and an agent might relay local complaints using language more likely to persuade a department. These interventions traveled through different channels and could be translated or summarized before reaching decision-makers. The historian should therefore ask who authored a statement, whether it was presented as a collective position, what diplomatic traditions it invoked, and how accurately officials conveyed it. Administrative correspondence is valuable, but the voice of an Indian agent should not be mistaken for the voice of the community whose interests the agent claimed to explain.

The broader historical lesson is that wartime service intensified debates about who possessed political authority. The same state could praise Indigenous men for defending democracy and deny or limit democratic participation at home. The same military system could accept volunteers while attempting to compel men whose nations had not accepted the government's interpretation of their treaty relationship. The outcome of P.C. 111 showed that Indigenous protest could influence policy, but it did not resolve the underlying conflict. After 1918, veterans and communities continued to use the experience of service to press claims about representation, land, and government accountability. The war brought these contradictions into sharper relief rather than creating them from nothing.

Section 94.16

Indigenous North America and the Uneven Geography of the War

Conditions differed by region. Alaska Native peoples, Southwestern nations, northern Indigenous communities in Canada, and borderland communities in the U.S.–Mexico region faced different citizenship rules, recruitment practices, and economic effects, and surviving evidence varies. Mexico was not a combatant, and the Mexican Revolution created a distinct situation for Indigenous communities. Inuit and First Nations under Canadian administration differed from Labrador Inuit under Newfoundland. Comparable military participation should not be assumed where evidence is limited.

The phrase 'Indigenous North America in the First World War' refers to many societies and legal jurisdictions, not to one recruitment system. Indigenous peoples lived in U.S. states and territories, Canadian provinces and reserve jurisdictions, Newfoundland and Labrador, Alaska, and the border regions of northern Mexico. The war reached these places through different combinations of enlistment, trade, transport, political restrictions, public health, and news. A comparative approach should therefore ask what changed for each region rather than assume that the same number of soldiers, military rules, or home-front opportunities applied everywhere. Where sources are lacking, the absence of a documented enlistment total must remain an evidentiary limit rather than a claim about community willingness.

Alaska offers one particularly revealing case. As a U.S. territory it was subject to national mobilization law but also had local political and administrative conditions distinct from those in the forty-eight states. Alaska Native peoples occupied varied relations to schools, missions, commercial fisheries, and federal administrators. Men and women encountered wartime demand through coastal shipping, fishing work, and other local industries as well as possible military participation. The journey to a recruiting center or training facility could be lengthy and expensive. A territorial record that does not identify a recruit's Indigenous community may obscure patterns that community historians can recover from family, church, and organizational sources. The chapter should not impose the better-documented Oklahoma or Great Lakes experience on Alaska merely for continental coverage.

Across the American Southwest, the war overlapped with border policing, agriculture, railroad employment, and the final years of revolutionary violence in Mexico. O'odham, Yaqui, and other peoples maintained relationships across an international boundary that mattered for military registration, commerce, and family movement. Mexico remained formally outside direct combat in the European war, while the Mexican Revolution generated its own conflict and displacement. Indigenous experiences in Sonora and Arizona were therefore not interchangeable with those of soldiers joining the American Expeditionary Forces in France. Family members might have encountered U.S. draft officers, employers seeking labor, or border authorities in different circumstances during the same year. This juxtaposition demonstrates how world war and regional revolution could intersect without becoming the same event.

The Canadian North and Labrador likewise require separate treatment. The Canadian federal government administered different northern populations through varied institutions, while Newfoundland governed Labrador within its own dominion's territorial framework. John Shiwak's documented service belongs to the Newfoundland Regiment, not a generic Canadian Inuit military contingent. Other Inuit experiences may appear primarily in trading records, mission correspondence, transport histories, or local memories rather than a standardized army roster. It would be misleading to present a lack of surviving wartime photographs as evidence of a lack of experience. The region's significance also lies in changing commodity networks and delayed communication, both of which affected how communities perceived a war being fought far beyond their homelands.

A comparative geography study should locate at least three different routes: a First Nations volunteer moving from southern Ontario to Canadian training and European service; a Choctaw or other Native American recruit traveling from Oklahoma to an American training camp and France; and a Labrador Inuk leaving the coast for St. John's, Britain, and the Western Front. It should then add the home-front geography of food, lumber, fisheries, and wartime medical care. These lines show that military mobility connected previously separated places while state borders still structured opportunity and obligation. The geographic story is therefore one of unequal routes into a shared global conflict, not a map on which Indigenous participation is spread evenly across North America.

Division V — Veterans, Postwar Inequality, and Indigenous Political Change

Section 94.17

The Armistice of 1918 and the Return of Indigenous Veterans

The armistice of November 11, 1918 ended fighting, but demobilization took months. Veterans returned with injuries, disabilities, grief, and the psychological effects of combat, often described at the time in terms such as shell shock. Public celebration of service coexisted with discrimination at home and difficulty finding work.

The armistice of 11 November 1918 ended fighting on the Western Front, but it did not send every soldier immediately home. Armies had to collect equipment, arrange transport, account for units, treat the wounded, and process discharges. Some men remained abroad for months or participated in assignments that continued beyond the armistice. The return journey could be exhausting and uncertain. Families awaiting news could not simply assume that the last day of combat was also the end of danger. Epidemic disease, transport accidents, and long-term medical problems complicated the passage from military to civilian status. The term 'demobilization' therefore describes a process rather than a single date.

Veterans returned to homes changed by absence. Household work may have been reorganized; a child might have grown old enough to assume responsibilities; a spouse or parent might have died; property or employment arrangements might no longer be available. Some communities greeted returning men with ceremonies and public recognition, while others focused on immediate needs. A medal or uniform might inspire pride, but neither could guarantee a livelihood. Re-entry into civilian life involved finding work, understanding benefits, negotiating medical care, and resuming responsibilities within one's nation. The ordinary tasks of return were made more difficult where government agents controlled funds, land use, or permission to pursue opportunities.

Wartime injuries carried enduring consequences. Soldiers wounded by artillery or rifle fire could require treatment long after discharge; gas injuries and infectious disease might affect health unpredictably. Contemporary discussions of 'shell shock' described a changing group of psychological and neurological problems that medical authorities did not always understand or treat consistently. Historians should not assign a modern psychiatric diagnosis to an individual without adequate evidence, but they can document the contemporary struggle to recognize injuries not readily visible. Canadian and American pension systems developed categories of disability and service connection that could determine whether a veteran received assistance. Official recognition of military service did not always translate into a satisfactory response to injuries.

Military comradeship sometimes offered an experience of conditional equality that conflicted with homecoming. Parks Canada records that some Indigenous veterans remembered serving beside non-Indigenous soldiers with more acceptance than they received in ordinary civilian institutions. Yet wartime military life itself was not free of racial stereotypes or discriminatory administration. The comparison should be neither romanticized nor dismissed: soldiers could develop mutual respect under fire and still encounter inequalities within a broader imperial hierarchy. On return, restrictions on voting, Indian Department authority, unequal access to settlement schemes, and racial barriers remained in place. This disconnect became a powerful subject of postwar political criticism.

Chronology provides an important safeguard against easy causation. The 1919 U.S. veterans' citizenship provision followed the armistice but did not automatically make every Native veteran a citizen; Canadian settlement policy had roots in 1917 and continued to change after 1919; larger citizenship and reform movements had their own prewar histories. To conclude that the armistice 'caused' equality would be false. More defensibly, war created new experiences, visibility, and arguments that people could use in disputes already underway. A chronological study should therefore place enlistment, conscription, protest, demobilization, benefits, and citizenship measures side by side, allowing students to distinguish immediate changes from longer institutional continuities.

Section 94.18

Veterans' Benefits, Soldier Settlement, and Unequal Treatment after the War

Canada's Soldier Settlement legislation provided land and loans to veterans, but First Nations veterans faced differences in eligibility, administration, and outcomes. Reserve lands were used for settlement by non-Indigenous veterans, so wartime service could be followed by policies that reduced Indigenous access to land. Canada's military history acknowledges substantial discrimination against Indigenous veterans in settlement assistance and related programs. American Indian veterans also faced inequalities in benefits and administration, though under different legal arrangements.

Postwar benefit policy revealed the gap between the public praise of military service and the practical control of land and resources. Canadian legislation developed a soldier-settlement framework beginning in 1917 and enlarged or revised it in 1919, aiming to assist returned personnel through land access, loans, and agricultural establishment. The program depended on assessments of eligibility, credit, training, and available property. Those conditions could work very differently for Indigenous veterans living under the Indian Act than for non-Indigenous veterans holding property in fee simple. Rather than describe the scheme as a single 1919 enactment, the chapter should track its legislative stages and the relationship between the Soldier Settlement Board and the Department of Indian Affairs.

For a status First Nations veteran, the ordinary mechanics of a land loan were complicated by rules concerning reserve land. Property held for the use and benefit of a band was not simply equivalent to a private freehold farm that could be mortgaged. Officials often proposed that veterans farm on reserves under arrangements such as location tickets, with Indian Affairs involved in approvals. The scale, security, and financial terms of assistance could be inferior to those available to other returned soldiers. One cannot infer identical treatment solely from a statute's general declaration that veterans deserved help. Researchers must compare the actual amount granted, terms of repayment, title structure, location of the farm, and whether the individual could exercise control without an agent's approval.

Land was also sought for non-Indigenous veterans, and government officials treated some reserve acreage as potential settlement property. The University of Saskatchewan's Gladue Rights Research Database describes reserve surrenders in Saskatchewan associated with soldier settlement, identifying multiple communities and more than seventy thousand acres involved in documented transactions. This figure is valuable as an example from a particular regional research project, not a comprehensive continental total. Circumstances differed by reserve: some transactions were presented as consensual, others occurred amid administrative pressure or controversy, and historians must investigate community records before assigning motive. The consequence, however, was clear where land passed out of reserve control: it reduced space available for Indigenous agriculture, settlement, hunting, and future development.

Veteran organizations and community witnesses later described discrimination in the administration of benefits and relief. Programs not formally prohibited to Indigenous veterans might be delivered through separate channels, produce smaller loans, or require additional permissions. Some assistance funds insisted that the Indian Department rather than the general fund should assume responsibility for Indigenous beneficiaries. Such decisions did more than deny money; they reinforced the notion that a soldier who had served alongside other Canadians was not entitled to deal directly with civilian institutions on the same terms. The contrast helps explain why military decorations and public ceremonies could coexist with frustration and continuing dependence on administrators.

In the United States, Indigenous veterans faced a different combination of federal military administration, Indian Affairs oversight, local discrimination, and land tenure issues. It would be inaccurate to import the Canadian Soldier Settlement program wholesale into the American context. U.S. veterans might encounter restrictions stemming from trust land, limited access to credit, or unequal civilian employment, but the specific legal programs and eligibility rules require separate documentary evidence. The overarching comparison is institutional: both states praised wartime sacrifice while retaining systems in which Indigenous people's access to land and public resources was shaped by special legal categories. Postwar policy therefore provides a measurable test of whether recognition in uniform had become equality in daily life.

Section 94.19

Military Service, Citizenship, and Changing Indigenous Political Expectations

An act of November 6, 1919 made U.S. citizenship available to qualifying Indigenous veterans through an application process. It was not automatic citizenship for every veteran; the 1924 Indian Citizenship Act, treated in Chapter 93, extended citizenship more broadly. Veterans' own accounts, including questionnaires recorded around 1919–1920, show some connecting service to demands for rights and recognition. Such testimony is retrospective and shaped by its purposes, and any named questionnaire should be checked in the original before quotation.

Citizenship became one of the most visible ways in which military service entered postwar politics. Before 1924, Indigenous people in the United States occupied several legal positions: some were citizens through allotment, marriage, treaties, or statutes; others remained noncitizens. Congress enacted a measure on 6 November 1919 providing a pathway to citizenship for qualifying American Indian veterans of the war against Germany. The statutory text tied eligibility to service in the American military or naval establishments, an honorable discharge, the individual's desire to obtain citizenship, proof and identification, and action before a court with jurisdiction. It protected tribal or other property interests. The critical point is that **citizenship was not automatically awarded to every Indigenous veteran** by the mere fact of service.

The 1919 measure was part of a longer political debate rather than its conclusion. Critics of paternalism had argued for recognition of individual rights before the war, and many Indigenous leaders disagreed over whether outside citizenship threatened treaty-based nationhood. The Indian Citizenship Act of June 1924 later extended federal citizenship more broadly to noncitizen Indigenous people born within the territorial limits of the United States, with a property-rights safeguard. Chapter 93 analyzes that law and continuing state voting restrictions in depth. Here the emphasis should remain on how war experience supplied one argument for inclusion, while neither an individual veteran's status nor the nation's sovereignty questions were resolved simply because Congress passed a citizenship law.

An unusually vivid record survives in veterans' questionnaires gathered in 1920 by Joseph K. Dixon. The Gilder Lehrman Institute publishes a teaching edition containing the words of named Native American veterans, including Joe High Elk of the Cheyenne River Sioux. High Elk described service in major battles and expressed hope that Indigenous people would enjoy freedoms he believed they were denied. His account is unusually valuable because it connects military work directly to a claim about political rights in the veteran's own recorded words. The document must nevertheless be treated as a response to a postwar questionnaire, not as an unmediated diary written during the battle. Dixon's collecting purposes and the survival of selected testimonies shaped what became visible.

Not every veteran shared the same interpretation. Some emphasized love of country, others remembered hardships, and still others were concerned with debts, employment, wounded bodies, or the loss of property during absence. The Gilder Lehrman collection also preserves testimony in which veterans relate battlefield experience, family finances, and resentment of people who profited from wartime work without serving. These accounts challenge any attempt to assign a single 'Indigenous veterans' voice' to the postwar period. They are best read as individual records that can be compared with legal changes, military files, and local histories. A passage praising American democracy should not erase one demanding rights; both might emerge from the same corpus and sometimes from the same respondent.

Canadian veterans faced different political conditions. Some gained a wartime voting qualification, but most status First Nations people continued to encounter restrictions within the federal franchise and Indian Act system. Frederick Ogilvie Loft, who served in the war and later founded the League of Indians of Canada in 1919, offers one example of a veteran using organizational networks to press a broader agenda. His activism was not simply a reward given by the state for military service; it was Indigenous political initiative built on earlier traditions and new wartime experiences. Across the continent, the defensible conclusion is that service expanded the repertoire of political evidence and leadership. Its effect depended on individuals, organizations, legal jurisdictions, and the persistence of older restrictions.

Section 94.20

Indigenous Veterans and the Legacy of the First World War

Memorials, decorations, remembrance practices, veterans' organizations, and oral histories keep military service present in Indigenous communities. Wartime experience contributed to political awareness and organizational connections, but service did not automatically lead to citizenship, equality, or self-determination. Its enduring political significance lay in the contrast between sacrifice and continuing colonial inequality, a theme taken up in Chapter 95's history of organized criticism of assimilation policy.

The legacy of the First World War cannot be captured by a count of uniforms or decorations. Indigenous men and women served in military and medical institutions, sustained households and regional production, donated supplies, contested conscription, and organized politically after the fighting. Those activities unfolded under multiple governments and among nations that did not agree on the proper meaning of service. Some veterans remembered recognition by comrades and the experience of travel; others emphasized loss, injury, institutional neglect, or unresolved claims to political rights. A chapter concerned with legacy should hold these experiences together rather than select only the stories most readily commemorated in national military histories.

Commemoration itself has a history. Official monuments, military cemeteries, community memorials, regimental histories, family photographs, and oral accounts did not all preserve the same names or narratives. Some soldiers became nationally famous because decorations and newspaper reports made their stories visible; many others left only a service number or a brief reference in a local record. Indigenous communities often remembered people through kinship and place even when broader national accounts ignored them. Later military commemorations could restore attention to individuals such as John Shiwak or Edith Anderson Monture, but those interpretations must be dated and distinguished from wartime testimony. Public recognition has expanded over time without making earlier archival silences disappear.

The war's political consequences were similarly unequal. In the United States, the 1919 veterans' citizenship provision and later general citizenship law changed formal legal relationships while failing to remove all barriers to voting. In Canada, First Nations protests influenced conscription policy, but postwar settlement administration and the Indian Act continued to restrict control over land and political participation. The emergence of veteran organizers such as Frederick Loft showed how wartime connections could support national political movements. These developments did not originate Indigenous intellectual or political life; Chapter 92 has already shown an established tradition of journalism and public leadership. Rather, the war added experience, urgency, and new arguments to institutions that Indigenous people were already developing.

A historical debate over whether the conflict advanced Indigenous equality should therefore distinguish dimensions of change. More individuals were publicly recognized as competent soldiers and nurses; some acquired military training, networks, and a limited new path to citizenship. At the same time, treaties were not renegotiated on equal terms, the transfer of reserve lands could accelerate, and racial restrictions survived the armistice. A positive account focused on access to military service and symbolic recognition may be plausible within a specific unit; a skeptical account centered on veteran benefits and community sovereignty may be equally persuasive in another context. The disagreement is not merely about whether the war was 'good' or 'bad,' but about whose experiences count and which indicators are used.

Continuity also matters. Indigenous nations continued to define belonging, maintain families, sustain languages and ceremonies, and contest administrative power before, during, and after the war. Their wartime choices made sense within those longer histories. The most defensible synthesis is that the First World War transformed some opportunities and expectations while leaving colonial structures substantially intact. Its aftermath became one of several contexts for the reform movements of the 1920s and the sharper critiques of assimilation considered in Chapter 95. The chapter should close neither with triumphal integration nor with an assertion that service accomplished nothing, but with a historically grounded understanding of how sacrifice, recognition, and inequality could coexist within the same communities and individual lives.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Before 1914, 1914–1918, and the Postwar Years

Before the war: Indigenous nations maintained political relationships and cultural institutions within systems of reserves, reservations, restricted citizenship, and uneven economic opportunities.

During the war: Individuals enlisted, communities debated voluntary and compulsory duty, women organized relief and nursing, and governments mobilized labor and resources on an unprecedented scale.

After the armistice: Veterans returned to different benefit systems, injuries, changing households and new public expectations; the U.S. created a limited citizenship provision in 1919, while discriminatory policies continued in both countries.

Continuity: Treaty claims, nationhood, family responsibilities, racial restrictions, and struggles over land remained powerful.

Historical synthesis: War changed opportunities and the language of recognition, but it did not extinguish Indigenous self-government or dismantle colonial administration. Change was uneven by person, nation, and jurisdiction.

Chapter Summary

The First World War unfolded within several Indigenous political worlds rather than one undifferentiated national population. Canada entered as an imperial dominion in August 1914, Newfoundland governed its own wartime affairs, and the United States entered in April 1917. Indigenous nations related to these states through treaties, territorial status, administrative restrictions, and histories of armed service that did not produce a shared understanding of military duty. The war therefore made visible distinct claims about alliance, citizenship, self-government, and the responsibility of individuals to their communities.

Recruitment responded to manpower shortages, expanding military infrastructure, and personal decisions. First Nations men volunteered despite inconsistent early Canadian practices; many American Indian men enlisted or were registered through Selective Service; and soldiers from Labrador served with Newfoundland. Commonly cited service figures indicate substantial participation, but they represent estimates from incomplete and unevenly classified records. Historians must distinguish registrants from recruits, conscripts from volunteers, and military service from overseas deployment before comparing the numbers.

Indigenous volunteers entered modern armies as individuals with varied motives. Some recognized duties to the Crown or United States, some sought wages or training, and others pursued travel or family traditions. Community leaders and relatives could disagree about enlistment, while others were united in support of volunteers but opposed the state’s asserted right to compel service. The political significance of a military uniform depended on the wearer’s nation, life experience, and the changing relationship between the soldier and government authority.

The Canadian Expeditionary Force, American Expeditionary Forces, and Newfoundland Regiment operated under distinct administrative and political institutions. Military training brought recruits into regimented environments that required discipline, technical skill, transport, and rapid communication. Indigenous men served as infantrymen, scouts, snipers, engineers, laborers and in many other military roles. Stereotypes describing Indigenous men as naturally suited to dangerous tasks should not be substituted for individual military careers or the skills developed through training.

Francis Pegahmagabow, Henry Norwest, Joseph Oklahombi, and John Shiwak each illuminate a different aspect of wartime service. Their biographies also show the limits of memorial history: decorations are documented but cannot reveal an entire interior life; formal enlistment entries may differ from recruiting narratives; and deaths or wounds affected families in ways military records rarely capture. Choctaw-language telephone communications demonstrated Indigenous technical and linguistic knowledge, even though Indigenous-language suppression had long characterized official schooling.

Indigenous women organized the home front, supplied troops, managed changing household labor, and entered professional wartime nursing. The Six Nations Women’s Patriotic League coordinated material aid from 1914, while Edith Anderson Monture crossed the Canadian-American border to build a nursing career and later serve in France. Such stories should not be treated as exceptions that define all women’s experiences. Most women’s essential work took place through local economies, family care, correspondence and community organizations that were only unevenly recorded.

The Canadian conscription crisis exposed the difference between voluntary participation and compulsory obligation. First Nations protests argued from political status, the absence of voting rights, and treaty relationships. Order-in-Council P.C. 111 of January 1918 exempted status Indians from compulsory combatant service while leaving possible noncombatant obligations. The Six Nations women’s petition invoked wampum diplomacy and concrete concern for young relatives. The controversy illustrates Indigenous political agency in wartime rather than an absence of patriotic participation.

The war brought shifts in markets and labor, and it could intensify economic vulnerability. Indigenous producers contributed food, timber, transport services, and other work, but high prices did not erase restrictions on land or capital. Service removed family labor and delayed income, while bereavement, illness, and the influenza pandemic imposed additional costs. The economic consequences continued after the armistice as veterans returned, prices changed, and settlement programs sought productive acreage.

Postwar assistance exposed institutional inequality. Canada’s Soldier Settlement legislation began in 1917 and was developed in 1919, but reserve tenure and Indian Affairs administration shaped the assistance actually available to First Nations veterans. Land sought for non-Indigenous soldier settlement sometimes reduced reserve acreage. In the United States, the November 1919 statute provided an optional citizenship pathway to eligible honorably discharged Indigenous veterans, while the broader Indian Citizenship Act followed in 1924. Neither veterans’ recognition nor citizenship laws automatically removed discrimination or all voting barriers.

The war’s political legacy lay in change and continuity together. Veterans and women gained experience, networks, and public visibility; some used those experiences in emerging organizations and appeals for rights. But treaties, Indigenous nationhood, colonial administration, unequal landholding, and restricted political participation remained central to life after 1918. Community memory and later commemoration have helped recover people who were overlooked in national narratives. These unresolved contradictions provide the bridge to Chapter 95’s account of reform and Indigenous criticism of assimilation.

Chapter Reflection

The First World War shows Indigenous nations and individuals making difficult choices under unequal conditions. Service, protest, and home-front work were all forms of political action.

The chapter reveals how recognition and rights can diverge. The war made Indigenous contributions visible, yet benefits, land, and political status continued to be shaped by colonial administration.

Those contradictions fed the reform movements and critiques of assimilation examined in Chapter 95, and wartime service remained a lasting element of Indigenous community memory.

Military service also generated a documentary imbalance. Some soldiers are represented by extensive personnel files and medals, while letters, women’s home-front contributions, and Indigenous-language accounts survive less consistently. A careful history must interpret these gaps, not convert the better-documented individuals into universal examples.

Political interpretation remains unsettled. Veterans’ citizenship provisions, conscription exemptions and public honors had meaning, but equality must be evaluated through access to voting, land, finance, decision-making and the preservation of treaty relationships. The same war that gave certain individuals new opportunities also exposed their communities to continuing colonial restrictions.

The experience of 1914–1924 belongs to a longer historical sequence. Indigenous people were political actors before the war and remained so afterward. Their decisions about service, protest and remembrance became part of the continuing struggle over rights, public representation and self-government.
