Horses and the Transformation of the Plains

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

The mounted bison hunter is one of the most recognizable images of Indigenous North America, and one of the most misleading when it is allowed to stand for the whole Plains past. The horse did not create Plains societies. It entered worlds of farming villages, trading towns, bison drives, river routes, and diplomatic alliances, and Indigenous peoples decided how, where, and why to bring it into their economies, families, and ceremonies.

Most popular images of Plains horse cultures come from the nineteenth century: ledger drawings, photographs, travelers' accounts, and later ethnographic work. These sources record real and important societies, but they show the end of a long process rather than its beginning. By the time such records were made, horses had been part of Indigenous life across the interior for generations. The questions this chapter asks are therefore earlier and more basic. How did a domestic animal introduced by Spanish colonists reach the grasslands, mountains, and plateaus of the continental interior? Who carried it there, and by what relationships? What did Indigenous nations have to learn, build, and reorganize to make horses useful? And who gained, who lost, and what did the new animal cost?

Part of the answer has changed recently. For much of the twentieth century, textbooks dated the horse's spread across the Plains largely to the aftermath of the Pueblo Revolt of 1680, when Pueblo and Spanish livestock were thrown into circulation. In 2023 an interdisciplinary team led by William Timothy Treal Taylor, publishing in Science, reported radiocarbon dates, ancient DNA, and skeletal and contextual analyses of horse remains from archaeological sites in Wyoming, Kansas, New Mexico, and Idaho, together with Indigenous knowledge. Their results indicate that domestic horses had reached parts of the central Plains and northern Rockies by the first half of the seventeenth century, well before 1680. That finding does not make the Pueblo Revolt irrelevant, but it moves the beginning of the story earlier and shifts attention from a single colonial rupture to long-running Indigenous networks of exchange.

This chapter treats the horse as an Indigenous historical project rather than a gift of colonization. Spanish settlements in New Mexico were important sources of animals, but the horse's circulation across the interior depended on Indigenous trading partnerships, kinship ties, diplomatic choices, and the practical knowledge of people who learned to breed, train, feed, and protect animals in environments very different from Iberian ranch country. Spanish authorities could neither administer nor continuously observe this circulation across thousands of kilometers of country, a point that matters for understanding both the pace and the geography of the transformation.

The consequences were large but uneven. Horses transformed transportation, bison hunting, household movement, trade, and warfare for many nations. They also created new forms of wealth and inequality, new targets for raiding, heavy demands for pasture and winter feed, and new ecological and strategic vulnerabilities. Some nations built large mounted hunting economies, others joined horses to village agriculture, and others used horses mainly for transport, trade, or occasional hunts. The chronology also varied: the evidence supports approximate periods of adoption, not a single date for the whole Plains and not a precise year for each nation.

The chapter draws on evidence of several kinds, each with its own strengths and blind spots: archaeological and genetic studies of horse remains, Spanish, French, and British colonial records, trader journals, Indigenous oral histories, pictographic winter counts, museum collections of equestrian material culture, and later ethnographic work. It treats Indigenous knowledge as historical evidence requiring the same careful contextual reading as any other source, and it is cautious about projecting nineteenth-century equestrian lifeways back onto the seventeenth century.

As the opening chapter of Volume V, this chapter also establishes a theme that runs through the volume: Indigenous power in an age of European empires was not simply a matter of surviving imperial pressure. Indigenous nations adapted to changing geopolitical circumstances and created new sources of power. The detailed histories of Comanche and Apache societies belong to Chapters 50 and 51, and the full treatment of Indigenous diplomacy belongs to Chapter 55. This chapter explains the broad transformation that made those histories possible.

Historical Context

The chapter begins in the sixteenth century, but its background stretches much further. Chapter 29 treated the Plains before horses: farming villages along the Missouri and its tributaries, bison-hunting communities, trading centers, and the networks linking grassland peoples to the Southwest, the Great Lakes, and the Mississippi Valley. Chapter 42 treated Spanish expansion on the northern frontier of New Spain, and Chapters 43 and 44 treated Pueblo colonization and the Pueblo Revolt of 1680. This chapter summarizes those developments only as needed.

Three contexts matter most. First, the Plains were not an empty or uniform space. They included tallgrass prairies in the east, mixed-grass and shortgrass country farther west, river bottomlands that supported maize agriculture, and a semi-arid climate with severe winters and recurrent drought. Bison, the great grazers of this ecosystem, supported many peoples, but not all peoples hunted them in the same way, and many combined hunting with gardens, fishing, gathering, and exchange.

Second, the Spanish colonial frontier in New Mexico was a zone of Spanish ranching, missions, forced labor, Pueblo agriculture, and Apache, Ute, and Plains trade. Livestock, especially horses, sheep, and cattle, were central to Spanish colonial economy and were also targets of Indigenous trade and raiding. Spanish officials restricted unauthorized Indigenous riding and sought to regulate access to horses, but those measures did not prevent Indigenous people from handling, acquiring, and exchanging horses through multiple channels.

Third, the late sixteenth through eighteenth centuries saw growing European colonial competition for the continent. French traders along the Mississippi and Missouri valleys, British traders on Hudson Bay, and Spanish settlers on the Rio Grande all sought Indigenous partners, goods, and allies. Indigenous nations did not simply respond to these empires; they used competing colonial connections to acquire horses, firearms, and trade goods, and to pursue their own political aims. This chapter's story belongs to that larger pattern, which Chapters 55 and 60 examine more fully.

Division I

The Plains before the Horse and the Meaning of Equestrian Transformation

Section 49.1

The Horse and the Transformation of Indigenous North America

Few animals have changed so many Indigenous societies in so short a historical span as the domestic horse. For peoples who had moved on foot, with human labor and dogs as their principal sources of transport, horses offered a very large increase in carrying capacity, speed, and range. A rider could follow bison herds farther, scout wider territory, carry heavier loads, and cross greater distances to trade, visit relatives, join ceremonies, or defend a community. These were not automatic benefits, because horses had to be acquired, fed, protected, trained, and bred, and they changed what households owed one another. But for the many nations that built successful horse economies, they became one of the most important instruments of economic and political innovation in the eighteenth-century interior.

The chapter's central question is how Indigenous nations turned a newly available animal into a resource for pursuing their own objectives. That wording matters. A narrative in which Europeans "introduced" the horse and Plains peoples "received" it hides the work of acquisition, knowledge transfer, and adaptation. Indigenous people observed horses in Spanish colonial settings, worked with them as herders and laborers, traded for them, captured them, bred them, and taught one another how to manage them across hundreds of kilometers. The animal was Iberian in origin, but equestrian societies in the interior were Indigenous creations.

The chapter also resists the opposite error of treating the horse as the sole cause of Plains power. Chapter 29 showed that Plains societies already included productive farming towns, specialized bison economies, long-distance trade, and political relationships. Horses entered those systems and interacted with them. Some existing practices were intensified, others were transformed, and some, such as communal bison drives and village agriculture, continued alongside mounted hunting.

The scale of the change is easiest to see by comparing what a single household could do before and after it gained reliable access to horses. A family that depended on dogs and its own members to move belongings had to limit the weight of its lodge, tools, and stored food, and its hunters could only reach animals within a day's walk of camp. A family with several well-trained horses could carry a larger lodge, bring home more meat from a distant kill, visit relatives in another valley, and attend a trade fair that had once been out of practical reach. Multiplied across thousands of households, such differences changed how nations arranged their seasons, their alliances, and their sense of their own territory.

It is equally important to see what the chapter does not claim. It does not claim that every Indigenous nation adopted horses, that those who did so gained uniformly, or that horse use erased older systems of knowledge. Many nations of the Arctic, Northwest Coast, Northeast, and Southeast had little occasion to rely on horses in the period studied here, and even on the Plains some communities used horses sparingly. The subject is therefore not "the Plains Indian" but a set of regionally varied Indigenous responses to a new resource.

Finally, the chapter follows the volume's governing theme. Volume V examines how Indigenous nations created and maintained power while European empires competed for the continent. Horses are one of the clearest examples because the animal came from the colonial world, yet the networks, institutions, and political uses that made it powerful were Indigenous.

Historical Vocabulary
Equestrian, Diffusion, and Pastoralism

Definition. Equestrian describes societies or practices organized around the riding and use of horses. Diffusion describes the spread of an object, technology, or practice from one community or region to another. Pastoralism usually describes economies based primarily on keeping herd animals for food and products such as milk, meat, and wool.

Historical context. Historians use "equestrian" for the many Indigenous nations that built substantial economic and political life around horses. "Diffusion" is useful for tracing the horse's movement, but it can mislead if it suggests passive spread. Each transmission required a decision, a relationship, and the transfer of knowledge.

Why it matters. These terms keep attention on the human choices and relationships that moved horses across the continent.

Potential misunderstanding. Plains horse economies were not pastoralist in the usual sense. Horses were used for transportation, hunting, trade, warfare, and wealth, but they were generally not the main source of food, and many equestrian peoples also hunted, farmed, fished, and traded. Applying "pastoralism" without qualification imports a model from Eurasia that does not fit most Plains cases.

Section 49.2

Plains Societies before Widespread Horse Adoption

Before horses became common, the Plains supported a variety of societies. Along the Missouri River and its tributaries, Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara communities lived in large villages of earth lodges and farmed maize, beans, squash, and sunflowers in river bottoms. On the central Plains, Pawnee villages along the Platte, Loup, and Republican drainages combined agriculture with seasonal bison hunts. On the southern Plains, Wichita-related communities farmed along rivers and traded widely. Other groups lived more mobile lives, following bison seasonally and exchanging hides, meat, and tools for agricultural produce, shell, stone, and other goods. The relative importance of farming, hunting, fishing, and gathering differed by place and over time.

These societies already had considerable political and economic sophistication. Villages were organized by kin groups, councils, ceremonial societies, and leaders who managed agricultural calendars, hunts, and diplomacy. Trading centers on the Missouri and other rivers, and long-established exchange between Plains communities and the Pueblo towns of the Rio Grande, linked grassland products, such as bison hides and meat, to agricultural goods and material from other regions. Chapter 29 treats this world in detail; here it is enough to emphasize that a horse arriving in such a setting entered a landscape of established relationships, not an economic void.

Historians also have to avoid reading the later Plains backward. The nineteenth-century societies best known from ethnography, with large mounted bands and extensive bison robe trade, were products of several generations of change. Dating earlier conditions requires archaeological evidence, early colonial descriptions, and Indigenous historical knowledge, each of which provides only partial views.

Pueblo-Plains exchange deserves particular emphasis because it shows that the Rio Grande and the grasslands were linked long before Spanish colonization. Archaeological evidence from towns on the Plains edge, such as Pecos, shows exchange in bison products and agricultural and manufactured goods with hunting peoples to the east. These relationships meant that when Spanish settlement introduced new animals and goods into Pueblo country, there were already roads, partners, and customary ways of exchange along which they could travel.

Farming villages deserve equal emphasis. On the Missouri, villages of earth lodges housed substantial populations, and their gardens produced surplus maize that could be stored for winter and traded. The women who tended those gardens controlled crops and storage, and their skills made villages reliable trading partners for mobile hunters. Villages were also political communities with councils, ceremonial organizations, and defenses, and they negotiated with neighbors for access to land and resources.

The mobile communities of the grasslands, for their part, were not simply wanderers without institutions. They organized seasonal rounds, maintained knowledge of river crossings, water, and bison movements, and sustained relationships with village and Pueblo partners. Seeing these societies as interdependent, rather than as separate "farmers" and "hunters," helps explain why horses spread so readily through existing exchange relationships.

Section 49.3

Dogs, Travois, Human Labor, and Prehorse Mobility

Before horses, the Plains were crossed on foot. People carried loads, and in many communities dogs pulled travois, which are frames of poles dragged behind an animal and loaded with goods. A Spanish chronicle of the Coronado expedition, associated with Pedro de Castañeda de Nájera, describes bison-hunting people on the Plains moving with dogs that dragged loads on poles. Scholars have often connected these Querechos to ancestors of some Apache peoples, an identification that remains debated, but the description is valuable because it documents dog-powered transport in the 1540s.

This was not a primitive form of mobility. It was a system with its own logic, in which households, camp sizes, hunting seasons, and travel distances were organized around what dogs and people could carry. Dog travois were useful for moving hide covers, poles, food, and household goods, but their capacity was modest, and every additional load meant more human labor. Much of that labor fell to women, who managed household equipment, hide processing, food preparation, and the movement of camp. Travel distances and the size of lodges were limited by what could be carried.

Pedestrian hunting techniques were highly developed. Communal drives guided bison into corrals, over cliffs, or into surrounds, and often required collective labor, ceremonial preparation, and precise knowledge of animal behavior and terrain. Some bison jump sites on the Plains were used over many centuries. Stalking, driving, ambush at water, and winter pursuit on snow were also used. These practices made substantial amounts of meat and hides available in a short time, but they depended on location, season, and cooperation.

The Coronado-era description of dogs dragging loads also hints at the labor structure of prehorse life. A dog's capacity limited the amount of goods per animal, and a family's wealth in dogs was a practical constraint on its mobility. Dogs required food, which meant that large numbers of dogs had to be fed from the same bison kills that supplied people. A household with many children and elders had to balance the number of dogs, the load carried by adult women and men, and the distance it could travel in a day.

Communal bison drives and surrounds drew on the coordinated work of many people and on specialized knowledge. Preparation could include scouting, building drive lanes or enclosures, and ceremonies to prepare hunters and honor animals, and success depended on weather, wind, and the behavior of herds. A well-run drive could supply a community with meat, hides, sinew, and bone for months. The labor of processing was equally large, and communities had to organize drying, storing, and transporting the product.

These achievements are part of the background against which the horse should be judged. They show that Plains peoples were capable of large collective projects and sophisticated environmental management long before horses. They also show why horses would have been immediately intelligible as a way of reorganizing work already understood to be central: moving goods, finding herds, and bringing meat home.

Everyday Life and Social History

Dogs, Travois, and Pedestrian Movement

Moving a household without horses was a coordinated, labor-intensive task. Families sorted what could be carried, what could be cached, and what should be left. Dogs, often trained from a young age, pulled small loads, while adults and older children bore much of the rest. Lodges and equipment were built with transport in mind, and the length of a day's travel was set by what the group could move together.

These practices shaped the household economy. Because transport was costly, communities tended to move less far and less often, and the effort of relocating camp influenced where they wintered, where they hunted, and when they gathered for communal bison drives. Later chapters on horse transport should be read against this background: horses did not simply make existing journeys easier, but allowed different journeys and larger households to move.

This account is a reconstruction based on early Spanish descriptions, archaeological evidence, and later ethnographic accounts, and the exact practices differed among nations.

Section 49.4

Horses in Ancient North America: Evolution, Extinction, and Reintroduction

Horses have a long history in North America, but the animals of the colonial era were not simply the survivors of that history. The genus Equus evolved in North America, and equids were present for millions of years before spreading to other continents. Native North American horses disappeared from the continent at the end of the Pleistocene, roughly twelve thousand to ten thousand years ago. The causes of that extinction, among them climate change, human hunting, and other ecological factors, remain debated and are discussed in Chapter 6.

The domestic horse emerged much later in Eurasia, in the several thousand years before the common era, and spread through trade, pastoral movement, and conquest. Iberian horses reached the Caribbean with Spanish colonists beginning in the 1490s, and Spanish settlers brought breeding stock to mainland New Spain in the sixteenth century. These were the animals from which most Indigenous herds on the Plains ultimately descended. Genetic research on archaeological horse remains, including the 2023 study discussed below, supports an Iberian-derived ancestry for early horses in the interior.

The distinction between deep evolutionary history and documented colonial reintroduction is important. The fact that ancient horses once lived in North America does not mean that domestic horses were already present in Indigenous societies before the sixteenth century. Some Indigenous oral traditions emphasize close relationships with horses, and some knowledge holders see horses as relatives returning to the land. Scholars treat those perspectives as significant forms of historical and cultural understanding, while also distinguishing them from the question, addressed by archaeological and genetic evidence, of when domestic horses first reached particular regions.

This chapter therefore relies on several kinds of evidence together. Archaeology supplies physical remains and dates. Genetics identifies ancestry. Spanish, French, and British records document colonial contacts. Indigenous oral histories and pictographic records preserve memories of acquisition and meaning. None is sufficient alone, and each helps correct the others.

Archaeologists and paleontologists reconstruct the earlier history of horses from fossils, and the story is one of repeated evolution and migration. Equids that evolved in North America spread across land bridges into Asia and beyond, and later lineages of horses diversified in Eurasia and Africa. The domestic horse lineages that were later brought to the Americas descended from animals domesticated in Eurasia, and their dispersal across that continent is traced through ancient DNA and archaeology.

The extinction of North American equids at the end of the Pleistocene belongs to the larger story of late Ice Age extinctions discussed in Chapter 6. Scholars have proposed climate change, the spread of human hunters, habitat change, and combinations of these, and no single explanation has gained universal agreement. For this chapter, the key point is the long absence: for thousands of years before the 1490s, there were no horses in North America, and no domestic horses at all.

Iberian colonists brought horses first to islands of the Caribbean and then to the mainland. The stock they carried was bred in a Mediterranean ranching tradition, hardy and used to dry country, and that heritage affected how animals fared on the Plains. Over generations, Indigenous breeding and local selection shaped herds suited to Plains conditions. Studies of ancient and modern genomes allow researchers to trace those lineages, and the 2023 study is part of that larger effort.

Division II

Spanish Colonial Horses and the Earliest Indigenous Adoption

Section 49.5

Spanish Horses and the Sixteenth-Century Northern Frontier

Spanish expeditions into the northern borderlands relied heavily on horses. The Coronado expedition of 1540–1542 moved from western New Spain to the Zuni country, the Rio Grande Pueblo towns, and east onto the Plains in search of the wealthy lands it had been promised. Its mounted soldiers, accompanied by livestock, Indigenous allies from central Mexico, and enslaved or conscripted workers, fought a war against Pueblo communities in the Tiguex region and later reached the grasslands of central Kansas, known in the Spanish record as Quivira. Chapter 42 treats these expeditions and their Indigenous allies and opponents.

For Plains peoples, the expedition provided a view of mounted Spanish power, but it was not a source of established horse herds. Older textbook accounts sometimes traced Plains horse populations to animals that escaped from Coronado or from Hernando de Soto's expedition in the Southeast. Modern scholars are generally skeptical of that explanation, because exploration parties lost and ate horses, but sustained breeding and exchange require continuing management and relationships. The widespread diffusion of horses depended on later, settled livestock operations.

The sixteenth-century evidence is nonetheless important. It shows that Indigenous people on the Plains and in the Southwest saw horses in action very early, understood their military and transport potential, and had reasons to seek them. It also marks a distinction the chapter maintains throughout: temporary exploration, which brings horses through a region, is different from a frontier of continuing breeding and exchange, which makes them available over generations.

The Coronado expedition was not an isolated Spanish venture. It was part of a broader sixteenth-century northward push from central Mexico, fueled by stories of rich cities, and it relied on Indigenous allies and laborers from central Mexico as well as Spanish soldiers. The expedition's route took it across territories of many Indigenous peoples, and its effects on them, including violence in the Tiguex region, are treated in Chapter 42. For horse history, what matters is that the expedition brought a large number of horses to the Southwest and Plains and that Indigenous communities had sustained opportunities to observe how they were used.

After the expedition returned to Mexico, Spanish activity in the region lapsed for decades. That gap is one reason historians doubt that escaped Coronado horses founded the later Plains herds. A small number of animals, left without human management, would have faced predators, winter, and limited numbers of mares, and the chronology of the archaeological record suggests that horses appeared in the interior in the next century, as the New Mexico colony developed.

The sixteenth century therefore sets conditions rather than producing results. It acquainted Indigenous peoples with horses and set colonial expectations about their military value, but the sustained circulation that transformed Plains economies depended on a permanent settlement and on Indigenous networks that were able to carry horses and knowledge.

Spanish contact with the Southwest did not end with Coronado in 1542, and the intervals between expeditions are part of the evidence. The Chamuscado–Rodríguez expedition of 1581, the Espejo expedition of 1582–1583, and the Castaño de Sosa expedition of 1590–1591 all entered Pueblo country with small mounted parties and livestock, so Pueblo communities along the Rio Grande saw horses repeatedly across the half-century before permanent colonization. Each visit was brief, and each depended on Indigenous guides, food, and information. The expeditions are better understood as episodes of exposure than as moments of introduction, and the record does not show that they left breeding herds in Indigenous hands.

The Plains sections of Coronado's route add a regional dimension. The Spanish accounts of the expedition describe the grasslands east of the Pecos country and a visit to Quivira, usually identified with villages in central Kansas, whose inhabitants are commonly linked by scholars to ancestors of Wichita-related peoples. The people of Quivira therefore saw Spanish horses and mounted soldiers in the early 1540s, within a world of earth-covered or grass dwellings, maize fields, and bison hunting that Chapter 29 describes. This documented early Plains encounter with horses thus involved farming communities and a mounted column that remained only briefly.

The same expedition shows why historians separate military use from economic adoption. Spanish cavalry were employed against Pueblo towns in the Tiguex war of 1540–1541, and Indigenous witnesses saw horses used as instruments of force. Seeing horses as weapons of an invading army did not give anyone the means to breed, feed, or ride them. It did give Pueblo, Apache, and Plains communities a clear understanding of what the animal could do, which matters for the way later opportunities to acquire horses were evaluated.

Section 49.6

New Mexico, Colonial Ranching, and Indigenous Knowledge of Horses

The Spanish colony of New Mexico, founded in 1598 under Juan de Oñate and later centered on Santa Fe, created what the sixteenth-century expeditions had not: a permanent livestock economy next to Pueblo towns and within reach of Apache, Ute, and Plains exchange networks. Ranches and mission estates raised horses, sheep, goats, and cattle, and the colony depended on animals for transport, farming, and defense. That economy relied on Indigenous labor, including forced labor and tribute obligations, and on Pueblo and other Indigenous people who worked as herders and laborers.

Such work gave some Indigenous people direct knowledge of horses: how to handle them, where to graze them, how to breed and train them, and how they could be used for transport and hunting. Spanish colonial authorities, however, tried to limit Indigenous access, and royal and local regulations restricted Indigenous horseback riding and, in some contexts, ownership, although licensed privileges and practical exceptions existed for certain Indigenous officeholders, allies, and colonial workers. The regulations reveal Spanish concern about Indigenous mounted power. They did not and could not prevent Indigenous people from learning, acquiring, and breeding horses, because colonial authority over a vast frontier was limited, and because Spanish settlements also depended on Indigenous labor and trade.

The experience of Pueblo workers in the colonial economy was complicated. Spanish colonization imposed tribute, labor demands, and religious pressure on Pueblo communities, as Chapter 43 explains, and the Pueblo Revolt grew from those pressures. Yet Pueblo people who worked with livestock also gained skills and, in some cases, access to animals. The relationship was one of coercion and opportunity at once, and historians should avoid describing it as a benefit of colonization. It was knowledge acquired under conditions of exploitation.

Apache and other neighboring peoples had different relationships with the colony. Some traded with Spanish settlements and with Pueblo towns, some raided herds, and some did both at different times. Spanish records of such raiding, though written from a colonial viewpoint, provide evidence that Indigenous people outside the colony were interested in horses and able to obtain them. Raiding was not the only channel, but it shows that Spanish restrictions were not effective barriers.

The size of the colony also matters. New Mexico was a small and relatively isolated settlement at the end of a long supply line from central Mexico. It had limited military and administrative capacity compared with the area it claimed, and its economy depended on Indigenous labor and trade. That weakness helps explain why Spanish authorities could neither control the horses in their own herds nor monitor what happened beyond the settled districts.

The colonial setting of New Mexico explains much about how knowledge of horses reached Indigenous people. After the 1598 settlement under Oñate, the colony's center moved within a few years, and Santa Fe was established as the capital in the early seventeenth century. Franciscan missions and Spanish households held herds, and both relied on Pueblo labor. Pueblo men and women worked in fields and with animals, and the missions' stables and pastures depended on their work. Such labor was demanded through tribute and obligation, as Chapter 43 explains, and it should not be read as a voluntary apprenticeship.

Spanish law distinguished among kinds of access. The 1568 royal restriction on unauthorized Indigenous riding concerned riding, and the source window below identifies it as a specific law; ownership and herding were not covered by an identical blanket ban. Colonial officials also issued licenses to individual Indigenous leaders and allies, and auxiliaries who campaigned with Spanish forces were mounted when it suited colonial purposes. The result was an uneven system in which riding was formally restricted, exceptions were granted when useful, and herding by Indigenous workers was a routine part of colonial economy.

Evidence from beyond the settlements shows that Spanish control was weak. Seventeenth-century Spanish documents complain of Apache and other raiders taking livestock from Pueblo towns and Spanish estates, and Spanish officials repeatedly asked for military help they could not always get. The colony was small, financed from distant Mexico City, and dependent on Pueblo labor and food. A frontier of that kind could restrict horses on paper far more easily than it could protect them in the field.

Section 49.7

Indigenous Acquisition: Exchange, Capture, Gift-Giving, and Herd Development

Indigenous communities acquired horses through many channels, and historians should avoid treating acquisition as a single event. Trade was important: Pueblo, Apache, Ute, and other peoples exchanged goods with Spanish settlers and with one another, and horses became part of those exchanges. Capture also mattered. Raids on Spanish ranches and herds supplied animals, and horses could also be taken from other Indigenous peoples. Gifts and alliances moved horses among communities, and horses given to an ally or relative carried obligations of reciprocity. Some Indigenous people acquired animals through colonial labor or service.

The most important long-term source was reproduction. A mare typically bears at most one foal a year, and young horses take years to mature, so herd growth is gradual. Building a breeding herd required acquiring mares as well as stallions, protecting animals through winters, and making choices about which animals to breed, keep, geld, trade, or ride. As herds grew, surplus animals could be traded onward, so that each community that built a breeding herd became a possible source for the next.

Knowledge moved with animals. Learning to ride, pack, hobble, graze, doctor, and breed horses required instruction from people who already knew, and such knowledge traveled through kinship, trade, captivity, adoption, and alliance. Where Indigenous networks already linked communities, those relationships carried horses and horse knowledge. Because horses are living property that reproduces, the same animal could be traded, bred, and traded again. The geography of diffusion was thus shaped by Indigenous social geography.

Consider what it took to build a breeding herd from a few animals. A community needed mares as well as stallions, and it had to keep them alive through winter, protect them from theft, and decide which foals to keep for riding or breeding. Colts took years to mature, and for the first years after acquisition a small herd gave limited returns while demanding constant care. The community that persisted through those years was making a long-term investment in a resource whose value it understood.

Trade partners' knowledge mattered as much as animals. A seller might also provide advice on feeding, training, or handling, and a kinsperson or ally might send a young relative to live with a horse-owning community to learn. Captives and adoptees who had worked with horses could carry skills into new communities, and marriages between nations brought together families with different experiences. The spread of horses was therefore also a spread of people and practices.

Because exchange involved many small transactions, the cumulative effect could be large while leaving little documentary trace. A horse traded at a Pueblo fair, given to a relative, bred, and traded again might pass through a dozen hands over a decade, and none of those transactions would be recorded by Spanish officials. This is one reason why the archaeological and Indigenous evidence is so important for reconstructing the early period.

The mechanisms of acquisition left different kinds of evidence, which is why each has to be assessed separately. Trade appears in colonial records only when Spanish officials or missionaries observed or complained about it, and in Indigenous memory when communities recall relationships with trading partners. Raiding appears in colonial documents as theft and in Indigenous narratives as warfare or as part of ongoing political relations. Gifts and marriage exchanges appear mostly in later ethnographic and oral sources. Reproduction appears in archaeological and genetic evidence for herds, and sometimes in estimates of how many animals a community managed.

These differences shape what historians can say about time. A horse recorded as stolen in a Spanish document in 1650 is a dated fact, but it does not tell us where the horse went. A horse recalled in an oral narrative as brought by a visiting trader belongs to a community's way of ordering its past, and the date may be uncertain by decades. A bone dated by radiocarbon gives a calibrated range for the animal's death, but not for its first owner. Combining the three produces a chronology whose precision varies, and the chapter's approximate dates reflect that variation rather than caution for its own sake.

Herd growth also depended on social organization. A household with a few mares could build a herd only if it had grazing, labor for protection, and the support of relatives who could lend or exchange animals. This is one reason horses spread more quickly among communities that already had dense kinship and exchange ties than among isolated ones. The animal moved along social infrastructure that Indigenous peoples had built for other purposes.

Section 49.8

New Archaeological Evidence for Horses before 1680

The 2023 Science study by William Timothy Treal Taylor and collaborators is the most important recent contribution to this chronology. The team studied horse remains from archaeological sites in the Great Plains, northern Rockies, and Southwest, including sites in Wyoming, Kansas, New Mexico, and Idaho, and combined several methods: radiocarbon dating of bone, ancient DNA to identify ancestry, skeletal and isotopic analyses to investigate diet, health, and use, and Indigenous knowledge to place the findings in context. The authors reported that some animals in the Plains and northern Rockies dated to the first half of the seventeenth century and showed ancestry consistent with Spanish-derived horses, and that the evidence did not support the view that the horse's arrival in the interior began only after 1680. They also reported a directly dated horse at Paa’ko Pueblo, New Mexico, whose chronology may precede the permanent Spanish settlement of 1598; the earlier introduction cannot be attributed solely to that colony.

The study also reported evidence that some animals were cared for and integrated into Indigenous practices, including indications interpreted as ceremonial or mortuary treatment. These findings matter because they suggest that early horses were already embedded in Indigenous social and spiritual relationships, rather than merely appearing as isolated animals.

The study's limits are equally important. Archaeological remains show that particular horses existed in particular places at particular times, within the uncertainties of radiocarbon calibration. They do not show how many horses were present, how widely they were used, or when an entire nation became extensively mounted. A dated horse in Wyoming does not mean that every northern nation rode regularly by the same date. The finding therefore changes the beginning of the story and the importance of Indigenous exchange, but it should not be turned into a claim that large-scale mounted societies already existed across the Plains in 1620. Large mounted hunting economies are documented later.

Radiocarbon dating of bone is powerful but has known complications. The date it produces is a statistical range, and it must be calibrated to calendar years using curves that are not smooth, which for the seventeenth century produces especially wide ranges. Diet can affect results, as can contamination. The authors of the 2023 study took steps to address such issues, and the results should be understood as ranges consistent with early seventeenth-century presence for some animals, not as year-specific records.

Ancient DNA adds an independent line of evidence. By comparing the genomes of archaeological horses to those of other populations, researchers can assess where the animals' ancestors came from. The reported Iberian-derived ancestry is consistent with early Spanish-connected transmission through Indigenous exchange networks; it does not establish any single route or exclude later contributions from French or British-associated horse stocks. Genetic evidence cannot show the route by which an animal arrived, but it narrows the possibilities.

The involvement of Indigenous knowledge in the study also reflects a changing relationship between archaeology and Indigenous nations. Collaboration allows researchers to interpret findings in light of Indigenous histories and priorities and to address questions, such as the treatment of animals and their significance, that physical remains alone cannot answer. Such collaboration is discussed more broadly in Chapter 1 and, regarding repatriation and research ethics, in Chapter 125.

The four archaeological contexts named in the 2023 study span a large area. Blacks Fork lies in southwestern Wyoming, in country crossed by routes between the Rockies and the Plains. The Kaw River site is in Kansas, in the central Plains. Paa'ko Pueblo lies in central New Mexico, in the Rio Grande region, and American Falls is on the Snake River in southeastern Idaho. Taken together, the sites show that early horses were not confined to one corridor, and they cover the zone through which later nations would trade horses between the Southwest, the central Plains, and the northern Rockies.

The Paa'ko evidence deserves special care. Researchers note that material from that pueblo may predate the permanent Spanish settlement of 1598. If so, horses were in contact with Pueblo communities before the colony became established, perhaps through the sixteenth-century expeditions or through exchange. The finding also warns against explaining all early circulation as a direct result of settled ranching in New Mexico. The evidence supports early presence at specific sites, and it does not yet allow historians to reconstruct each pathway.

Archaeological context also bears on the question of who made the deposits. Identifying the Indigenous communities associated with a site requires consultation with descendant nations, careful reading of the surrounding assemblages, and caution about modern labels. The chapter therefore does not assign these four contexts to particular modern nations. What the study establishes is that Indigenous contexts in these regions included domestic horses in the seventeenth century, that some animals were managed and used, and that some were treated in ways that suggest ceremonial significance.

Section 49.9

The Pueblo Revolt of 1680 and the Acceleration of Horse Exchange

The Pueblo Revolt of 1680 was a coordinated uprising in which Pueblo communities, together with some Apache and other allies, expelled the Spanish from New Mexico for more than a decade. Chapter 44 examines its causes, leadership, and course. For the history of horses, the revolt mattered because it disrupted Spanish control of livestock in the colony. Pueblo communities and their neighbors gained access to animals, and the movement of horses through Pueblo, Apache, Ute, and Plains networks probably increased as Spanish authority collapsed and as the Spanish reconquest followed in the 1690s.

Older histories often treated the revolt as the starting point for the Plains horse revolution, and the idea remains in many general accounts. It had a strong evidentiary basis in the sources available when it was developed: Spanish documents recorded large livestock losses, and later Plains horse cultures appeared to be eighteenth-century phenomena. The 2023 research complicates it. The revolt could accelerate a diffusion already under way; it did not begin it.

The revolt's effect on livestock is documented in Spanish accounts. Spanish settlers fled south to the El Paso area, leaving behind much of their property, and Pueblo communities, which held the country for more than a decade, took over fields and animals. The situation left many horses in Pueblo and neighboring hands, and exchange with Apache, Ute, and Plains peoples increased, according to scholars who have studied the period. After the Spanish reconquest of the 1690s, restoration of colonial control did not return all animals to Spanish ownership.

Why does this matter if horses were already moving before 1680? The answer lies in scale and in the nature of exchange. A large increase in the number of horses available in Pueblo and neighboring communities could supply more animals to northern and eastern networks and strengthen the position of Indigenous intermediaries who controlled routes and fairs. The revolt thus could have intensified diffusion without creating it.

The debate also illustrates how historians revise accounts as new evidence emerges. The older view was reasonable given the evidence available, and its authors drew careful attention to livestock losses and eighteenth-century observations. What changed was the arrival of new methods and collaborations that could date animals and identify their ancestry. The lesson for students is that historical chronologies are provisional, and that their revision does not mean earlier scholars were careless.

Spanish sources describe the immediate consequences of the revolt. In August 1680 Pueblo forces besieged Santa Fe, and the surviving colonists and many Pueblo allies withdrew south toward El Paso del Norte. The retreat left much property behind, and Pueblo communities controlled the upper Rio Grande until Diego de Vargas began the reconquest in 1692–1693. The details of the uprising are the subject of Chapter 44. For the history of horses, the significant point is that for more than a decade the colony's herds were no longer under Spanish control.

What happened to those animals is harder to document than the circumstances of their loss. Spanish records emphasize what was lost, and Pueblo and neighboring communities left few written accounts. Historians generally infer that Pueblo, Apache, Ute, and Plains traders obtained animals and that the exchange of horses intensified, but the extent can only be estimated indirectly. The earlier presence of horses at Blacks Fork, Kaw River, and American Falls means that the revolt was not a precondition for horses on the Plains. It may nonetheless have changed how many animals were available to the communities that had them, and how quickly exchange moved.

The post-1692 period also shows continuity. Spanish authorities returned, Pueblo communities negotiated their relationship with the restored colony, and Apache, Ute, and Comanche relations with New Mexico continued to center on trade and raiding. The first Spanish record of Comanche people in New Mexico dates to the early eighteenth century, and Chapter 50 treats their history. In this sense 1680 divides the story into before and after for New Mexico, while for the Plains it belongs to a longer sequence.

Division III

The Indigenous Diffusion of Horses across Western North America

Section 49.10

Southern Plains Networks and the Rio Grande Borderlands

The southern Plains and the Rio Grande borderlands formed one of the most active zones of horse exchange. Pueblo towns such as Pecos and Taos had long traded with bison-hunting peoples to the east, exchanging maize, cotton cloth, and other agricultural goods and crafts for bison products and hides. Spanish colonization overlaid a new set of relationships on this older system: colonial officials, missionaries, soldiers, and settlers entered the exchange, and Spanish horses, metal tools, and textiles became new trade goods. Trade fairs, notably at Pecos and Taos, brought together Pueblo, Apache, Spanish, and later Comanche and other Plains participants.

Apache peoples were especially important in this early phase. Several Apache groups, including those later known as Plains Apache and Lipan, hunted bison, traded with Pueblo towns, and raided and traded with Spanish settlements. Their access to horses and their mobility linked the Rio Grande to the Plains. Chapter 51 treats Apache histories in full. Other intermediaries included the Jumano, who traveled between the Rio Grande, Texas, and the Plains as traders, and the Wichita-related peoples and Caddo to the east, who linked the southern Plains to the Mississippi Valley and French Louisiana.

These networks help explain why horses moved without a central distributor. A horse obtained at a trade fair could be exchanged with a visiting hunter, given to a relative, or taken in a raid, and each recipient could carry it into a different network. The Spanish authorities who tried to control horse access in New Mexico had little ability to watch this diffusion across hundreds of kilometers of country. It also meant that horse access varied greatly. Communities close to Spanish and Pueblo trade centers generally gained animals earlier, while peoples farther away depended on intermediaries and on the continuing accumulation of herds.

Trade fairs reveal how a regional exchange system operated. At Pecos and Taos, Pueblo communities hosted visitors who arrived with hides, meat, captives, and horses, and who left with maize, textiles, tools, and other goods. Spanish officials sought to regulate and tax some of these exchanges and to take part in them, but they could not control the participants. In later years Comanche and other nations became prominent at such fairs, as Chapter 50 describes.

Captive exchange was part of the picture. Apache and other peoples took and traded captives, and Spanish settlers acquired Indigenous captives for labor, a system treated in Chapter 53. The movement of people along with horses meant that knowledge of animals, languages, and relationships also moved. These relationships were often coercive and violent, and they help explain how tensions along the frontier developed.

The role of Wichita and Caddo peoples shows that southern Plains diffusion did not run only north and east from New Mexico. French traders from Louisiana exchanged goods with these peoples, and horses moved toward the Mississippi Valley and the Gulf, while French goods moved west. The southern Plains were therefore a meeting ground of Spanish and French colonial trade systems as well as an Indigenous exchange zone.

Documentary evidence for the southern Plains becomes denser in the early eighteenth century, but it reflects increasing colonial contact rather than the beginning of the exchange. French officials in Louisiana, including Louis Juchereau de St. Denis at Natchitoches after 1714, traded with Caddo and neighboring peoples, and French expeditions such as that of Étienne de Bourgmont in 1724 sought alliances with Plains nations whom the French called Padouca. The identification is debated: some early uses appear to refer to Plains Apache peoples, while other records use the name for Comanche groups or communities of uncertain identity. Such records show that horses were already important commodities and that Indigenous nations used competing Spanish and French connections to get what they wanted.

The Pecos and Taos fairs deserve particular attention because they linked agricultural, mobile, and colonial economies. Pueblo hosts provided a secure meeting place, and visitors from the Plains brought hides, meat, and captives. Spanish settlers participated, and officials tried to tax and regulate the fairs, but their control was limited. Because Pueblo communities mediated the exchange, they gained access to goods and information, and they also shared the risks of hosting armed visitors.

Wichita-related communities occupy a distinctive place in this system. Their villages on the southern Plains combined agriculture with trade, and their location between Spanish, French, and Plains zones made them valuable partners. Eighteenth-century Spanish and French records describe their towns, their commerce in horses and other goods, and the pressure they later faced from raiding and from the expansion of mobile nations. The horse economy did not benefit all communities equally, even within a single region.

Section 49.11

Ute, Shoshone, and Rocky Mountain Exchange Networks

North of New Mexico, mountain corridors and high basins carried horses toward the interior West. Ute peoples in the southern Rockies and the Colorado Plateau lived close to Spanish New Mexico and had extensive contact with Pueblo and Spanish communities. They were among the earliest Indigenous peoples to use horses extensively, and their trade networks reached northward. Shoshone and related Numic-speaking peoples in the Great Basin and Rocky Mountain region obtained horses through Ute and other intermediaries, and by the first half of the eighteenth century were riding in the northern Rockies and on the northwestern Plains.

Mountain geography shaped the pattern. Passes, river valleys, and high basins offered routes, but also separated communities and made horse access unequal. Communities in grassy valleys could support larger herds, while those in arid or heavily forested country had less forage. Intermediaries who controlled routes and fairs could profit by trading animals northward, and horses acquired in one place often became trade goods in another. Chapter 50 describes how some Numic-speaking peoples who moved onto the southern Plains became the Comanche, so that Shoshone and Comanche histories were linked.

The Shoshone's early advantage had a political meaning. A recollection later recorded from a Piegan elder, discussed in Section 49.13, described Shoshone forces with horses confronting northern peoples who had begun obtaining guns, and indicates that early access to one technology did not guarantee lasting dominance. As more nations acquired horses and firearms, the balance of power shifted. Smallpox epidemics in the later eighteenth century also seriously weakened some communities, a subject treated in Chapter 40 and mentioned again below.

Ute communities illustrate how proximity to Spanish New Mexico shaped early access. Their territory bordered Pueblo and Spanish settlements, and they traded and sometimes fought with them. Ute bands acquired horses, adapted them to mountain and basin conditions, and became active intermediaries. Their relationships with Comanche and Shoshone communities carried horses and equestrian skills to neighbors.

Shoshone communities were widely dispersed across the Great Basin, the Snake River country, and the Rockies. Some, such as groups in the Snake River plain, used horses for hunting and travel and had access to bison country, while others in arid basins used them less. The diversity within Shoshone communities is a reminder that "the Shoshone" includes many bands with different economies and relationships.

Mountains also served as barriers to colonial observation. Spanish, French, and British authorities knew little about the Rockies and Great Basin in the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, and mounted Shoshone and Ute communities appeared in colonial records only when contact increased. This is a clear example of why documentary silence does not mean absence. The 2023 findings in the northern Rockies are a reminder that significant developments occurred outside the written colonial record.

Ute and Shoshone histories are linked by language and by the mountain routes between them, but the evidence differs. For the Ute, Spanish records from New Mexico document contact in the seventeenth century, including trade and raiding, and later records document the alliance between Ute and Comanche communities in the eighteenth century. For the Shoshone, early evidence comes largely from Indigenous memory and from later accounts, including the Saukamappee narrative, and from the archaeological finding of an early horse at American Falls on the Snake River. The Snake River country is Shoshone territory in later documents, though an association of that site with a particular modern nation requires consultation and further evidence.

Later observers confirm the importance of Shoshone horse herds. When the Lewis and Clark expedition reached Shoshone country in 1805, it found a mounted people who traded horses with the explorers and who explained that mountain Shoshone also hunted bison on the plains east of the Rockies. This account dates to the early nineteenth century, so it describes the end of the period examined here, but it shows that the networks reconstructed for the eighteenth century continued to function.

Mountain geography also shaped political vulnerability. Communities in the Snake River plain and Rocky Mountain valleys had grass and winter shelter, while those in the high basins had less. Smallpox, which struck Shoshone communities in the late eighteenth century according to later accounts, and the expansion of Blackfoot-speaking peoples with guns reduced Shoshone power on the northwestern Plains. The pattern shows that early advantage in horses was not permanent.

Section 49.12

The Central Plains: Pawnee, Wichita, Arikara, and Neighboring Nations

On the central Plains, horses were adopted by village-based communities as well as mobile hunters. Pawnee peoples, with their villages on the Platte, Loup, and Republican drainages, acquired horses in the late seventeenth or early eighteenth century, and Wichita-related peoples and Caddo were important in southern pathways. French records of the early eighteenth century describe horses among Plains peoples in the Missouri and lower Platte regions, and French traders from Louisiana exchanged goods with Caddo, Wichita, and Osage communities, whose networks also carried horses.

Horses did not make these communities abandon agriculture. The Pawnee typically planted in spring, left their villages for the summer bison hunt, returned for harvest, and went out again for a winter hunt. Horses lengthened the range of these hunts, made it easier to carry meat and hides home, and increased the volume of goods available for trade. Maize, beans, and squash remained central, and stored harvests continued to provide security against hunting failures.

The central Plains were also a zone of political interaction. In 1720 a Spanish expedition under Pedro de Villasur, sent from New Mexico into the Platte region, was attacked and largely destroyed by Pawnee and Otoe forces near the Platte, according to Spanish accounts of survivors. The episode illustrates that Indigenous nations controlled the central Plains' political geography, that Spanish information about the region was limited, and that horses and firearms were already part of regional power. Chapter 55 discusses Indigenous diplomacy and the balance of power in more detail.

Pawnee society illustrates the interplay between agriculture, hunting, and politics. Pawnee peoples were organized into several related groups, each with villages, leaders, and ceremonial bundles, and they held a calendar that coordinated planting, harvest, and hunts. Horses extended their hunting range and carried more meat and hides home, and they also made trading journeys easier. The Pawnee became important participants in trade with other nations and with colonial traders, and their villages remained political centers.

Wichita villages in the southern Plains also combined agriculture and trade with horses, and their position between Spanish and French spheres made them important intermediaries. Their communities faced pressure from raiding and, later, from the expansion of Comanche and other nations, but they remained significant traders for much of the eighteenth century. Caddoan-speaking Arikara communities to the north likewise combined farming with trade and horses.

The Villasur expedition shows how Spanish officials misunderstood the Plains. The expedition set out from Santa Fe to investigate reports of French activity and Pawnee relations, and it was destroyed by Pawnee and Otoe forces. Spanish officials learned that the central Plains were controlled by well-armed and organized Indigenous nations with their own interests and alliances, and that Spanish influence there was limited.

The Pawnee illustrate how village peoples used horses within a political system. Pawnee peoples lived in several allied divisions, each with its own villages, and their annual cycle combined planting, summer and winter hunts, and the return to villages for harvest. Spanish records and later accounts show that Pawnee communities traded with Spanish and French agents and with other Indigenous nations, and the Villasur defeat of 1720 involved Pawnee and Otoe forces. The Spanish party was destroyed in present-day Nebraska, according to the standard account, and the survivors' reports informed Spanish views of the region for years.

The Villasur episode should not be read as a simple demonstration of horse power. Spanish forces also had horses, and the outcome depended on surprise, numbers, and the allies and information that Pawnee and Otoe leaders could draw on. What it does show is that central Plains nations were able to resist an expedition from New Mexico, and that Spanish authorities could not project power across the plains with a small column. Chapter 55 takes up the diplomacy that followed.

Arikara and Osage evidence adds further variation. Arikara villages on the upper Missouri were agricultural and commercial centers, and their relations with Mandan, Hidatsa, and mobile peoples shaped regional exchange. Osage communities in the Missouri and Arkansas valleys acquired horses in the eighteenth century and used them for hunting and warfare, and their position between French Louisiana and the Plains gave them an influential role. These examples show the central Plains as a set of interacting societies with different relationships to the animal.

Section 49.13

The Northern Plains: Crow, Blackfoot, Lakota, Cheyenne, and Other Peoples

The northern Plains adopted horses later than the south in most accounts, and the evidence for northern chronologies is best treated as approximate. Some peoples encountered horses in the late seventeenth or early eighteenth century, while regular use of horses for hunting and travel is documented more widely from the middle of the eighteenth century. The 2023 evidence for early horses in the northern Rockies and on the Plains means that individual animals may have been present earlier than older accounts supposed, but it does not mean that all northern nations were mounted by 1620.

For the Blackfoot-speaking peoples, an important source is an account recorded by the fur trader and surveyor David Thompson in 1787–1788 from Saukamappee, an elderly man living among the Piegan, who recalled the arrival of horses and guns around 1730. In the account as Thompson recorded it, the Piegan and their allies faced Shoshone forces that already had horses while they themselves were beginning to obtain guns. Historians such as John C. Ewers used this account, along with Blackfoot oral traditions and early trader records, to reconstruct the horse's arrival in Blackfoot country. Crow, Hidatsa, and Shoshone trading relationships carried horses through the northern Rockies and Missouri valley. The Crow, who separated from Hidatsa relatives and came to occupy the upper Yellowstone region, became important horse traders and mounted hunters in the eighteenth century.

For Lakota peoples, Indigenous historical records add a perspective. Lakota communities moved onto the Plains from the east and the Missouri River valley during the eighteenth century, and winter counts, pictographic annual records, indicate when horses became part of Lakota life. North Dakota historical studies, for example, distinguish Lakota familiarity with horses around 1700 from regular horse use by the middle of the eighteenth century. Cheyenne communities also moved west during the eighteenth century and incorporated horses into new economic and political arrangements. Assiniboine and Plains Cree, who traded with the Hudson's Bay Company, also acquired horses in the eighteenth century and entered conflicts and alliances with Blackfoot and other mounted peoples.

The northern Plains illustrate why a single chronology cannot describe a region. A Blackfoot-speaking community on the plains of what is now southern Alberta and northern Montana, a Crow community on the Yellowstone, and a Lakota community near the Missouri had different neighbors, different routes of exchange, and different histories of contact with colonial traders. Each acquired horses within a network that included different intermediaries, and each remembered the event in its own way. The convergence on mounted bison hunting by the middle and later eighteenth century was a result of multiple histories rather than a single movement.

The Crow case shows how kinship and trade could function together. Crow communities originated from a split with Hidatsa relatives on the Missouri and moved west, and they maintained ties with the Missouri villages, where they traded for maize and other goods. In the eighteenth century they also traded with Shoshone communities, and they became intermediaries who moved horses from the Rockies toward Missouri villages. Their geographical position thus gave them influence over trade, and they used it.

Lakota communities were still developing their Plains way of life during the eighteenth century. Their movement onto the Plains involved relationships with other nations, trade with Missouri River villages, and eventually the building of a large equestrian economy. The Lakota example reminds us that equestrian societies were not static; they were still being formed in the very period when colonial observers began to describe them. The detailed history of the Lakota belongs to later chapters, including those on the nineteenth-century Plains wars.

For the northern peoples, the intersection of horses and firearms was significant. The traders who supplied guns, such as those operating from Hudson Bay and later the Saskatchewan River, were connected to Cree and Assiniboine middlemen, and those nations in turn traded with Blackfoot-speaking peoples. The mounted peoples of the northwestern Plains thus obtained horses mostly from the south and west and guns largely from the north and east. Their position between these two streams gave them strategic advantages, and they used them against neighbors.

The sources for the northern Plains differ in kind. Henday's journal comes from the eighteenth century, but his original journal is lost and four surviving versions diverge on important details. Saukamappee's account was recorded by Thompson in 1787–1788, and it recalls events decades earlier. The Long Soldier winter-count entry discussed above records a horse-related episode for the 1801–1802 winter in the Long Soldier count, which belongs to a particular community and keeper. None of these sources provides an independent, community-wide date for first acquisition.

Crow history illustrates how the sources can be combined. Crow people trace separation from Hidatsa relatives, and their later position on the Yellowstone placed them between Missouri River villages and Rocky Mountain peoples. Trader and explorer accounts from the early nineteenth century describe Crow herds and trade, while Crow oral traditions preserve their own understanding of the change. Together they suggest an eighteenth-century rise, but the exact sequence for Crow adoption is less well documented than the general pattern.

Cheyenne evidence shows migration and adaptation. Cheyenne communities, whose earlier agricultural villages on the Missouri are known from archaeology and later accounts, moved westward during the eighteenth century and developed a mounted way of life that combined hunting, trade, and warfare. For Lakota communities, the movement onto the Plains and the adoption of horses were part of a larger reorganization of alliances and territories. Chapters treating later Plains warfare will return to these histories; here the point is that horse adoption was embedded in migration and political change, not a separate technical event.

Lakota Winter Counts as Evidence for Horse Adoption

Creators: Lakota keepers of winter counts · Dates: surviving counts mostly date from the later eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, and some include earlier entries compiled retrospectively · Type: pictographic annual records on hide, muslin, or paper, associated with oral commentary

Context: A winter count names each year, "winter," by a notable event, represented by a pictograph. Keepers used the images to recall the year's events and to recite community history. Several winter counts were collected, copied, and published by outsiders in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

What winter counts can show: They preserve Lakota selections of significant events, including raids, epidemics, treaties, and the acquisition or importance of horses. For example, the Long Soldier winter count, held by the Minneapolis Institute of Art, has an image associated with 1801–1802 that identifies the arrival of horses for the particular community remembered by its keeper. That is not a date for the first horse among all Lakota communities. Comparing counts kept by different families and communities helps establish chronology.

Limitations: The earliest portions of some counts were reconstructed retrospectively; translating years into Gregorian dates requires comparison with other sources, and different keepers remembered different events. The Long Soldier example is a source-specific historical memory from around 1801–1802, not proof that no Lakota person encountered a horse earlier. Students should identify the particular keeper and entry before making a general chronology.

Perspective: The records reflect Lakota priorities and views of their own history, and they are not primarily records of Spanish or French events.

Section 49.14

The Plateau, Great Basin, and Wider Western Horse Networks

Beyond the Plains, horses moved among the peoples of the Columbia Plateau and Great Basin. Nez Perce, Cayuse, Umatilla, Palus, Flathead, and Kootenai communities, and Shoshone and Northern Paiute peoples, obtained horses through Rocky Mountain and Great Basin exchange. The 2023 study's findings from the northern Rockies and Idaho suggest that horses were circulating through these western networks earlier than older accounts allowed, though the evidence for individual nations remains approximate.

Plateau societies incorporated horses into existing seasonal economies. Salmon fishing on the Columbia and its tributaries, root gathering, and trading at fishing sites remained central, but horses made it easier to travel to the Plains for bison, to carry goods to trade fairs, and to maintain political relationships across the Rockies. Nez Perce and Cayuse communities were later known for breeding horses, and the Appaloosa horses of the Nez Perce are one well-known later example, though breeding practices developed over time and should not be projected back to the earliest period. This chapter focuses on exchange networks; later Plateau histories belong in other chapters.

These western networks show that horses moved along lines of Indigenous exchange that cut across the boundaries of what later became colonial claims. Spanish, French, British, and later American authorities knew little of this circulation until they encountered mounted peoples in the field. The narrative of how horses reached the Plains and Plateau is therefore, to a large extent, an Indigenous history that must be reconstructed from Indigenous knowledge, archaeology, and the fragmentary accounts of outsiders.

Plateau peoples had long histories of their own that preceded and shaped their adoption of horses. Salmon fisheries on the Columbia and its tributaries supported substantial populations, trade fairs at fishing sites such as those near the Dalles attracted visitors from far away, and kin ties linked communities along the rivers. Horses fit into this setting as a means of reaching bison country, carrying goods to fairs, and visiting relatives. The Plateau economy was not replaced by horses but extended by them.

Nez Perce oral histories and later accounts describe horses as an important part of community life, and Nez Perce herds and breeding practices became well known to outsiders in the nineteenth century. Earlier chapters on the Plateau, such as Chapter 31, discuss the region's peoples before horses. For this chapter, the key point is that the Plateau, like the Plains, was part of a western network through which horses and horse knowledge circulated, and that Plateau nations were active participants in it.

The Great Basin presents a different pattern. Many Basin communities lived in arid country with limited pasture, and horses were useful but harder to maintain. Northern Shoshone and Bannock groups with access to grassy valleys and bison country used horses extensively, while groups in drier areas used them less or became targets of raiding. The history of the Basin in this period therefore shows horse-based inequality between communities, with ecological conditions as one important factor.

Plateau evidence has an important later anchor. When the Lewis and Clark expedition met the Nez Perce in 1805, it encountered communities with substantial horse herds and with established patterns of travel across the Rockies to hunt bison. This observation is from the early nineteenth century, so it cannot date adoption, but it indicates that the horse economy of the Plateau was well established. Nez Perce oral histories place the arrival of horses in earlier generations and emphasize their integration into seasonal life.

Cayuse and other Plateau communities also developed reputations in the nineteenth century as horse breeders and traders, and Salish-speaking communities east of the Cascades participated in similar networks. The Columbia River fisheries remained a centerpiece of the economy. Horses added a means to bring dried salmon and other goods to the Plains and to carry back bison products, and they linked the Plateau to Plains trade fairs and to the Missouri River. The significance of horses for Plateau peoples therefore lay in expanding exchange rather than replacing the fisheries.

Section 49.15

Learning to Ride, Train, Breed, and Manage Horses

Horsemanship was a body of knowledge that Indigenous communities had to build. The animals that reached the interior were Iberian-derived horses, but their management in Plains winters and grassland ecosystems required adaptations that Spanish ranching practice did not supply. Indigenous people learned from Spanish and Pueblo herders, from neighboring nations who already had horses, and from their own experience of what worked. They developed methods of catching, haltering, hobbling, and gentling animals, of training young horses for riding, packing, or hunting, and of choosing which animals to ride, breed, geld, trade, or keep as herd members.

Breeding decisions mattered. Stallions, geldings, and mares had different roles. Many communities kept most males as geldings for riding and hunting, since geldings were generally easier to manage, and maintained a smaller number of stallions for breeding. Specially trained buffalo runners, fast and responsive animals that could carry a rider close to bison and turn quickly, were highly valued and cared for. Pack animals and general riding horses were used for other work. Over generations, some nations developed reputations for particular horses or breeding practices, and horse quality became one of the things communities traded and judged.

Knowledge was learned by doing and passed on through families and communities. Experienced riders taught younger ones, specialists trained animals, and trade and kinship carried techniques from one nation to another. The ability to manage horses was thus a skill in which Indigenous nations invested, and the differences in that skill helped create differences in prosperity between households and communities.

Several features of Spanish colonial horse culture influenced Indigenous practice, but Indigenous people modified them. Spanish horsemen used heavy saddles and metal bits, and trained horses in specific styles. Indigenous riders adopted some of these techniques but often used lighter equipment, rawhide bridles, and different methods suited to hunting bison. The adaptation of techniques to Plains conditions is a clear example of selective borrowing: communities kept what worked and changed what did not.

Training methods varied. Some communities trained horses gradually, using patience and handling from a young age, while others used more forceful methods. Observers in the nineteenth century described a variety of practices, and their descriptions often reflected their own assumptions about Indigenous people. Historians should therefore treat such descriptions cautiously and look for Indigenous accounts of training practices.

The importance of breeding is also visible in the language of value. Horses of particular colors or characteristics were prized, and communities developed preferences. Some nations became known for their horses and traded them widely. The qualities that made a good buffalo runner or pack animal were learned through experience and shared through exchange. In this way, horse knowledge became a form of cultural capital that gave certain families and nations an edge.

Ewers's study of Blackfoot horse culture, based on interviews and documents from the mid-twentieth century and earlier sources, is the fullest account of how one northern Plains people categorized horses. Informants distinguished animals by use, such as buffalo runners, war horses, and pack animals, and they described the selection and training of each. Because the interviews were conducted with elders in the 1940s and 1950s about earlier decades, they illustrate nineteenth-century practices and memory of earlier generations rather than the first adoption. They remain valuable because they preserve Indigenous categories and explanations.

The Hidatsa evidence recorded by Gilbert Wilson from Buffalo Bird Woman and other informants in the early twentieth century offers a village perspective. Her accounts describe the care of horses within a farming household and the work required of women, men, and children. Like Ewers's material, they describe a later period, and they are most useful for showing how a village economy could incorporate horses. Historians combine such sources with earlier trader accounts and archaeological evidence, noting each source's date and community.

Historical Vocabulary
Herd, Breeding, Grazing, and Equestrian Mobility

Definition. A herd is a group of horses managed together by a household, band, or community. Breeding is the controlled reproduction of animals. Grazing is the feeding of animals on pasture, and equestrian mobility is the increased range, speed, and carrying capacity that horses gave to travel and transport.

Historical context. These terms describe the practical conditions of horse economies. A community with large herds needed grazing land, water, labor to protect and move animals, and knowledge of breeding and winter survival.

Why it matters. Equestrian mobility was not free; it depended on herd management, and herd management shaped where people lived, traveled, and traded.

Potential misunderstanding. "Wild horses" in later accounts often refers to feral animals descended from domestic stock, not to a native wild population. Free-roaming horses do not by themselves tell us who owned their ancestors.

Section 49.16

Maintaining Herds: Water, Grazing, Winter Feed, and Protection

Keeping horses alive and productive required continuing labor and attention. Herds needed pasture in summer, water throughout the year, and protection from predators, accidents, disease, and theft. Winter was the main problem. On the northern Plains, snow and cold could cover forage and cause large losses, and communities adopted measures such as moving herds to sheltered river bottoms where grass and trees were available. Observers in the nineteenth century recorded that some northern communities, including Missouri River villagers and Crow, fed horses the inner bark and branches of cottonwood trees in hard winters, a practice that was labor intensive and that may reflect a longer tradition, though the evidence for its early date is limited.

Herds also required defense. Horses were valuable and portable, and communities guarded them from raiders. Village-based peoples sometimes kept valuable horses close to lodges at night, while mobile groups arranged herders and watchers. Children, adolescents, and adults all participated, and responsibility for protecting herds shaped daily routines. These were ordinary tasks of a horse economy and were as important as better-known hunting and warfare activities.

The costs of horses meant that a large herd was an investment and a risk. Families with many animals had to commit labor, place camps near grazing, and accept the possibility that a hard winter, a raid, or a disease could erase years of work. Understanding that cost makes it easier to see why horses were a form of wealth that required ongoing management rather than a simple technological advantage.

Winter was both a challenge and an opportunity for organization. Many communities moved to winter camps in river valleys with shelter and wood, and they arranged herds to graze on available grass, often at some distance from camps. In severe winters they had to find extra feed or accept losses. The decision about when and where to move involved weighing the needs of humans and animals and the risks of attack or scarcity.

Water was a constant concern, especially in dry seasons and in the southern Plains where drought could dry streams and springs. Communities that knew reliable springs and rivers had an advantage, and they defended such sites. Knowledge of water sources was a form of expertise that was shared among relatives and allies. Access to water also shaped trade routes and encounters, since travelers needed to pass by dependable sources.

Disease and injury demanded care. Communities treated animals with plant medicines and techniques, and specialists with particular knowledge were respected. Such expertise was learned over generations and was an important part of the horse economy. Records of these practices are limited and mostly late, but they show that horse care was a developed body of Indigenous knowledge.

The Hidatsa and Mandan accounts recorded by Wilson and by later ethnographers include detailed descriptions of winter care. Horses were kept in or near the village in the coldest months, fed with cottonwood boughs and bark cut by household members, and brought out to graze when conditions permitted. These practices demanded labor from the whole household, and cutting and hauling cottonwood was a cost that did not exist for households without horses. They show how a village economy absorbed the work of horse care within its existing seasonal organization.

Mobile peoples faced different problems. Edwin Denig and other traders who wrote about the Assiniboine and Crow in the mid-nineteenth century described how herds were moved to sheltered valleys in winter and how heavy snow could cause losses. Those sources are later than this chapter's main period and reflect trader viewpoints, but they agree with Indigenous accounts that winter management was among the most important determinants of a herd's survival. Historians infer that similar constraints applied earlier, while recognizing that the scale of herds and settlement patterns changed.

Everyday Life and Social History

Caring for Horses in a Plains Community

A horse-owning community organized much of its day around the animals. Herds were driven to water and grass in the morning and brought back or watched in the evening, newborn foals needed protection, injured animals needed treatment, and winter required moving to places with shelter and feed. Boys and girls took part in herding, and adults managed larger movements and breeding. When enemies or thieves threatened, herd defense became everyone's concern.

This account is a composite reconstruction based on later ethnographic and trader sources and on archaeological evidence, and it should not be taken as a description of a specific community in a specific year. Practices varied among nations, regions, and periods.

Section 49.17

Saddles, Bridles, Ropes, and the Transformation of the Travois

Indigenous equestrian equipment was adapted from Spanish models and from Indigenous technology and materials. Saddles were made of wooden frames covered with rawhide, or of padded forms, and varied among nations and uses. Women's saddles, hunting saddles, and pack saddles differed in design. Bridles, bits, and halters were made of rawhide, braided hair, and, where available through trade, metal. Ropes and lariats of rawhide and hair were used to catch, tether, and lead animals. Each of these items required materials, craft skill, and time, and their quality could affect an animal's performance and comfort.

Transportation changed as well. The travois, long used with dogs, was enlarged and adapted for horses. A horse travois consisted of two long poles, tied across the animal's shoulders and trailing behind, with a platform or net carrying the load. A horse could carry or drag much heavier loads than a dog and could do so over longer distances. Estimates of the loads carried vary by source and animal, and should be treated as approximations, but ethnographic accounts consistently describe a large increase over dog transport. This made possible larger lodge covers, more household goods, and the movement of children, elders, and the sick.

The materials used in equipment came from Indigenous economies. Bison and elk hides provided rawhide for saddles and bridles, sinew was used for sewing and binding, and wood from local trees was used for saddle frames and travois poles. The production of equipment drew on women's skills in hide processing and on craft specialists. The increasing demand for equipment spurred production and exchange within communities.

Decoration and design also developed. Saddles, bridles, and horse gear were decorated with beads, quills, paint, and other ornaments, and they expressed the identity and status of owners. Much of the surviving decorated gear in museums dates from the later nineteenth century and reflects trade goods such as glass beads, which were not available earlier. This is a good example of why students must be careful with dating objects. Earlier gear would have been plainer or decorated with materials such as porcupine quills, and little of it has survived.

The design of travois also reflected practical engineering. The length of poles, the point of attachment, and the platform design affected stability and the comfort of the animal and the load. Different nations used different designs suited to terrain, and some designs allowed people, such as children or the elderly, to be carried. These adaptations illustrate that Indigenous technology was not static but responded to new conditions.

Dogs remained part of the transport system long after horses arrived. Buffalo Bird Woman's accounts describe dogs hauling loads in Hidatsa households in the nineteenth century, and other ethnographic sources describe continued use of dogs for light loads and short trips. The persistence of dog travois shows that horses did not replace dogs everywhere or immediately. It also shows that households used different animals for different tasks, depending on distance, load, and the number of horses available.

The development of horse gear is difficult to trace in the earliest period because organic materials rarely survive. Rawhide, wood, and hair decay, and most museum collections date from the nineteenth century when trade goods were available. Historians therefore rely on later objects, ethnographic descriptions, and a few early visual and written sources, and they note when equipment may reflect later technology. The central inference, that Indigenous communities adapted riding and packing equipment to their own materials and needs, is well supported, while specific designs for the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries are not.

Section 49.18

Mounted Bison Hunting and the Reorganization of Subsistence

For many nations, mounted bison hunting became the most visible change. A rider on a trained horse could approach a herd, select animals, and kill them with bow, lance, and, later, firearms, and could pursue animals across greater distances than a hunter on foot. Mounted hunters could hunt more flexibly in different seasons and places, and they could bring home more meat and hides, especially when a community had adequate numbers of skilled riders and trained animals. Hides and robes could be produced in quantities for trade, which increased the importance of processing labor in the household.

The older methods did not disappear immediately. Communal drives, jumps, pounds, and surrounds continued in many places, in some cases alongside mounted hunting, and offered advantages when the target was a large group of animals. Some communities remained mostly pedestrian hunters, or used horses mainly for transport and for supplementary hunts. Indeed, mounted hunting had risks: horses and riders could be injured or killed in chases, and success depended on skilled animals and riders.

The mounted hunt required both skill and organization. Hunters on trained horses approached herds, often downwind, and selected animals to kill. A skilled rider could kill several animals in a short chase, but the work was dangerous, and falls and gorings could injure or kill riders and horses. After the hunt, butchering, transporting, and processing meat and hides involved large numbers of people, mostly women, and it had to be done quickly before spoilage.

Communities also regulated hunting. Many Plains peoples had hunt societies or leaders who organized the hunt, prevented individuals from scattering the herd before the group was ready, and enforced rules. These institutions had earlier roots in communal drives and were adapted to mounted hunts. They show that equestrian hunting was a collective enterprise governed by social rules rather than a purely individual pursuit.

The shift to mounted hunting increased the production of hides for trade, and some communities specialized in producing robes for exchange with traders and neighboring peoples. This commercial dimension gave women's labor an economic significance that could extend beyond the household. The long-term implications of a commercial robe economy for bison populations and communities are discussed in Chapter 81.

The chronology of mounted hunting is clearer for the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries than for the early period. Trader accounts, including Henday's, describe mounted hunting on the northern Plains in the mid-eighteenth century, and Indigenous recollections place its development in earlier generations. Archaeological evidence from bison kill sites shows that communal drives and jumps continued for long periods, and the persistence of such sites alongside mounted hunting indicates that communities used different methods in different circumstances.

Processing was an essential part of the system. A successful kill produced large quantities of meat, hides, and other materials that had to be butchered, dried, and transported quickly. Ethnographic accounts from several Plains nations emphasize women's skilled and heavy labor in these tasks, and note that increased kills required more hands. Hides intended for trade required additional work, and women's expertise in tanning and robe making determined the quality of the products. The expansion of mounted hunting therefore depended on household organization as much as on riders.

Section 49.19

Mobility, Seasonal Camps, and the Movement of Households

Horses altered the scale and rhythm of residential movement. Where dogs and human carrying limited what could be moved, horses allowed families to carry larger lodges, more equipment, and more food, and to travel farther between camps. Some communities increased the distance of seasonal rounds, moved more frequently to follow bison and grass, and gathered in larger groups at certain times of year for ceremonies, hunts, and trade. Others moved less than before because horses made it easier to bring supplies from a distance.

These changes had implications for family life. Childcare, the care of the elderly, and the transport of the ill were easier when animals could carry them. Women, who managed much household transport and camp setup, took on new tasks of horse handling and packing, and had greater capacity to transport goods. Larger camps could create new organizational challenges, such as managing grazing around camp, and could increase tensions over access to pasture and hunting grounds.

The geography of movement also connected distant peoples. Riders could reach trade fairs, relatives, and ceremonial gatherings that had been less accessible, and communities could respond more quickly to threats. Mobility therefore increased contacts and conflicts alike. Regional differences remained: communities in rugged mountain country, dense river bottoms, or arid basins experienced mobility differently from those on open plains.

The scale of seasonal movement differed among communities. Some northern and central Plains peoples made long circuits between winter river valleys, spring camps, and summer gathering places, while others, particularly village-based communities, made shorter trips from fixed bases. In both cases, horses allowed greater flexibility: families could respond to the movement of herds or to threats and could extend visits to relatives and allies.

Large summer gatherings became more feasible. When many bands came together for ceremonies, trading, and political discussions, horses carried the necessary goods and supported the large camp. Such gatherings strengthened alliances and provided occasions for marriages and exchange, and they were an important part of political life. They also required organization to manage grazing and hunting around the camp, and communities developed rules and leaders for this purpose.

Movement also had costs. Longer journeys exposed travelers to hazards, including attack, bad weather, and food shortages, and families had to plan carefully. The ability to travel farther could also increase tensions between neighbors when hunting grounds overlapped. These dynamics help explain the growth of both alliance and conflict in the eighteenth-century Plains.

Residential patterns varied by nation and period. Village peoples such as the Mandan, Hidatsa, and Pawnee maintained permanent villages and left them for seasonal hunts, while peoples such as the Cheyenne and Lakota developed a more mobile pattern in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Even within the same nation, communities adjusted their movement to ecological conditions and to threats. Written accounts describing large camps and long journeys come mostly from the nineteenth century, and they should be read as evidence of what horse economies could become rather than of their earlier forms.

Travel also brought communities into regular contact with each other. Trade fairs, ceremonies, and council gatherings required travel, and the ability to move carrying capacity enabled communities to host or attend them. The Mandan–Hidatsa villages were nodes in such networks, and communities came from long distances to trade there. The resulting contacts could be peaceful or hostile, and the same mobility that supported alliance could also support raiding.

Section 49.20

Horses and the Continuing Importance of Agricultural Villages

The popular picture of Plains horse culture often leaves out farming villages, but agricultural peoples were among the most important participants in the horse economy. Mandan, Hidatsa, Arikara, Pawnee, and Wichita communities incorporated horses without giving up their gardens. National Park Service material on the Missouri River villages describes how horses increased the transportation and hunting opportunities of village peoples while agricultural settlements continued.

The Missouri River villages, with their large earth lodges, maize storage, and trade centers, were hubs for exchange between agricultural and mobile peoples. Village women grew maize, beans, squash, and sunflowers, and stored surpluses, which they exchanged with hunting peoples for meat, hides, and other goods, and, increasingly, for horses. Village men and women used horses for hunting trips and for hauling meat and goods back to village storage. Mounted hunting parties might leave in summer and winter while the villages stayed in place. This pattern combined the stability and storage capacity of agriculture with the mobility of horses.

Villages also took on new roles as markets and as targets. Because they held stored food and trade goods, they attracted visitors and raiders, and their defenses, including palisades and fortifications, remained important. Epidemics, especially the smallpox epidemics that struck Missouri River peoples in the late eighteenth century, hit densely settled villages severely. The village-and-horse combination was therefore adaptable but not secure.

The Missouri River villages offer a clear case study of adaptation. The villages, with populations that were substantial for the Plains, were built on terraces above the river with large earth lodges, fortified in many cases, and surrounded by gardens in the bottomlands. Women cultivated multiple varieties of maize, beans, squash, and sunflowers and stored them in underground caches. The villages' position at the junction of routes connecting the northern and southern Plains made them natural trading centers.

The interaction between villagers and visiting hunters was mutually beneficial but also unequal at times. Visiting groups traded meat and hides for maize and other goods, and the villages' organization provided hospitality and political relationships. Horses enabled visitors to come from farther away and to carry more goods, and villages could also send trading expeditions out. At the same time, villages faced attacks from some visiting groups and had to maintain defenses and diplomacy.

Epidemics hit the villages hard. The smallpox epidemic that spread through the northern Plains in the early 1780s caused severe mortality among Missouri River villagers, and its effects on village populations and regional power are discussed in Chapter 40 and in later chapters. After the epidemic, surviving communities consolidated and moved. The villages' history shows that the success of the horse-and-agriculture combination was real but contingent on factors beyond community control.

The Knife River villages in present-day North Dakota, occupied by Hidatsa and Mandan communities, are among the best documented village centers. La Vérendrye visited Mandan villages on the Missouri in 1738–1739, and later traders and explorers described villages of earth lodges, extensive gardens, and busy trade. Archaeological work at the villages, supported by National Park Service stewardship, has documented the houses, storage pits, and cultivated areas that anchored the economy. These sources collectively show that agricultural production and trade continued to be central through the eighteenth century.

Hidatsa accounts recorded by Wilson describe the household division of labor in gardening, including women's ownership and management of fields and seed. This agricultural base explains why villages could support trade and why mobile visitors valued them as partners. Horses entered this system as means of transport and hunting, and the villages' gardens and storage remained the foundation of food security. The mixed economy was thus resilient but also dependent on peace and health.

The 1781 smallpox epidemic that struck Mandan and other village communities, documented in later accounts and National Park Service histories, showed the vulnerability of dense settlements. Mandan communities reorganized after severe losses, and similar disruptions affected neighbors. The epidemic did not end village agriculture or trade, but it reduced populations and changed alliances. Avoiding later demographic figures for this earlier event keeps the account within what the sources support.

Section 49.21

Horses as Commodities, Diplomatic Gifts, and Instruments of Exchange

Horses were both goods and relationships. They were traded for hides, food, tools, and other valuables, and they were given as gifts to cement alliances, mark marriages, honor guests, and settle disputes. Trade fairs and village markets became places where animals changed hands, and the movement of horses from sources of supply toward areas of demand shaped regional exchange. Missouri River villages, Rio Grande Pueblo towns, and the fairs of the southern Plains were important meeting points, and the commerce in horses was linked to trade in robes, maize, metal goods, and, later, firearms.

Gifts mattered because horses were valuable and living property. A gift of horses created obligations of reciprocity and could signal respect, alliance, or the offer of peace. Accepting or refusing such a gift was a political act. Traders from outside the region noticed these practices and often described them through the lens of their own commercial interests. Indigenous trading partners, for their part, evaluated European traders by what they could provide and whether the trade served their purposes, and several of the sources discussed below show that mounted peoples did not simply adapt to colonial commerce on colonial terms.

The distinction between a gift and a commodity was not always sharp. A horse given to an ally might be understood as a gift while also serving as part of an exchange in which the giver expected a return, in the form of goods, support, or loyalty. Observers who saw such transactions often described them as trade, but Indigenous participants may have understood them in terms of relationship and reciprocity. This is why historians must read trader and colonial accounts with caution.

Prices and values of horses varied widely depending on place, time, and the animal's qualities, and surviving records provide only scattered information. Rather than reconstruct precise price series, which the evidence does not support, historians generally describe the pattern: horses were more valuable near the edges of the supply zones, where they were scarcer, and a horse that moved north or east through several exchanges could be worth a great deal more to its final owner than to its first seller. This gradient created incentives for intermediaries.

Trade in horses also connected to trade in captives and in firearms. As noted above, nations with more access to guns and horses had advantages over those with less, and exchange patterns shaped the military balance. Trade could therefore be both a source of alliance and a source of rivalry, and it contributed to the shifting geography discussed below.

Missouri River trade fairs provide some of the clearest evidence of how horses circulated commercially. Visitors from the west and south brought horses and other goods to the Mandan and Hidatsa villages, and returned with maize and trade goods obtained from traders on the river and from the north. Observers in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries described such exchanges, and Crow people in particular were reported as suppliers of horses from the Rockies. These accounts show a pattern of exchange in which horses flowed from areas of supply toward areas with more demand.

The role of intermediaries affected who benefited. Communities that controlled routes or hosted fairs could obtain goods, collect gifts, and influence terms. Crow, Hidatsa, Mandan, Arikara, Ute, Comanche, and others each served as intermediaries in different times and places. Historians therefore describe the system as a network rather than as a linear chain, and they note that Indigenous merchants and diplomats operated by their own rules and interests.

Section 49.22

Horse Wealth, Political Influence, and Unequal Access

Horses were a form of wealth, but how that wealth worked differed among nations. Owning many horses could provide material security, military capability, and the ability to give generously, trade, and support followers. Households with many animals could host guests, lend horses to relatives, and participate in ceremonies and exchanges. Those with few animals depended more on others and were more vulnerable to loss. Differences in access also existed between communities: those close to sources of supply or with good pasture could build herds more easily than those far away or short of forage.

Horse wealth did not automatically produce political authority. In many Plains societies, leadership depended on reputation for generosity, wisdom, bravery, and ability to hold followers, and wealth was expected to be shared. A wealthy man or woman who did not give could lose standing. Horses could be loaned, gifted, or redistributed, and communities developed norms to limit hoarding. Historians therefore have to avoid treating horses as a simple measure of rank, and should consider how each nation's social institutions shaped their meaning.

Wealth could also be used for social purposes. In several nations, wealthy individuals gave horses away at ceremonies, in honor of relatives, or to help those in need, and the giving was a source of prestige. A person known for generosity might have many followers and allies. Such customs show how Indigenous values shaped the uses of horse wealth and limited its accumulation.

Women's and children's roles in wealth should also be considered. In some societies women owned horses and could give them away or inherit them, and children received horses as gifts. The distribution of ownership within a household affected how wealth was used and who had a say in decisions. Historians should therefore avoid treating horse wealth as simply a male attribute.

Unequal access between communities mattered at the regional level. Nations with large herds could trade horses to those with fewer and gain political advantages, while those who had few horses might find themselves vulnerable to raids and to competition for hunting grounds. The combination of internal and external inequalities contributed to the shifting power relationships of the eighteenth century.

Ewers's Blackfoot material provides a detailed picture of horse wealth, though it comes from later informants. According to his sources, individuals with many horses could lend animals to relatives, support followers, and take part in ceremonial exchanges, and generosity increased standing. Poor families might own few animals and depend on relatives. These accounts describe a society in which wealth and generosity were intertwined, and they caution against assuming that wealth alone conferred authority.

Comparative evidence from other nations suggests that patterns varied. Among village peoples, wealth was also tied to land and stored food managed largely by women, so horses were one form of wealth among several. Among mobile peoples, horses might be the main portable wealth, and the loss of a herd to raiding or winter could be disastrous. Because the evidence for each nation comes from different periods, historians describe these differences cautiously.

Historical Debate

Horse Wealth, Prosperity, and Inequality

The question: Did horses mainly create greater inequality and hierarchy within Plains societies, or did social norms of generosity keep wealth in circulation?

Interpretation A — Inequality increased: Ethnographers such as John C. Ewers described differences between horse-rich and horse-poor households in Blackfoot society, and some scholars have emphasized the dependence of poorer families on richer ones and the uneven distribution of animals between communities.
Interpretation B — Norms limited accumulation: Others emphasize that expectations of generosity, loaning, gift-giving, and redistribution restrained hoarding, and that status derived from giving rather than keeping.
Interpretation C — Variation across nations: A further view stresses that societies differed in how they treated property and leadership, so no single model applies.

Section 49.23

Horse Raiding, Herd Defense, and the Economics of Conflict

Horses made raiding a distinct and important form of conflict. A raid to take horses was a relatively low-risk way to increase a herd, damage an enemy, and earn prestige for young men, and horse raiding became a recognized part of political and military life in many nations. Raiding parties could travel long distances, take animals quietly, and withdraw before pursuit. Success could bring wealth to the raiders and their families, while loss could harm victims for years.

Raiding was not identical to war. Horse raids differed from conflicts over territory, retaliation for killings, the taking of captives, or attacks on villages, although these forms of violence often overlapped and escalated. Communities responded with herd guards, protected camps, and retaliation, and raids could trigger feuds or alliances. Where nations had established relationships, they sometimes agreed to peace or exchanged gifts of horses to settle conflicts. Raiding also affected Spanish, French, and later American settlements, whose herds were targets, and colonial records of such raiding are an important source, though they reflect colonial views of Indigenous violence.

Raiding created risks as well as opportunities. Families lost animals, communities lost members, and the cycle of retaliation could spread. Raiding also fed the economics of captivity and exchange, subjects treated in Chapter 53. In some regions, horse raiding contributed to long-running conflicts that reshaped alliances and settlements.

Young men's participation in raids was tied to social recognition. In many nations, success in obtaining horses brought prestige and the ability to provide gifts, and raiding parties were often led by experienced men who organized the expedition. Such expeditions followed customs of preparation, travel, and return, and the distribution of captured animals was governed by norms of sharing. These practices linked individual ambition to communal values.

Defensive strategies included the use of guards, the placing of horses in protected sites, and alliances. Villages built corrals or kept horses near lodges at night, and mobile camps posted watches. Retaliation was also common: a community that lost horses might send a party to take them back or to punish raiders. Cycles of raiding and retaliation could last for years and could escalate into larger conflicts.

Colonial settlements suffered from raiding as well. Spanish ranches and missions lost animals, and officials responded with military expeditions and efforts to regulate trade. These responses often failed to stop raids and sometimes provoked further conflict. Colonial sources record such events from the viewpoint of victims, and Indigenous accounts offer other perspectives, in which raiding might be understood as part of a political relationship, a response to Spanish violence, or a way of obtaining needed resources.

Spanish and French documents provide many accounts of raids on settlements, and they reflect colonial concerns. Spanish officials in New Mexico wrote repeatedly about losses of livestock, and in the eighteenth century about the activities of Comanche, Apache, and Ute raiders. Because these documents were written to request help or justify campaigns, they emphasize the damage done, and they rarely explain Indigenous aims. Indigenous accounts, where they survive, frame raiding in terms of relations among nations, retaliation, and obligations.

The social function of raiding is better documented for the nineteenth century. Ethnographic accounts from several Plains nations describe young men seeking horses, the preparation of raiding parties, and the distribution of the spoils. These accounts illustrate norms that may have existed earlier, but they cannot be assumed to describe the seventeenth century. What the evidence does support is that horses became a prime target of conflict as they became valuable, and that communities responded with defense and retaliation.

Section 49.24

Mounted Warfare and the Transformation of Military Strategy

Horses transformed the possibilities of war. Mounted forces could scout wide areas, concentrate quickly, strike at distant targets, pursue retreating enemies, and withdraw before reinforcements arrived. They could carry supplies and wounded warriors and move whole families away from danger. For nations with large herds and skilled riders, these capacities enlarged the range of strategic choices, and the shifting balance of power in the eighteenth century reflected those capacities.

Horses did not make war simple or uniformly decisive. Terrain, season, forage, and the availability of firearms shaped outcomes, and pedestrian fighters remained important, especially in forests, villages, and rough country. Fortified villages such as those of the Mandan, Arikara, and Pawnee continued to be formidable targets, and defenders could use their familiarity with terrain and stored supplies. Firearms spread in parallel with horses. The Saukamappee account recorded by David Thompson associates the arrival of horses and guns among the Piegan and their allies with a period of warfare against the Shoshone, and shows that the relative advantages of each technology shifted with who had access to it.

The 1720 destruction of the Villasur expedition illustrates that Plains nations could defeat Spanish forces, even though Spanish forces had horses and firearms. The outcome depended on local knowledge, alliances, and surprise. Mounted warfare was thus one part of a broader system in which diplomacy, ecology, and demographic capacity also mattered.

Firearms complicated the picture. They were expensive, required powder and shot, and were not always more effective than bows in the hands of skilled mounted hunters and warriors, especially when used from horseback. Nonetheless, they gave advantages in certain situations, and their distribution through trade networks shaped regional power. Nations with access to both guns and horses were generally better placed than those with neither, but the details varied by place and period.

Military organization was also changing. Many Plains nations had warrior societies, leaders chosen for particular expeditions, and councils that made decisions about war and peace. Such institutions adapted to mounted warfare by developing tactics, signals, and training. Evidence for the details is largely from the nineteenth century, so historians must be careful about projecting later practices backward.

Defensive warfare remained significant. Fortified villages, mobile camps with scouts, and the use of terrain were important. Horses could also be a liability in defense: a camp full of horses could be a target, and a loss of animals could cripple a community's mobility. The relationship between horses and war was therefore double-edged, offering both power and vulnerability.

Military logistics shaped what horses could do. A mounted war party needed animals in good condition, forage along its route, and knowledge of water, and long campaigns strained herds. In winter, forage shortages limited operations, and in summer, drought and heat did. These constraints help explain why campaigns often followed seasonal patterns and why communities sought allies with access to pasture and water.

The Villasur defeat and later episodes show that Plains nations could organize coordinated action against outside forces, but they also show that campaigns were limited by supply and politics. Fortified villages offered security, and attackers rarely had the means to besiege them for long. Mounted warfare therefore expanded the range of actions available to Indigenous nations without removing the importance of terrain, defense, and alliance.

Section 49.25

Equestrian Power and the Reorganization of Plains Geopolitics

By the middle of the eighteenth century, horses and associated trade, firearms, and alliances had changed the political geography of the Plains and surrounding regions. In the south, Comanche communities, with allies such as the Ute, expanded onto the southern Plains and altered relationships with Apache, Wichita, and Spanish settlements, a history examined in Chapters 50 and 51. In the northwest, Shoshone communities were important early horse users but later faced competitors who gained horses and guns, and epidemic disease in the late eighteenth century damaged them. On the northern Plains, Crow, Blackfoot-speaking peoples, Cheyenne, Lakota, and others expanded, contracted, and reorganized their territories and alliances in response to horses, trade, and conflict.

These shifts did not follow a simple pattern. Power changed hands, alliances formed and dissolved, and villages, mobile bands, and trading centers all played roles. Some nations expanded their hunting territory and trade reach, others were pushed to the margins or forced to adapt, and village peoples continued as trading centers while facing new threats. Colonial powers were part of this geography, as suppliers of goods and as political actors, but they did not control it.

Colonial authorities observed these changes with concern and, in some cases, with efforts to adapt. Spanish officials in New Mexico and Texas saw the rise of powerful mounted nations and tried to make alliances or trade arrangements with them. French officials in Louisiana sought partners on the Plains. British traders in the north tried to draw mounted nations into the fur trade. In each case, colonial officials had to negotiate with Indigenous powers and could not simply impose their will.

The expansion of some nations came at the expense of others. Shifts such as the movement of Comanche communities onto the southern Plains and of Lakota communities westward involved displacement, conflict, and alliance. The Apache, who had held territory on the southern Plains, faced pressure and moved or adapted, as Chapter 51 explains. These movements illustrate that the Plains were a dynamic political landscape.

At the same time, many nations preserved autonomy through alliances, diplomacy, and adaptation. Village peoples maintained their positions through trade and defense, and mobile nations maintained theirs through mobility and military capacity. The overall pattern was a multipolar system in which no single nation or colonial power dominated, and this pattern is the setting for the diplomatic institutions discussed in Chapter 55.

The reorganization of Plains geopolitics involved several overlapping processes. Some nations moved onto the Plains from adjacent regions, some expanded their ranges, and some were displaced. The Lakota moved westward from the Missouri region, Cheyenne communities shifted onto the high plains, and Crow communities established themselves along the Yellowstone. In the south, Comanche movement onto the plains changed relationships with Apache and Wichita peoples. These movements were influenced by access to horses, trade, and guns, as well as by climate, conflict, and demographic change.

Interpretation requires caution. Historians describe the eighteenth century as a time of shifting balances, but the details are contested, and documentary coverage varies by region. Chapters 50 and 51 narrate the Comanche and Apache cases, and Chapter 55 examines the diplomacy that connected these nations to colonial powers. Here the principal point is that horses contributed to a more fluid political geography in which Indigenous nations negotiated from positions of real strength.

Section 49.26

Women, Horse-Related Labor, and the Household Economy

Women were central to equestrian economies. In many Plains nations they were responsible for setting up and taking down lodges, packing and moving household goods, processing hides and meat, preparing food, and caring for children and the elderly. Horses changed these tasks. A horse travois and pack animals made it possible to move larger households and heavier loads, and increased bison kills meant more meat and hides to process. The labor of dressing robes for trade was substantial, and the growth of robe production increased the demand for women's skilled work.

Women also took part in horse care, though the pattern differed among nations. In some communities women owned horses, rode, packed, and managed animals for their households, and in some Plateau and Plains societies women could hold significant herds. In others, men held most horse wealth and made most decisions about breeding and trade. Because later sources often reflected the observers' assumptions about gender, historians have to read them critically and avoid assuming identical divisions of labor across all Plains nations.

The effect on women's status was therefore mixed. Greater mobility and more goods could create opportunities, such as expanded trade in hides and greater control over household resources, while the increased workload, especially in hide processing, could be burdensome. Some marriage practices, discussed below, also changed as horses became part of exchanges. The history of horses is not complete without women's labor.

The sources for women's history in this period are limited and uneven. Colonial and trader accounts were written mostly by men who often saw women's work only partially, and ethnographic accounts from the nineteenth and twentieth centuries relied on informants and observers with their own perspectives. Some of the most valuable evidence comes from women's own accounts recorded in later oral histories and from material culture such as hide-working tools, equipment, and clothing. Historians increasingly combine these sources to recover women's roles.

Hide processing deserves special attention because it was both a major labor and a major source of economic value. Preparing a robe or lodge cover required scraping, soaking, softening, and often smoking and decorating hides, a process that could take days and required specialized skill. Women who excelled at the work gained respect and could produce goods for trade. The expansion of bison hunting and trade in robes therefore increased the economic importance of women's expertise, though it also increased the burden of work.

Women's roles in diplomacy and kinship also mattered. Marriages between nations often involved women who connected communities, and women's kin networks were routes through which horses, goods, and information moved. Women also took part in ceremonial life and in some communities held significant spiritual authority. A history of horse cultures that includes only male hunters and warriors omits the relationships and labor that made those activities possible.

The accounts of Buffalo Bird Woman recorded by Gilbert Wilson remain among the fullest descriptions of Indigenous women's labor in a Plains village. She described agricultural work, food preparation, and the care of household goods, and also the integration of horses into the household economy in Hidatsa society of the nineteenth century. Such accounts show women as managers of fields and stores, and they also show how horse care added tasks. They describe a particular community and period, and they should not be generalized without evidence.

For mobile nations, Ewers and other ethnographers recorded that women packed and moved households, put up lodges, and processed hides. The skill required for tanning and for making durable lodge covers was considerable, and good work was respected. In several nations women owned horses, and Nez Perce and other Plateau accounts mention women as horse owners and riders. These variations underline the need to specify nation and period rather than describing a single pattern.

Section 49.27

Children, Training, Knowledge Transmission, and Equestrian Skills

Children learned horse skills early. Ethnographic accounts of several Plains and Plateau peoples describe children riding at young ages, helping herd animals, and learning from parents, grandparents, and other relatives. Boys and girls both took part in herding, and older children were often responsible for watching horses near camp. Training combined practical instruction, practice, and the observation of skilled riders, and it was tied to social identity as well as to economic needs.

Learning to ride and manage horses was part of becoming a competent member of the community. A young person who could care for horses contributed to the household economy and acquired skills valued in hunting, travel, and, for some, military service. Grandparents and other kin, who often had deep knowledge of animals, passed on lore about breeding, horse behavior, and care.

The skills spread through families and nations, and children also moved between communities through adoption, captivity, and marriage, carrying knowledge with them. The transmission of horse knowledge across generations was a major reason the horse economy could persist and expand.

The training of children included both formal and informal elements. Many communities valued precocious skill and celebrated a child's first successes, such as a first ride or first catch. Older relatives monitored children's activity and corrected mistakes. Such learning was embedded in daily life, and children contributed to the household while learning.

Children also faced risks. Falls, injuries, and encounters with raiders were dangers, and the work of herding could expose young people to attack or harsh weather. Communities developed practices to protect children, such as assigning older youth to supervise younger ones. These dangers were part of the costs of a horse economy.

Gender shaped children's training in some nations, with boys and girls learning different skills, though in practice many learned to ride and handle horses. Sources on this topic are largely from the nineteenth century and reflect the assumptions of observers, so historians should be careful. The important point is that horse knowledge was transmitted across generations through family and community, and that children's contributions were part of the economy.

Ethnographic accounts for several nations describe children's early involvement with horses. Blackfoot sources recorded by Ewers describe boys receiving horses and training as they grew, and Hidatsa accounts describe children helping with herding and care. Such sources reflect twentieth-century recollections of earlier generations, and they provide valuable detail about learning but not firm evidence for the earliest period.

Learning involved supervision by older relatives and a gradual increase in responsibility. Children's work, such as herding horses to water or bringing them in at night, contributed to the household economy and exposed children to risks. Because children left few records of their own, historians rely on adults' recollections, and they should consider whose perspectives are missing.

Section 49.28

Horses, Kinship, Marriage, Inheritance, and Gift Exchange

Horses became part of kinship obligations. In many Plains nations, documented in nineteenth-century sources, horses were included in marriage exchanges, in which a man's family gave horses to the family of a bride, or in which families exchanged gifts. Horses were also given to honor relatives, to mark the naming or recognition of children, to compensate for injuries, and to thank those who helped. Such gifts expressed and strengthened relationships.

Inheritance varied. In some societies, horses passed to sons, daughters, or other relatives according to local custom, while in others they were distributed at death or given away in memorial gifts. Historians should avoid claiming that every nation adopted the same institutions. The documented examples are valuable, but they come from specific nations and periods, mostly later than the early adoption era, and they should not be generalized without evidence.

What the evidence does show is that horses were integrated into existing systems of reciprocity, kinship, and gift exchange. Their value derived not only from their economic uses but also from the relationships they created and sustained.

Marriage practices illustrate how horses were integrated into kinship. In several nations documented in nineteenth-century sources, a groom's family gave horses to the bride's family as part of the marriage arrangement, and the bride's family often reciprocated with gifts. These exchanges created and affirmed bonds between families. Scholars caution against describing them simply as purchases of brides, since the exchanges were generally reciprocal.

Succession and inheritance were also affected. When a person died, relatives might distribute horses among kin, give them to those who had cared for the deceased, or sacrifice some at the funeral. Such practices reflected beliefs about property, generosity, and the relationship between the living and the dead. The 2023 findings of ceremonial treatment of early horses suggest that such associations began early.

Gift exchange extended beyond kin. Horses were given to guests, to seal agreements, and to honor visiting leaders, and the ability to give horses was a measure of a person's capacity and generosity. Over time, networks of gift exchange could extend across nations and provide pathways for diplomacy. Understanding these customs helps explain why horses spread through relationships and how they supported political alliances.

Marriage exchanges involving horses are documented in nineteenth-century sources for several nations, including Blackfoot and Crow, and they show that families used horses in relationships. The details varied: some exchanges were reciprocal, with gifts from both families, and the amounts reflected the resources and status of the families. Because they come from later periods, they illustrate institutions that had developed over time, and they cannot be dated precisely.

Funeral and memorial practices also involved horses in some nations, including the killing or gifting of animals. Such practices are recorded in ethnographic and traveler accounts of the nineteenth century, and the 2023 archaeological evidence for early ceremonial treatment of horses suggests that links between horses and mortuary practice may have older roots. The connection is suggestive rather than conclusive, since the archaeological contexts are few and their cultural affiliations require further study.

Section 49.29

Horses in Ceremony, Oral Tradition, and Indigenous Religious Life

Horses entered Indigenous religious and artistic life. Oral traditions recorded among several peoples describe the origin of horses and the first relationships between people and horses. Ewers recorded Blackfoot accounts that call the horse by a term often translated as "elk dog," linking the new animal to existing ideas about dogs and large game, and some versions describe horses coming from beneath a lake. Lakota speakers have used a name often translated as "sacred dog" or "mysterious dog," which also draws on earlier relationships with dogs. These names and stories show how Indigenous people placed a new animal within their own conceptual worlds.

Horses also appear in ceremonial practices, in songs, in painted and carved images, in dances, and in mortuary customs. Some animals were honored, given as gifts to sacred societies, or buried with owners. The 2023 research reported archaeological indications of ceremonial or mortuary treatment of some early horses. Later twentieth-century recordings of Lakota religious life, such as accounts of the Horse Dance, show how horses became part of ceremony and vision, though they reflect later periods and the circumstances in which they were recorded.

Horses also appear in visual culture. Pictographic art, including rock art and later painted hides and ledger drawings, depicts riders and horses. Some rock art on the Plains and in the Rockies shows horses and is attributed to the period after horses arrived, and archaeologists study such images as evidence of how communities represented the new animal. These images require careful dating and interpretation, and they should not be assumed to be eyewitness records of particular events.

Ceremonial uses of horses included dances, songs, and rituals to honor or ask help from horses. Many communities believed that horses had spiritual power and could be protected or strengthened through ceremony. Warriors sometimes decorated their horses in ways that expressed visions or protective powers. Such practices were rooted in broader Indigenous understandings of animals and spiritual relationships.

The recorded oral traditions about the origins of horses reveal how communities understood their relationship with the animal. Stories describing horses as gifts from spiritual beings or as relatives emphasize obligations of care and respect. Such traditions are living knowledge in many communities today and should be respected as such. Historians who use them should cite them with attention to context, source, and the wishes of the community.

The Blackfoot "elk dog" name and Lakota names translated as "sacred dog" appear in sources of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and historians treat them as evidence about Indigenous classification rather than as dates. Their significance lies in showing how an unfamiliar animal was understood through existing relationships with dogs and large game. Indigenous knowledge holders can explain these names in context, and scholars cite them carefully.

Lakota accounts of the Horse Dance, such as those associated with Black Elk and recorded in the twentieth century, show how horses figured in visionary and ceremonial life. Such accounts reflect specific individuals, circumstances, and recording processes, and they cannot be treated as descriptions of seventeenth-century practice. They nonetheless show that horses became part of religious life in ways that communities themselves emphasized. Using them responsibly requires attention to provenance and to the community's right to determine how its traditions are shared.

Section 49.30

Social Differentiation, Authority, and the Limits of Equestrian Prosperity

Horse ownership could change social standing without determining it. Wealthy households had greater capacity to give, lend, host, and support followers, and their generosity could strengthen their influence. Those with few animals relied on relatives and on the generosity of others, and could be drawn into relationships of dependence. In some nations, young men without horses sought them by raiding, which could increase both their standing and the risks to their communities.

Expectations of generosity moderated these differences, and leadership generally depended on more than property. Wisdom, bravery, ceremonial knowledge, kinship ties, and diplomatic skill all mattered, and horse wealth had to be used in ways the community valued. The gap between wealthy and poor households also varied with the herd's size, the community's institutions, and the success or failure of raids and hard winters. A hard winter could reduce even large herds quickly.

Equestrian prosperity therefore had limits. It was exposed to climate, disease, raiding, and the pressures of regional competition, and it depended on continuing labor and relationships. Seeing horse wealth as both an opportunity and a source of vulnerability avoids both a romantic picture of universal prosperity and a one-sided picture of inequality.

Authority in many Plains communities was shaped by consensus and persuasion, and leaders could not simply command. A leader with many horses could use them to reward followers, but followers could also leave and join other groups. This flexibility limited the permanence of hierarchy. In some nations, formal offices and societies also restrained individuals.

The experience of poor households deserves attention. Families that lost horses to raids, disease, or winter might depend on relatives or on generosity, and some young men with few resources turned to raiding or to service with wealthier relatives. In some cases, the poorest households went without horses altogether and lived more as they had before. Social support networks mitigated but did not eliminate these inequalities.

Variation between communities was also large. A community in a favorable location with plentiful grass and good relations with neighbors might prosper, while a neighboring community might struggle. The distribution of horses was thus shaped by ecology, politics, and chance as well as by individual skill. A balanced account must include both prosperity and hardship.

Differences in wealth took forms specific to each society. In Blackfoot accounts recorded by Ewers, leaders were often men of wealth, but they were also expected to give generously, and wealth that was not shared could lose a person standing. In village societies, leadership drew on ceremonial roles, clan relationships, and agricultural status as well as horses. These patterns show that horse ownership interacted with institutions in different ways.

Historians also note that few sources record the experiences of poor families. Trader and ethnographic accounts focus on leaders and public events, and the voices of those with few horses are less often preserved. Reading against the grain, historians infer from descriptions of loans, gifts, and raids that many people depended on others' generosity. This inference is plausible but should be stated with its limits.

Section 49.31

Horses, Grasslands, Water, and Ecological Relationships

Horses were large grazers entering an ecosystem already shaped by bison, elk, pronghorn, and other animals. Their effects depended on how many there were, where they grazed, and how they were managed. Historians have argued that growing horse populations competed with bison and other grazers for forage and water, especially in river valleys that provided winter shelter. Pekka Hämäläinen, in his 2003 study of Plains horse cultures, argued that large herds placed heavy pressure on river-bottom forage and contributed to an ecological and economic crisis for some mounted societies in the nineteenth century.

These arguments are important but should be handled carefully. Evidence for the numbers of horses and bison in the eighteenth century is limited, and estimates of bison populations vary widely. Documented effects, such as local overgrazing near camps and the heavy use of winter bottomland, are different from claims of widespread degradation across the Plains in the period examined in this chapter. Other factors, including climate, commercial hunting, disease, and later settler expansion, also influenced bison, and Chapter 81 addresses the destruction of the buffalo economy.

The relationship between bison and horses is a matter of competition but also of shared history. Both are grazers and both used river valleys in winter, and communities that depended on both had to consider the effects of each. The degree to which horse herds displaced bison from favored habitats probably varied by location and is difficult to measure from available records. Archaeological evidence such as pollen records and faunal remains may eventually provide more detail.

Human hunting pressure also affected bison. As communities with horses hunted more and traded more robes, they increased demand on herds, though not as dramatically as the commercial hunting of the nineteenth century. The cumulative effect of Indigenous hunting in the eighteenth century is debated, and Indigenous peoples also developed practices to manage hunting and conserve herds, such as ceremonial regulations. The ecological story is therefore a complicated interplay of multiple factors rather than a simple tale of overexploitation.

Fire and other environmental management practices also shaped Plains ecology. Indigenous peoples burned grasslands for various purposes, which affected vegetation and animal movement. Horses entered a landscape already modified by human activity, and their effects must be considered together with those of fire, hunting, and climate. This broader view guards against attributing environmental change to a single cause.

Scholarly disagreement about the ecological effects of horses is worth stating plainly. Hämäläinen's 2003 article argued that horse herds pressured winter forage and contributed to economic strain among mounted peoples. Other historians, including Dan Flores in his study of southern Plains bison and horses in the early nineteenth century, emphasized interactions among bison, horses, climate, and trade. These works differ in emphasis and in the regions and periods they treat, and neither is a survey of the eighteenth-century Plains as a whole.

The evidence available for grassland conditions is indirect. Early travelers' descriptions of grass, water, and animals offer glimpses, and later environmental records provide context, but quantitative estimates for eighteenth-century herds and forage are unreliable. Historians therefore describe plausible mechanisms and local examples while avoiding numerical claims that the sources cannot support. This restraint is part of the chapter's method.

Section 49.32

Herd Expansion, Reproduction, and Free-Roaming Horses

Horse populations grew through reproduction, capture, and trade. Under favorable conditions of forage and water, herds could increase, but growth was constrained by the slow reproduction of horses, by deaths from winter, disease, and accidents, and by the human choices to ride, breed, trade, kill, or give away animals. Free-roaming horses appeared in many regions, sometimes after animals escaped or were released, and later observers often described such animals as "wild."

The presence of free-roaming horses does not by itself establish where they came from or how many animals Indigenous communities owned. Some free-roaming horses descended from Spanish stock, some from Indigenous herds, and some from animals that moved among both. Explanations that attribute Plains horse populations to a single source, such as stray Spanish horses, overlook the evidence for managed Indigenous herds and exchange networks. Historians therefore need to combine records of ownership, archaeological remains, and genetic evidence to interpret herd history.

The rate of herd growth could be impressive under favorable conditions but was limited by management choices. A community could keep a large number of mares to produce foals, or it could keep mostly geldings for riding and hunting, which would increase usable animals but not herd size. The balance between these strategies reflected a community's needs and resources. Archaeological and genetic evidence can sometimes shed light on herd composition, though such evidence is limited.

Escaped or abandoned animals could form free-roaming groups, especially in southern regions with milder winters. Later observers often reported large "wild" herds and assumed they descended from Spanish escapes. As explained above, many such herds probably included animals from Indigenous and colonial sources, and distinguishing among them requires evidence. Capturing free-roaming horses also provided another way for Indigenous communities to obtain animals.

The movement of horses back and forth between managed and free-roaming states complicates ecological interpretation. A herd that is partly managed and partly free-roaming has different effects than a fully managed or fully feral herd, and these effects vary by region. Historians and ecologists continue to study the issue, and the evidence for the early period is limited.

Free-roaming horses are a case where assumptions can mislead. A traveler who saw unattended horses might assume they were wild descendants of Spanish animals, but many were owned herds grazing at large, or animals that had escaped from Indigenous or colonial owners. Genetic and archaeological evidence can sometimes distinguish ancestry but not ownership. Historians therefore treat reports of wild herds as sources about observers' perceptions, and look for evidence of management, trade, and capture.

Management systems also varied. Some communities kept herds close, while others let them graze at distance under watch, and still others traded or sold surplus animals. These choices affected herd growth and vulnerability, and they reflected local conditions. The overall growth of horse populations in the eighteenth century resulted from many such decisions rather than from a single process.

Section 49.33

Climate, Drought, Disease, and Winter Mortality

Climate was a constant constraint. The Plains experience cold winters, summer heat, and recurrent drought, and the period of this chapter fell within the long climatic interval often called the Little Ice Age, which affected regions differently. Hard winters could cause major losses among horses, and drought reduced forage and water. Northern communities faced deeper snow and colder temperatures, while southern communities faced drought and heat more often. Nations adapted through movement, sheltering, winter feeding, and diversified economies.

Disease affected animals and people. Horses suffered from injuries, parasites, and illnesses, and communities developed remedies and care practices. Human epidemics, especially smallpox in the late eighteenth century, affected Plains and Missouri River peoples severely, reducing populations and changing the balance of power. Chapter 40 treats epidemics and demographic catastrophe. The combination of environmental and demographic stress meant that horse-based prosperity could be fragile.

Climate records provide context for these challenges. Tree rings and other proxies show periods of drought and cold in the Plains and Rockies during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, though the chronology and severity varied by region. Such evidence helps historians consider how communities coped and why some moved or changed practices. Because proxy records are imperfect, historians should avoid linking particular events to particular climate episodes without strong evidence.

Winter strategies reflected local conditions. Northern communities relied on sheltered river valleys and stored feed, while southern communities faced fewer snowstorms but more drought. Communities with more diversified economies, such as those combining agriculture and hunting, had more options. Communities relying heavily on horses and bison could be exposed to simultaneous failures of both.

Disease among humans also interacted with horse economies. When epidemics reduced the population, fewer people were available to care for herds and defend them, and communities might lose both labor and animals. Survivors might also move or merge with other groups. These cascading effects show how environmental and demographic factors could combine to produce major changes.

Drought is a significant but unevenly documented factor. Tree-ring and other climate reconstructions indicate multi-year droughts in parts of the Plains during the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, but their timing and severity differ by region, and linking them to particular historical events requires caution. Where drought reduced forage and water, communities with diverse economies, such as village agriculture combined with hunting, had more options than those depending mainly on hunting and herds.

Winter mortality is better documented in trader and ethnographic accounts, though mostly for the nineteenth century. Those accounts describe heavy losses in severe winters and the strategies used to reduce them. The consistency of such accounts across nations supports the inference that winter was a major constraint in the earlier period as well, but exact figures cannot be reconstructed.

Section 49.34

Uneven Transformation: Southern, Central, Northern, and Village-Based Societies Compared

The adoption of horses followed different paths in different places. On the southern Plains, proximity to New Mexico and Texas gave early access to animals, and mounted hunting and raiding became important for some peoples, while others, such as Wichita villagers, combined farming and horses. On the central Plains, Pawnee and neighboring villages joined mounted hunts to agriculture and trade. On the northern Plains, adoption came somewhat later in most accounts, and the harsh winters shaped herd management. In the Rockies and on the Plateau, horses supported travel, trade, and seasonal movement between regions.

Within each region, communities made different choices about how much to invest in horses, how to use them, and how to combine them with older practices. Some became primarily mounted hunters, some mixed agriculture and mounted hunts, and some retained substantial pedestrian and riverine practices. These choices reflected ecology, political circumstances, and cultural priorities, not merely access to animals.

The southern Plains illustrate the benefits and risks of proximity. Communities near New Mexico and Texas had early access to horses but also faced Spanish raids, epidemics, and pressure from neighbors. The Comanche's rise, described in Chapter 50, depended on a combination of location, alliances, and skills, but it also relied on conditions that others did not share. Southern communities that lacked such conditions, or that were displaced, experienced the same horse economy differently.

The central Plains show the strength of mixed economies. Pawnee and neighboring peoples combined agriculture and mounted hunting, which gave them resilience but also exposed them to the risks of both. Their villages were stable centers but could be targets, and their dependence on seasonal hunts required a balance of labor.

In the north, the combination of harsh winters, longer distances, and later adoption produced distinct patterns. Mounted hunting became central for many nations, but winter survival required careful management. In the mountains and Plateau, geography and seasonal economies produced yet other patterns. Taken together, these cases show that no single model of horse adoption fits the entire region.

Comparing regions reveals how different buffers operated. Village peoples had stored crops that cushioned bad hunting years, mobile peoples had flexibility to move to better grass and kin networks that offered assistance, and Plateau peoples had salmon and root resources. Each system had weaknesses: villages were targets and vulnerable to epidemics, mobile groups depended on herds and bison, and Plateau groups depended on fish runs and trade routes. No system was invulnerable.

This comparison also guards against environmental determinism. Similar ecological conditions produced different strategies because societies differed in institutions, alliances, and priorities. The adoption of horses was therefore not a mechanical response to ecology but a series of decisions by communities with distinct histories, as the earlier sections of the chapter have shown.

Section 49.35

Indigenous Power and the Plains at the End of the Eighteenth Century

By about 1800, horses had become central to Indigenous economic and political life across much of the Plains, Rockies, and Plateau. Mounted nations controlled trade routes, hunting territories, and diplomatic relationships, and colonial powers on the edges of the region depended on Indigenous cooperation to trade and travel. The Spanish in New Mexico and Texas, French and later Spanish authorities in Louisiana, British traders to the north, and the new United States all had to deal with powerful Indigenous nations on the Plains.

This power was real but uneven. Nations such as the Comanche, Lakota, Crow, and Blackfoot-speaking peoples benefited from equestrian mobility, while others, including some village peoples hit by epidemics and some groups pushed from earlier territories, faced greater pressure. Equestrian power also depended on ecological conditions, regional alliances, and trade, all of which could change. Scholars such as Hämäläinen have emphasized both the opportunities and the vulnerabilities of horse-centered lifeways, a theme that continues in later chapters. The synthesis of Indigenous power across the continent around 1800 is the subject of Chapter 60.

Colonial powers' relationship with Plains nations was shaped by this power. Spanish authorities in the later eighteenth century increasingly sought peace and trade with Comanche and others, recognizing that military solutions had limits. French and later Spanish administrators in Louisiana also negotiated with Plains nations. British traders in the north depended on Indigenous middlemen and mounted trading partners. The United States, which acquired Louisiana in 1803, would soon confront the same realities.

The end of the eighteenth century was thus a period of both strength and vulnerability for Plains nations. Their power rested on horses, trade, and alliances, but it also depended on bison populations, health, and diplomatic relationships. The nineteenth century would test these foundations through epidemics, the expansion of the fur and robe trades, and the arrival of American settlers and armies, topics discussed in later volumes.

This chapter's conclusion should therefore be balanced. Horses became an important instrument of Indigenous innovation and power, and the societies that built horse economies did so through knowledge, labor, and diplomacy. The transformation was neither immediate nor uniform, and it brought significant costs and risks alongside opportunities. Understanding both sides is essential to understanding the Plains in the age of empires.

The synthesis of Indigenous power around 1800 should note both evidence and uncertainty. Colonial records show that Spanish, French, British, and American officials treated Plains nations as powers to be negotiated with, and Indigenous accounts emphasize their own autonomy. At the same time, epidemics, ecological pressures, and expanding trade networks were reshaping the region, and these developments produced winners and losers. The chapter's conclusion is therefore that horses contributed to Indigenous power without guaranteeing its permanence, which Chapter 60 will place in a wider continental context.

Section 49.36

Reconstructing the Horse's Spread: Archaeology, Genetics, Colonial Records, and Indigenous Traditions

The history of the horse's spread must be reconstructed from several kinds of evidence, each with different strengths. Radiocarbon dating can place a bone in a time range, but it cannot say who owned the animal or how it was used. Ancient DNA can identify ancestry but not social meaning. Colonial records document Spanish, French, and British contact but often miss Indigenous exchange and rarely record what happened beyond the settlements. Trader journals provide eyewitness views but limited coverage. Indigenous oral histories and winter counts preserve community memory and meaning, though dating them requires care.

The 2023 research is a good example of why combining sources matters. Older chronologies built mainly on European observers dated the spread late because Europeans reached many regions late. Archaeological evidence showed that horses had arrived earlier than observers recorded, and Indigenous knowledge helped place the findings in cultural context. Each kind of evidence checks the others, and none should be treated as complete.

The strengths and limits of each kind of evidence should be kept in view. Colonial records are abundant for some places and silent for others, and what they say reflects the interests of their authors. Indigenous oral histories and winter counts are rich in meaning but require careful chronology. Archaeological evidence provides independent dates but is limited by preservation and by the locations where researchers have worked. Genetic evidence is powerful but depends on the availability of samples.

Collaboration across these approaches is increasingly important. Indigenous scholars and communities have contributed to research design, interpretation, and dissemination, and their involvement has improved the quality and relevance of the work. This collaborative model also raises ethical questions, discussed in Chapter 1, about consent and control of knowledge, and it provides a model for future research.

For students, the lesson is methodological: when sources disagree, historians do not simply choose one. They examine how each source was created, what it can show, and how it can be corroborated. The history of the horse is a case study in how historical knowledge advances through such comparison.

Source criticism clarifies what each evidence type can answer. For example, Henday's journal can show that horses were prominent on the northern Plains in the mid-1750s, but the fact that the original is lost and the surviving versions differ reminds us to read it as a transmitted text. Saukamappee's account preserves a memory recorded decades after the events, so it can show how an elder understood the arrival of horses and guns, not the exact date. The Taylor et al. study provides independent dates, but for specific sites. Combining these sources shows both strengths and limits.

Indigenous authorship and collaboration are increasingly central to the field. Tribal historic preservation offices, Indigenous scholars, and knowledge holders participate in research design and interpretation, and some of the most important recent work, including the 2023 study, incorporated Indigenous knowledge. This collaboration improves the questions asked and the cultural context available, and it reflects broader changes in archaeology and history discussed in Chapter 1.

Section 49.37

Beyond the Mounted Warrior: Equestrian Culture, Historical Memory, and Indigenous Continuity

The familiar image of the Plains mounted warrior is incomplete. It tends to omit agricultural villages, women's labor, children's training, economic management, diplomacy, and regional variation. It also tends to present horse culture as a timeless Plains tradition rather than as a series of Indigenous innovations over generations, and to treat it as an inevitable outcome of colonization. Seeing horse culture as an Indigenous historical achievement means paying attention to the knowledge, labor, and decisions through which communities built it, and to the costs and inequalities that came with it.

Horses continue to matter in many Indigenous communities today through ceremony, athletics, ranching, rides commemorating historical events, and cultural education, though this chapter does not survey those later developments. The history in this chapter shows that Indigenous nations are not simply recipients of outside technologies but active shapers of the changes they adopt. That understanding prepares students for the detailed Comanche and Apache histories in the next two chapters.

The familiar images of mounted warriors derive largely from nineteenth-century artists, photographers, and writers, who often portrayed Plains peoples as romantic figures outside history or as doomed adversaries of American expansion. Those images shaped popular memory and often ignored Indigenous diversity and ongoing history. Indigenous artists and scholars have challenged such images and offered alternative representations that emphasize community, knowledge, and continuity.

An accurate history must therefore include the farmers, traders, caretakers, ceremonial leaders, and diplomats who built and sustained horse cultures, as well as the hunters and warriors. It must also recognize variation among nations and over time. The transformations described in this chapter were the work of many people over generations, and their legacy continues in Indigenous communities.

Finally, the chapter has shown how history can change as new evidence and collaborations emerge. The 2023 research did not diminish the achievements of Plains horse cultures; it gave them a deeper history and placed Indigenous exchange networks at the center. This is the kind of historical thinking the review materials invite students to practice.

Critique of the mounted-warrior image should address its sources. Much of the imagery derives from nineteenth-century photographs, paintings, and exhibitions, many produced for non-Indigenous audiences and shaped by their expectations, including Wild West shows. Ledger drawings by Indigenous artists provide different perspectives, showing events and values important to the artists, though they too date from the nineteenth century and from specific communities. Using them as evidence requires identifying artist, nation, date, and context.

A more accurate account would place horses within the history of Indigenous economies, politics, and families. It would include the gardeners of the Missouri villages, the women who processed hides, the children who tended herds, the diplomats who arranged trade, and the knowledge keepers who preserved memory. It would also acknowledge that the history of Indigenous horse cultures is a continuing story in communities where horses remain part of ceremony, education, and ranching. That is the perspective this chapter has tried to reflect.

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has argued that Indigenous nations turned a newly available animal into an instrument of economic, political, and cultural innovation, and that the transformation was uneven, costly, and shaped by existing Indigenous societies. The materials that follow summarize the chapter, define its key terms, and provide questions, writing prompts, and a research project that ask students to practice the historical thinking this chapter models.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined how domestic horses entered Indigenous societies and how Indigenous nations used them to pursue their own economic, political, and cultural objectives. It argued that horses did not create Plains civilization. They entered worlds of farming villages, bison economies, and trading networks that were already sophisticated.

The prehorse Plains relied on human labor, dog travois, communal drives, and extensive exchange. Horses reached those societies as Iberian-derived animals reintroduced to a continent where native equids had disappeared at the end of the Pleistocene. The distinction between deep evolutionary history and colonial reintroduction guided the chapter's use of evidence.

Spanish expeditions and the permanent colony of New Mexico provided early sources of animals and knowledge, but Spanish authorities could not control Indigenous access. Indigenous people acquired horses through trade, capture, gifts, and reproduction, and learned horsemanship through work with Spanish and Pueblo herders and through relationships with neighbors.

The 2023 interdisciplinary study showed that domestic horses had reached parts of the central Plains and northern Rockies by the first half of the seventeenth century. The Pueblo Revolt of 1680 therefore accelerated an existing diffusion rather than beginning it. The evidence supports approximate periods of adoption, not exact dates for each nation.

Diffusion followed Indigenous networks: Pueblo, Apache, Ute, Shoshone, Wichita, Caddo, Pawnee, Mandan, Hidatsa, Crow, and Blackfoot connections, among others, carried horses and horse knowledge across the interior and toward the Plateau. Regional timing varied, and northern adoption is documented most clearly from the eighteenth century.

Horses changed transportation, bison hunting, residential mobility, trade, and warfare, but they also required heavy investment in pasture, water, winter care, and defense. Village peoples joined horses to agriculture rather than abandoning it, and older practices continued alongside mounted hunting.

Horse wealth created opportunities and inequalities, and raiding became an important form of conflict. Women's labor, children's training, kinship exchange, and spiritual relationships were integral to equestrian economies, and Indigenous traditions placed the horse within their own conceptual worlds.

Ecological and climatic constraints, disease, and competition limited horse-based prosperity. Scholars debate how large these effects were in the eighteenth century, and the evidence is limited. By about 1800, equestrian power helped many nations preserve or expand political autonomy, though not all benefited equally.

The chapter also showed why combining archaeology, genetics, colonial records, trader journals, and Indigenous knowledge produces a better history than any single source. The mounted warrior image omits agriculture, women, children, and diplomacy, and it is better understood as one part of a larger Indigenous achievement. This understanding prepares students for Chapters 50 and 51.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows that Indigenous power in an age of empires was often built through innovation, not just survival. Horses came from colonial settlements, but Indigenous nations turned them into instruments of mobility, trade, and diplomacy, and they did so through knowledge, labor, and relationships that colonial authorities could not see or control.

The chapter also shows how historical chronology changes with evidence. Evidence from archaeology and genetics, together with Indigenous knowledge, moved the beginning of the story earlier and turned attention from a single colonial event to long-running Indigenous networks.

The transformation was uneven and costly. It brought wealth, inequality, ecological pressure, and new vulnerabilities, and it affected women, children, villages, and mounted hunters differently. Understanding that complexity prevents the mounted warrior from becoming a stereotype.
