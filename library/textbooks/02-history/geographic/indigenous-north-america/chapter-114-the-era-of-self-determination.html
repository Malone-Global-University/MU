Chapter Opening

In the second half of the twentieth century, Indigenous nations in the United States moved from a federal policy that sought to end their political existence toward one that increasingly acknowledged them as governments able to run their own schools, clinics, and public programs. The change was real, but it was neither sudden nor complete, and it was won, not granted.

In 1966, on the Navajo Nation in northeastern Arizona, a school opened at Rough Rock that was unusual in one specific respect. Its operating funds came from the federal government, through the Office of Economic Opportunity and the Bureau of Indian Affairs, but it was governed by a school board made up of local Navajo residents rather than by a Bureau administrator answering to officials far away. Decisions about hiring, about the place of the Navajo language, and about what children should learn were, for the first time in a Bureau-funded school of this kind, placed substantially in the hands of the community. Rough Rock was small, and it was experimental. It nonetheless posed a question that Indigenous leaders, federal legislators, and administrators would spend the next three decades answering: if Indigenous communities could govern their own institutions, what should the federal government's role be?

This chapter examines how that question was answered between roughly 1960 and 1994. It follows the transition from the termination policies of the 1950s, which sought to end the federal relationship with tribal nations, toward the policy known as self-determination, which the federal government formally adopted in 1970 and enacted into law in the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975. It then follows that policy through its major institutional consequences: tribal control over elementary and secondary schools, the founding of tribally chartered colleges, the expansion of tribal roles in health care, housing, and social services, the growth of tribal administrative departments, the self-governance movement of the 1980s, and the Tribal Self-Governance Act of 1994.

The central argument of the chapter is that self-determination did not create Indigenous sovereignty. It provided new legal and administrative mechanisms through which Indigenous nations could exercise authority they had long claimed and defended. The distinction is basic. As Chapter 1 explained, Indigenous nations are political communities whose authority predates the United States. Treaties, statutes, and court decisions recognized, constrained, and sometimes attacked that authority, but they did not originate it. The self-determination era is therefore best understood as a period in which federal law began to make room, unevenly and with significant conditions, for the practical exercise of governing power that Indigenous nations had never relinquished.

The chapter is also a history of institutions and of ordinary lives. Presidential messages and acts of Congress are central evidence, but a law becomes historically meaningful only when it changes what happens in a school, a clinic, a housing office, or a tribal council chamber. Throughout the chapter, students should ask who actually gained decision-making power under each reform, who paid for the services being delivered, which responsibilities were transferred and which were retained by federal agencies, and how different communities, with different resources and political traditions, experienced the same federal statute differently. Several of the nations discussed here, among them the Navajo Nation, the Menominee, the Cherokee Nation, and the Pacific Northwest nations that pioneered self-governance, were quite unlike one another.

The evidence is diverse. Historians draw on presidential messages, congressional hearings and committee reports, the text of statutes and their later amendments, Bureau of Indian Affairs and Indian Health Service records, reports of federal oversight bodies, tribal council minutes and constitutions, newsletters and periodicals produced by Indigenous organizations, court decisions, and the testimony and memoirs of participants. Each has limitations. Federal documents tend to emphasize policy intentions and administrative categories. Tribal records are uneven in survival and accessibility. Oral history captures the experiences of teachers, nurses, council members, and parents that written records overlook, but it requires the same critical handling as any other source.

Chapter 114 has defined boundaries. The history of termination itself is told in Chapter 104, and the Red Power movement that increased the political pressure for reform is told in Chapters 108 through 112. The fisheries litigation that confirmed treaty rights is the subject of Chapter 113 and appears here only as an example of expanding tribal administration. The Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act, tribal courts and jurisdiction, economic development and gaming, tribal colleges in later decades, and the Indian Child Welfare Act belong to Chapters 115, 118, 121 and 122, 124, and 131 respectively. Developments in Canada, where a different legal and political history unfolded, are treated in Chapter 117; students should not treat the American statutes examined here as representative of Indigenous policy across the continent.

Self-determination is neither a triumphant story of the end of colonial control nor a story of empty rhetoric. It is a story of Indigenous governments gaining operational responsibility and political leverage, and of the federal government retaining legal power, financial control, and, in many respects, the final word. Understanding that mixture, and the institutions Indigenous nations built within it, prepares students for the struggles over land, jurisdiction, and nationhood that follow in Part XX.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why termination and related assimilationist policies produced a political crisis that led to their repudiation in the late 1960s and 1970s.
  2. Trace the role of tribal governments, national Indigenous organizations, lawyers, and community activists in creating the demand for self-determination.
  3. Analyze President Nixon's July 8, 1970, Special Message to Congress and distinguish the significance of the presidential policy statement from the legislation needed to implement it.
  4. Describe the Menominee termination and restoration experience and explain what it reveals about the consequences of termination and the means of reversing it.
  5. Explain the purposes and mechanics of the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975 and of self-determination contracting.
  6. Distinguish the administration of federal programs from the exercise of inherent Indigenous sovereign authority.
  7. Assess the continuing federal trust responsibility and explain why increased tribal administrative control did not eliminate federal obligations or federal financial and regulatory power.
  8. Evaluate the transfer of educational, health, housing, and social-service functions to tribal control, including its effects on ordinary communities.
  9. Compare the development of tribal institutions across different Indigenous nations and regions, accounting for variation in resources, political traditions, and administrative capacity.
  10. Distinguish self-determination contracts from self-governance compacts and explain why some tribal governments sought the latter.
  11. Interpret the historical debate over whether self-determination meaningfully expanded Indigenous autonomy or left tribal governments structurally dependent on federal institutions.
  12. Connect the institutions built during this era to later struggles over land, jurisdiction, economic development, and nationhood.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did federal policy shift from termination toward self-determination, and why did that shift take years rather than occurring with a single presidential announcement?
  2. In what ways did Indigenous political action, through institutions as well as protest, shape the content of federal reform?

  8. How should historians weigh evidence for meaningful autonomy against evidence of continuing dependence on federal funding and oversight?
  9. In what sense did the self-determination era lay foundations for later Indigenous legal, political, and economic initiatives, and in what sense did it limit them?

Historical Context

To understand why self-determination mattered, students need a clear picture of the system it modified. By the middle of the twentieth century, relations between the United States and Indigenous nations rested on a layered inheritance. Treaties and the constitutional treatment of tribes as political entities distinct from the states supplied the foundation of the federal relationship. A large body of nineteenth-century legislation and Supreme Court doctrine had added the concept of a federal trust responsibility while also asserting broad federal power over tribes. The reservation system, the Bureau of Indian Affairs (BIA), and, from 1955, the Indian Health Service (IHS) delivered services to reservation communities and, on paper, to individuals as the beneficiaries of treaty and statutory obligations. In practice, Bureau superintendents, agency offices, and Washington officials made most administrative decisions. Chapters 86, 87, and 97 trace how allotment, boarding schools, and the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 shaped this environment, and students should consult them for details.

The Indian Reorganization Act had encouraged many nations to adopt written constitutions and elected councils. Those governments gave tribes a formal political structure recognized by federal law, but they often operated under constitutions that followed Bureau models and required approval from the Secretary of the Interior for many actions. Councils frequently controlled little money of their own. In many communities the Bureau's agency superintendent remained, in practical terms, the dominant local authority, and tribal governments functioned in significant part as advisory bodies or as intermediaries between the community and the federal administration. This is an important caution against assuming that political institutions existing on paper held real power. It also means that the self-determination era required not only legal change but the development of administrative capacity that had been systematically limited.

The 1950s then brought a deliberate attempt to dismantle the relationship altogether. House Concurrent Resolution 108 in 1953 declared Congress's intention to end federal supervision of tribes "as rapidly as possible," a phrase that signaled the guiding purpose of termination, and a series of individual acts ended federal recognition of specific nations, including the Menominee and the Klamath. Public Law 280, also passed in 1953, extended certain state criminal and civil jurisdiction over Indian country in several states, and federal relocation programs encouraged individuals to move to cities. Chapter 104 treats these policies in full; Chapter 105 examines relocation. For present purposes, the essential point is that termination was not simply a bureaucratic adjustment. It converted entire political communities into ordinary citizens and corporations and exposed their land and institutions to loss.

By the 1960s the consequences of termination, the continuing poverty of many reservation communities, and the paternalism of Bureau administration were under intense criticism, within Indigenous communities and in the wider political atmosphere of the civil rights era. The Great Society programs of the mid-1960s created a new federal channel, because the Economic Opportunity Act of 1964 allowed tribal governments to apply directly for and administer antipoverty funds, bypassing the Bureau. Congressional investigations, the activism examined in Chapters 107 through 112, and sympathetic administrators accelerated pressure for reform. Self-determination emerged from the convergence of these streams, rather than from one source.

Students should hold two clarifications in mind. First, the phrase self-determination in this chapter refers to a federal policy and set of statutes. The inherent right of Indigenous nations to govern themselves is a separate and older claim. Second, the legal framework examined here is specific to the United States. In Canada, the federal government's 1969 White Paper proposing the elimination of special Indian status provoked a strong Indigenous response, including the Indian Association of Alberta's 1970 statement known as the Red Paper, and the National Indian Brotherhood's 1972 policy paper on Indian control of Indian education. These developments paralleled American ones but followed a different legal logic, discussed in Chapter 117.

Division I — From Termination to Self-Determination · Section 114.1

The Crisis of Federal Termination Policy

By the early 1960s, termination had produced a crisis of purpose within the federal government and of survival within the affected communities. As Chapter 104 explains, the policy rested on the assumption that Indigenous peoples would prosper once federal supervision ended and their lands and resources were placed under ordinary legal control. The experiences of the nations actually terminated, especially the Menominee and Klamath, pointed in the opposite direction. Tribal assets were converted into corporate or individual holdings, services previously supplied through the federal relationship disappeared or shifted to state and local governments without adequate financing, and the political communities themselves lost the legal standing through which they had negotiated with other governments.

It is important to distinguish formal termination from the wider assimilationist environment. Formal termination meant that Congress passed a statute ending federal recognition of a specific nation, a status that affected a relatively small number of communities but was catastrophic for them. A much larger number of nations retained federal recognition while living under the pressure of the same ideas. They were subject to Public Law 280 jurisdiction in some states, to relocation programs that drew population toward cities, to budget reductions justified by the goal of eventual withdrawal, and to a Bureau administration that still decided most local matters. The threat of termination, whether or not it was carried out, discouraged investment in tribal institutions, since leaders could not be sure that the federal relationship would endure. Self-determination would later address both categories: restoring terminated nations and reorganizing the administration of those that remained recognized.

Bureau paternalism was a second source of discontent. Reservation residents encountered federal employees who administered programs by regulation and who frequently answered to Washington rather than to the community. Tribal councils had to obtain approval for contracts, budgets, and sometimes ordinary actions. The administrative arrangement was not the same everywhere; some superintendents were more consultative than others, and some Indigenous employees worked within the Bureau to improve it. But the structural problem was widely recognized, including by federal investigators. Critics argued that a system designed around supervision could not build the capacity for self-government that it claimed to be preparing. Where capacity was needed, the argument went, it would have to be developed through practice.

The crisis was also political. Termination had been justified in part as a way to reduce federal spending and treat Indigenous people equally with other Americans. By the 1960s, those arguments clashed with the civil rights movement's emphasis on both equality and the right of communities to determine their own futures. Many Indigenous leaders articulated a position that was distinct from both older paternalism and simple assimilation: equal citizenship in the United States was compatible with, and did not displace, the continuing political existence of tribal nations. That position, developed in tribal councils and intertribal gatherings, framed the demand examined in the next section.

Section 114.2

Indigenous Political Mobilization and the Demand for Self-Government

The demand for self-government did not begin with the occupations and marches that drew national attention after 1969, and it did not come from a single organization. It was assembled across the 1950s and 1960s through tribal council resolutions, intertribal conferences, congressional testimony, litigation, and the work of Indigenous lawyers, administrators, and community organizers. The National Congress of American Indians, founded in 1944, opposed termination from the outset and lobbied against the specific bills that would end federal recognition (see Chapter 107). Its work gave tribal governments a means of coordinating positions that no individual council could have matched.

A significant moment occurred in 1961, when several hundred Indigenous delegates from dozens of nations met in Chicago for the American Indian Chicago Conference, organized under the leadership of the anthropologist Sol Tax and Indigenous participants, and issued the Declaration of Indian Purpose. The Declaration is a useful example of how national Indigenous political language was changing. It insisted on continued federal responsibility and on the right of Indigenous communities to make decisions about their own affairs, and it opposed termination. It also reveals disagreement, because delegates from different regions debated the proper role of the federal government. A younger generation of activists, many of them students, found the conference too cautious, and the National Indian Youth Council, formed in 1961, pressed for more assertive action (see Chapter 108).

The Great Society created practical opportunities. The Economic Opportunity Act of 1964 and the Office of Economic Opportunity allowed tribal governments to receive federal antipoverty money directly for Community Action Programs, and many tribes created offices, hired staff, and administered budgets for the first time without Bureau intermediation. On the Navajo Nation, the Office of Navajo Economic Opportunity, begun in the mid-1960s, became a large administrative operation. The experience demonstrated that tribal governments could manage substantial public funds and programs, and it produced a cohort of Indigenous administrators who would later staff tribal governments. These programs were limited, politically contested, and often poorly funded, and they should not be romanticized. Still, they made self-administration a practical rather than hypothetical possibility.

Legal and political advocacy mattered as well. Indigenous attorneys and non-Indigenous allies challenged state and federal actions in court, and the Supreme Court's 1968 decision in Menominee Tribe v. United States held that the Menominee's hunting and fishing rights had survived termination of the federal relationship, a ruling that complicated the claim that termination had erased tribal legal existence. At the same time, Indigenous leaders worked with sympathetic officials. Robert L. Bennett, an Oneida, served as Commissioner of Indian Affairs from 1966 to 1969, the first Indigenous person to hold the office in nearly a century. Direct action, treated in Chapters 109 through 112, then magnified a demand that institutional advocacy had already framed. The sequence matters: protest altered the political atmosphere, but the principal proposals that eventually became law had been shaped by years of work within tribal and national organizations.

Section 114.3

The Nixon Administration and the Policy Reversal of 1970

President Lyndon Johnson's March 6, 1968, message to Congress, "The Forgotten American," had already repudiated termination and used the language of self-determination, and Congress in April 1968 passed the Indian Civil Rights Act, which required tribal governments to observe a set of civil protections modeled partly on the Bill of Rights and, for the future, required tribal consent before additional states assumed Public Law 280 jurisdiction. The Johnson years therefore established important precedents. It was Richard Nixon's July 8, 1970, Special Message to Congress on Indian Affairs, however, that most clearly stated the policy turn in presidential terms and that has since become the usual reference point for the beginning of the self-determination era.

Nixon's message rejected termination as a policy goal, criticizing it as based on false premises and as having created fear and uncertainty among Indigenous communities. It declared that the federal government should instead support Indigenous self-determination, a policy under which tribes would assume control of programs affecting them, without the federal government being relieved of its legal responsibilities. This last point was central. The message did not propose that the United States withdraw its commitments; it proposed a new relationship in which federal obligations would continue while Indigenous governments gained control over how services were delivered. It also recommended legislation allowing tribes to contract to run federal programs and, should they later wish, to return programs to the federal government, an arrangement later called retrocession.

The message contained other proposals, some of which were enacted and some of which were not. Nixon urged Congress to return the Blue Lake area to Taos Pueblo, to restore the Menominee's federal status, to create an Assistant Secretary of the Interior for Indian Affairs, to increase funding for health and education, to support Indian business development, and to establish an Indian Trust Counsel Authority that would give Indigenous nations independent legal representation in disputes with other federal agencies. Congress acted on Blue Lake that same year and on Menominee restoration in 1973, and the Interior Department created the Assistant Secretary post in 1977. The independent trust counsel proposal, however, did not become law. This partial enactment illustrates the first editorial standard of the chapter: a presidential statement could redirect policy and open political space, but legislation, appropriations, and administrative implementation were required to change anything on the ground.

Historians have advanced several explanations for the administration's willingness to adopt this stance. Some stress the personal influence of White House staff and advisers sympathetic to Indigenous concerns, including Leonard Garment and Bradley Patterson, and of Indigenous leaders who had access to the administration. Others point to political calculation, since support for Indigenous self-government was inexpensive relative to other domestic programs and consistent with a conservative rhetoric of decentralization and local control. Others emphasize the pressure created by activism, including the occupation of Alcatraz that began in 1969 (Chapter 109). The most persuasive interpretations treat these factors as interacting rather than competing. Students should also remember that the same administration oversaw the federal response to the Wounded Knee occupation of 1973 (Chapter 112), a reminder that a government can endorse self-determination in principle while resisting specific Indigenous political actions.

Historical Vocabulary
Self-Determination and Sovereignty

Definition. In this chapter, self-determination refers to the federal policy, adopted in 1970 and elaborated in statutes from 1975 onward, of increasing Indigenous governments' participation in and control over federal programs and services. Sovereignty refers to the inherent political authority of an Indigenous nation to govern itself, which Chapter 1 explained as predating the United States.

Historical context. Policy-makers adopted the phrase self-determination partly because it implied Indigenous decision-making without requiring Congress to resolve the contested legal boundaries of sovereignty. International and Indigenous usage of the term was broader; Indigenous leaders often meant not merely participation in programs but the continuing power of nations to determine their own political status.

Why it matters. Treating the two terms as synonyms would imply that tribal governments received their authority from the policy. The better reading is that federal law adjusted the conditions under which an existing authority could be exercised.

Potential misunderstanding. Because the statutes used the language of contracts and funding, some readers assume the era concerned only administration. Administrative capacity was indeed the principal object of the legislation, but it supported, and was sometimes used to expand, claims of political authority.

Context: Delivered about a year and a half into the administration, the message followed a period of congressional and executive review of Indian policy and the political ferment of the late 1960s. It addressed Congress, which controlled the legislation and appropriations on which any reform depended, as well as Indigenous communities and the wider public.

"The time has come to break decisively with the past and to create the conditions for a new era in which the Indian future is determined by Indian acts and Indian decisions."

Excerpt from the published text of the message. Students should consult the full text, available in the Public Papers of the Presidents and in digital archives, to verify wording and read the specific recommendations. Read Nixon's complete message.

Paraphrase of related passages: The message went on to describe termination as wrong in its premises and in its effects, to emphasize that the federal government's obligations arising from treaties and other commitments should continue, and to recommend specific measures, including contracting authority for tribes and the restoration of Blue Lake and Menominee status.

Analysis: The source is evidence of the formal policy position of the executive branch and of the language through which that position was publicly expressed. It shows that the administration framed self-determination as compatible with, and dependent on, continued federal responsibility.

Limitations: A presidential message states intentions and recommendations. It cannot show whether they were enacted, funded, or implemented, or how Indigenous communities experienced them. It was also produced by speechwriters and advisers, and it reflects the priorities of its authors.

Perspective: The perspective is that of the executive branch of the United States. Missing are the voices of tribal governments, community members, and Congress, as well as skeptical Indigenous responses.

Section 114.4

The Menominee Restoration and the Reversal of Termination

The Menominee Nation of Wisconsin provided the most influential test of whether termination could be reversed. The Menominee possessed a forest of more than 200,000 acres, managed over generations through a sustained-yield timber operation centered on the mill at Neopit, and the nation had been regarded as relatively self-supporting, which made it an attractive candidate in the eyes of termination's advocates. The Menominee Termination Act of 1954 provided for the end of federal supervision, and, after a transition period, the process was completed in 1961. The reservation became Menominee County, the tribal assets were transferred to a corporation, Menominee Enterprises, Inc., and tribal members became shareholders. The population affected was roughly three thousand people.

The consequences were severe. The new county lacked the tax base to support services, and the former federal relationship had been the basis for services such as health care, which now came under severe strain. The corporation sought revenue to meet obligations to the new county and other costs, and it developed land for sale to non-Indians, including through a lake-front development project, steps that Menominee critics saw as dismantling the land base itself. Poverty and illness increased. Termination therefore became, in the eyes of many observers, evidence for the opposite of its premise: rather than liberating a community, it produced a self-inflicted fiscal and social crisis.

The restoration movement grew from within the community. In 1970, Menominee activists, including Ada Deer, founded Determination of Rights and Unity of Menominee Shareholders (DRUMS), which opposed land sales and organized demands for restoration of the nation's status. Deer, a social worker who had left Wisconsin for professional training and returned to take up the cause, became a leading spokesperson, lobbying in Madison and Washington and building alliances with non-Indian supporters. The movement employed many tactics: legal argument, public education, political pressure on state and federal officials, and the 220-mile march from Keshena to Madison that began October 2, 1971. Importantly, it also had to overcome internal divisions among the Menominee over strategy and over the relationship between the corporation and the tribe.

Congress passed the Menominee Restoration Act, which President Nixon signed on December 22, 1973. It restored federal recognition and the federal-tribal relationship, returned the Menominee's status as a tribe, and provided a process through which the nation would draft a new constitution and reconstitute its government. Restoration was not instantaneous or complete. The Menominee had to rebuild institutions after years of corporate and county governance, negotiate the transfer of lands and assets, and resolve disputes with the county, the state, and some of their own members who had acquired interests during the termination period. The restored reservation status was formalized in 1975; members accepted a new tribal constitution in November 1976, and the first tribal legislature under that constitution was elected in February 1979. Meanwhile, the nation worked to resume control of institutions, services, and its forest enterprise. Recognition in 1973 therefore preceded the full reconstruction of representative government.

The Menominee case mattered for three reasons. First, it demonstrated that termination could be undone by political action, setting a precedent for other nations; in the following years Congress restored recognition to a number of terminated nations, including the Siletz in Oregon (1977), and the courts, in a case settled in 1983, restored seventeen California rancherias. Second, it showed the importance of Indigenous organizing at the community level, since the restoration movement succeeded because of Menominee initiative rather than only federal benevolence. Third, it gave the federal government a concrete example for repudiating termination. The return of Blue Lake to Taos Pueblo on December 15, 1970, a restoration of roughly 48,000 acres sacred to the Pueblo after decades of struggle, reinforced the changed political climate, though that history belongs primarily to land-claims chapters such as Chapter 119.

Division II — The Legal and Administrative Foundations of Self-Determination · Section 114.5

The Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975

Nixon's message called for legislation, but nearly five years passed before Congress enacted the central statute. The delay reflects the realities of the legislative process and the complexity of the issue. Members of Congress debated how much discretion the Interior and Health, Education, and Welfare departments should retain, how contract costs should be paid, how federal employees would be protected, and whether tribes with limited administrative experience could safely assume large programs. Bureau officials, concerned about their own positions and about service continuity, were not uniformly supportive. Tribal leaders, for their part, pressed for strong guarantees of the right to contract and warned against provisions that would let federal officials reject tribal proposals at will.

The legislation that emerged was Public Law 93-638, the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act, signed by President Gerald Ford on January 4, 1975. Senator Henry Jackson of Washington, Senator James Abourezk of South Dakota, and Representative Lloyd Meeds of Washington were among the congressional figures prominent in its development, and tribal advocates and the National Congress of American Indians were important participants. The Act's declaration of policy affirmed the federal government's continuing relationship with and responsibility to Indigenous peoples while committing Congress to a policy that would permit an orderly transition from federal domination of programs to Indigenous participation in their planning, conduct, and administration. In Title I, the Act authorized the Secretaries of the Interior and of Health, Education, and Welfare, on the request of a tribal governing body, to contract with tribes and tribal organizations for the operation of programs the federal government would otherwise have provided for Indians. Other provisions addressed Indian education, including the involvement of school boards and parents, and amended existing law such as the Johnson-O'Malley Act of 1934, under which the federal government had paid states to educate Indian children.

Several features of the Act deserve emphasis. It established a procedure by which tribes could request to take over programs and by which the agencies could decline only for stated reasons, subject to procedural protections. How to pay the administrative and indirect expenses of operating those programs remained a serious problem from the outset; the express statutory requirement to add contract support costs to program funding was established by the 1988 amendments, not by the original 1975 enactment. It gave tribes the ability to return programs to federal operation. It also addressed federal personnel, allowing federal employees to be detailed to tribes. The Act did not, however, transfer ownership of federal programs to tribes, relieve the federal government of its trust obligations, or define tribal jurisdiction. It created a contractual mechanism through which a tribe would operate a service on behalf of the federal government, according to the terms of a negotiated agreement, with federal funds.

The legislative history reflects the interplay of principles. Supporters presented the Act as honoring treaty relationships by recognizing Indigenous competence and as improving services by placing decisions with the people affected. Concerns appeared about whether the law would be interpreted generously, whether funding would match responsibilities, and whether the shift might, in the worst case, be used by future administrations to reduce federal commitments. These concerns are not mere hindsight; they were voiced at the time, and tribal advocates insisted on safeguards, some of which were later strengthened in the 1988 amendments discussed in §114.18. The same session of Congress also established the American Indian Policy Review Commission, which examined the structure of Indian policy and issued a report in 1977, discussed in §114.19.

Cause and Effect

Why Federal Indian Policy Changed, 1960s–1975

Long-term causes

Persistent Indigenous assertion of political identity; the treaty and trust framework that made the federal relationship a legal one; decades of criticism of Bureau paternalism; and the survival of tribal governments created or encouraged by earlier reforms.

Short-term causes

The visible failures of termination, especially for the Menominee and Klamath; Great Society programs that allowed tribes to administer funds; the civil-rights-era climate; sustained intertribal lobbying; and the direct-action movements that drew national attention after 1969.

Immediate trigger and context

The Johnson and Nixon administrations' policy statements, favorable congressional leaders, and a political calculation that decentralization could be consistent with fiscal and administrative aims.

Transformation and effects

Repudiation of termination in 1970, restoration of the Menominee in 1973, and the 1975 Act, which created a mechanism for tribal assumption of federal programs, with consequences discussed in the remainder of the chapter.

Historical complexity: No single cause is sufficient. Activism without legislative allies would not have produced statutes, and sympathetic officials without Indigenous organization would have had no concrete program to enact. The outcome was also contingent: termination might have continued had its effects not been so visibly damaging, and the resulting policy was a compromise with continuing federal powers, not a pure expression of Indigenous demands.

Section 114.6

Self-Determination Contracts and the Transfer of Federal Programs

In ordinary use, the phrase 638 contract became shorthand for the process through which a tribal government or tribal organization assumed responsibility for programs previously run by the Bureau of Indian Affairs or the Indian Health Service. The mechanism was in principle straightforward. A tribe, acting through its governing body, would request to operate a particular program, such as a road-maintenance operation, a law-enforcement unit, a realty office, a school, or a health clinic. The federal agency would review the proposal, negotiate a contract specifying the services, the funds, the reporting requirements, and the standards, and then transfer the funds and, where appropriate, personnel and equipment. The tribe would hire staff, manage the program, and report to the agency, which retained monitoring and oversight functions.

In practice, contracting was demanding. Tribes had to develop the capacity to negotiate with federal officials, comply with accounting and reporting standards, and manage personnel systems. Early contracts tended to be small and aimed at discrete functions. Federal agencies sometimes responded slowly or restrictively, declining proposals or imposing detailed conditions, and tribes had to learn to use the Act's procedural protections. Funding presented another challenge: tribes received the amount the agency spent on the program, but administering it created expenses, such as audits, insurance, and central administration, that were not necessarily covered. The issue of contract support costs became a long-running dispute and is addressed in §114.8.

Contracting also changed employment and expertise. When a tribe took over a program, many federal employees, including Indigenous ones, transferred to tribal employment or left federal service, and tribes developed their own personnel systems. The Bureau's Indian preference policy, which had long given Indigenous applicants priority in hiring, was upheld by the Supreme Court in Morton v. Mancari (1974) as a political, not a racial, classification arising from the federal relationship with tribes, a decision that reinforced the legal distinction between Indigenous political status and race that Chapter 1 emphasized. Tribal employment preferences in contracted programs likewise built a local workforce.

Contracts did not apply uniformly across the nation. Alaska Native villages and regional nonprofit organizations became important contractors, in a legal context shaped by the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act and examined in Chapter 115. Large nations such as the Navajo had resources and experience; many small nations did not. Some tribes pooled resources through consortia or intertribal organizations, and some chose not to contract at all, preferring continued direct federal service for reasons ranging from limited resources to caution about assuming liability. This variation is an essential point for interpreting the era: the same law offered very different opportunities to different communities.

The distinction between management of federal programs and inherent sovereign authority remains the key conceptual issue. A 638 contract was an agreement through which a tribe performed federal functions under terms negotiated with a federal agency. The authority of a tribal government to govern its territory and citizens, to enact laws, to tax, and to operate courts did not depend on a contract. Yet the two were not entirely separate. Administering programs generated experience, staff, budgets, and political confidence, and tribal governments that managed such programs successfully could argue more persuasively for broader powers. Contracting could therefore function as a practical foundation for the exercise of political authority even though it was not the source of that authority.

Section 114.7

The Transformation of the Bureau of Indian Affairs

As tribes assumed more program responsibilities, the Bureau of Indian Affairs underwent a gradual transformation, though it did not disappear. The Bureau's older role as a direct provider of services, operating schools, maintaining roads, managing forests, and supervising reservation affairs, shifted toward a role that combined remaining direct services with contracting, technical assistance, funding administration, trust management, and coordination with tribal governments. The change varied across regions: in areas where many tribes contracted, area and agency offices shrank in their service functions, while in regions with fewer contractors the Bureau remained a primary provider.

Leadership of the Bureau also changed. Louis R. Bruce, a Mohawk and Oglala Lakota, served as Commissioner from 1969 to 1973, and he and his successors worked in a political environment of reform and conflict, including the occupation of the Bureau's Washington headquarters in 1972 (Chapter 111). In 1977 the Interior Department created the position of Assistant Secretary for Indian Affairs, and Forrest Gerard, a Blackfeet, became its first occupant, giving the Bureau's leadership a higher departmental rank. Ada Deer, of the Menominee, later held the same office. The appointment of Indigenous administrators was meaningful, but it did not remove structural constraints: the Bureau remained part of the Department of the Interior, subject to the Secretary, to the White House budget process, to congressional committees, and to the statutes it administered.

The Bureau also remained influential through regulation, budget, and oversight. Federal regulations governed contracting, procurement, and reporting; the Bureau decided, within limits, how to distribute funds among tribes and programs; and it retained authority over many trust functions, including approving certain leases, timber sales, and land transactions. A tribal government that contracted a program usually remained dependent on the Bureau for funding, interpretation of rules, and technical support. Moreover, the federal workforce did not unanimously welcome tribal contracting, which threatened jobs and institutional roles, and the Bureau's response varied by office and by period. Tribal leaders sometimes described a mixture of cooperation and resistance within the same agency.

These developments complicate any simple narrative of decline. The Bureau's responsibilities in trust management of lands and resources continued, and later controversies over the federal government's management of Indigenous trust funds, which surfaced prominently in the 1990s, showed that the Bureau retained significant duties. The self-determination era thus changed the Bureau's relationship to tribal governments without ending its importance, and the movement from direct provision toward coordination is best described as partial and ongoing.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Administering Indian Programs, 1950s–1994

What Changed

  • Direct federal administration of many programs gave way to tribal contracting and, later, compacting.
  • Tribal governments developed administrative departments, budgets, and professional staff.
  • Federal policy shifted from seeking to end the federal relationship to affirming a government-to-government relationship.
  • Indigenous people increasingly held senior federal positions and managed programs affecting their own communities.

What Stayed the Same

  • The federal government retained trust responsibilities, legal authority, and control of appropriations.
  • Tribal programs depended heavily on federal funding and were subject to federal rules.
  • Many Indigenous communities continued to experience poverty and inadequate services.
  • Indigenous nations continued to assert political authority that predated federal programs.

Timing and pattern: Change was gradual, uneven, and contested. It began in the mid-1960s, accelerated after 1975, and continued to develop through the 1980s and early 1990s. Large nations with experienced administrators experienced it earlier and more fully; small or remote communities often encountered more limited change. Why the pattern matters: It cautions against reading the era either as a simple liberation or as a mere rebranding of federal control.

Section 114.8

Federal Trust Responsibility and the Limits of Administrative Autonomy

The legislation of the self-determination era depended on a principle that Indigenous leaders had long insisted upon and that federal officials repeatedly reaffirmed: the United States owed Indigenous nations obligations that arose from treaties, statutes, and the federal government's long course of dealing, and those obligations did not disappear when tribes administered programs. The Supreme Court had used the vocabulary of trust and guardianship since the nineteenth century, and in Seminole Nation v. United States (1942) it spoke of the government's moral obligations of the highest responsibility and trust. In the 1970s and 1980s, courts and Congress continued to work out what the trust responsibility meant in practice, in cases such as United States v. Mitchell, decided in 1980 and again in 1983, which distinguished between a general trust relationship and specific statutory duties capable of supporting damages claims.

Historical Vocabulary
Trust Responsibility

Definition. The federal trust responsibility is the body of legal and moral obligations that the United States owes to Indigenous nations and individuals, arising from treaties, statutes, executive actions, and judicial doctrine, including duties relating to the protection of lands, resources, and the provision of services.

Historical context. The concept developed in nineteenth-century Supreme Court decisions that described tribes as dependent in some respects while recognizing their distinct political status. It served at times to justify federal control and at times as the basis for claims against the government.

Why it matters. The doctrine allowed advocates of self-determination to argue that Indigenous control of programs should not release the federal government from financial and legal obligations.

Potential misunderstanding. Trust responsibility is not simply a synonym for benevolent guardianship, and not every aspect is judicially enforceable. Its specific content depends on the relevant treaty, statute, or fiduciary relationship.

The practical question was therefore where the line fell between administrative responsibility, which tribes were assuming, and legal responsibility, which remained federal. Congress wrote this distinction into the 1975 Act's declaration of policy and into later amendments, and Indigenous leaders emphasized that contracting should be an exercise of choice rather than an abandonment by the government. When a tribe operated a program, the federal agency was expected to continue funding it, but the agency also remained accountable for the performance of the obligation it had contracted out. This created a continuing need for oversight, which could slide from legitimate monitoring into the very micromanagement the policy was meant to reduce.

Money was the persistent difficulty. Tribal programs depended on annual congressional appropriations, which varied with political and fiscal conditions. Contract support costs, meaning qualifying indirect and other administrative expenses a tribe incurred when managing a contracted program, were frequently underfunded, leaving tribes to cover shortfalls from other funds or to reduce services. Congress explicitly required the addition of contract support costs to the base program amount in the 1988 amendments; that statutory protection did not exist in the same form in the original 1975 law. Yet appropriations and payment disputes continued; the issue reached the Supreme Court in the twenty-first century in cases such as Cherokee Nation v. Leavitt (2005) and Salazar v. Ramah Navajo Chapter (2012), both decided in favor of tribal contractors. These later rulings lie beyond the chapter's principal chronology, but they illustrate that the financial terms of self-determination remained disputed long after the statute's enactment.

Federal oversight also remained a feature. Contracts required reporting and audits, and federal agencies and oversight bodies periodically examined tribal performance. Tribal leaders recognized the need for accountability, particularly for the use of public funds, but objected to inconsistent requirements and to oversight that treated tribal governments as less capable than state or local governments. The tension was real: transferring responsibility without adequate resources could undermine services and generate criticism that blamed tribes for failures rooted in funding. The point that should be kept in mind is the third editorial standard of this chapter. Greater administrative responsibility did not automatically mean financial autonomy, and a government can operate a program while remaining bound by funding conditions and legal requirements established elsewhere.

Division III — Education, Health, and Community Institutions · Section 114.9

Tribal Control of Elementary and Secondary Education

Education was among the first and most emotionally significant fields in which self-determination took institutional form. For generations, schooling had been an instrument of assimilation (Chapters 87 and 88). Bureau and mission boarding schools had removed many children from their families and suppressed Indigenous languages, and Bureau day schools and public schools often gave Indigenous parents little voice. The Senate's 1969 report of its Special Subcommittee on Indian Education, titled Indian Education: A National Tragedy—A National Challenge and associated with Senator Robert Kennedy's initiative and Senator Edward Kennedy's chairmanship, documented widespread failure and the absence of Indigenous control, and it provided a powerful public argument for reform.

Experiments in community control preceded the statutes. At Rough Rock, the school board composed of local Navajo residents set policies, hired staff, and introduced Navajo language and culture into the curriculum, while other Navajo communities pioneered related experiments: at Ramah, a chapter meeting established the Ramah Navajo School Board on February 6, 1970, and the board incorporated on February 10. These organizers became leaders in the movement for community-controlled education. These schools relied on contracts and grants, and their founders learned practical lessons about budgeting, teacher recruitment, and relations with federal officials. Their example showed that parents and tribal authorities could govern schools and that Indigenous language and cultural instruction could be part of the curriculum, though the schools also faced difficulties, including financial instability and disputes about standards.

Federal law steadily expanded these opportunities. The Indian Education Act of 1972, Title IV of the Education Amendments of that year, established a federal Office of Indian Education, funded programs in public schools serving Indian students, and required parent committees to participate in planning those programs. This was important because most Indigenous children attended public schools, not Bureau schools. The 1975 Self-Determination Act enabled tribes to contract to operate Bureau schools and strengthened the role of Indian school boards. The Education Amendments of 1978 reorganized the Bureau's education system, gave local school boards authority over matters such as hiring and curriculum in Bureau-funded schools, and set standards and funding formulas. In 1988 the Tribally Controlled Schools Act further authorized grants for tribally operated schools, offering an alternative to contract arrangements.

What did control mean in practice? School boards could hire principals and teachers, increasingly including Indigenous educators; they could adopt bilingual programs, a possibility reinforced by federal bilingual education legislation from 1968; they could revise curricula to include local history and culture; and they could involve elders and parents in school life. Control had limits. Federal funding formulas, accreditation requirements, testing mandates, and the shortage of trained Indigenous teachers constrained choices, and many schools remained poorly funded and isolated. Educational accountability also generated debate: critics of the Bureau system argued that local control was needed to improve results, while others warned that unrealistic expectations or inadequate resources could leave community schools to bear blame for outcomes they could not control alone. The history of language instruction in particular, examined further in Chapter 123, shows that school control was a necessary but not a sufficient condition for cultural continuity.

Section 114.10

Tribal Colleges and the Expansion of Indigenous Higher Education

Higher education followed a similar logic. Few Indigenous students attended college in the early 1960s, and those who did generally left their communities to attend institutions with little connection to Indigenous life. Many Indigenous leaders concluded that communities needed their own postsecondary institutions, rooted in reservation settings, able to train teachers, nurses, administrators, and technicians, and committed to Indigenous languages and knowledge. The pioneering institution was Navajo Community College, chartered by the Navajo Tribal Council in 1968 and opening in 1969, with Robert Roessel, who had been involved in the Rough Rock experiment, as its first president. It was the first college established and controlled by an Indigenous nation.

Other tribally controlled institutions soon appeared, especially on Plains reservations, and several colleges formed the American Indian Higher Education Consortium in the early 1970s to share resources and advocate for support. The Navajo Community College Act of 1971 provided direct federal funding for the Navajo institution, and the Tribally Controlled Community College Assistance Act of 1978 created a broader framework for funding tribal colleges through the Bureau of Indian Affairs. These institutions combined vocational programs, general education, and often teacher training, while grounding instruction in local language and culture. They also served adults and families in communities that lacked other nearby institutions.

The historical significance of the colleges lay partly in their contribution to administrative capacity. A tribal government that wished to take over programs needed accountants, program managers, health professionals, and teachers, many of whom were trained locally. The colleges thus connected educational self-determination to governance. They also faced chronic funding limitations, since federal appropriations rarely matched authorizations and tribes themselves had limited revenue, and their accreditation and long-term stability required sustained effort. This chapter reserves the broader history of tribal colleges, including their later growth and land-grant status, for Chapter 124, and here emphasizes their emergence as institutions of educational self-determination.

Section 114.11

The Indian Health Service and Tribal Health Administration

Health care exposed the stakes of administrative responsibility in an especially direct way. Federal responsibility for Indigenous health had roots in treaties, which sometimes included provisions for physicians or medical care, and in later statutes. Responsibility passed from the Bureau of Indian Affairs to the Public Health Service in 1955, creating the Indian Health Service. The IHS expanded facilities and personnel and addressed some diseases effectively, but by the 1960s and 1970s many Indigenous communities faced shortages of clinics and hospitals, inadequate staffing, remote locations, and health outcomes worse than those of the general population. Historians and health researchers have used IHS reports, congressional hearings, and community testimony to document these problems, while recognizing that data quality varies and that comparisons require care.

The Indian Health Care Improvement Act of 1976, signed on September 30, 1976, declared a federal commitment to raising Indigenous health status and authorized funding for facilities, health professionals, and services. Among other measures, it addressed the recruitment and training of Indigenous health workers, provided for urban Indian health programs, and allowed IHS facilities to receive reimbursement from Medicare and Medicaid, creating a new revenue source. The Act was authorized in specific amounts, but appropriations did not always follow, a recurring pattern that reflected the broader tension between legal promise and fiscal reality.

Self-determination contracting allowed tribes and tribal organizations to take over clinics, programs, and in time hospitals previously run by the IHS. One early form of local involvement was the Community Health Representative program, begun in 1968, which trained community members to provide health education, basic services, and liaison between residents and professionals, and which became an important source of employment and local knowledge. Tribes and intertribal health boards, among them the National Indian Health Board established in 1972, helped build networks of administrators and advocates. Under Title I of the 1975 Act, tribes could assume clinic operations, and some established independent health departments, hired Indigenous physicians and nurses, and integrated traditional healers or culturally informed practice with biomedical care. The distinction between contracting and compacting is chronological: the 1975 law enabled IHS program contracts, while Congress authorized an IHS self-governance compact demonstration under Public Law 102-573 in 1992. The demonstration was therefore already available near this chapter's 1994 endpoint; Congress made the IHS self-governance program permanent in 2000 under Public Law 106-260.

Assessing the relationship between local control and health outcomes requires caution. Tribal operation could improve responsiveness, cultural appropriateness, and employment, and communities often valued control for those reasons. Yet outcomes also depended on funding, the availability of professionals willing to work in remote areas, infrastructure, poverty, and wider social conditions that no single health program could resolve. A tribe operating a clinic with inadequate funds might provide better access and more respectful care while still lacking resources for specialized treatment. The historical record therefore supports a measured conclusion: local control changed who managed and shaped services and increased tribal institutional capacity, while resource constraints continued to limit what those institutions could accomplish.

Section 114.12

Housing, Social Services, and Community Development

Education and health were highly visible, but self-determination also reached the quieter institutions of everyday life. Housing on many reservations in the mid-twentieth century was scarce and often substandard, and it was financed through a patchwork of federal programs. Beginning in the 1960s, tribes established housing authorities under tribal or state law to administer federally funded housing, including the Department of Housing and Urban Development's Mutual Help program, in which families contributed labor or other resources toward eventual ownership. Housing authorities hired staff, managed construction contracts, and set rules for tenants and homebuyers, becoming among the largest and most visible tribal institutions. They also revealed difficulties that tribal housing programs have faced, including the complexity of trust land ownership, financing, and the need for utilities and infrastructure. A fuller reform of Indian housing law, the Native American Housing Assistance and Self-Determination Act, came in 1996, after this chapter's endpoint.

Employment and training offered another channel. Under the Comprehensive Employment and Training Act of 1973 and related legislation, tribes could operate employment programs as sponsors, in the same period that the Economic Opportunity programs were being reorganized, and tribes also administered programs such as Head Start, nutrition and elder services, and social services. The Bureau's own social-services functions, including general assistance and certain child and family services, could also be contracted. These programs mattered because they generated jobs on reservations where private employment was limited and because they gave tribal governments a role in the delivery of services that affected families directly. They also exposed tribal governments to the volatility of federal budgets, as shifts in national priorities, including reductions in the early 1980s, could rapidly alter local program capacity.

Locally designed development initiatives illustrate the potential of the period. In Oklahoma, the Cherokee Nation's community development efforts of the late 1970s and 1980s, in which Wilma Mankiller was a prominent figure, supported projects such as the self-help water and housing work in the rural community of Bell, where residents contributed labor to improve their own infrastructure. Such initiatives were important less because they were typical than because they showed an approach in which tribal government, federal funds, and community participation worked together. Elders, community workers, and ordinary families were not passive beneficiaries, but contributors whose knowledge and labor shaped the results.

Students should distinguish these developments from the child-welfare legislation of 1978, which addressed the removal of Indigenous children by state and private agencies and is examined in Chapter 131. The two histories are related, because both concerned the capacity of Indigenous governments to protect families, but they involve different legal mechanisms. Here the focus is on how self-determination reorganized routine institutions of public life.

Everyday Life and Social History

What Self-Determination Looked Like in a Community

For many residents, self-determination was not an abstract policy but a change in who made familiar decisions. A parent might notice that a school board, composed of neighbors, now set the school calendar or decided whether the Native language would be taught. A patient might encounter a community health representative who spoke the language and knew the family, rather than a rotating federal employee. A young adult might find employment in the tribal housing authority, the tribal government's finance office, or a college that had not existed a generation earlier. An elder might be invited to advise on curriculum or on the cultural content of a health program.

Not all changes were welcome or evenly distributed. Tribal employment could create jealousies and questions about favoritism in small communities. Residents sometimes criticized tribal administrators as remote or unresponsive, and programs depending on year-to-year federal funding could be abruptly curtailed. Reservation communities also differed enormously: a densely populated nation with an established bureaucracy and a small, isolated community with a part-time council experienced self-determination in very different ways. Historians reconstruct such experiences from oral histories, tribal newsletters, school and clinic records, and local newspapers, noting that these sources are uneven in survival and sometimes reflect the perspective of those who held office or wrote the records.

Division IV — The Reconstruction of Indigenous Governing Capacity · Section 114.13

Tribal Governments and the Exercise of Political Authority

Taking responsibility for programs required governments able to carry that responsibility, and the self-determination era therefore saw considerable change in the structure of tribal governments themselves. Many governments operating in 1970 had been created under the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 or under comparable federal frameworks (Chapter 97). Their constitutions often followed models prepared by federal officials and required secretarial approval of important actions. During the 1970s and 1980s, many nations revised their constitutions, expanded their councils' powers, created executive offices and administrative departments, and established or strengthened judicial systems. Some adopted new names that expressed political identity: the Navajo Tribal Council, for example, adopted the name Navajo Nation for the government in 1969, and the Cherokee Nation prepared a revised constitution approved for referendum in 1975 and ratified by Cherokee voters on June 26, 1976, replacing earlier arrangements and reaffirming national governmental authority.

Nations approached governmental authority in different ways, reflecting their own histories. Some, like the Navajo, developed a large centralized government with a legislative council, a chairman or president, and an extensive bureaucracy. The Pueblos of New Mexico generally retained governance systems in which religious and civil authorities of each Pueblo, operating under longstanding community traditions, coexisted with elected officials who dealt with federal agencies. The Haudenosaunee communities included both elected councils recognized by governments of the United States and traditional leadership based on clan and confederacy structures (Chapter 26). The Hopi villages maintained village-based authority, and disputes between traditional leaders and the elected Hopi Tribal Council, created under the Indian Reorganization Act, reflected competing views of political legitimacy. In Oklahoma, nations that had been dismantled by allotment and statehood rebuilt government on foundations that had been weakened but not extinguished.

These examples show why a distinction is needed between federal-recognition frameworks and Indigenous nations' own sources of legitimacy. Federal recognition, formalized through statutes, treaties, administrative practice, and, from 1978, the Interior Department's acknowledgment regulations for unrecognized groups, determined whether the United States dealt with a community as a tribal government. It did not create the community's political traditions. Many Indigenous people grounded the legitimacy of their governments in kinship, ceremony, treaty relationships, and community consent, and some disputed the authority of governments organized under federal templates. The self-determination era brought these differences into the open, because contracting required a recognized governing body to sign agreements, and federal law often treated the elected council as the relevant authority.

Political authority in this period was exercised in several domains: negotiating with federal and state governments, controlling membership, regulating land use and business, operating courts and law enforcement, and providing services. The extent of authority in each domain depended on federal law and court decisions, some of which restricted it. The Supreme Court's decision in Oliphant v. Suquamish Indian Tribe (1978), for instance, held that tribes lacked criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians, illustrating that tribal governments' expanded administrative capacity coexisted with judicial limits on their powers. The jurisdictional questions are the subject of Chapter 118, and this chapter notes them to emphasize that capacity to administer did not equal unlimited authority to govern.

Section 114.14

Building Tribal Administrative Institutions

The daily work of government consists of routine functions that rarely attract historical attention but determine whether programs operate. Taking over a clinic, a school, or a roads program meant establishing budgets, hiring and supervising employees, keeping financial records, purchasing supplies, complying with audit requirements, writing grant applications, managing property, and responding to federal reporting. For tribal governments accustomed to limited roles, assembling these capacities was a major task. A nation that had previously employed a handful of people might in a few years manage a payroll in the hundreds, and it needed accounting systems, personnel policies, legal advice, and procurement rules that previously had been unnecessary.

Nations met these needs in different ways. Many created administrative departments for finance, planning, human resources, education, and health, and established tribal codes governing employment and contracts. They hired accountants, attorneys, and program managers, some of them Indigenous professionals who had been trained in the new tribal colleges or through federal scholarships, and some of them outside consultants. Technical assistance from federal agencies, intertribal organizations, and universities supplemented tribal resources. A recurring question was how to maintain the political authority of elected or traditionally constituted leaders while relying on professional staff, since specialized administrators could acquire considerable influence over decisions. Some nations adopted systems of oversight, such as legislative review of budgets and independent audits, to address this tension.

Administrative institution-building also required legal development. Tribes drafted codes on employment, procurement, and sovereign immunity, established tribal courts or strengthened existing ones, and negotiated agreements with state and local governments. Legal advisers, including both tribal staff and outside counsel, became important participants in tribal governance, and Indigenous law schools and legal services organizations such as the Native American Rights Fund, founded in 1970, supported this development. The process was uneven: nations with larger populations and revenues could afford professional departments, while smaller ones depended on a few individuals, consortia, or federal technical help.

The administrative transformation also raised a question of accountability to whom. A contractor had to document expenditures for the federal agency, but a tribal government also owed an account of the same program to its citizens. Tribal officials could face competing pressures when federal forms, performance categories, or procurement rules did not match the priorities expressed at community meetings. Governments consequently had to develop their own budget procedures, personnel policies, records systems, and channels for public complaint, not merely copy federal paperwork. Such institution building was consequential precisely because a school, clinic, or housing department became a place where citizens expected their government to explain choices and correct failures.

The costs of administration illustrate the connection to funding discussed in §114.8. An accounting office, an audit, or an insurance policy was necessary to operate a program but did not directly deliver services, and if federal funds did not cover such costs, tribes had to divert resources. This administrative dimension is easy to overlook, yet it was an essential element of whether self-determination was workable. A policy that transferred responsibility without funding the capacity to carry it out risked setting tribal governments up for criticism and failure, which is why contract support costs became so central to tribal advocacy.

Section 114.15

Natural Resources, Land Administration, and Tribal Decision-Making

Natural resources were among the most consequential areas in which expanded tribal capacity mattered, because land and its resources were the basis of Indigenous economies and political claims. Under the trust system, the Bureau of Indian Affairs and other federal agencies managed forests, rangelands, agricultural leases, minerals, and water on Indigenous lands, and the Bureau's decisions about timber sales, grazing permits, and leasing had large effects. Tribal governments, in the earlier period, frequently had little say in these decisions beyond approving or objecting to proposals.

Self-determination changed the possibilities. Tribes developed natural-resource departments, employed foresters, range managers, fisheries biologists, and planners, and exercised more influence over management plans. The Menominee forest enterprise, restored after the nation's reconstitution, was a well-known example of a tribal operation managing timber on a sustained-yield basis. Intertribal organizations, such as the Intertribal Timber Council formed in the 1970s, and, in the area of energy and minerals, the Council of Energy Resource Tribes, founded in the mid-1970s, provided technical support and political coordination (see Chapter 136 for later energy and mining conflicts). Fisheries management offered an especially visible illustration: following the 1974 federal court decision in United States v. Washington (Chapter 113), Pacific Northwest tribes established the Northwest Indian Fisheries Commission and employed their own biologists and enforcement staff as co-managers of the resource with the state. Such arrangements show how legal victories and administrative capacity reinforced each other.

Effective government required both decision-making authority and the capacity to implement decisions. A tribe might hold treaty rights to fish, but without biologists, data, and enforcement personnel it could not participate effectively in managing the fishery. Conversely, a well-staffed department without legal authority could only advise. The experience also demonstrated the continuing federal role: many land and resource transactions still required Bureau approval, and trust management, including the accounting of income from tribal and individual Indian trust assets, remained a federal responsibility. This was a limit of administrative autonomy, and tribal governments pursued greater control through legislation and litigation in the following decades.

These developments did not create a single national model of resource control. Tribal forestry, grazing, fisheries, and mineral programs began from different ecological settings and different legal powers. Timber management required inventories, harvest schedules, and market planning; grazing administration involved leases and range conditions; fisheries co-management required biological data and negotiations across jurisdictions. The growing need for specialized employees made education and administration directly relevant to stewardship. In every setting, however, the ability to employ staff and gather evidence remained distinct from the power to approve or refuse a lease, harvest, or project when federal law retained that decision.

Section 114.16

Tribal Leadership, Community Participation, and Internal Political Debate

Indigenous communities were not unified in their views of how self-determination should operate. Differences existed about the structure of government, the balance between traditional and elected authority, the use of federal funds, the pace of change, and the role of outside expertise. These disagreements were an ordinary part of political life, as in any society, and they shaped the institutions that emerged. Treating the period as a conflict only between tribes and the federal government overlooks the internal debates through which communities defined their own priorities.

Leadership itself was changing. Women held prominent positions in tribal government and community institutions, and their roles included service as council members, program directors, and chiefs. Ada Deer's leadership in the Menominee restoration and Wilma Mankiller's election as deputy chief of the Cherokee Nation in 1983 and as Principal Chief in 1985 are among the best-known examples, though many less publicized women directed schools, clinics, and community programs. Younger professionals trained in law, education, and administration entered government alongside elders and traditional authorities, and the relationship among these groups could be cooperative or tense. Some communities placed substantial authority with elders and religious leaders in the evaluation of programs touching cultural matters, while others emphasized electoral accountability.

Self-determination also raised concerns about replacing external paternalism with local institutions that might be equally unresponsive. Critics in various communities argued that councils or administrators could monopolize jobs and resources, that political factions could use program funding for patronage, or that tribal governments could neglect those with less influence. Some of these conflicts became violent or constitutional crises. On the Pine Ridge Reservation in the early 1970s, conflict over tribal government and federal funding formed part of the background to the Wounded Knee occupation (Chapter 112). The Navajo Nation experienced a serious political crisis in 1989 over allegations of corruption involving its then-chairman and the conflict that followed, which led to a reform in which the nation established distinct legislative, executive, and judicial branches. These episodes illustrate that institution-building required accountability mechanisms and that communities themselves often demanded them.

Cultural continuity was a further subject of debate. Some leaders saw administrative modernization as a means to protect language, ceremony, and land, while others worried that bureaucratic structures and federal reporting requirements could erode community norms. These debates did not follow a single pattern. They are best read as evidence that self-determination was an arena of Indigenous political argument, not merely the implementation of a federal program.

Division V — Consolidation, Self-Governance, and Historical Consequences · Section 114.17

The Self-Governance Movement of the 1980s

By the early 1980s, tribal leaders who had used the 1975 Act extensively had identified its limitations. Contracting proceeded program by program, with each contract carrying its own negotiation, budget line, and reporting. A tribe operating a dozen programs might therefore deal with a dozen federal offices and sets of requirements, even though from the community's perspective those programs were parts of a single set of needs. Tribal governments could not easily move money between programs to meet local priorities, redesign services, or reduce administrative duplication. Federal approval processes remained cumbersome, and the Bureau retained significant discretion over what counted as an eligible program and what funds were available.

The political environment also shifted. President Reagan's statement on Indian policy of January 24, 1983, reaffirmed a government-to-government relationship, rejected termination, and endorsed tribal self-government and economic development, showing continuity in the policy's rhetoric across administrations. At the same time, the early 1980s brought reductions in federal domestic spending, which in real terms cut many Indian programs and increased tribal interest in more flexible administration and in revenue beyond federal appropriations. A presidential commission on reservation economies, convened in 1983, emphasized economic development and government capacity, and later chapters (121 and 122) explore the expanded tribal economic initiatives of the decade.

The self-governance movement drew strongly on Pacific Northwest tribes, notably the Lummi and Quinault, whose leaders argued that a nation with proven administrative experience should be able to negotiate a single agreement with the Interior Department, receive funds in a consolidated amount, and decide how to allocate them. They envisioned a relationship in which the tribe's government set priorities, and the federal role shifted from approving each program to reviewing aggregate performance and honoring continuing trust obligations. Tribal leaders also stressed a conceptual shift: self-governance was framed not as the federal government delegating tasks but as a government-to-government relationship in which a tribal government negotiated with the United States on the basis of its political status.

Congressional support developed through hearings and advocacy by tribal leaders, intertribal organizations, and some members of Congress. Federal officials and some tribal leaders raised concerns. Federal agencies worried about accountability for funds, the appropriate level of oversight, and the effect on programs, and some tribes feared that a new framework would create inequalities between participants and non-participants or reduce services if funding were not secure. The result was an incremental approach: a demonstration project to test the concept before establishing a permanent system.

Section 114.18

The 1988 Demonstration Program and the Tribal Self-Governance Act of 1994

The 1988 amendments to the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act, enacted as Public Law 100-472 on October 5, 1988, addressed both the problems of contracting and the demand for self-governance. They strengthened tribal rights in the contracting process, including the procedures and appeals when agencies declined proposals, and, crucially, added an express statutory requirement for contract support costs in addition to the underlying program amount. In Title III, they authorized a Tribal Self-Governance Demonstration Project within the Department of the Interior, allowing a limited number of tribes, up to twenty, to negotiate agreements and receive funds for programs they could administer with greater discretion. The same year, Congress passed the Tribally Controlled Schools Act, authorizing grants for tribal schools.

The demonstration entered implementation in the early 1990s, but initial agreements preceded 1991: the Quinault Indian Nation and the United States agreed to a self-governance compact on June 27, 1990, with an intended October 1990 term start subject to the compact's effective-date conditions. Early annual funding arrangements and implementation followed, including during 1991. The date of a negotiated compact must not be conflated with the start of all of its funded operations. Participating tribes, including Northwest nations that had been central to the movement, gained opportunities to negotiate annual funding agreements, redesign programs, and reallocate funds within agreed parameters. Evaluations and congressional testimony by participants suggested improved efficiency and responsiveness, though they also revealed continuing negotiation over which programs and funds could be included. The experience persuaded Congress to make the model permanent.

The Tribal Self-Governance Act of 1994, Public Law 103-413, signed on October 25, 1994, established a permanent self-governance program within the Department of the Interior. It added a new title to the 1975 Act and allowed eligible tribes, after planning and a showing of financial stability, to negotiate compacts and annual funding agreements covering a broad range of Bureau programs, along with some others of special geographic or historical significance to the tribe. IHS self-governance was developing on a related but distinct timetable: Public Law 102-573 extended demonstration compacting authority to the Indian Health Service in 1992, within this chapter's period, while Public Law 106-260 established a permanent IHS self-governance program in 2000. The 1994 Act made the Interior program permanent, not the IHS program.

Historical Vocabulary
Contract and Compact

Definition. A self-determination contract (under Title I of the 1975 Act) transfers responsibility for a specific federal program to a tribe, usually with detailed terms and reporting. A self-governance compact (under the 1994 framework) is an agreement between a tribe and the federal government that establishes the overall government-to-government relationship, with annual funding agreements covering multiple programs and greater flexibility in budgeting and administration.

Historical context. The compact model emerged from tribal dissatisfaction with program-by-program contracting and was tested in the 1988 demonstration project before being made permanent in 1994.

Why it matters. The distinction reveals changing ideas about the relationship between tribal governments and the United States, from agency-style administration toward negotiation between governments.

Potential misunderstanding. A compact does not eliminate the federal trust responsibility or the dependence on appropriations, and it does not itself determine tribal jurisdiction. Not all tribes chose or qualified for compacting.

The differences between contracts and compacts were practical. Under contracts, programs remained discrete, with the Bureau specifying the activities and standards, whereas compacts consolidated funds, reduced the number of required approvals, and allowed tribes to redesign programs in accordance with their own priorities, subject to statutory limits and reporting. Compacts also allowed tribes to deal more directly with the Department's leadership through the Office of Self-Governance than through local Bureau offices. Yet funding remained dependent on congressional appropriations, trust functions remained federal, and the tribes still had to demonstrate financial management capacity to qualify. The 1994 Act therefore refined, but did not replace, the structure created in 1975.

Section 114.19

Achievements, Uneven Implementation, and Continuing Constraints

Assessing the era requires attention to both accomplishments and limits. The achievements were substantial. Indigenous governments assumed responsibility for programs that had been run by federal officials, and in doing so they built administrative departments, trained personnel, and developed experience in public management. Tribal schools and colleges created institutions that served communities and advanced language and cultural instruction. Health programs expanded local participation. The reversal of termination restored nations such as the Menominee, and the political commitment to a government-to-government relationship was repeatedly reaffirmed. Perhaps most important, tribal governments acquired a larger role in decisions affecting daily life and a stronger platform for the legal and political claims examined in later chapters.

Implementation, however, was uneven. Institutional capacity depended on population, existing administrative experience, geography, and access to resources. A large nation with a diversified government and access to professional staff could take on many programs, whereas a small nation with a part-time council and limited funds might find contracting burdensome. Geography mattered, since remote communities faced higher costs and difficulty recruiting professionals. Economic conditions also varied; the era preceded the expansion of tribal gaming and enterprise discussed in Chapters 121 and 122, and many nations relied almost entirely on federal appropriations. Federal agencies themselves differed in their willingness to implement the policy, and the experience of a tribe could depend on particular officials as much as on the statute.

Constraints persisted. Federal appropriations did not keep pace with needs, and contract support costs remained disputed. Tribal governments continued to face jurisdictional limitations, such as those in Oliphant and under Public Law 280, and federal oversight remained extensive. The American Indian Policy Review Commission, created in 1975 and chaired by Senator Abourezk, issued a final report in 1977 that recommended strengthening tribal governments and reaffirming Indigenous sovereignty, but Congress did not adopt its recommendations as a whole, and Representative Lloyd Meeds issued a dissent that argued for limits on tribal authority. In the late 1970s, a backlash emerged among some non-Indian groups and legislators who sought to limit treaty rights and tribal jurisdiction, producing bills that Congress ultimately did not pass but that demonstrated the political fragility of the policy.

Historians therefore describe the era as a period of significant but conditional expansion. The conditions included dependence on federal funding, the continued power of federal agencies, and the legal doctrines that limited tribal authority. The following debate presents the principal ways scholars and participants have interpreted this mixed record.

Historical Debate

Did Self-Determination Expand Indigenous Autonomy or Preserve Structural Dependence?

The question: Did the policies of the self-determination era meaningfully expand the political autonomy of Indigenous nations, or did they leave tribal governments structurally dependent on federal institutions, funding, and rules? The question concerns degree, institutional design, and evidence, and it does not reduce to a choice between complete independence and continued federal domination.

Interpretation A — Meaningful expansion: Scholars and tribal leaders who emphasize expansion point to concrete changes: tribes managed schools, clinics, and programs; controlled hiring and priorities; developed professional governments; and gained negotiating leverage with federal agencies. They argue that administrative capacity is a foundation of political authority and that compacting, in particular, redefined the relationship as one between governments.
Interpretation B — Persistent dependence: Others stress that tribal programs were funded by federal appropriations, governed by federal regulations, and exposed to budget cuts and oversight, and that tribes often took on responsibilities without matching resources. They argue that the framework could make tribal governments administrators of federal programs, with accountability running upward to federal agencies more than to their own citizens.
Interpretation C — Mixed and variable outcomes: Many historians emphasize that outcomes varied across nations and over time, and that the same institutions could be both a source of autonomy and a channel of dependence. On this view, the more useful question is under what conditions, and for which nations, contracting and compacting expanded actual decision-making power.

Evidence: Relevant evidence includes statutory texts and regulations, contract and compact terms, appropriations records, tribal budgets and council records, federal oversight reports, litigation over contract support costs, oral histories, and comparisons of different nations' experiences.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement is partly interpretive (what counts as autonomy), partly about scale (how many communities experienced meaningful change), and partly about evidence, since measuring decision-making power is more difficult than counting programs.

Why it matters: How historians answer shapes whether self-determination appears as a foundation of contemporary nationhood or as a modernized form of federal administration, and affects how later developments are read.

Section 114.20

The Era of Self-Determination in Historical Perspective

The era examined in this chapter transformed the institutional landscape of Indigenous nations in the United States. In the span of about three decades, federal policy moved from the active pursuit of termination to formal commitments to self-determination and self-governance, and Indigenous nations moved from environments dominated by federal administration to ones in which many operated their own schools, clinics, and departments. The transformation depended on Indigenous organization, from community movements like that of the Menominee to the intertribal advocacy of national organizations, and on a convergence of federal leaders, Congress, courts, and the wider political climate.

The chapter's central argument bears repeating. Self-determination did not create Indigenous sovereignty. It provided new legal and administrative mechanisms through which Indigenous nations could exercise authority they had long claimed and defended. The mechanisms mattered because rights and claims are not self-executing: a nation that asserts authority must also be able to deliver services, manage resources, hire staff, and make decisions that its citizens can see. At the same time, the mechanisms were limited, since they operated within a federal framework that retained power over funding, regulation, and key aspects of jurisdiction. The period should therefore not be described as the completion of decolonization or as the disappearance of federal constraints.

The four editorial distinctions of this chapter summarize the interpretation. Sovereignty and self-determination are not synonyms. Policy reversal was gradual and required legislative, administrative, and tribal action. Program administration is not equivalent to complete governmental autonomy. And institutional change must be understood in the lives of communities, in classrooms, clinics, offices, and homes, not only in presidential statements and statutes. A student who can explain each of these points has grasped the principal historical lesson of the period.

The distinction between legal possibility and institutional practice provides the chapter's final historical test. Congress could authorize tribal program management, but each community still confronted the work of negotiating with agencies, developing trained staff, deciding priorities, maintaining services, and persuading citizens that new institutions answered to them. A nation that chose to keep an eligible program under federal operation did not thereby surrender sovereignty; choosing whether, when, and how to administer that program could itself express self-government. The uneven outcomes of the era are therefore not exceptions to its history but evidence of what implementation meant across diverse nations.

The institutions strengthened during these years became the foundations for later developments. Tribal governments with administrative experience were better positioned to pursue land recovery and the protection of sacred and cultural resources (Chapter 119), to assert water rights (Chapter 120), to defend jurisdiction in courts (Chapter 118), to build economic enterprises (Chapters 121 and 122), to revitalize languages (Chapter 123), and to develop tribal colleges (Chapter 124). The experience also shaped the contemporary governments examined in Chapter 139. Part XX turns to these struggles over land, law, and nationhood, which build on, and in some cases contest the limits of, the administrative framework that this chapter has described.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the transition of federal Indian policy in the United States from termination toward self-determination and self-governance between roughly 1960 and 1994. Its central argument is that self-determination did not create Indigenous sovereignty but provided new legal and administrative mechanisms through which Indigenous nations could exercise authority they had long claimed and defended. The period is best understood as institutional reconstruction: the rebuilding and expansion of tribal governing capacity within, and sometimes against, the constraints of the federal system.

Termination left a legacy of economic disruption, political conflict, and fear. Formal termination ended federal recognition of specific nations such as the Menominee and Klamath, while the wider assimilationist environment, including Public Law 280, relocation, and Bureau paternalism, affected nations that retained recognition. Indigenous opposition developed through tribal councils, the National Congress of American Indians, the Chicago conference of 1961, legal advocacy, and later direct action. Great Society programs allowed tribes to administer funds directly and provided early models of tribal program management and community-controlled schooling.

Presidents Johnson and Nixon adopted the language of self-determination, and Nixon's July 8, 1970, message formally rejected termination. The message was a policy commitment that required legislation, appropriations, and administration. Congress returned Blue Lake to Taos Pueblo in 1970 and restored the Menominee in 1973, the latter following a community-led campaign that demonstrated how termination could be reversed.

The Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975 established the principal legal mechanism: self-determination contracts through which tribes could assume administration of eligible federal programs. The Act did not transfer sovereignty or eliminate federal responsibility. It created contractual relationships, subject to federal funding and regulation. The Bureau of Indian Affairs shifted gradually from direct service provision toward contracting and coordination, while retaining substantial powers, especially in trust management. Disputes over contract support costs and appropriations illustrated that administrative responsibility did not equal financial autonomy.

Education, health, housing, and social services became principal arenas of change. Community-controlled schools such as Rough Rock, the Indian Education Act of 1972, the 1975 Act, the Education Amendments of 1978, and the Tribally Controlled Schools Act of 1988 shifted decision-making toward school boards, parents, and tribes. Navajo Community College and the Tribally Controlled Community College Assistance Act of 1978 launched tribal higher education. The Indian Health Care Improvement Act of 1976 and health contracting increased tribal participation in health care, and housing authorities and employment and training programs expanded the institutions of everyday life.

Tribal governments themselves changed. Nations revised constitutions, created administrative departments, developed professional staff, strengthened courts and codes, and expanded their roles in natural-resource management, as in Pacific Northwest fisheries co-management. Their approaches differed, reflecting distinct political traditions, from the large centralized government of the Navajo Nation to Pueblo systems that combined traditional and elected authority. Communities also debated the structure of government, the roles of elders, women, and younger professionals, accountability, and cultural continuity, showing that self-determination was an arena of Indigenous political argument.

Dissatisfaction with program-by-program contracting led to the self-governance movement of the 1980s, championed by nations such as the Lummi and Quinault. The 1988 amendments authorized an Interior demonstration project and established explicit contract-support funding requirements. Initial compacts were agreed by 1990, with early funding arrangements following in 1991; Congress authorized an IHS demonstration in 1992 and made the Interior framework permanent in the Tribal Self-Governance Act of 1994. Compacts consolidated programs and increased flexibility, but remained subject to appropriations and federal trust functions.

The period produced significant but conditional gains. Implementation was uneven because of differences in capacity, geography, and resources; constraints included funding shortfalls, jurisdictional limits such as those in Oliphant, federal oversight, and political backlash. Historians debate whether the outcome represented meaningful autonomy, persistent dependence, or a variable mixture. The institutions built during these years became foundations for later struggles over land, jurisdiction, economic development, language, and nationhood, discussed in the chapters that follow.

Johnson-O'Malley Act
A 1934 law authorizing federal contracts with states and others for the education and services of Indian children, later amended by the 1975 Act.
Tribally controlled school
A school operated by a tribe, tribal organization, or community school board, by contract or grant, rather than directly by the federal government.
Tribal college
A postsecondary institution chartered and controlled by a tribe, beginning with Navajo Community College in 1968.
Community health representative
A community member trained to provide health education and services; a program begun in 1968.
Indian Health Service (IHS)
The federal health agency created within the Public Health Service in 1955 to provide health services to Indigenous people.
Administrative capacity
The institutional ability of a government to manage budgets, personnel, records, and programs.
Paternalism
An approach in which an authority makes decisions for a community on the grounds that it knows the community's interests better than the community does.
Indian Civil Rights Act (1968)
Federal law requiring tribal governments to respect specified individual rights and requiring tribal consent for new state assumption of Public Law 280 jurisdiction.
Public Law 280
A 1953 law extending certain state jurisdiction over Indian country in several states.
American Indian Policy Review Commission
Congressional commission (1975–1977) that reviewed federal Indian policy and recommended strengthening tribal governments.
Co-management
Shared authority over a resource, such as fisheries, between a tribal government and another government.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 114 matters because it shows how legal recognition becomes practical power. Indigenous nations did not need the federal government to invent their political existence, but they did need to operate schools, manage budgets, deliver health care, and make decisions that their citizens could see and evaluate. The self-determination era is the history of how that capacity was rebuilt after decades in which federal policy had suppressed it.

The chapter also reveals a larger pattern in Indigenous history: change was produced by Indigenous initiative operating through available institutions, from community organizing among the Menominee to intertribal lobbying and tribal council decisions, and the resulting arrangements were compromises. They expanded authority while leaving significant federal power in place. Students should be wary of narratives that present either complete victory or empty rhetoric, and should instead ask which conditions made change possible and which constrained it.

The institutions of this period shaped everything that follows in the textbook. Experienced tribal governments became litigants, negotiators, employers, and regulators, and their capacity underlies the struggles over land, jurisdiction, water, economic development, language, and knowledge examined in Part XX and later chapters. The era also left unresolved questions about funding, accountability, and the relationship between administrative programs and inherent authority that remain relevant in the twenty-first century.

Finally, the chapter reminds students that national policy has local meaning. A statute is only the beginning of a history that unfolds in classrooms, clinics, council chambers, and homes. Understanding that history requires attention to both the documents of governments and the experiences of communities, and to the nations whose continuing political life is the subject of this textbook.
