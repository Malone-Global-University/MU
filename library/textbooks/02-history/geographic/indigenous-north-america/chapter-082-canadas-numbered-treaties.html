Chapter Opening

At Lower Fort Garry in August 1871, Anishinaabe representatives and commissioners acting for the Crown reached an agreement whose meaning did not fit neatly inside the document later printed as Treaty One. The commissioners returned to government business, yet disputed promises remained. Four years later the Canadian Privy Council recognized an additional memorandum associated with the negotiations. This unusually visible paper trail reveals the defining historical problem of the Numbered Treaties: what obligations arose when peoples with different legal traditions reached an agreement through speeches, interpreters, ceremonies, and written articles?

Between 1871 and 1921, Canada concluded eleven numbered treaties with First Nations across extensive areas of the prairies, forests, and northern interior. The Crown sought a recognized foundation for territorial expansion, agricultural settlement, transportation, and resource development. Indigenous nations approached negotiations as political communities concerned with land, protection, continued livelihood, and future generations. Both the pressures and the goals changed across time. The first treaties were concentrated around Manitoba and the southern prairies; later agreements extended into areas newly important for mining, timber, routes, and petroleum.

The history is neither a straightforward story of equal partners freely exchanging land nor a sequence in which Indigenous people did nothing but receive policies devised in Ottawa. First Nations negotiators demanded conditions, compared precedents, delayed decisions, challenged commissioners, and later pressed the Crown to fulfill its obligations. At the same time, government officers controlled much drafting, translation, fiscal decisions, surveying, and subsequent administration. The balance of power was uneven and changed as settlement advanced and the buffalo economy contracted.

The surviving records are unusually rich but not self-interpreting. Printed treaty texts express Crown legal terminology; commissioners’ reports describe negotiations through the eyes of officers accountable to government; diaries may record oral assurances missing from the final articles; Indigenous community-held documents and oral histories preserve other understandings. These genres must be compared without pretending that any single one records every word spoken at an intercultural council. The chapter places five substantial documentary windows at points where the historical evidence reveals particularly important problems of interpretation.

Geography matters as much as law. A reserve was not the same as a treaty territory, and neither necessarily reproduced older Indigenous homelands. A mapped treaty area could contain farmers, equestrian hunting communities, fishing settlements, and families whose seasonal rounds crossed river basins. The consequences of an agreement depended on whether reserves were surveyed at suitable sites, whether assistance arrived, and whether the promise to continue hunting, fishing, or trapping remained meaningful as new settlers and companies entered the region.

Chapter 81 examined the near-destruction of the buffalo economy, a crisis affecting many southern treaty negotiations. Chapter 82 investigates the diplomacy, formal terms, and implementation of the agreements. Chapter 83 will examine Métis political resistance, while Chapter 84 studies the later reservation and reserve administrative order. Keeping these responsibilities distinct allows this chapter to treat treaties as historic political relationships rather than reducing them either to battlefield epilogues or to a summary of subsequent bureaucratic control.

The central question is how First Nations and the Crown negotiated the Numbered Treaties, why their understandings often diverged, and what happened when promised obligations were tested after the signing. The chapter closes in 1921, with a brief look toward the continuing recognition of treaty rights. It does not assume that the relationship ended at the final signature or that modern legal recognition supplies a substitute for reconstructing the negotiations on their own historical terms.

Historical Context

The nineteenth-century northwest of present-day Canada was home to many Indigenous political communities with distinct languages, economies and territories. Cree, Anishinaabe, Dene, Nakoda, Blackfoot Confederacy nations and others organized relationships around rivers, grasslands, forests, trade routes and seasonal movements. The Métis emerged as a distinct people within regional networks of kinship, commerce and political organization. Hudson’s Bay Company posts depended on these existing communities and did not replace their governments. A map showing company claims cannot be read as proof of company ownership of Indigenous lands.

Britain and Indigenous nations had long entered into agreements concerning peace, exchange and territory. The 1763 Royal Proclamation and the Robinson Treaties of 1850 provided later Crown negotiators with precedents, but they had been made in different settings. First Nations did not necessarily adopt the Crown’s understanding of exclusive title when they participated in diplomatic protocols. The ability to interpret a treaty requires attention both to the language of government and to the legal practices of the participating peoples.

Confederation in 1867 and the 1870 acquisition of company territorial claims accelerated Canada’s efforts to establish a transcontinental state. Surveyors, emigrants, railway promoters and police entered the region. At Red River, Métis resistance revealed that peoples already governing the land could not simply be ignored. South of the international border, American expansion and military campaigns provided another context for Canadian officials’ urgency. Chapter 83 treats the Métis conflicts themselves; here they help explain why the Canadian government sought formal agreements.

The destruction of bison herds made the mid-1870s profoundly different from the years just after Confederation. For many Plains communities the ability to sustain independence through hunting was weakening as commercial slaughter and other pressures mounted. Leaders responded with different strategies, including negotiating aid, preserving movement, seeking reserve land, and delaying adhesion. Northern Cree, Dene and Anishinaabe communities later faced a different combination of mining interest, timber extraction, and transport routes, although continued access to land remained equally crucial.

Treaty documents survive in various forms: printed copies of articles, orders establishing commissions, commissioners’ correspondence, treaty paylists and survey plans. They need to be read alongside Indigenous records, oral histories, and historical scholarship. The agreements contain words that later readers may find familiar—cession, reserve, annuity, adhesion—but those words do not automatically disclose the whole negotiated relationship. The chapter proceeds by matching each term to its historical context and tracing what happened after the formal agreement.

Indigenous Diplomacy, Confederation, and the Origins of the Numbered Treaties

Section 82.1

Indigenous Sovereignty, Diplomacy, and Treaty Traditions Before Confederation

Before the Dominion of Canada began signing the Numbered Treaties, Indigenous nations possessed long histories of governance, territorial responsibility, diplomacy, and binding agreements. Anishinaabe, Cree, Dakota, Dene, Nakoda, Blackfoot, and other peoples organized political life through institutions adapted to their own societies. Authority might rest in hereditary offices, influential leaders, councils, kin relationships, or ceremonial responsibilities; the distribution of power varied across nations and changed over time. Negotiating with outsiders did not mean these polities lacked jurisdiction over their territories. For many communities, a treaty was a way to regulate relationships between peoples who would continue to exist alongside each other, not a mechanism for dissolving one party's authority.

Treaty diplomacy operated through protocols that made commitment visible and memorable. Speakers might consult relatives and other leaders before presenting a position; hosts and visitors exchanged gifts; pipes, feasts, or other ceremonies could mark the gravity of a council. Agreements could address peace, passage, mutual aid, access to resources, or reciprocal responsibilities. The significance of a ceremonial act cannot be reconstructed simply by translating it into the language of a modern deed. An agreement's meaning was retained through oral teaching, community memory, and continuing performance. Treaties were thus political relationships capable of being renewed, challenged, or reaffirmed. Neither their form nor their meaning was uniform across the Indigenous world.

The Crown had its own developing treaty practices. The Royal Proclamation of 1763 set out an imperial framework under which the Crown claimed a central role in acquiring Indigenous lands; it did not create Indigenous possession or make Indigenous governments derivative of British authority. In practice, the imperial government sought alliances and accommodations in territories it could not command by force. The Robinson treaties along Lakes Superior and Huron in 1850 became important precedents for territorial cession clauses, reserves, annual payments, and expectations of continued resource use. But such earlier arrangements emerged from particular communities and circumstances; they did not establish that later northern and prairie peoples understood the same legal language identically.

An important difficulty is that 'consent' has different possible referents. A Crown commissioner might ask whether selected chiefs agreed to a prepared text. Indigenous participants might understand that a council had authorized an agreement to share country under a reciprocal relationship, subject to promises and ongoing duties. A signature or mark cannot itself reveal the content of translated speeches or the boundaries of a leader's mandate. It is equally inaccurate to assume that every Indigenous delegation rejected land surrender in the same terms. The historical task is to establish the expectations of particular negotiators through the fullest available evidence, including their community-held traditions and the written record.

Land itself was integral to this divergence. The familiar treaty phrase concerning cession, release, or surrender expresses the Crown's claim to exclusive jurisdiction and ownership. Indigenous legal traditions often understood territory through intergenerational responsibilities, uses shared or contested with neighbors, and obligations to animals, waters, and places. These relationships could be compatible with newcomers' residence under negotiated conditions without being equivalent to permanent alienation of a nation's country. Aimée Craft's work on Treaty One, grounded in Anishinaabe legal thought, shows why histories confined to an English document overlook relationships and expectations that structured the negotiations.

The Numbered Treaties therefore originated where two diplomatic systems met under conditions of sharply unequal power, yet with no single inevitable outcome. Indigenous representatives demanded recognition, aid, security, and conditions for survival; Crown commissioners sought territorial arrangements supporting state expansion. Each side could learn from earlier agreements, revise its strategy, or dispute what had happened. Establishing those competing but internally varied forms of diplomacy is indispensable before examining the eleven treaties signed from 1871 to 1921.

Section 82.2

Canadian Confederation and the Expansion into the Northwest, 1867–1870

Confederation in 1867 produced a Dominion government with ambitions extending far beyond the four founding provinces. Its leaders imagined an east–west transportation system, agricultural settlement across the prairie interior, access to minerals and timber, and a Pacific connection that would strengthen the new polity against absorption into the United States. Their maps depicted immense northern and western territories as prospective spaces for national development. Yet these lands were not vacant. First Nations and Métis communities maintained governments, property relationships, seasonal economies, and commercial routes long before Ottawa claimed administrative control. The contradiction between metropolitan territorial planning and Indigenous political life generated much of the pressure for treaties.

The Hudson's Bay Company held a royal charter relating to the immense drainage basin known as Rupert's Land and maintained posts throughout the northwest. The company had traded with and depended on Indigenous peoples; its charter did not constitute an extinguishment of their rights. Negotiations among Britain, the company, and Canadian authorities led to the transfer of Rupert's Land and the North-Western Territory to Canada in 1870. Canada treated the transfer as acquiring jurisdiction and large-scale development opportunity. Indigenous nations had not sold their lands to the company, and the transfer had not been negotiated with them. Officials nevertheless began to organize surveys, immigration policy, and institutions for regions they had scarcely administered.

At Red River, the expansion prompted organized Métis resistance to an imposed transfer and surveys conducted without consultation. The resulting negotiations produced the Manitoba Act in 1870, making a new province and setting out provisions concerning land and institutions. Chapter 83 examines those political events in depth. For the present chapter, the point is that Canadian expansion immediately encountered existing communities capable of making demands and changing federal policy. It also revealed how inadequate it was for a government to assume that a transaction among colonial institutions resolved the claims of the people occupying the country.

Railway policy gave territorial ambitions urgency. An eventual route to the Pacific required corridors, town sites, timber, water access, and the assurance that construction and settlement would proceed without continual conflict. Surveyors and prospectors entered Indigenous territories before treaty consent had been obtained. Crown officials feared that unilateral settlement would damage relations and threaten investment; Indigenous peoples saw survey lines and newcomers as claims to land they had not relinquished. The United States' expansion across its own western territories intensified Ottawa's determination to secure a Canadian presence. The North-West Mounted Police, created in 1873 and dispatched west in 1874, would later form part of this state-building process.

The Dominion's authority over 'Indians, and Lands reserved for the Indians' under section 91(24) of the Constitution Act, 1867, did not answer the question of how lands would be acquired or what relationships existed with First Nations. Government departments increasingly represented their work as protective, promising reserves and agricultural assistance while seeking lands for settlers. Such language can hide the unequal terms of policy: Crown negotiators controlled much of the written drafting, possessed state finances and enforcement institutions, and assumed that Indigenous economies would change toward European-style farming. Indigenous leaders could bargain, delay, refuse, or seek modifications, but they could not easily control the pace of settler entry.

Treaties consequently became an instrument for aligning legal claims, administration, and projected development. Their sequence reflected where the state expected settlement or resource interest, and where First Nations insisted that newcomers could not simply proceed without agreement. This was not a mechanical response to Confederation: decisions by Indigenous leaders and the varying costs of enforcement mattered. It explains why the first agreements were concentrated near Manitoba in 1871, while northern treaty-making expanded decades later when routes and extractive industries made the far north newly important to federal planners.

Cause and Effect

How Canadian expansion produced negotiating pressure

Long-term conditions included British imperial treaty practice, Canadian plans for a transcontinental state, and established Indigenous sovereignty. The 1870 transfer of Hudson’s Bay Company territorial claims placed these interests in direct conflict. Railways, telegraphs, surveyors, police and settlement prospects increased Ottawa’s urgency, while First Nations demanded that newcomers recognize their rights. The first numbered treaties emerged from this interaction, not from a single predetermined cause.

Immediate consequence: Treaties One and Two were concluded near Manitoba in 1871. Longer-term consequence: treaty commissions became one tool of Canada’s territorial expansion, while Indigenous political leaders continued contesting their meaning and implementation. Distinguish a cause of Crown urgency from a reason particular First Nations leaders agreed to meet.

Section 82.3

How Treaties Were Negotiated: Councils, Interpreters, Ceremonies, and Written Agreements

A treaty council was neither a routine administrative appointment nor a simple ceremony marking an already settled transaction. Its participants sometimes traveled hundreds of miles to a designated place; groups arranged camps, debated the purpose of the gathering, and consulted among chiefs, headmen, families, and other influential voices. Commissioners arrived with instructions from Ottawa, an expected budget, and particular terms they were authorized to offer. The two parties did not necessarily agree about what the meeting was for. One side might speak of acquiring title; the other could approach the gathering as a negotiation over coexistence, protection, and the government’s responsibility to its new neighbors.

Words moved through several languages. On the southern prairies, Anishinaabemowin, Cree, and different Blackfoot-related languages could be heard; northern commissions required interpreters conversant with Dene, Cree, and regional dialects. Interpreters had substantial practical power. A description of 'land surrender' might not have a transparent equivalent in the language of the listeners, while an Indigenous statement about sharing land or preserving a livelihood might be abbreviated in a government report. Translators also carried social affiliations and their own judgments. Their presence does not automatically make negotiations invalid; it does mean that historians must avoid treating a polished English text as a stenographic transcript of an entire intercultural exchange.

Government reports recorded speeches selectively. Commissioners such as Alexander Morris later published or transmitted accounts that defended their decisions to superiors and readers elsewhere. These are indispensable evidence of what Crown representatives said they did, including the formal promises offered and reasons given for acceptance. Yet reports could compress hours of discussion, omit Indigenous consultations conducted beyond the recorder's hearing, or portray disagreement as settled by the act of signing. Indigenous oral histories, community-held notes, later petitions, and records prepared by translators can preserve other dimensions. Comparing the sources is more illuminating than choosing a single source as unquestionably complete.

The physical acts of treaty-making also deserve attention. Chiefs and headmen selected to speak or sign did not necessarily wield centralized authority over all families occupying a mapped district. Representatives could make marks on a document prepared in English; medals, flags, clothing, or gifts were exchanged; feasts or pipe ceremonies could confirm a solemn political relationship. The government might understand these things as supplementary tokens of allegiance. Indigenous participants might understand them as public acts affirming a bond that entailed enduring reciprocal duties. A subsequent disagreement over gifts, supplies, or land therefore cannot be dismissed as a trivial misunderstanding: the items could embody a commitment made before witnesses.

An adhesion was a later act by which a community joined a treaty already concluded. Adhesions complicate the neat sequence of eleven dates. Some communities were absent from the original council because the territory was vast, conditions of travel were severe, or their leaders opposed the terms; others entered negotiations when changing circumstances made participation urgent. Signatures continued to be gathered in subsequent years, sometimes decades later. An historical map bearing a treaty number thus compresses multiple encounters, local negotiations, and shifts in policy into one colored region. Students must distinguish the initial agreement, its subsequent adhesions, and its later implementation.

This evidentiary method will guide the case studies. For every important negotiation, ask what Crown officials wanted, what particular First Nations sought, how leaders discussed proposed terms, what the written text eventually said, and whether later conduct matched the understanding announced at the council. Whenever the chapter presents a primary-source paraphrase, it identifies its genre and limits rather than fabricating a quotation. The purpose is not to eliminate disagreement but to explain its historical origins and the evidence available to assess it.

Historical Vocabulary

Treaty, Crown, and treaty adhesion

Treaty names a solemn political agreement, but its meaning cannot be established by the word alone; it depends on the negotiating parties and their legal traditions. Crown refers to the sovereign authority represented by commissioners, an institution rather than a synonym for every later official. Adhesion is an additional agreement by a community to join an existing treaty, sometimes many years after the original council.

Do not confuse the year on a numbered treaty map with the date on which all affected nations first negotiated their obligations. A community’s decision to adhere later was a consequential historical choice and could involve an altered bargaining environment.

Section 82.4

Treaties 1 and 2, 1871: Manitoba and the First Numbered Agreements

The first Numbered Treaties emerged where Canadian settlement policy confronted Anishinaabe political and territorial claims near the newly created province of Manitoba. On August 3, 1871, representatives negotiated Treaty One at Lower Fort Garry, also known as the Stone Fort; Treaty Two followed at Manitoba Post on August 21. The Crown sought territories around the Red and Assiniboine river systems and the expanding settlement area. Indigenous leaders sought stable relationships, a continuing home, assistance for farming alongside hunting and fishing, and assurances that their people would not simply be displaced by newcomers. The two meetings did not produce identical communities or intentions, but they opened a recognizable treaty framework.

The federal commissioners' prepared language emphasized cession and promised specified reserves, monetary payments, and forms of assistance. Anishinaabe participants were not strangers to negotiation with the Crown or Hudson's Bay Company. They drew on their own legal understandings and expectations that a pledge made in council would remain binding. Treaty One in particular exposed a gap between the spoken exchange and the printed document. Chiefs and families maintained that additional undertakings had been made, involving agricultural animals, tools, clothing, and other material provisions. The government's memorandum described them as 'outside promises,' an administrative phrase that does not make them outside the parties' understanding of the agreement.

The distinction soon became practical. A family contemplating planting needed usable animals, seeds, implements, and a place to cultivate; a promise of future assistance offered little if officials did not provide it at the appropriate season. Officials disputed the status or cost of provisions not printed in the main text, while Indigenous parties insisted that the commitments were part of the treaty. This dispute generated correspondence and repeated advocacy. It demonstrates why historians must consult not only the signed treaty but also memoranda, orders, letters, and First Nations accounts of the council. A formal archival record is not identical with the whole negotiation, but even the Crown's own papers acknowledge the existence of additional commitments.

On April 30, 1875, an Order in Council accepted the written memorandum attached to Treaty One as part of Treaties One and Two and directed action on unfulfilled promises. It also addressed annual payments. Early provisions had specified an annuity of three dollars per person; later government decisions raised the figure to five dollars subject to the arrangement recorded in the order. The chronology matters. It would be incorrect to suggest that the printed agreement was fully implemented in 1871 or that every disputed promise was promptly and satisfactorily discharged after 1875. The government's recognition of the memorandum constituted a significant documentary acknowledgment, but implementation remained an ongoing political relationship.

Treaties One and Two are distinctive in another respect. Unlike the explicit hunting and fishing clauses found in later Numbered Treaties, the original written articles of these first agreements did not expressly set out a continuing right to hunt and fish over the ceded lands. This absence must not be read automatically as proof that Indigenous participants believed their livelihood had been surrendered. The difference illustrates the limitations of inferring a party's understanding from silence in the English text. Treaty rights have histories of interpretation as well as formal wording, and an historically responsible textbook keeps the two questions separate.

Aimée Craft's study, Breathing Life into the Stone Fort Treaty, examines Anishinaabe law and kinship as foundations of Treaty One's meaning. Her analysis makes the familiar 'outside promises' dispute more than a disagreement about a few supplies. It asks how governments account for responsibilities created through a relationship and ceremonial exchange. The first treaties should therefore be read as the opening of a lasting and contested connection, not simply as 1871 land transactions that happened to require administrative follow-up four years later.

Section 82.5

Treaty 3, 1873: The Anishinaabe and the Northwest Angle Negotiations

Treaty Three was signed at the Northwest Angle of Lake of the Woods in October 1873 after earlier Crown efforts to reach an agreement had failed. The region was geographically indispensable to Canada's desired east–west route linking Lake Superior with Manitoba and the western territories. Rivers, portages, and lakes were existing Anishinaabe homelands and transportation systems, not vacant corridors waiting for a railway or wagon road. Communities had seen travelers, surveyors, and construction workers enter their territory, and leaders insisted that Canada could not treat passage and settlement as rights acquired without negotiating with them. The repeated failure of earlier approaches gave Indigenous negotiators leverage that materially affected the eventual terms.

Crown commissioners returned with greater willingness to consider demands about payments, reserves, resource access, and practical means of maintaining livelihoods. Alexander Morris became a central public voice for the negotiations, but his published report was an official reconstruction, not a neutral transcript. Indigenous leaders had their own methods of collective discussion and priorities concerning the durability of any agreement. They were aware that travelers and workers might disturb important hunting, fishing, and gathering places. For the Anishinaabe, the issue was not simply how many acres the government would mark on a reserve plan but how the entire relationship to country would continue with newcomers present.

Treaty Three became an important benchmark for the western treaties that followed. The written settlement offered larger reserves than Treaties One and Two, often expressed as one square mile for each family of five, and a five-dollar annual payment. It set out continuing rights to hunt and fish over lands not taken up or otherwise governed by the written exceptions, and included annual provisions for ammunition and twine. These clauses mattered because fishing and hunting sustained both local households and exchange relationships. Yet a treaty article guaranteeing resource activity was not the same as a guarantee that later governments would refrain from allocating places to settlers, timber enterprises, or other uses. The text contained the seeds of later disagreement about the geography of continued Indigenous rights.

A particularly valuable record is known as the Paypom Treaty. This community-held document is associated with notes made by Joseph Nolin during the 1873 negotiations, a recorder engaged on the Anishinaabe side. Copies and provenance are complicated; the surviving text must not be treated as an unproblematic verbatim transcript. Nonetheless, comparison of the Paypom account with Morris's published version gives students an opportunity to ask why promises might appear differently in records made for different audiences. The existence of such evidence makes it impossible to pretend that only one official English narrative survives. Historians examine its transmission, language, the role of the Nolin recorders, and disagreements over what was offered.

The negotiations also had consequences for other treaty makers. Later commissioners frequently treated Treaty Three as a point of departure when setting terms, and First Nations elsewhere learned about the concessions made at the Northwest Angle. Treaty precedents thus traveled in both directions: Ottawa used existing agreements as fiscal and administrative models, while Indigenous representatives could demand provisions comparable to those obtained by neighboring communities. That circulation undermines any portrayal of later treaty parties as isolated negotiators confronted with wholly unprecedented proposals.

Treaty Three is therefore both a regional history and a lesson in historical method. Its terms arose from Anishinaabe bargaining in a strategically critical area, and its surviving sources reveal disputes over interpretation that were present at the beginning, not manufactured only by later litigation. Students should read the official articles, the government's account of the council, and Indigenous-held documentary and oral histories together, asking what each can and cannot establish about the promises made in 1873.

Section 82.6

Treaty 4, 1874: Qu'Appelle and the Treaty-Making Crisis on the Prairies

In September 1874, Cree, Saulteaux, and other First Nations representatives gathered for negotiations in the Qu'Appelle region of present-day Saskatchewan. Treaty Four's principal agreement was concluded on September 15, with a supplementary agreement later that month. The meeting took place as survey parties, a proposed telegraph line, and the newly organized North-West Mounted Police extended Canadian institutional presence into territories that local nations had never ceded. Crown officials described this entry as part of national development and orderly government. Many Indigenous leaders regarded it as a matter requiring explanation and consent before settlement proceeded.

The government's own authorization for the treaty commission is revealing. A July 1874 Order in Council connected the urgency of treaty-making to police movement, a boundary survey, and plans for a telegraph across Indigenous country. These were not incidental background facts: they show that Canada anticipated its own activities might deepen Indigenous concern. The government wanted a formal settlement of claims before such projects advanced much farther. The commission included Alexander Morris, David Laird, and W. J. Christie, whose instructions and eventual report help historians reconstruct Crown priorities. The order also reveals an administrative desire to prevent annuities from increasing beyond established precedents.

At the council, questions about the Hudson's Bay Company were central. Cree and Saulteaux leaders challenged the assumption that the company's transfer of its claims to Canada entitled a new government to dispose of the country. The company had traded in these lands; it had not bought away Indigenous political existence. Commissioners sought to reassure the assembly about the Queen's intentions, but that answer could not erase the contradiction between an overseas sale and Indigenous jurisdiction. Disagreement also concerned selection of leaders for negotiation, the benefits that families would actually receive, and how any new relationship would affect mobility. The council required several meetings and cannot be reduced to a single moment of acquiescence.

The written articles provided reserves, annuities, hunting and fishing arrangements, assistance related to agriculture, and annual ammunition and twine supplies in forms broadly comparable to Treaty Three. They also set out differentiated allowances to chiefs and headmen. The choice of particular benefits was not merely ceremonial. Leadership payments, implements, and provisions were bound to the organization of communities expected to withstand settler expansion, new diseases, and changes in the bison economy. The promise of reserve land posed an additional question: where would it be located, and would the eventual survey secure access to water, hay, wood, and places that mattered to a particular nation?

Treaty Four's geography extended well beyond the site of the council, and adhesions over subsequent years brought additional communities under its terms. The famous description 'Treaty Four territory' represents an administrative district containing different Indigenous homelands, historical alliances, and local relations. Treating all of its peoples as a single negotiation party would conceal how decisions were made by particular bands and leaders. The written clauses also do not eliminate questions about how the participants understood the Crown's promise of protection and the duration of their own obligations.

For the larger chapter, Treaty Four reveals the relationship between infrastructural expansion and treaty-making. The Canadian state did not wait for all First Nations to request an agreement before moving west. Yet neither were Indigenous participants passive. They interrogated the premise of territorial transfer, drew on earlier treaty precedents, and sought provisions that would make the Crown's assurances meaningful in daily life. This mixture of administrative urgency and Indigenous political agency became a recurring feature of later negotiations.

Section 82.7

Treaty 5, 1875: Lake Winnipeg, Northern Communities, and Expanding Treaty Coverage

Treaty Five began in 1875 among Cree and Anishinaabe communities around Lake Winnipeg and neighboring waterways. Its geography differed significantly from the grassland districts central to the first prairie treaties. Rivers and lakes linked households to fish, waterfowl, trapping grounds, transport routes, and seasonal places of work. Trading posts connected this regional economy to larger markets but did not erase local authority over movement and subsistence. An agreement designed through a southern agricultural template could not simply be transferred north without consequences: provisions valuable in wheat-growing country might be less useful to people whose incomes and food depended on fishing or the fur trade.

Crown officials nevertheless approached treaty-making with familiar administrative assumptions. Reserve lands, annual payments, and assurances about schooling appeared in the written agreement, though provisions and land allocations differed from Treaty Three and other agreements. Federal negotiators generally sought a legal basis for settlers and commercial activities while limiting the continuing cost of obligations. Indigenous leaders sought recognition of their established homes and protection of access to the resources that sustained them. The distance between these objectives becomes clear when a clause concerning agricultural implements is read beside a community's dependence on a fishing station or a particular winter trapping route.

Treaty Five is especially important because it cannot be adequately assigned to one date. The original agreements of 1875 were followed by adhesions in later years, reaching farther into what is now northern Manitoba and adjacent regions. Later signings occurred under altered conditions and should not be presumed to have reproduced the same negotiating atmosphere as the earlier meetings. Travel constraints, local knowledge of treaty benefits elsewhere, changing trade fortunes, and differences among Crown commissioners shaped participation. A community joining decades later encountered a different government, economy, and regime of territorial administration from the one experienced by the original delegates.

The treaty's historical geography also exposes a limitation of the conventional map. A shaded region labeled 'Treaty 5' may conceal waterways that served as territorial arteries and communities that entered the agreement at very different times. Students should trace routes between major lakes and rivers, identify principal settlement and trade locations, and then ask whether the form of reserve allocation made sense for people who moved seasonally among several resource sites. Distinguish the administrative boundaries created by the Crown from Indigenous landscapes of use, memory, and inter-community relationship. These geographies overlap but are not interchangeable.

In some northern districts, concerns about outside fishing, timber, and transportation interests became increasingly significant. The Crown's assurances of continued livelihood were meaningful only if reserve choices and land-use decisions left communities able to maintain those activities. A reserve located at a settlement could not replace the economic importance of wider access to lakes and trapping areas. Official treaty texts and subsequent correspondence must therefore be assessed against actual patterns of implementation. A benefit listed on paper might not respond to a community's immediate needs; a continuing right in the text might be narrowed later by government decisions about resource access.

Treaty Five marks a transition within the treaty system. It retained many institutional features first developed nearer Manitoba while opening an extensive northern treaty history that would continue with Treaties Eight through Eleven. Its lesson is not that northern Indigenous people were less interested in diplomacy but that a single Crown treaty model operated across markedly different environments. The terms and meanings of agreement must be tested against those places and against the voices of communities whose economies were not organized primarily around agricultural settlement.

Section 82.8

Treaty Promises: Land, Annuities, Education, Agricultural Assistance, and Hunting Rights

By 1875 the first five treaties had established a recognizable repertoire of written promises, but there was no single identical package. Reserves, annuities, one-time gratuities, recognition of chiefs and headmen, supplies, agricultural assistance, education, and continued resource activities appeared in differing combinations. The size of land allocations and the amount of annual payments changed from one agreement to another. A fair comparison must therefore read the actual articles instead of claiming that all First Nations accepted one standard exchange. A treaty's provisions were products of negotiation, geography, government policy, and the degree of leverage available to particular Indigenous delegations.

The concept of a reserve is frequently misunderstood. In the Crown's written system a specified portion of the larger treaty area was to be set apart for the use and benefit of a First Nation. These tracts were not the same thing as all of the nation's ancestral territory, and a reserve survey did not necessarily occur promptly after treaty signing. Where continuing hunting and fishing provisions existed, they generally referred to activities throughout a much wider territory, subject to qualifications in the text and subsequent disputes about their meaning. The contrast between a comparatively small reserve and extensive treaty territory is foundational to understanding later disagreements over land and resources.

The articles of Treaties One and Two are a critical exception to easy generalization. They did not expressly include the continuing hunting and fishing clause found in later agreements. Treaty Three, by contrast, contained express rights to hunt and fish on lands not taken up under the stated conditions, and subsequent treaties developed provisions in related but not identical wording. This does not settle the question of what the First Nations understood at the earlier councils. It establishes the more limited documentary point that the printed texts differ. Students should avoid both errors: silently inserting a provision where none appears and concluding that its absence describes the full diplomatic relationship.

An annuity was a recurring payment rather than a single final purchase price handed over at the moment of surrender. The sums were often small relative to the resources and land that the government claimed to acquire, but their recurring character made them symbols of a continuing Crown obligation. Some provisions also supplied flags, medals, clothing, or special payments to designated leaders. Whether recipients understood such items as compensation, gifts confirming an alliance, or evidence of the Crown's duty to care for its treaty partners cannot be decided by their monetary value alone. The experience of delayed or disputed annuities shows why performance mattered as much as the original promise.

Agricultural assistance similarly requires contextual reading. Ploughs, harrows, livestock, seed, instruction, and other tools appear because commissioners expected First Nations to transition toward settled agricultural livelihoods. Some communities wished to adopt cultivation alongside older pursuits, and demanded the means to do so effectively. Others relied principally on fishing, trapping, or mixed production, where a standard agricultural package could be ill matched to local conditions. Officials sometimes emphasized the civilizing or assimilative intent of their policy; Indigenous negotiators might accept instruments as a means of improving community security without accepting a project to erase their societies.

Treaty schooling provisions must also be distinguished from the later institutional history of compulsory residential education. A written promise to provide schools when desired or otherwise specified created an expectation of educational support, but it does not establish consent to the later coercive residential-school regime. That distinction prevents Chapter 82 from absorbing Chapter 87. More broadly, the treaty promises were not just a ledger of items. They operated within relationships of protection, land use, political coexistence, and accountability. The fundamental historical test is not whether a clause can be found but whether the two parties understood the commitment similarly and how the Crown acted once the negotiations ended.

Historical Vocabulary

Annuity, reserve, and treaty hunting rights

An annuity is a recurring treaty payment, whose repeated distribution could affirm the continuance of a relationship. A reserve is a specific land tract set aside under the treaty framework; it is not another term for the much wider treaty territory. Treaty hunting and fishing rights are written provisions addressing continued resource activity, often with exceptions tied to land use.

Precision matters: Treaties One and Two do not contain the express hunting-and-fishing clauses of several later numbered treaties. One cannot simply fill that textual silence with a generic clause, nor infer from the silence that Indigenous negotiators agreed to abandon all traditional pursuits.

Treaty 6, 1876: Cree Leadership and Negotiations at Fort Carlton and Fort Pitt

Treaty Six was negotiated in August and September 1876 at Fort Carlton and Fort Pitt in what is now Saskatchewan. Its historical setting was more precarious than that of many earlier treaties. The Plains buffalo economy was contracting, commercial hunting had penetrated increasingly distant ranges, outbreaks of disease threatened communities, and settlers and officials expected the Canadian west to become an agricultural region. Cree leaders confronted not only the arrival of a new government but also the prospect that the material system sustaining autonomy would become unreliable. The resulting negotiations must be interpreted as strategic efforts to secure a viable future under pressure, not as unthinking acceptance of Canadian rule.

At Fort Carlton, influential Cree leaders including Mistawasis and Ahtahkakoop took part in deliberations with Lieutenant-Governor Alexander Morris and other Crown representatives. Their decisions were informed by observation of changing conditions and by debate within Cree political life. Not all leaders possessed the same territorial authority or pursued identical goals. Some believed a treaty could protect land, recognize community leadership, and obtain equipment and assistance before the loss of the herds worsened. Others feared that government promises would not be honored and that the language of surrender threatened the political future of their descendants. The existence of internal disagreement was part of diplomacy, not evidence that Cree communities had no coherent political institutions.

Peter Erasmus, a Métis interpreter with extensive knowledge of Cree and English-speaking society, was among those who helped make the discussions possible. His later reminiscences are valuable but require the same scrutiny as commissioners' reports: memory, later experiences, and editing can shape how an earlier encounter is represented. Morris's account likewise portrays the negotiations through the eyes of a Crown officer charged with obtaining agreement. The best reconstruction compares such narratives with treaty articles, petitions, and First Nations historical accounts. It asks not just whether a clause appears on the page but why leaders demanded it and how the parties explained it at the council.

The meeting at Fort Carlton was followed by negotiations at Fort Pitt. Although the Crown presented an established treaty framework, the Cree requests led to important distinctive undertakings concerning famine, epidemic illness, cultivation, and medical support. Those commitments are examined separately in the next section because they were central to the bargain's meaning. At Fort Pitt, different local leaders encountered the commissioners, and the written agreement included arrangements that distinguished the two districts in how certain assistance would be administered. Treating the two meetings as one undifferentiated ceremony obscures that specificity.

A negotiated treaty also required choices about where people would live and whether they would continue to hunt and move across the country. The written terms anticipated reserves, government assistance for agriculture, annual payments, and qualified continuation of hunting and fishing. But the collapse of buffalo populations and increasing settler pressure made it uncertain whether families could sustain older livelihoods even where they believed treaty rights protected them. Indigenous leaders sought guarantees of survival precisely because a formal legal right could not by itself restore an ecological system or deliver food during famine. Their diplomatic strategy combined defense of continuing rights with efforts to obtain material support.

Treaty Six became an important precedent in both historical memory and later debates over Crown obligations. It also illustrates why signing was not the last political decision a community could make. Other Cree leaders did not join at the original councils, and adhesions followed as circumstances changed. The treaty thus represented a series of decisions, not a declaration of universal Cree consent. Its significance lies in the way Cree negotiators made the crisis of livelihood a subject of explicit Crown commitments—and in the later disputes over whether Canada understood those commitments as Indigenous leaders did.

Section 82.10

The Medicine Chest, Famine Clause, and the Special Obligations of Treaty 6

Treaty Six is especially remembered for provisions associated with health, epidemic disease, and hunger. These are not incidental lines of technical language. They emerged from a negotiation in which Cree leaders confronted the possible destruction of their established food economy and sought assurances that the Crown would provide help when extraordinary hardship struck. Canada was asking First Nations to accept a new territorial and political relationship while the future of hunting was increasingly uncertain. An agreement promising payments but remaining silent about starvation could not adequately address the danger. The treaty's distinctive clauses therefore reveal a link between Indigenous bargaining and the material conditions of the 1870s.

The written famine-and-pestilence clause set conditions for Crown assistance when people covered by the treaty were overtaken by a general famine or epidemic. The text made relief contingent on the Crown being satisfied and on certification by Indian Agents; the character and extent of aid were to be determined by the Chief Superintendent of Indian Affairs. These conditions are essential to an accurate reading. A broad statement that the Crown promised food whenever it was needed loses the wording's discretionary and administrative features. Equally, a narrowly bureaucratic reading that treats the clause as devoid of meaningful obligation overlooks why it was negotiated and how communities understood protection during catastrophe.

The medicine-chest article is similarly precise. The printed text provided for a medicine chest at the house of each Indian Agent, for Indigenous use at the agent's direction. It did not literally prescribe a twentieth- or twenty-first-century comprehensive health-care system. Later First Nations understandings and legal-political debates have connected the promise to a larger obligation of health care, drawing on negotiation history, the spirit and intent of the agreement, and changing circumstances. A historical textbook should present that later interpretation as an interpretation, not substitute it for the words of the 1876 article. Students can then examine how agreements are read when a society and its medical needs change profoundly over time.

Treaty Six also included temporary assistance for agriculture. After specified reserves were agreed upon and surveyed, funds could be spent on provisions for people cultivating the soil over a three-year period; the conditions differed in some respects between the Fort Carlton and Fort Pitt groups. The amount and timing were controlled by government officers. This was a major limitation from the standpoint of a family needing to begin planting before the survey had been completed or before promised tools arrived. A government could claim it was acting within the document while households experienced delay at the point of most urgent need. The clause thus joined survival assistance to an administrative process that could itself impede effective implementation.

The additional agricultural equipment, cattle, and other promised support also reflected a contested vision of the future. Officials often presumed that Indigenous peoples would become sedentary farmers and eventually cease to require extensive territory. Some Cree families did cultivate and sought practical aid, but agricultural adaptation was not synonymous with consenting to an end of their nationhood, languages, laws, or wider rights. The distinctions matter because nineteenth-century reports frequently use terms of 'civilization' and 'progress' that present assimilation as benign. A source-critical history must reconstruct what officials intended without adopting those judgments as objective descriptions of Indigenous goals.

In later years the meaning of treaty relief became a test of Crown performance. The collapse of buffalo herds, inadequate supplies, and decisions by federal officials could place communities in precisely the conditions their leaders had anticipated. A complete assessment requires case-specific records of hunger, agent correspondence, and community advocacy rather than declaring, without evidence, that the Crown either always fulfilled or categorically ignored every obligation. What is secure is that Treaty Six's unusual provisions resulted from purposeful Cree negotiation and created textual commitments capable of being invoked long after the signing. Their enduring importance lies in the tension between narrowly written administrative conditions and a relationship understood by many First Nations as one of protection and reciprocal responsibility.

Treaty 7, 1877: The Blackfoot Confederacy and the Southern Alberta Treaty

On September 22, 1877, Treaty Seven was concluded at Blackfoot Crossing on the Bow River in present-day Alberta. The treaty involved representatives of the Siksika, Kainai, Piikani, Tsuut'ina, and Stoney Nakoda peoples, whose political relationships and territorial histories were not identical. Crowfoot, associated with Siksika leadership, and Red Crow of the Kainai are among the best-known participants, though a textbook should resist reducing the agreement to two personalities. David Laird and James F. Macleod represented the Crown. The meeting took place after years in which buffalo decline, cross-border violence, the whiskey trade, and pressure from settlers and traders had profoundly altered conditions on the northern Plains.

The Canadian government's goals were connected to projected settlement and the expansion of its authority. Officials wished to obtain the legal claims they believed would permit newcomers, ranching, roads, and future railway development. Their reports often contrasted Canadian peacekeeping with the violence of the American frontier, using the presence of the North-West Mounted Police to present the Crown as a stabilizing protector. Indigenous leaders certainly assessed security and the risk of conflict, but their evaluation was more complex than accepting an official story of benevolent government. They confronted the loss of a food system and the arrival of strangers claiming control over lands to which their nations had long-standing ties.

Treaty discussions passed through interpreters and ceremonial forms whose meanings did not map neatly onto the English legal formula of cession. Crown accounts focused on the land clauses, reserves, annuities, and promised supplies. Blackfoot and other Treaty Seven oral traditions have emphasized agreements about peace, coexistence, and sharing the land, together with expectations of continuing support. The landmark collaborative study The True Spirit and Original Intent of Treaty 7, undertaken with Treaty 7 Elders and Tribal Council and scholars including Walter Hildebrandt, Dorothy First Rider, and Sarah Carter, documents testimony that challenges a simple surrender narrative. Its evidence should be described as nation-specific testimony collected and interpreted in a later historical project, not as if it were a verbatim recording from 1877.

The dispute over meaning had immediate material consequences. A reserve could offer some protection from settler encroachment but could also confine access to the broader land that supplied animals, plants, and culturally important places. A promise of cattle, tools, or food mattered enormously when buffalo supplies were rapidly dwindling. Yet the scale of the economic transition officials anticipated was far greater than a small package of equipment could finance. Treaty promises were thus evaluated in relation to a social catastrophe whose speed many negotiators could not know. Families' decisions to participate, remain outside, or move between districts reflected their own calculations under these changing circumstances.

The communities gathered at Treaty Seven should not be merged into a single voice. Siksika, Kainai, Piikani, Tsuut'ina, and Stoney Nakoda possessed distinct institutions and histories; individual leaders also disagreed on questions of security and accommodation. The role of an interpreter, the selection of delegates, and the government's expectation that named signatories could speak for broad territories are all important subjects of analysis. A historian can establish the printed terms of the treaty and describe the Crown's territorial purpose while recognizing that the negotiating record may not capture the full Indigenous understanding. Neither source category should be used as an excuse to neglect the other.

Treaty Seven provides the chapter's clearest example of divergent accounts of one meeting. It was a consequential treaty in the history of Canadian territorial expansion, and it was also a treaty Indigenous peoples continued to invoke as evidence of a solemn political relationship. Its study should leave students with a precise question: when representatives reach agreement through different languages and understandings of land, what evidence establishes the commitments each believed had been undertaken? This is not simply a retrospective legal puzzle. It is a historical question rooted in the circumstances of the council and the later experience of promises fulfilled, postponed, or disputed.

Section 82.12

Indigenous Consent, Leadership Disagreement, and Treaty Adhesions

A map of the numbered treaties can give the impression that between 1871 and 1877 a series of regions changed status on eleven neat dates. The reality was far more complicated. Some First Nations negotiated during the original commission visit; others sent representatives later; still others resisted the proposed terms or were absent because distance, seasonal activities, or disagreements made participation impossible. A treaty's first signing and the history of the communities subsequently recorded under its number were different events. Adhesions did not merely complete a clerical process. They were additional political decisions taken in different conditions and sometimes under sharply increasing pressure.

Indigenous political authority helps explain why disagreement mattered. A chief could be a respected spokesman without possessing unilateral power to alienate all the lands used by every family in a district. Councils, kin groups, religious authorities, and other leaders contributed to community deliberation. Crown commissioners often preferred clear lines of representation and lists of signatories because their own administration required them. Historians must therefore distinguish what the government believed a mark established from the authority that an Indigenous community understood it had granted to a negotiator. A signature is strong evidence that a ceremony occurred; it is weaker evidence of complete uniformity within a nation.

Big Bear, a prominent Plains Cree leader, provides a revealing case. He did not adhere to Treaty Six at the original Fort Carlton or Fort Pitt negotiations, objecting to the proposed arrangements and seeking stronger assurances. Over subsequent years, declining buffalo numbers, hunger, and government pressure narrowed the alternatives available to his people. He finally joined Treaty Six in August 1883. His decision cannot be interpreted solely as either an early miscalculation corrected later or uncomplicated surrender. It took place in materially different conditions from those existing in 1876. The history of the intervening years helps explain why the same leader's bargaining position changed.

Community decisions also involved disagreement about how to use new institutions. Some leaders sought reserves at sites their people already occupied or cultivated; some pressed for additional equipment before settlement; others intended to preserve hunting and seasonal movement as long as possible. Adhesions offered pathways to promised assistance but also placed people within an administrative framework that Canada increasingly controlled. Officials might interpret an adhesion as confirmation of the surrender language of an earlier treaty. Indigenous participants might understand it as joining a relationship that included earlier oral commitments. The interpretive problem was therefore reproduced, not eliminated, when additional signatures were obtained.

Treaty chronology needs to be understood at several scales. At the continental scale, treaty-making accompanied Canada's state-building. At the regional scale, the loss of the buffalo, introduction of railroads, and emergence of agricultural districts changed bargaining conditions. At the community scale, a winter of hunger, the death of a leader, or the failure to receive expected supplies could alter a council's decision. A timeline showing 1871, 1873, 1874, 1875, 1876, and 1877 is helpful, but students should compare it with local histories that continue into the 1880s and beyond.

The historical lesson is not that consent was either wholly free or wholly absent in every case. Those categories erase the uneven power and real choices within which treaties were negotiated. It is possible to recognize Indigenous leaders as serious diplomats while examining coercive circumstances, unequal information, and the failure of the state to meet promised obligations. It is also necessary to avoid assigning to every signer an identical interpretation of land or a common attitude toward the Crown. This combination of agency and constraint is essential to understanding the treaty relationship as an ongoing, contested political process.

Section 82.13

Reserve Selection, Land Surveys, and the Geography of Treaty Implementation

The agreement to set aside reserves did not itself determine where their boundaries would run. After signing, officials and surveyors were expected to consult communities, identify locations, calculate acreage under the applicable treaty formula, and produce plans recognized by government administrators. Every stage could generate conflict. A desirable site needed more than an abstract allotment of acres. Water, arable soil, hay meadows, firewood, travel connections, existing settlement, burial grounds, and access to fish or game were critical to whether the land could sustain a community. A reserve surveyed in an inconvenient location might satisfy the government's numerical accounting while defeating the practical purpose of Indigenous negotiators.

The scale of land is central. Treaty territory was the expansive region covered by the Crown's claim of cession or surrender. A reserve was a particular tract intended to remain for the community's use under the treaty framework. These were not alternative names for the same space. A person who continued to hunt or fish in the wider territory might be exercising an activity addressed in treaty provisions rather than entering land to which the nation had no continuing relation. Confusing the two geographies creates a false history in which all rights and responsibilities were automatically confined inside reserve lines on the signing date.

Surveying was also a political act. A line drawn on an official map translated complex land relationships into parcels legible to the Canadian state. When officials delayed a survey, placed a reserve somewhere other than the community's expected site, or reduced access to an adjacent resource, the discrepancy could damage trust in the treaty. First Nations petitions and subsequent correspondence show that questions of land location and adequacy did not disappear after the council. Survey plans, field notes, letters, and oral geographical knowledge provide different evidence about what had been promised and why particular tracts mattered. They should be interpreted together, not as interchangeable descriptions.

In the southern treaties, the value of a site was often linked to the viability of farming as well as the protection of existing villages. In wooded or water-centered country, lakes, trapping routes, fishing stations, and river mouths could be more decisive than cultivation. The Treaty Five and Treaty Nine areas particularly expose the limitations of making agricultural land formulas the measure of all Indigenous need. A map of Treaty Eight's northern region raises different questions still: how might a reserve or a parcel of land in severalty relate to a livelihood dispersed over large hunting and trapping districts? Geographic analysis clarifies the practical stakes of what can otherwise look like technical clauses.

Students should use historical maps carefully. A modern map of numbered treaty boundaries is a useful index to the state-defined agreements but may present linework assembled or standardized long after the original negotiations. Indigenous homelands, seasonal movements, and shared territorial uses do not necessarily appear on it. Nor do county or provincial borders accurately represent the location of every nineteenth-century community. The strongest geography study places treaty areas alongside river systems, historic posts, settlement corridors, and independently sourced community maps, while making clear when a map is a later reconstruction rather than a contemporary treaty artifact.

This chapter confines itself to the treaty dimensions of reserve creation: what was promised, how location and quantity were chosen, and how disagreements arose. The fuller systems of permits, movement restrictions, reserve administration, and comparative Canadian–American confinement belong in Chapter 84. The distinction matters because a treaty provision for reserved land cannot be treated as consent to every later government rule imposed on the reserve. The historical question here is whether the Crown's performance in surveying and protecting promised land matched the agreements that First Nations understood they had made.

Reserve and treaty territory

A reserve refers to specified land set apart for First Nations use through treaty and administrative arrangements. Treaty territory refers to the much wider region described in the agreement. Rights and responsibilities can relate to locations far outside a reserve.

Section 82.14

Annuities, Agricultural Assistance, Schools, and the Fulfillment of Crown Promises

The practical life of a treaty began when families asked what would happen after the commissioners departed. An annual cash payment could help purchase necessities at a trading post, but it could not feed a household if game disappeared and officials delayed delivery. A promise of oxen or implements mattered only if animals were healthy, equipment suitable, and families able to use them at the correct planting season. A promised school raised questions about who would teach, where it would stand, and whether education would support community purposes. The treaty should therefore be studied not only as a formal legal text but as a series of expectations tested repeatedly in everyday economic and political life.

Annuity days created recurring encounters between First Nations members and Crown officials. They could serve as moments to receive payments, renew contact, seek clarifications, or protest failures. The amounts were modest, and administration could become entangled with government lists of recognized recipients. Yet the annual recurrence was politically meaningful. A payment was visible evidence that the Crown had undertaken continuing responsibilities; accepting it did not necessarily mean that every land dispute or unfulfilled promise had been resolved. Indigenous leaders could point to the annuity precisely because it made the relationship's ongoing character difficult to deny.

Agricultural benefits provide a second window into implementation. Treaty clauses enumerating seed, equipment, livestock, and assistance reflected both Indigenous requests and the government's desire to direct people toward farming. Success required adequate land, skilled labor, technical knowledge, seasonal planning, markets, and access to supplies. As Sarah Carter's study of prairie reserve farming demonstrates, failure or constrained production cannot be explained simply by assuming that Indigenous farmers were unwilling or incapable. Government rules, poor land decisions, inadequate resources, and unequal market access all affected results. Indigenous families often adopted productive strategies that officials undervalued because they did not fit an assimilative administrative ideal.

Specific correspondence should be read before assigning a motive to any failure. An agent's report might attribute a poor crop to weather, insufficient tools, or supposedly deficient effort. Community petitions might identify the precise shipment that failed to arrive or the land the surveyor selected against local wishes. Both documents reveal how their authors framed responsibility. Historians should investigate the timing and scale of provision delivery and distinguish a genuinely unforeseen hardship from a government decision to delay, restrict, or redirect aid. A treaty clause promising support did not give the federal government unlimited discretion to replace the community's priorities with its own.

School clauses also require disciplined interpretation. Several treaties included undertakings regarding educational provision; their presence shows that schooling formed part of negotiations about the next generation. But a promise to establish schools at requested or appropriate locations should not be confused with later consent to policies of compulsory family separation and residential assimilation. Those institutions and their effects are studied in Chapter 87. Here the relevant questions are what the text promised, what Indigenous leaders hoped education might provide, how officials framed schooling, and whether requested facilities and resources materialized. Treating 'education' as a timeless, self-evidently identical category would blur those differences.

One can reconstruct everyday life without inventing an imaginary child's voice or a fictional elder's speech. Choose a documented petition from a treaty community about seed, livestock, a reserve survey, or annual payments. Place it beside the relevant treaty article and the official reply. Ask what season of the year the complaint arose, how a delay could affect food or income, and what remedy the petitioners requested. Such analysis shows the human scale of treaty implementation and recognizes Indigenous people as active users of documentary and political channels, not simply recipients of governmental decisions.

Section 82.15

Hunting Rights, Settlement, and the Restriction of Indigenous Mobility

In many Numbered Treaties the written text recognized continued hunting, trapping, or fishing across extensive lands while also reserving to the Crown authority to take up portions for settlement, mining, lumbering, and other purposes. These provisions created a fundamental tension. A family might understand the treaty as allowing the maintenance of a seasonal round across its customary country. Government departments could interpret the same wording as permitting increasing portions of that country to be allocated to settlers or industry. Neither description can be assessed in isolation from the treaty's exact language, the negotiations, and the subsequent geography of land use.

The first two Numbered Treaties illustrate why exact wording matters. Treaties One and Two did not contain the express continuing hunting and fishing clause characteristic of later treaties, although Indigenous expectations of ongoing livelihood were not thereby made historically irrelevant. Treaty Three's provisions, and those of several later treaties, spelled out rights with exceptions and conditions. The northern treaties increasingly addressed trapping and fishing directly because these pursuits remained central to local life. Rather than giving students a single formula, the chapter should show how variations in the printed articles interacted with the particular activities a community needed to continue.

The practical ability to exercise a right depended on conditions beyond the treaty document. In the south, bison disappeared and fences, ranches, farms, and railway corridors expanded across the country. Chapter 81 analyzes the environmental and commercial causes of the herds' collapse; Chapter 82 examines what this meant for a promise to hunt. A government could describe an extensive legal liberty while the animals on which the livelihood depended had largely vanished. The restriction could then arise from several sources at once: physical changes, private settlement, military or administrative decisions, and competing interpretations of treaty language.

In northern regions, resource pressures took different forms. Rivers and lakes remained vital routes, while timber, minerals, fish, and later oil attracted outside operators. A treaty that recognized hunting and fishing might still leave disputes about the placement of a road, a logging operation, or a reserve boundary. The Crown's ability to authorize a project was not understood by every First Nation as proof that it could permanently displace an activity central to the agreement. This distinction between government assertions of legal power and Indigenous understandings of the treaty relationship is essential to historically fair analysis.

It is important not to collapse the concepts of hunting rights, sovereignty, and freedom of movement. They overlap but are analytically distinct. A treaty might contain a clause dealing with subsistence activities without addressing every aspect of political government or ceremonial relationship to land. Conversely, a community's continued assertion of jurisdiction could reach beyond the explicit resource provisions. Later rules about leaving a reserve, receiving permits, or the conduct of Indian Agents belong mainly to the administrative history in Chapter 84. In this section they appear only where they illustrate how official conduct affected the promises negotiated at treaty councils.

A productive source exercise compares two historical places: a southern treaty territory crossed by settlement railways and a northern treaty territory with extensive trapping routes. Students can locate the treaty text's resource clause, identify the Crown's stated exceptions, and ask what contemporaries expected would remain available in practice. They must then compare those assumptions with the actual chronology of settlement and environmental change. Such an exercise turns a seemingly abstract legal qualification into a concrete question about the relationship between written promises and lived access to country.

Section 82.16

Treaty Complaints, Indigenous Political Advocacy, and the Meaning of Crown Responsibility

Treaty-making did not end when negotiators returned to Ottawa. Chiefs, headmen, councils, and families continued to evaluate the Crown's performance, raise complaints, and seek modifications or fulfillment. Petitions and delegations were forms of Indigenous diplomacy, not evidence that the communities had abandoned their own political institutions. In many cases, leaders appealed directly to the promises made at the original councils and to their continuing relationship with the Crown. That vocabulary allowed them to challenge government conduct from within the treaty framework rather than accept officials' descriptions of the agreement as a fully completed transaction.

Complaints arose over issues large and small. A reserve might not have been surveyed when promised. An annual payment could be disputed. Agricultural supplies might arrive too late, in unsuitable condition, or in quantities inadequate for the number of households. Hunting areas could be affected by surveys or newcomers. The question was not always whether officials had read a provision incorrectly; sometimes the central dispute was whether a public assurance made orally had the same force as an English clause. Treaty One's outside promises are an early and well-documented example, but the underlying pattern appeared across different treaty regions and decades.

Indigenous political advocacy used multiple channels. Leaders sought meetings with agents, commissioners, or visiting officials; communities submitted petitions; delegations sometimes traveled to administrative centers; local councils discussed the consequences of nonperformance. Where surviving records are principally government letters, the historian must recognize that the departmental file may contain only a filtered trace of what Indigenous speakers asked. A reply from Ottawa might summarize a request in the vocabulary of an official's own policy. An Indigenous community's historical account can help identify obligations or grievances that did not receive equal documentary attention.

The distinction between 'the Crown' and a particular government office becomes important here. Commissioners often represented themselves as speaking for the Queen and offering enduring protection. Later administrators could treat aid or reserve decisions as ordinary matters of annual budgets and departmental discretion. From the perspective of a First Nation that had entered into a solemn treaty, changing ministers or administrative rules did not erase the underlying commitment. This tension is not resolved merely by declaring that every officer acted in bad faith; it is a structural feature of agreements interpreted differently by political parties with unequal control over implementation.

Many historical disputes also reveal the limits of evaluating the treaty solely through eventual court decisions. Litigation can provide important evidence and legal interpretations, but not every grievance was litigated, and the source record is much wider than legal judgments. The historian's task is to reconstruct what was represented at the council, what was written, how communities understood it, and what actions followed. A government might claim strict compliance with an article while failing to meet expectations it had encouraged during negotiations. Conversely, a later demand might involve a new application of a treaty relationship rather than a word-for-word demand known in 1871 or 1899.

Treaty complaints thus belong at the heart of the history, not in an appendix describing 'problems after signing.' They show First Nations using continuing political authority to test the promised relationship and to insist that obligations should endure. They also explain why the numbered treaties remain active in Indigenous political memory and constitutional debates today. This section ends the implementation division without retelling the entire reservation system: Chapter 84 will trace the coercive institutions that developed, while Chapter 82 has established how those institutions interacted with promises that were supposed to limit or guide Crown power.

Division V

The Northern Treaties, 1899–1921, and the Enduring Treaty Relationship

Section 82.17

Treaty 8, 1899: The Peace–Athabasca Region and the Northern Treaty Model

After Treaty Seven in 1877, the Crown did not immediately negotiate another agreement covering the far northern interior. This pause reflected government priorities rather than the absence of Indigenous nations or their territorial rights. Communities across the Peace and Athabasca regions continued hunting, fishing, trapping, trading, and conducting diplomatic relationships. Ottawa's interest intensified with the Klondike gold rush, travel through northern river routes, and anticipation of mineral and settlement development. The government increasingly saw the territory as economically strategic and wanted what it regarded as a secure legal foundation for entry. Treaty Eight was first negotiated in 1899 across portions of present-day Alberta, British Columbia, Saskatchewan, and the Northwest Territories.

The commission included David Laird as chairman, James Andrew Joseph McKenna, and James Hamilton Ross. Its work required journeys among communities whose economies and political priorities differed from the wheat-growing districts of the southern prairies. Cree, Dene, and other First Nations representatives sought assurances about continued hunting, fishing, trapping, and their capacity to remain in established places. The commission's official report is especially valuable because it records the government's expectation that northern Indigenous peoples would not quickly become agriculturalists. But that report also defended the commissioners' success in obtaining agreement, and it should not be treated as the only surviving perspective.

Treaty Eight included promises of annual payments, land allocations, and continued resource activities under specified conditions. It also provided a choice between reserve lands for bands and land in severalty for individuals or families in circumstances specified by the agreement. This choice is significant because officials did not anticipate that everyone would immediately settle into large agricultural villages. At the same time, official categories of reserves and individual parcels did not necessarily correspond to Indigenous territorial relationships that crossed river basins and seasonal hunting districts. One can recognize the treaty's adaptation to northern conditions while examining how it still enabled far-reaching Crown claims of cession.

Interpreters and missionaries played consequential roles during the negotiations. The written record includes names and details of meetings at places such as Lesser Slave Lake, Peace River Landing, and Fort Vermilion, but the overall process extended across additional communities and later adhesions. A leader's decision at one post cannot be assumed to speak for all inhabitants of the treaty area. Local deliberations occurred within existing networks of kinship, trade, and religious affiliation; interpretations of the Crown's assurances could differ. Historians should establish which meeting is being described rather than quote an assurance from one place as if it were universally repeated everywhere.

The treaty commission assured Indigenous participants that their normal means of earning a livelihood would continue, an understanding recorded in the commissioners' report. The exact language, context, and audience matter. Northern families could view this reassurance as central to accepting an agreement; the printed text also reserved to the Crown powers to take up land for specified purposes. Later conflicts over resource extraction and land use exposed the tension between these two elements. A close comparison of the treaty article and the commissioner's explanation helps students see how the meaning of a guarantee changes when one reads it as a discrete clause or as part of the entire negotiation.

Treaty Eight marks a shift in the motives for numbered treaty-making. The first prairie treaties were bound closely to agricultural settlement, railway projects, and the buffalo crisis. The northern series arose from resource development and increased access to areas long governed by Indigenous peoples. The Crown still sought a wide territorial surrender, but the terms and assurances had to address a different economic world. This northern example demonstrates both the adaptability of federal treaty administration and the persistence of conflicting views over what was guaranteed about land and livelihoods.

Section 82.18

Treaty 9, 1905–1906: The James Bay Treaty and the Problem of Oral Promises

Treaty Nine, commonly known as the James Bay Treaty, was negotiated during 1905 and 1906 across large regions of what is now northern Ontario. It involved Cree, Anishinaabe, and other First Nations whose lives were linked to rivers, forests, trading posts, and hunting, fishing, and trapping territories. In this case the province of Ontario as well as the federal government had a direct institutional interest in the agreement. The distribution of Crown land and resource authority between Ottawa and the province shaped the negotiation and the design of reserve provisions. An arrangement appearing on a map as one treaty thus also arose from disputes and cooperation between different arms of settler government.

Treaty commissioners traveled by canoe and other means among communities dispersed across a vast northern region. Duncan Campbell Scott and Samuel Stewart represented federal interests while Daniel George MacMartin represented Ontario. Their expedition required Indigenous guides, local knowledge, and interpreters, many of whom had links to trading posts. The commissioners offered treaty payments, reserves, and provisions concerning continued hunting and fishing while describing the agreement as a means of permitting future non-Indigenous activity. Yet even the government's own records show how challenging it was to convey the legal significance of land surrender through short councils mediated by translation.

MacMartin's 1905 diary is a crucial contemporary record. It includes an entry about the council at New Post, associated with present-day Taykwa Tagamou, recording assurances that people could continue hunting and fishing as before. Such entries cannot automatically be taken as a full transcript of Indigenous speech, because the diarist was a commissioner with his own perspective. Nevertheless, the diary demonstrates that an official present at the negotiations understood that assurances about continuing livelihoods were made and regarded them as worth noting. It is especially significant when compared with the printed treaty's qualifications regarding land taken up for settlement, mining, lumbering, and other activities.

Nishnawbe Aski Nation and treaty-area First Nations have drawn on community oral histories, documentation, and the MacMartin record to emphasize the continuing obligations of the Treaty Nine relationship. Their historical interpretations are not interchangeable with a departmental summary: they concern what people understood they were agreeing to and what obligations they believe the Crown assumed. One should not infer that all communities at every 1905 or 1906 council heard the exact same words. But neither should the official printed text be treated as if it disproves oral assurances recorded by a commissioner. The evidentiary task is to identify the source, its origin, the particular council, and the limits of generalization.

The geographical stakes were substantial. Treaty Nine covered a country of river routes, forest resources, and seasonal livelihoods, not simply agricultural land ready for ploughing. The Crown's claim to open lands for mining, lumbering, and settlement could affect areas vital to hunting, trapping, and community continuity. Written reserve allotments, though important, could not substitute for wider systems of resource use. Provincial involvement complicated the issue because jurisdictional claims over land did not necessarily coincide with what First Nations believed the Crown had promised. The question of who would fulfill treaty commitments became more, rather than less, difficult in a multi-government setting.

Treaty Nine thus offers an exemplary documentary comparison. Students can read the official articles, MacMartin's dated diary entries, the commissioners' report, and Indigenous treaty-history materials. Each provides different kinds of evidence: formal terms, contemporaneous observations, official institutional interpretation, and community memory. Their differences are not an inconvenience to be edited away. They are the core of a historically responsible account of the agreement and demonstrate why the meaning of the Numbered Treaties cannot be reconstructed exclusively from their printed cession clauses.

Section 82.19

Treaties 10 and 11, 1906–1921: Northern Saskatchewan and the Mackenzie Basin

Treaty Ten, first negotiated in 1906 with subsequent work in 1907, concerned communities in northern Saskatchewan and adjoining areas of the northwest. As with Treaty Eight, Crown interest reflected projected resource development and access to territories where Indigenous livelihoods remained deeply connected to lakes, forests, wildlife, and commercial trapping. The treaty commission operated within conditions that differed from the first agricultural agreements near Manitoba. First Nations sought security for their established means of subsistence and recognition of community territories, while officials worked toward a formal surrender of the broad region described in the treaty. The significance of such agreements cannot be judged only by the area of land on the Crown's map.

Treaty Ten also demonstrates the need to separate First Nations treaty negotiations from the government's handling of Métis land claims. Officials offered Métis scrip through a distinct land-or-money system intended by the federal government to address and extinguish claimed Métis Aboriginal title. The process sometimes accompanied treaty commissions, but scrip was not a Numbered Treaty made with Métis communities on equivalent nation-to-nation terms. Nor did receipt of an individual certificate necessarily reflect the collective political aspirations of the communities concerned. Treating scrip as simply a treaty substitute would misdescribe both the instrument and the historical power imbalance through which it operated.

Fifteen years later, Treaty Eleven extended the system into the Mackenzie River basin. Norman Wells oil discoveries in 1920 sharply intensified federal interest in formalizing its territorial claims, and the principal agreement followed in 1921, with additional signings and adhesions in 1922. Dene and other northern communities approached officials with concerns about continuing livelihoods, security, and obligations that might accompany a new political relationship. Government negotiators worked under restricted instructions and offered terms modeled on earlier treaties. A federal treaty research report emphasizes that the commissioners' room to bargain was limited and that the pressure for agreement came substantially from mineral development rather than an Indigenous request for a general territorial surrender.

The Treaty Eleven commission included Inspector H. A. Conroy, whose work depended on travel, interpreters, missionaries, traders, and local guides. Government records describe efforts to reassure Indigenous people that their activities would continue, but historians must distinguish the nature and documentation of assurances from the legal wording in the printed treaty. Many communities understood the councils in relation to ongoing relations with the Crown and the protection of their livelihood rather than as transactions fully transferring ownership of their homelands. These historical interpretations should be grounded in local testimony and records rather than generalized as a single Dene position.

The difference between the 1906–1907 and 1921 negotiations is itself significant. The world of early twentieth-century northern Saskatchewan was linked to trapping and transportation pressures, while the Mackenzie basin treaty followed major new interest in petroleum and mineral resources. Canada's treaty program thus expanded when regions formerly peripheral to national development became commercially salient. The chronology undermines the idea of a uniform inevitable advance from treaty number one to number eleven. Treaty-making was discontinuous, shaped by state priorities, local decisions, and changing estimates of economic value.

Treaty Eleven was the last of the eleven original numbered treaties, but it did not bring Indigenous treaty-making in Canada to an end. Nor did it settle, by its signing alone, every issue concerning land, rights, and the content of Crown assurances. The northern treaties expose the same broad tension found in the south: governments treated written cession as securing territorial authority, while Indigenous communities assessed the agreements by the relationships and continued livelihoods they believed had been promised. That tension survived well beyond 1921.

Section 82.20

The Numbered Treaties as Continuing Political and Historical Obligations

Across fifty years of numbered treaty-making, the Canadian state used agreements to support territorial expansion, transportation, agricultural settlement, and later resource extraction. First Nations entered negotiations for varied reasons, including security, protection from newcomers' encroachment, access to assistance, and preservation of land-based livelihoods. The written treaties expressed the Crown's objective of acquiring sweeping land rights, but their histories also contain Indigenous demands, oral commitments, ceremonial acts, community records, and continuing disputes. A balanced account must hold these strands together rather than assume either that all parties shared one understanding or that every encounter followed an identical coercive script.

The southern agreements of 1871–1877 arose amid the immediate extension of Canadian government into the prairies and the accelerating buffalo crisis. Their commissions developed recurring clauses concerning reserves, annuities, cultivation, schooling, and hunting. Indigenous representatives pressed for improvements and assessed terms against the changing economic situation. Treaties One and Two's outside promises, Treaty Three's competing records, and Treaty Six's health and famine clauses show that Indigenous bargaining mattered materially to the texts and to later interpretations. Treaty Seven demonstrates how a printed cession formula may coexist with community-held understandings of sharing and continuing relationship.

The northern treaties of 1899–1921 were influenced by different development horizons. Mining, timber, river travel, and petroleum made regions of northern Canada more important to Crown planners. The continued importance of hunting, fishing, and trapping altered how commissions described the expected future of Indigenous livelihoods. Treaty Eight's commissioners' assurances and Treaty Nine's diary evidence complicate any view that government negotiators merely read land articles and acquired identical consent everywhere. Treaties Ten and Eleven show that even the later commissions operated with distinct purposes, constraints, and relationships to other federal policies such as Métis scrip.

Crown obligations were not completed at the signing. Surveying reserves, making annuity payments, distributing equipment and livestock, supporting education, and respecting resource-related promises were repeated tasks. First Nations judged Crown performance against what had been agreed, wrote petitions, sent delegations, and maintained their own treaty histories. The distinction between formal fulfillment and substantive performance is not an invitation to replace evidence with accusation. It requires careful comparison: what exactly did an article state, what assurances accompanied it, what action was taken, and how did the people affected describe the result?

The deepest historical controversy concerns the treaty relationship itself. For the Crown, cession articles were instruments for consolidating jurisdiction and opening lands to newcomers. Many First Nations have maintained that the agreements authorized forms of sharing and reciprocal coexistence, not a total abandonment of their political existence or responsibility to the land. Scholars working with Indigenous oral histories and legal traditions emphasize that apparently familiar legal words may fail to reproduce what was communicated in council. Other accounts emphasize the practical concessions and administrative restrictions embedded in the written bargains. These are not interchangeable interpretations, and students should compare their evidence rather than decide the question through rhetoric.

The Constitution Act, 1982, in section 35 recognizes and affirms existing Aboriginal and treaty rights; it did not create those rights in 1982. Modern interpretation lies beyond this chapter's principal period, but this constitutional recognition illustrates why the old agreements remain politically and legally consequential. Their histories cannot be closed as if they were obsolete administrative documents. At the same time, contemporary legal language must not be projected back uncritically onto the vocabulary of an 1871 or 1905 council. Responsible historical analysis explains both continuity of claims and change in institutions through which those claims are made.

Chapter 82 therefore sits between three distinct histories. Chapter 81 described the destruction of a major Indigenous economic foundation; Chapter 83 will examine Métis political struggles and resistance; Chapter 84 will analyze the developing reservation and reserve order. This chapter has established the diplomatic agreements through which Canada represented its expansion as legitimate and the promises through which First Nations understood they had secured enduring responsibilities. The difference between treaty, territorial claim, and lived implementation is the bridge to the histories that follow. It is also the basis for understanding why Indigenous nations continued to assert treaty relationships as a source of political authority rather than a memory of surrender alone.

Historical Debate

Land surrender, land sharing, or differently understood treaty?

The question: did the numbered treaties operate primarily as permanent land-surrender transactions, continuing nation-to-nation commitments about shared life, or agreements understood differently by their parties? The Crown’s written cession language supports its claim of territorial acquisition. Indigenous legal traditions and community-held histories often emphasize sharing, protection, reciprocal obligations and continuing authority. Historical scholarship examines translation, negotiating contexts and the divergence between text and practice.

Evidence: treaty articles, commissions’ instructions, speeches preserved in official reports, diaries, community oral histories, later petitions, surveys and actual implementation. Disagreement concerns interpretation of the original encounter and the weight of different records, not whether the documents exist. The task is not to manufacture two perfectly symmetrical viewpoints: state power and access to documentary publication were unequal. Student task: construct the strongest interpretation possible from at least three contrasting sources, identify uncertainties, and specify evidence that could change the conclusion.

Continuity and Change

From nineteenth-century councils to continuing Crown responsibilities

Change: the Dominion acquired increasingly extensive authority over land allocation, settlement, and resources; administration and law developed in directions the treaty parties could not fully anticipate. Continuity: First Nations continued to invoke obligations arising from councils, treaties and Indigenous diplomatic understandings.

The Constitution Act, 1982 recognizes and affirms existing Aboriginal and treaty rights, rather than creating them. This later framework does not replace historical investigation of what participants meant in 1871–1921. Compare one early promise, one record of implementation, and one later claim to identify both continuity and transformation.

Documentary reading: Federal chronology and constitutional rights; Constitution Act, 1982, section 35. These sources should be read in light of their authorship and institutional perspectives.

Chapter Summary

The Numbered Treaties were eleven principal sets of agreements negotiated from 1871 through 1921 across western and northern Canada. Their history begins before Confederation, in Indigenous political and diplomatic traditions and in Crown practices of treaty-making. Indigenous nations possessed existing governments and territorial relationships. The Crown’s legal language of land cession did not necessarily reproduce the meanings that leaders gave to the councils.

Confederation in 1867 and the territorial transfer of 1870 increased the Dominion’s desire to acquire a legal basis for settlement, railways, and resource development. These plans entered Indigenous homelands whose people had not ceded them. The growing presence of surveyors and institutions such as the North-West Mounted Police helps explain why treaties were negotiated first around Manitoba and the southern prairies.

Treaties One and Two in 1871 were followed by disputes over assurances beyond the printed articles. An 1875 Order in Council recognized the outside-promises memorandum. The case illustrates why a treaty cannot be interpreted through the main English document alone and why promises must be examined through their later implementation.

Treaty Three in 1873 established important later precedents in reserve allocations, annual payments and written hunting-and-fishing clauses. The Paypom Treaty tradition associated with Joseph Nolin’s notes allows a critical comparison of Crown and Indigenous-held records. Treaty Four in 1874 connected diplomacy directly to the advance of surveys, police and transportation plans.

Treaty Five in 1875 entered a northern lake-and-river economy in which fisheries, trapping and seasonal routes were central. Later adhesions mean that the first signing date did not describe every community’s negotiating experience. The early treaties varied in their provisions, and the first two lacked the explicit continuing hunting-and-fishing clauses found in several later texts.

Treaty Six in 1876 arose amid rising concern over hunger, disease and the decline of the buffalo economy. Cree leaders such as Mistawasis and Ahtahkakoop negotiated with Crown officials. The printed text contains distinctive medicine chest and famine provisions, whose exact administrative wording must be separated from their wider historical and contemporary interpretation.

Treaty Seven in 1877 involved distinct First Nations and leaders at Blackfoot Crossing. Government records emphasize cession and promised benefits; treaty-area Indigenous historical accounts frequently emphasize peace, sharing and continuing responsibilities. Comparing these sources exposes the consequences of translation and unequal record keeping.

Treaty implementation required reserves to be identified and surveyed, annuities to be distributed, and promised agricultural or educational assistance to be supplied. First Nations frequently pressed for fulfillment through councils, petitions and delegations. A reserve was a small designated land tract within a much wider treaty territory; official boundaries did not exhaust older Indigenous territorial relationships.

Treaties Eight through Eleven extended the system north between 1899 and 1921. Klondike-era routes, mining, timber and petroleum influenced the federal government’s interest. The commissioners for Treaty Eight recorded assurances about continued livelihoods. Treaty Nine’s written document, the MacMartin diary, and Indigenous accounts demonstrate the value of sources beyond formal articles. Treaty Ten and Eleven involved further northern agreements and different development circumstances.

The treaties were not historical episodes completed when a signature was made. They created and were understood to create continuing relationships and obligations, the extent and fulfillment of which remained contested. Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982 later recognized and affirmed existing Aboriginal and treaty rights. The history of negotiated promises is thus essential for understanding the reservation order, later Indigenous advocacy, and continuing political relationships.

Chapter Reflection

The Numbered Treaties show how the same historical event can have more than one documentary life. A written article may survive in numerous official reprints, while a community may retain accounts of promises expressed through an interpreter or ceremony. Neither the existence of an official document nor the survival of oral history exempts the historian from investigating provenance, context and purpose. These practices make the narrative more demanding but also more accurate.

The strongest explanation recognizes Indigenous agency without ignoring unequal power. Leaders negotiated, delayed, asked for specific undertakings and criticized government performance. Canada controlled the machinery of settlement, drafting and much subsequent administration. Both sets of facts are necessary to understand what the treaty relationship could mean to its different participants.

The agreements also demonstrate that the geography of governance extends beyond a line drawn on a map. The territory covered by a treaty could include many distinct First Nations homelands; reserve allotments addressed only particular tracts; resource relationships and continuing obligations crossed those boundaries. The relationship between text and land use is therefore central to any assessment of treaty performance.

Chapter 83 will turn to Métis political struggles, and Chapter 84 to the reservation and reserve order. The distinction matters: a negotiation over continuing obligations was not the same thing as consent to every later federal policy. Students should carry forward the chapter’s habit of asking what was promised, what was understood and what subsequently happened.
