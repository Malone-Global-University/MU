Chapter Opening

Two Indigenous nations, two very different answers to the same question. The Osage Nation holds the minerals beneath its former reservation in common and has administered oil and gas leasing for over a century. The Navajo Nation Council, in 2005, prohibited uranium mining and processing across its territory. Both are exercises of resource sovereignty.

In 1906 the United States divided the surface of the Osage Reservation in Oklahoma into individual allotments, yet reserved the oil, gas, and other minerals beneath it to the Osage people collectively. The reservation lay above one of the most productive oil fields of the early twentieth century. The arrangement made the Osage, for a time, a conspicuously wealthy people, and it also made them targets: of guardianship systems that diverted their income, of fraud, and in the 1920s of a wave of murders aimed at gaining access to Osage mineral income. The Osage mineral estate survives today, administered through an Osage Minerals Council, as a rare case in which a collective mineral interest endured the allotment era.

On April 19, 2005, a very different decision was made in Window Rock, Arizona. The Navajo Nation Council voted to adopt the Diné Natural Resources Protection Act, which prohibits uranium mining and uranium processing on sites within Navajo Indian Country. The Council acted after six decades in which uranium from Diné lands fueled the American nuclear industry, left several hundred abandoned mines, and left a legacy of contamination and illness that the Council's own findings described in detail. The act did not erase that legacy, and it did not resolve disputes over jurisdiction. It did establish that the authority to say no is a form of sovereignty distinct from the authority to say yes.

Placed together, these two cases frame the central problem of this chapter. Possessing a resource is not the same as controlling what happens to it. The Osage hold a mineral estate, but the federal government approves leases and the Osage Nation has contended repeatedly with the limits of that control. The Navajo Nation could prohibit one industry at the cost of foregone revenue, but could not by statute undo the environmental consequences already created. Across North America, Indigenous nations have occupied every position along a spectrum that runs from enthusiastic development partner to determined opponent, and many have occupied several positions in different decades and for different resources.

This chapter examines the relationship between Indigenous nations and the extractive industries that mined, drilled, and burned the continent's nonrenewable resources. It begins with the long history of Indigenous mineral use before European colonization and traces the expansion of colonial and settler mining. It then examines the legal architecture of surface and subsurface estates; the uranium, coal, oil, gas, and hard-rock industries and their consequences; and a series of case studies drawn from Canada, Mexico, and Greenland as well as the United States. The final division considers Indigenous-owned enterprises, consultation and consent, the contemporary demand for critical minerals, and the question of what resource sovereignty ultimately means.

The evidence available to historians is varied and uneven. Archaeology and mineralogical sourcing reveal precolonial extraction and exchange. Colonial, corporate, and government records document leases, royalties, production, and regulation, but usually from the perspective of those who wrote them. Indigenous government publications, legislative resolutions, court records, community testimony, and oral histories supply the perspectives that corporate and state records omit. Scientific studies, environmental assessments, and epidemiological research establish exposure pathways and health risks, though they have limits that students must learn to recognize. Contemporary projects present a further difficulty: legal and project statuses change quickly, and this chapter records the status of cases as verified in late 2026 while flagging where students should check for updates.

The chapter does not portray Indigenous peoples simply as victims of industrial development, and it does not assume that Indigenous nations are uniformly opposed to resource extraction. Some nations have pursued mining and energy development as pathways to revenue, employment, and institutional capacity. Others have refused projects on cultural, environmental, or public-health grounds. Many have negotiated conditions, built regulatory agencies, or purchased equity. These are different political strategies chosen under different economic circumstances, and the chapter treats them as such.

The chapter's argument is that control over mineral and energy resources has become one of the principal arenas in which Indigenous sovereignty, territorial authority, economic independence, and environmental responsibility are contested and exercised. It follows Chapter 135's treatment of living resources and treaty stewardship by turning to nonrenewable resources whose deposits are depleted when extracted, and it prepares for Chapter 137's treatment of the pipelines and infrastructure that carry extracted resources across Indigenous territories. The decisive historical question is not whether Indigenous economic development and environmental stewardship can coexist, but who holds the authority to determine the relationship between them.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe how Indigenous societies extracted, worked, and exchanged minerals before sustained European colonization.
  2. Explain how mineral discoveries and colonial and settler mining accelerated the occupation of Indigenous territories.
  3. Distinguish among surface rights, subsurface rights, mineral estates, leases, concessions, and royalties, and identify the jurisdiction in which each meaning applies.
  4. Trace the development of United States federal mineral-leasing law from the Indian Mineral Leasing Act of 1938 to the Indian Mineral Development Act of 1982 and later reforms.
  5. Analyze the history of uranium mining and milling in the Navajo Nation and its occupational, environmental, and public-health consequences.
  6. Evaluate the Diné Natural Resources Protection Act of 2005 as an exercise of Indigenous legislative authority to refuse extraction.
  7. Compare coal, oil and gas, and hard-rock mining as resource systems with different economic opportunities and environmental risks.
  8. Assess the Osage Mineral Estate and Fort Berthold petroleum development as models of Indigenous mineral ownership and governance.
  9. Distinguish among consultation, negotiated benefits, and Indigenous consent in the Canadian, Mexican, American, and Greenlandic frameworks.
  10. Interpret the Mount Polley, Voisey's Bay, Tecoltemi, and Greenland uranium cases as evidence of different institutional arrangements.
  11. Evaluate the claim that critical-mineral demand during the energy transition can advance Indigenous sovereignty without reproducing earlier patterns of dispossession.
  12. Explain why resource sovereignty is measured by authority over extraction, conditions, benefits, regulation, and accountability, rather than by mineral wealth alone.

Historical Context

Students arrive at this chapter with several earlier bodies of knowledge. Volumes I and II established that Indigenous societies quarried stone, mined copper, and exchanged obsidian, shell, and turquoise over very long distances (Chapters 12, 14, 20). Volume IV examined Spanish mining on the northern frontier of New Spain (Chapter 42), and Volume VI examined the California Gold Rush (Chapter 70). Volume VII examined the Great Sioux War and the Black Hills (Chapter 77). Volume VIII examined allotment (Chapter 86), and Volumes IX and X traced the reorganization of tribal governments and the era of self-determination (Chapters 97 and 114). Chapters 133 to 135 introduced Indigenous ecological knowledge and the governance of fire, fisheries, and wildlife. This chapter draws on those foundations but does not repeat them.

What distinguishes extractive industries from the resource systems of Chapters 134 and 135 is that their resources do not regenerate on human timescales. Wildlife and fisheries can be managed so that harvests continue across generations. Uranium, coal, oil, gas, and metal ores can be removed only once. Indigenous peoples who live above such deposits therefore confront choices with long consequences: whether extraction occurs, on what terms, who profits, who bears the risks, and what is left behind. The distinction does not make Indigenous mineral use unsophisticated, but it means that decisions about extraction cannot be corrected by later restraint in the way that harvest regulations can.

Extraction in Indigenous territories also unfolded within settler-state legal systems that separated ownership of the land surface from ownership of what lay beneath it. In the United States, tribal trust lands, individual allotments, and non-Indian fee lands coexisted within or adjacent to reservations, each with its own mineral rules. In Canada, the Crown generally holds mineral rights, and reserve lands are governed by federal statutes that treat minerals as held for the benefit of First Nations. In Mexico, the Constitution vests ownership of subsurface resources in the nation, and the state grants concessions to private operators. In Greenland, the Self-Government Act provided the framework under which the Greenlandic government assumed mineral resource responsibilities in 2010. Section 136.3 explains these distinctions in detail, because the remainder of the chapter depends on them.

Three broader forces shaped twentieth-century extraction. First, wars and industrial growth created demand: the Cold War for uranium, postwar electrification for coal, and the automobile and petrochemical economy for oil and gas. Second, federal and provincial governments positioned themselves as trustees or regulators, and their interests often aligned with industry rather than with Indigenous communities. Third, from the 1970s Indigenous nations began building their own legal, regulatory, and commercial institutions, and from the 2000s international norms such as the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples gave those efforts new vocabulary. Chapter 121 treats Indigenous economic development generally, Chapter 118 the jurisdictional doctrine of tribal courts, and Chapter 120 water rights; this chapter treats extraction specifically.

Contemporary readers should also note a method point. Many figures used in debates over mining (tonnage extracted, royalty payments, numbers of jobs, numbers of abandoned mines, volumes of spilled waste) come from agencies, companies, or advocacy organizations whose interests differ. This chapter prefers primary legal documents, agency records, and Indigenous government publications, identifies the source of significant figures, and avoids giving numbers whose basis cannot be verified. Where a project is only proposed, approved, under construction, or in production, the chapter distinguishes those stages.

Five Dimensions of Resource Sovereignty

To compare very different cases, this chapter uses a framework of five dimensions of Indigenous resource governance. An Indigenous nation may hold substantial authority in one dimension while remaining constrained in another, and this unevenness is one of the chapter's recurring themes.

1. Ownership and titleWho holds legal or beneficial title to the surface and the subsurface, and under what jurisdiction.
2. Decision authorityWho may approve, condition, or refuse a project, including the authority to say no.
3. Regulation and enforcementWho writes and enforces environmental, safety, and labor standards, and monitors compliance.
4. Benefits and revenueWho receives royalties, taxes, equity, jobs, and contracts, and how benefits are distributed.
5. Accountability and remediationWho is responsible for closure, cleanup, compensation, and long-term monitoring.

Receiving royalties (dimension 4) does not establish control over project approvals (2), environmental regulation (3), or reclamation (5). Holding beneficial title (1) does not by itself confer unrestricted authority to develop. A nation may exercise strong regulatory authority yet lack ownership of the minerals. These distinctions allow the chapter to avoid equating royalties with sovereignty and to evaluate each case by asking which dimensions an Indigenous government actually controlled.

Division I

Historical Foundations of Resource Extraction

Section 136.1

Indigenous Peoples, Minerals, and the North American Landscape

Indigenous societies of North America were mineral users and miners long before European colonization. The best-documented early example is the copper of the Lake Superior region. Native copper, found as nearly pure metal in the Keweenaw Peninsula and Isle Royale, was collected and cold-hammered into tools, ornaments, and ceremonial objects by peoples of the Archaic period, in a tradition archaeologists call the Old Copper Complex. Dating varies by site and method, but the tradition spans several thousand years. Copper from the region later moved through exchange networks across the eastern woodlands, appearing in Hopewell ceremonial contexts (Chapter 20) and in the repoussé plates of Mississippian centers such as Cahokia and Etowah (Chapters 21 to 23). Working copper in this way involved extraction, annealing, and shaping, and demonstrates specialized knowledge, though it did not involve smelting on the scale of Andean or Mesoamerican metallurgy.

Other minerals supported equally important systems. Obsidian, a volcanic glass prized for sharp edges, was quarried at sources in the West, and Yellowstone obsidian reached Ohio Valley sites. Turquoise from sources in the present-day Southwest was mined, worked, and traded, and turquoise ornaments from Chaco Canyon and related centers show its ceremonial and economic importance, with exchange linking the Southwest to northern Mexico (Chapter 14). Salt was gathered from salt lakes, springs, and evaporation sites, and its sources, such as Zuni Salt Lake, became sacred places as well as commodities. Catlinite, or pipestone, quarried in present-day Minnesota, supplied pipes used in diplomacy and ceremony across the continent, and the quarry remains important to Dakota and other peoples. Mineral pigments such as ochre, hematite, and cinnabar served painting, burial, and ritual uses, and stone quarries supplied chert, flint, and other materials for tools, such as the Knife River flint of the northern Plains.

These activities were embedded in knowledge systems. Quarrying, prospecting, and sourcing depended on detailed geological observation that Indigenous communities transmitted through apprenticeship, story, and place names. Modern archaeologists use geochemical sourcing to match artifacts to deposits, which reveals exchange routes but cannot by itself reveal who controlled a source, what rules governed access, or what meanings a mineral carried. For these questions, historians must combine archaeology with oral tradition and ethnographic records, and must remain cautious about equating the sacredness of a site with ownership as modern law understands it.

The difference between this precolonial utilization and later industrial extraction is one of scale, organization, and purpose rather than of sophistication. Precolonial miners generally extracted quantities consistent with local technology, within social systems that regulated access, and for use in tools, ornament, and ritual. Industrial extraction, by contrast, operated through corporations and states, moved materials at continental and then global scale, and depended on capital, wage or coerced labor, and legal systems that treated minerals as commodities. The remainder of this chapter concerns that second system and Indigenous nations' complex relations to it.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Extractive Industries

Definition. Extractive industries are enterprises that remove nonrenewable resources, such as metals, coal, oil, natural gas, and uranium, from the earth for sale or industrial use.

Historical context. The phrase is a modern analytical category. It groups activities that earlier societies organized very differently, from Indigenous quarrying to colonial silver mining to twentieth-century corporate mining and drilling. Its common feature is that the resource is depleted by use.

Why it matters. Because extractive projects have finite life spans and leave waste, decisions about them differ from decisions about renewable resources: closure, cleanup, and post-extraction economies must be planned alongside development.

Potential misunderstanding. Calling precolonial mineral use "extraction" does not imply it was environmentally or economically equivalent to industrial mining. Scale, organization, and legal context differ profoundly.

Section 136.2

Colonial Expansion and the Transformation of Mineral Economies

European colonization transformed mineral extraction in two ways: it linked deposits to commercial networks that reached across the Atlantic, and it introduced legal systems in which minerals belonged to crowns and later states. In New Spain, silver discoveries at Zacatecas in the 1540s and subsequent mining districts in the north drew Spanish settlers, merchants, and miners into the territories of Indigenous peoples, including the groups Spanish sources called Chichimecas. Mining labor drew on coerced systems and on migration of Indigenous laborers from central Mexico (Chapters 39 and 42 treat conquest and the northern frontier in detail). The point for this chapter is that colonial mining created demand for land, labor, water, and wood, and that its camps and roads became instruments of occupation.

In the nineteenth century, settler states in the United States and Canada experienced a series of gold rushes whose effects on Indigenous peoples were rapid and severe. Gold discovered on Cherokee land in Georgia in the late 1820s increased pressure for Cherokee removal (Chapters 65 and 66). The California Gold Rush that began in 1848 brought hundreds of thousands of newcomers and was followed by violence, forced labor, and demographic catastrophe among California Indian peoples (Chapter 70). Gold seekers in Colorado in the late 1850s increased settler pressure on Cheyenne and Arapaho lands in the years before Sand Creek (Chapter 74), and the discovery of gold in the Black Hills contributed to the breakdown of the 1868 treaty settlement and the Great Sioux War (Chapter 77). Later, the Klondike rush of the 1890s brought thousands of prospectors into the Yukon, with consequences for Hän and other northern peoples.

Mining settlements generated further changes. Camps became towns; roads and railroads followed; and ranching, timber, and farming economies grew around them. Settler governments extended territorial administration, surveyed land, and organized militias to protect miners. Indigenous peoples responded in varied ways: some traded food, guided prospectors, or worked in mines and related industries; others resisted, negotiated, or were driven from their homelands. Treating these communities simply as obstacles or victims obscures that they made strategic choices under changing conditions, but the structural pattern is clear: where valuable minerals were found, settler-state commitments to Indigenous territorial guarantees weakened.

Industrial mineral demand also altered political relations. Treaties negotiated before discoveries were frequently revised or abrogated after them, and federal officials sometimes treated mineral wealth as a justification for reducing reservations. The legal categories that emerged, discussed in Section 136.3, reflected this history: minerals on allotted or reserved lands became subjects of federal administration and approval rather than purely Indigenous decision-making.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Mineral Discoveries, Territorial Expansion, and Dispossession

Long-term causes

Commercial and later industrial demand for metals; settler-state policies of territorial expansion; legal doctrines treating minerals as state or crown property.

Short-term causes

Specific discoveries (silver in northern New Spain, gold in Georgia, California, Colorado, and the Black Hills) publicized widely and drawing prospectors ahead of formal authorization.

Immediate trigger

Influx of miners into Indigenous territories, followed by demands for protection, roads, and treaty revision.

Event or transformation

Treaty boundaries redrawn or ignored; removal, warfare, or reservation reduction; creation of mining towns and transportation infrastructure.

Immediate effects

Violence, displacement, loss of hunting and gathering grounds, labor coercion, disease exposure, and pollution of streams.

Long-term consequences

Reduced land bases; legal regimes that separated mineral rights from Indigenous control; later disputes over remediation and compensation.

Historical complexity: Discovery did not mechanically produce dispossession. Outcomes depended on Indigenous political strength, federal and colonial policy, geography, and timing. Some communities negotiated, adapted, or temporarily blocked developments, and some mineral frontiers closed or reopened over time.

Section 136.3

Land Ownership, Mineral Rights, and the Subsurface Estate

Understanding resource conflicts requires understanding that land and minerals are legally separable. The surface of a parcel may belong to one party and the minerals beneath it to another, creating what is commonly called a split estate. Ownership of surface land does not necessarily confer ownership of minerals, and ownership of minerals does not automatically confer a right to develop them without regulatory approval, environmental review, or payment for surface disturbance. These distinctions explain why an Indigenous nation can hold land yet lack authority over what lies beneath it, or hold minerals yet depend on others' approval to develop them.

In the United States, Indigenous lands fall into several categories. Tribal trust lands are held in trust by the federal government for a tribe; individually allotted lands, created largely by the General Allotment Act (Chapter 86), are held in trust or fee by individual Indians and often descended into fractionated ownership; and non-Indian fee lands lie within many reservations. Mineral interests may follow the surface or have been reserved separately. Development of tribal and allotted minerals has generally required leases approved by the Secretary of the Interior, with the federal government acting as trustee and the Bureau of Indian Affairs and the Bureau of Land Management as administrators. Royalties and rents flow through the Interior Department's systems. Tribes may also exercise governmental powers, including taxation and environmental regulation, though the scope of those powers is a subject of Chapter 118.

Canada's arrangement is different. The Crown, in right of the provinces or the federal government, holds title to most minerals, and mining on Crown land is regulated by provincial or territorial law through claim-staking and permitting regimes. On First Nations reserves, which are federal lands set aside under the Indian Act, mineral and oil and gas development is governed by federal statutes and regulations that require surrender or designation of rights by the First Nation and Crown administration. Modern treaties and land-claim agreements such as the Nunavut Agreement and the Labrador Inuit Land Claims Agreement created Indigenous-owned lands with defined subsurface rights and royalty-sharing, and treaty rights (such as those under the numbered treaties) shape the duty to consult. Chapter 117 treats Canadian constitutional law generally.

Mexico follows yet another model. Article 27 of the 1917 Constitution vests ownership of subsurface resources in the nation, which grants exploration and exploitation concessions to private operators. Indigenous and agrarian communities hold land as ejidos and comunidades, but their land rights do not carry ownership of minerals. The 1992 constitutional and legal reforms opened ejido land to market transactions and expanded private investment in mining, and later reforms, including the 2023 amendments to the Mining Law, added consultation requirements for Indigenous and Afro-Mexican communities (Section 136.15 explains the Tecoltemi litigation that preceded them). Greenland, as a self-governing territory within the Danish realm, controls mineral resources under the 2009 Self-Government Act, and the population is overwhelmingly Inuit, so the relevant authority is a public government rather than separate tribal governments.

A mineral estate can therefore be understood as a collection of legal powers rather than a simple physical object. The rights to explore, exclude other operators, receive rents, authorize development and recover damages can be held by different institutions. A lease may convey a limited development interest without conveying ownership of the underlying estate. An environmental permit, in turn, does not convey mineral title. This distinction is especially consequential where allotted parcels are fractionated among many heirs, where roads must cross separately owned land, or where a proposed operation may affect neighboring communities with no share in the lease income. In every case the historian should identify the relevant property interest and the source of each asserted governmental power. Modern treaty settlements in Canada also vary: a grant of surface title, a right to a share of public royalties and ownership of specific subsurface parcels are three different arrangements. None can be inferred from another.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Mineral Estate

Definition. A mineral estate is the legal interest in minerals beneath the surface of land. In United States law it may be owned separately from the surface estate; in Canada, Mexico, and Greenland, ownership of subsurface resources is vested differently, as described above.

Historical context. The separation of surface and mineral interests grew with industrial mining, when owners and governments sold or reserved subsurface rights independently of land use. For Indigenous nations the separation became consequential in the allotment era, when surface lands were divided but minerals were sometimes retained collectively, as in the Osage case.

Why it matters. Whoever owns the mineral estate may receive royalties, but approval, regulation, and cleanup may fall under different authorities.

Potential misunderstanding. The word "estate" suggests control, yet beneficial ownership held through federal trust is not the same as unrestricted authority. Legal terms must be read within their jurisdiction.

Section 136.4

From Colonial Extraction to Indigenous Resource Sovereignty

The early federal leasing system placed Indigenous minerals largely in the hands of the Interior Department. Before 1938, leasing on tribal lands proceeded under varying statutes and by negotiation with the Department, often at low royalty rates and with little tribal oversight. The Indian Mineral Leasing Act of 1938 standardized the process: it allowed tribal lands to be leased for mining with the consent of the tribal council and the approval of the Secretary of the Interior, typically by competitive bidding, with royalties paid to the tribe. The act was intended to give tribes a more regular system, and the Indian Reorganization Act (Chapter 97) provided the model of tribal councils that could consent to leases. In practice, the system often favored operators: leases could be long, royalty rates fixed early might lag behind market conditions, and the trustee's approval did not guarantee that tribal interests were protected.

The mid-twentieth century saw tribes challenge these arrangements. The 1960s and 1970s coincided with a surge in energy prices and with the rise of Indigenous political organization (Chapters 107 to 114). In 1975 tribes with energy resources established the Council of Energy Resource Tribes to share technical expertise and press for better terms. Tribal governments pursued litigation and renegotiation concerning royalty rates, severance taxes, and environmental regulation. In the 1982 decision in Merrion v. Jicarilla Apache Tribe, the Supreme Court upheld a tribal severance tax on oil and gas production, affirming an inherent tribal power to tax non-Indians doing business on tribal land. Federal courts have been less consistent when tribes alleged breach of trust: in 2003, for example, in United States v. Navajo Nation, 537 U.S. 488 (2003), the Court held that the government was not liable for breach of trust in approving a coal-lease royalty arrangement, because the relevant statutes did not create the specific duty alleged.

Congress responded to tribal pressure with the Indian Mineral Development Act of 1982. The act authorized tribes to enter minerals agreements, including joint ventures, production-sharing arrangements, service contracts, and leases, rather than being confined to the lease-and-royalty format, though the Secretary of the Interior retains approval authority. It allowed tribes to negotiate more flexible terms and to participate as operators or equity partners. Later legislation, notably the Indian Tribal Energy Development and Self-Determination provisions of the Energy Policy Act of 2005, authorized tribes to apply for tribal energy resource agreements that can reduce federal approval requirements for qualifying agreements. These reforms moved tribes from being passive lessors toward being negotiators and, in some cases, regulators, but they did not eliminate federal review or resolve jurisdictional disputes.

Outside the United States, parallel developments occurred through land-claim agreements in Canada (Section 136.13), the constitutional reforms in Mexico (136.15), and the transfer of authority to Greenland (136.16). The common thread is a shift, uneven and incomplete, from externally administered extraction toward Indigenous participation in decision-making. That shift is not linear: federal and provincial governments have at times narrowed or contested tribal authority, and Indigenous governments themselves have disagreed about the right balance of development and protection.

The transition from leasing to more elaborate agreements did not occur because Congress abandoned trusteeship. It reflected a change in the demands made by Indigenous governments. A royalty can be calculated from gross production or a defined value; a contractual share in profits can instead depend on deductions for costs. A government that merely receives a payment cannot necessarily audit the underlying accounts or choose the pace of extraction. The 1982 law opened a greater range of negotiated arrangements, but the advantage of flexibility depended on the ability to evaluate them. Legal counsel, accountants, geological specialists and access to production records became part of the institutional infrastructure of self-determination. The 2003 Navajo Nation decision, moreover, concerned the particular legal basis for a monetary damages claim; it did not extinguish the general federal trust relationship. These developments belong to the history of Indigenous political capacity as much as to the history of business contracts.

Section 136.5

Uranium Mining and the Indigenous Southwest

The Colorado Plateau, a region of canyons and mesas spanning parts of Arizona, Utah, Colorado, and New Mexico, contains uranium and vanadium deposits and is the homeland of Diné, Pueblo, Ute, Hopi, Paiute, and other peoples. Uranium-bearing ores were first mined in the early twentieth century for radium and vanadium, and from the 1940s uranium became strategically central. During and after the Second World War the federal government sought uranium for nuclear weapons, and the Atomic Energy Commission, established in 1946, became the sole purchaser of domestic uranium until 1966, offering guaranteed prices and incentives that stimulated prospecting and mining. Cold War demand later broadened as nuclear power plants required fuel. The federal government's procurement system, in other words, created the market that made uranium mining on Indigenous lands profitable.

Navajo lands saw particularly intensive development. According to the Environmental Protection Agency, nearly 30 million tons of uranium ore were extracted from Navajo lands between 1944 and 1986, and the EPA has identified more than 500 abandoned uranium mines on the Navajo Nation. Mining companies obtained leases or permits from the Navajo Tribal Council and the federal government, and mills such as the one at Shiprock, New Mexico, operated in the region. Other Indigenous communities were affected as well: the Laguna Pueblo hosted the Jackpile-Paguate open-pit mine, and the Spokane Reservation in Washington hosted the Midnite Mine, illustrating that uranium extraction was not limited to the Navajo Nation.

Employment was important. Many Diné men found work in the mines in an economy with few wage opportunities, and mining jobs supported families and sustained some communities. Workers often lacked information on radiation risks, ventilation was inadequate in many mines, and language barriers and limited regulatory enforcement compounded the danger. Federal health researchers began studying miners in the early 1950s, and the link between radon exposure and lung cancer in uranium miners became well established in epidemiological literature. Federal and state authorities did not adopt stringent ventilation standards until the 1960s, and decades passed before miners and their families received any compensation. The distribution of benefits and risks was uneven: national security and industrial priorities generated benefits across the country, while the exposures concentrated among workers and nearby residents.

Responsibility for these outcomes was shared and contested. Federal agencies set procurement policy and regulated health and safety; mining companies designed and operated the mines and mills; and the Navajo Tribal Council approved leases, often with limited information and limited regulatory capacity. The Navajo Nation of that period had few of the technical agencies later created. Historians therefore emphasize institutional context rather than attributing outcomes to a single actor, while recognizing that those with greatest knowledge of the hazards, namely federal agencies and companies, bore substantial responsibility for informing and protecting workers.

The early uranium economy was also a product of the organization of the federal supply chain. Private firms explored for and extracted ore; mills processed material; the federal procurement system purchased uranium concentrates. These were connected economic activities, but they were not one institution. Procurement incentives could increase the number of mines without simultaneously increasing the capacity of the authorities charged with inspecting them. The geological distribution of ore deposits, rather than the locations of existing hospitals or roads, determined where many of the new jobs appeared. Some mines were small and operated intermittently; others formed part of larger enterprises with processing facilities. A community could therefore experience the expansion of a nationally important industry without acquiring the infrastructure that might have protected its workers or supported a diversified local economy. The guarantee of a market encouraged investment, while the eventual change in purchasing arrangements exposed operators and workers to a different set of economic pressures.

The relationship between federal administration and Diné employment deserves particular attention. Tribal leasing was one route by which the federal trust estate entered national industrial markets. For a government responsible for a geographically extensive territory, leases offered income at a time when public services were limited and wage work was scarce. Yet the bargaining positions of governments, companies and laborers were far from equal. An agreement to develop a deposit did not amount to an informed choice by every worker or every household that might later be exposed to waste. Workers were employees whose protection depended on workplace rules and their enforcement. Residents who used wells or grazing land were affected through different pathways, and many were not parties to a mining contract at all. A useful historical distinction is therefore between the political decisions of a council, the commercial decisions of a lessee, and the practical choices available to families seeking income.

Uranium workers acquired industrial skills, earned wages and formed occupational relationships that became part of twentieth-century Diné social history. Work schedules connected rural households with mines and mills, and earnings supported children, relatives, livestock and other activities. A history that discusses only hazards risks obscuring the importance workers attached to their labor and the material conditions that made mining attractive. A history that discusses only jobs, on the other hand, treats information about occupational risks as if it were an optional benefit rather than a basic responsibility of employers and regulators. Language, literacy and unequal access to medical institutions affected how warnings were understood and whether workers could document illness. Exposure accumulated over time; a person could leave mining before symptoms became evident. The consequences of industrial employment were consequently experienced well beyond the period for which wages were paid.

The chronology of scientific knowledge is relevant to responsibility. Radon and its decay products in underground mines posed dangers that could be reduced by effective ventilation and occupational monitoring. Researchers studied uranium workers while the industry expanded, and later epidemiological evidence strengthened the association between exposure and lung cancer. But the existence of research did not automatically produce a rule, an inspection, or an effective control at each workplace. Distinguishing these stages avoids the misleading impression that knowledge and regulation developed at the same pace. It also explains why workers and their descendants later demanded access to records: the ability to establish where and when an individual had worked could be crucial to a compensation claim. Records originally generated for production or payroll acquired a new significance as evidence of exposure.

Comparisons with Laguna Pueblo and the Spokane Tribe further demonstrate why the chapter cannot treat the Navajo Nation as a proxy for all affected peoples. The Jackpile-Paguate and Midnite Mine sites had different operating histories, geology, ownership and remediation arrangements. In the former case, the environmental implications of a large open-pit operation differ from the occupational conditions of underground mines. In the latter, the relationship among a mining operator, a tribal land base and subsequent federal cleanup illustrates another configuration of responsibility. Each site must be studied in its own documentary setting. Their shared significance lies not in identical health outcomes but in the recurrent question of whether national mineral production generated adequate protections and enduring economic opportunities in the communities from which the mineral came.

Section 136.6

Uranium Contamination, Public Health, and Environmental Justice

Uranium mining, milling, and waste storage are distinct activities with distinct hazards. Mining exposes workers to radon gas, radioactive dust, and silica. Milling crushes ore and chemically extracts uranium, leaving tailings that contain radioactive decay products, heavy metals, and chemicals. Abandoned mines and waste piles left open after closure can release dust and contaminate soil and water. Many abandoned mines on the Navajo Nation were left without reclamation when production ended, and the EPA has conducted assessments and cleanup actions at numerous sites, including removal of contaminated soil and structures and assessment of unregulated water sources. Some homes were built using mine waste or contaminated materials, and the EPA has identified and addressed such structures as part of its program.

The Church Rock spill of July 16, 1979, is the best-known single event. At a uranium mill operated by United Nuclear Corporation near Church Rock, New Mexico, an earthen dam holding a tailings pond failed, releasing a large volume of acidic, radioactive liquid, roughly 94 million gallons in commonly cited figures, along with approximately 1,100 tons of solid tailings, much of which was intercepted by an emergency catchment; most of the liquid entered the Puerco River. The release is frequently described as among the largest accidental releases of radioactive material in United States history by volume, though comparisons depend on the measure used. It occurred months after the Three Mile Island accident, which drew far greater national attention. Residents, whose livestock and children used the river, reported inadequate warnings, and community members and Navajo officials criticized the government's response. The event illustrated that the principal hazard of the nuclear fuel cycle for these communities was not only accidents at reactors but the waste of mining and milling. Students should distinguish between the mine operations, the mill, and the tailings impoundment, because responsibility, regulation, and remediation differed for each.

Health evidence must be treated with care. Epidemiological studies have documented elevated lung cancer among uranium miners, including Navajo miners, linked to radon exposure, and the federal Radiation Exposure Compensation Act of 1990 created a compensation program that included uranium miners, expanded in 2000, expired in 2024, and was restored and broadened on July 4, 2025, by Public Law 119-21. For residents who lived near mines or used contaminated water, studies have identified exposure pathways, such as ingestion of contaminated water and inhalation of dust, and have examined health outcomes, including kidney disease and birth outcomes, in ongoing research such as the Navajo Birth Cohort Study. Establishing that uranium exposure caused a particular individual's illness is far harder than establishing population-level risk, because many diseases have multiple causes and records of past exposure are incomplete. This chapter therefore distinguishes documented exposure pathways, epidemiological findings at the population level, and the causes of any individual illness.

Indigenous advocacy shaped the response. Former miners, families, and community organizations pressed for compensation, remediation, medical recognition, and accountability, drawing attention to the lack of medical studies, the absence of oversight, and the disproportionate burden on a low-income Indigenous population. Their efforts connect to the broader environmental justice movement, which argues that the siting of hazardous activity has often followed lines of race, poverty, and political powerlessness. Federal responses included five-year EPA plans for Navajo uranium cleanup, and the 2014 Tronox settlement involving the former Kerr-McGee operations yielded approximately $1 billion in funds recovered by EPA in 2015 for work on about 50 mines; a separate settlement announced in 2017 made approximately $600 million available for work at 94 mines. Cleanup remains incomplete, and the Navajo Nation has created agencies, including the Navajo Nation Environmental Protection Agency and the Diné Uranium Remediation Advisory Commission, to participate in remediation and advocacy.

The two temporal scales of contamination are particularly important. The Church Rock breach was a sudden incident that can be dated to a single day, whereas abandoned waste piles, unprotected openings and contaminated water sources constitute hazards that can persist for decades. A community affected by both experiences overlapping problems: the immediate need to identify safe water and protect livestock, and the longer need to determine which materials and locations still pose a risk. Remediation thus requires an inventory of sites, sampling, assessment of exposure, engineering design, funding and decisions about where contaminated material will be placed. Removing a waste pile from one location without adequately managing its destination can merely redistribute risk. The objectives of cleanup must therefore be defined in terms of reduced exposure and durable protection rather than the number of sites visited or contracts awarded.

The financial history of remediation reveals another aspect of sovereignty. The 2014 Tronox settlement arose from litigation over corporate responsibility for environmental liabilities. EPA obtained approximately one billion dollars in 2015 for work associated with roughly fifty abandoned Navajo uranium mines; the 2017 settlement concerning ninety-four mines supplied another major source of cleanup financing. These amounts were earmarked for specific work and do not constitute payments to every affected person. The settlements made it possible to investigate and address selected sites, but the work proceeds through separate assessment and implementation decisions, often involving multiple jurisdictions. A settlement amount is therefore evidence of recovered resources and legal accountability, not proof that contamination has been eliminated. Federal cleanup, tribal environmental review, and compensation to qualifying workers are separate processes with different objectives, schedules and evidentiary requirements.

RECA provides a complementary example of a legislative remedy rather than a cleanup program. Congress enacted the original law in 1990 and expanded it in 2000. Its authorization expired in 2024; on July 4, 2025, Public Law 119-21 reauthorized and broadened it. As of November 26, 2026, the Department of Justice specifies that eligible uranium occupations are covered for work between January 1, 1942, and December 31, 1990, subject to the statute's other requirements, and that claims must be filed by December 31, 2027. The filing deadline is distinct from administrative processing and payment. This changing chronology matters because earlier generations of workers could be excluded by statutory cutoffs even when they had worked in the industry. Legislative eligibility uses defined criteria and presumptions; it is neither a universal payment to all residents of affected areas nor a substitute for medical diagnosis and research.

Community demands have also concerned the design of research itself. A study that takes environmental samples but does not return results in a form residents can understand may fail to meet a community's immediate needs. An investigation of a rare disease in a small population may have wide statistical uncertainty, even when residents have legitimate concerns. Both propositions can be true: evidence may be insufficient to attribute a specific illness, and conditions may nevertheless justify preventive action and additional monitoring. Indigenous environmental agencies and community organizations can contribute local knowledge about historical land use, locations of unrecorded waste, water sources and the ways households actually use the landscape. That information can improve the relevance of scientific investigations without requiring that Indigenous authority derive from external scientific approval.

Everyday Life and Social History

Work, Water, and Households in Mining Communities

For Diné families in the mid-twentieth century, the mines meant wages in a region with limited employment, and in many families several generations worked underground or in mills. Mine camps, trading posts, and wage economies altered household routines, and families who stayed in the same area for generations incorporated mine sites into daily landscapes: livestock grazed near waste piles, children played near open pits, and unregulated springs and wells supplied drinking and livestock water. Women, who often managed households and sheep, bore the burdens of caring for sick relatives and of the loss of water sources used for ceremonies and herding. The Navajo Nation's own findings in 2005 cited losses to traditional ceremonial plants and potable water supplies. Historians rely on oral histories and community testimony to recover these experiences because company and government records seldom described them.

Section 136.7

The Diné Natural Resources Protection Act and the Right to Refuse Extraction

By the late twentieth century, uranium demand had fallen and most mines on Navajo lands had closed, but the industry sought to return. In the 1990s, a company proposed in-situ leach mining and milling near Crownpoint and Church Rock, in communities that relied on groundwater from aquifers that the project might affect. Residents organized groups such as Eastern Navajo Diné Against Uranium Mining and worked with the Southwest Research and Information Center and legal advocates. They argued that the same communities had already borne the costs of earlier extraction and that groundwater, which supplied local drinking water, could not be risked. The company, which held title to some of the land, contended that the project could be conducted safely and that its right to proceed was legal. Disputes over whether the project sites lay within Navajo Indian Country, for jurisdictional purposes, reached federal courts, and the Navajo Nation Council's authority to regulate on non-trust lands within the region was contested.

The Council responded legislatively. On April 19, 2005, after extended debate and amendment, it adopted the Diné Natural Resources Protection Act as resolution CAP-18-05; the Navajo Nation President signed it on April 29, 2005. The resolution's findings declare the wise and sustainable use of natural resources a matter of paramount governmental interest and an exercise of Navajo sovereignty, invoke the Fundamental Laws of the Diné as the cultural and legal foundation for protecting land, water, and life, and recite the damage from past uranium mining and processing, including abandoned mines and tailings and the absence of medical studies in affected communities. Its operative prohibition, discussed in the Primary Source Window below, bars uranium mining and uranium processing on sites within Navajo Indian Country, a term the act defines by reference to Navajo law and federal statute.

The act has been described as significant in several respects. It grounded a resource decision in Diné fundamental law rather than only in economic or regulatory analysis. It affirmed that the Navajo Nation Council could prohibit an activity entirely rather than merely regulate it. And it stated that no further damage should occur from uranium mining, and none from processing until adverse effects from past activities had been eliminated or substantially reduced to the Council's satisfaction. The act did not resolve all questions: jurisdictional disputes over lands that the Navajo Nation regards as within Navajo Indian Country continued, remediation obligations of companies and the federal government remained, and the ban itself coexisted with economic arguments from communities who had benefited from mining employment. The vote itself was recorded as 63 in favor and 19 opposed in the resolution, though some press accounts reported different totals.

Why is a power to refuse as significant as a power to approve? A nation that can only accept or condition projects negotiates from a position in which refusal is not a credible option. The ability to decline, even at foregone revenue, changes the bargaining position of every future negotiation and expresses a judgment about intergenerational responsibility. For resource sovereignty as this chapter defines it, refusal is a mark of decision authority (dimension 2). It is not necessarily better or worse than development; the point is that sovereignty includes the capacity to choose.

The act is best read as an institutional decision made in the aftermath of a documented historical experience, not as a general rule applicable to all minerals and all lands in North America. Its territorial definition is material to its legal operation; a federal license, the Navajo Nation's assertion of jurisdiction, and ownership of the site may raise separate issues. The Council also treated environmental protection as a question of its own law. This is significant because it locates the source of the decision in Indigenous political authority and cultural responsibility rather than in a concession voluntarily made by a company. The resolution's recorded vote also illustrates that a governmental decision can be validly adopted amid disagreement. Historians should distinguish that formal decision from the position of every Diné individual and from the regulatory enforcement of the decision in a contested jurisdiction.

Section 136.8

Coal Mining, Black Mesa, and the Navajo–Hopi Energy Economy

Coal extraction on Indigenous lands expanded in the 1960s and 1970s as the western United States sought electricity for growing cities. The Black Mesa region of northeastern Arizona, in lands shared by the Navajo and Hopi nations, became a centerpiece. In the 1960s Peabody Coal Company, later Peabody Western Coal, signed leases with the Navajo Nation and the Hopi Tribe, approved by the Interior Department, for coal on Black Mesa. Two linked systems developed. The Black Mesa Mine supplied the Mohave Generating Station in Nevada through a long coal-slurry pipeline that used water from the Navajo Aquifer, and the Kayenta Mine, which began operating in the early 1970s, supplied the Navajo Generating Station near Page, Arizona, by railroad. Electricity from these plants served cities in the Southwest, including those supplied by the Central Arizona Project's pumping demands.

The economic effects were considerable. Coal royalties and employment provided substantial portions of Navajo and Hopi government revenue, and the Hopi Tribe relied on coal royalties for a large majority of its budget during these decades. Mine jobs paid higher wages than many alternatives and employed hundreds of Navajo workers. The mines also generated environmental and social costs: land disturbance, dust, concerns about water quality and aquifer depletion, and, for people living nearby, effects on grazing land, springs, and sacred sites. The slurry pipeline's use of billions of gallons of groundwater over decades became a central concern for Hopi and Navajo communities who depended on springs and wells, and grassroots groups such as the Black Mesa Water Coalition, founded by Navajo and Hopi youth in the early 2000s, advocated for ending groundwater use and for a transition to other energy sources.

Disagreements existed within and between the nations. The Hopi Tribal Council signed leases while traditional Hopi leaders disputed the Council's authority and opposed the leases; Navajo communities displaced by the Navajo–Hopi land partition of the 1970s faced separate hardships; and workers valued jobs even as others questioned the environmental costs. These divisions illustrate that Indigenous responses to extraction have never been uniform. By contrast, the Northern Cheyenne in Montana petitioned in 1974 to reverse approval of coal leases and permits issued in 1969–1971; Congress authorized negotiated cancellation arrangements in Public Law 96-401 on October 9, 1980, an example of a nation choosing refusal on cultural and environmental grounds while neighbors pursued coal development.

The end of the Black Mesa and Kayenta system came through economics and regulation rather than a single decision. The Mohave Generating Station closed at the end of 2005, ending slurry deliveries; the Kayenta Mine continued supplying the Navajo Generating Station until both ended operations in 2019 amid declining coal competitiveness, regulatory pressure, and changing utility priorities. Closure brought job losses and loss of revenue for the Navajo and Hopi governments, and prompted debates over economic transition, including solar development and diversification. Reclamation of the mines proceeds under federal surface-mining requirements administered by the Office of Surface Mining Reclamation and Enforcement, and the Navajo and Hopi nations have engaged in monitoring and planning for post-mining land use. Closure is not the end of environmental responsibility: reclamation and monitoring obligations continue for years afterward, and restoration of the prior landscape is not guaranteed. The Navajo Transitional Energy Company, established by the Navajo Nation, later purchased other coal assets, including the Navajo Mine in New Mexico, showing continued Navajo participation in coal ownership even as the Kayenta operation ended.

The coal economy was built through a network of contracts that extended far beyond the reservation. Mine operators supplied fuel to generating stations, generating stations supplied electrical utilities, and electricity supported urban growth and water infrastructure. The communities that lived near mines thus participated in a regional system of industrial development without necessarily controlling its ultimate purposes. A coal royalty was calculated under the terms of a lease; the value of electricity sold elsewhere was distributed through a different set of arrangements. Local wages and royalties could be substantial and still account for only part of the economic value of the larger system. The question of resource sovereignty concerns both the terms on which that network was established and the authority to alter those terms when environmental or economic conditions changed.

Groundwater became a particularly important point of dispute because the value of water to industrial operations differed from its value to the people who depended on springs, wells and grazing areas. Measuring industrial withdrawals is not the same as establishing the cause of a particular spring's decline; hydrological evidence, historical records and local observations are required for that assessment. Yet even where parties disagreed about precise causation, the industrial allocation of water raised a governance question: which institution should determine the acceptable use of a scarce resource? Indigenous concerns could not be reduced to the price of water supplied to the mining company. Water sustained households, animals and places of ceremonial significance, benefits not fully captured by commercial accounts.

The two mine closures demonstrate the economic implications of dependence. The Black Mesa Mine ceased coal production in 2005 when the Mohave Generating Station stopped operating; the Kayenta operation ended production in 2019 as the Navajo Generating Station approached closure. Neither date establishes that all associated leases, reclamation obligations or monitoring responsibilities had ended. Workers who had spent years acquiring mine-specific skills faced difficult employment transitions, and governments that depended on royalties had to reconsider public budgets. A just transition in this setting means more than replacing one source of electricity with another: it entails decisions about training, local employment, revenue, land restoration and community control of future enterprises. Those decisions may require public investment long after a mine's last sale. The Northern Cheyenne example demonstrates a different historical approach: challenging federal lease approval before production had become the basis of the local economy. Comparing these cases clarifies why the timing of intervention changes the set of choices a nation faces.

Section 136.9

Oil Wealth and the Osage Mineral Estate

The Osage people, whose homelands once spanned much of the Missouri and Arkansas river valleys, were removed to a reservation in present-day northeastern Oklahoma in the 1870s, on land purchased from the Cherokee. When the federal government prepared to allot reservation lands in the early twentieth century, Osage leaders negotiated terms that differed from the general allotment pattern (Chapter 86). The Osage Allotment Act of 1906 divided the surface among members of the tribe on the roll, yet reserved the oil, gas, coal, and other minerals to the Osage tribe as a whole. Each original enrollee received a headright, an entitlement to a proportional distribution of mineral estate income rather than title to an individual mineral parcel. The roll created some 2,229 original headrights.

Oil development transformed the reservation. Leases to operators generated royalties on a scale that made the Osage, as a group, among the wealthiest per capita in the United States in the 1910s and 1920s, with payments reaching tens of millions of dollars in the mid-1920s. This wealth drew intense attention. Congress and Oklahoma courts established guardianship systems for Osage people deemed incompetent, and unscrupulous guardians diverted funds; businesses and officials sought to profit from Osage spending. In the 1920s, a series of murders of Osage people, with the aim of acquiring headrights, produced what is often called the Osage Reign of Terror. Dozens of Osage were killed, and the exact number remains debated. A federal investigation in the mid-1920s, an early major case for what became the Federal Bureau of Investigation, resulted in convictions of some perpetrators, including William Hale. Congress amended the headright laws in 1925 to restrict the inheritance of headrights by non-Osage persons. The episode demonstrates that collective mineral ownership did not shield individuals from exploitation, and that federal trusteeship could fail in practice.

The mineral estate itself survived. The Osage Nation identifies itself as the beneficial owner of the Osage Mineral Estate, with legal title held in trust by the United States. The Osage Minerals Council, a body of elected Osage members, administers the estate under the governing statutory framework, including leasing decisions that remain subject to federal approval, with the Bureau of Indian Affairs Osage Agency participating. Statutory reforms in 2004 reaffirmed Osage self-government and the Council's role, and the Osage Nation adopted a new constitution in 2006. An important distinction is between ownership of the mineral estate, which belongs to the tribe, and the individual headright, which is a share in distributions rather than title to minerals. Headrights can be inherited and are held by members and, in some cases, non-Osage heirs, so that distributions are an individual entitlement distinct from the tribe's governmental ownership.

Contemporary disputes illustrate that the Osage Nation still contends with the question of authority. In United States v. Osage Wind, decided in 2017, the Tenth Circuit held that excavation of rock for wind-turbine foundations in the Osage Mineral Estate constituted mining that required a lease, even though the project sought to produce electricity rather than sell minerals. The case shows that the mineral estate has relevance beyond oil and gas, including in the development of renewable energy, and that ownership of subsurface interests can give an Indigenous nation leverage over projects that disturb them.

The Osage example also demonstrates that financial distribution and governmental budgeting must be analyzed separately. A headright payment to an individual is not equivalent to a tax receipt available for public expenditure. The mineral estate's collective ownership makes lease administration a governmental concern, but the statutory rules for distributions determine who receives the resulting income. The distinction helps explain both the opportunities associated with the estate and the severity of attempts to acquire headrights through inheritance, fraud and coercion. The history of trusteeship must therefore be evaluated against its stated protective purpose and its actual operation. The existence of a federal approval process cannot by itself establish that the interests of the beneficiaries were protected. Nor does collective ownership imply that every Osage citizen has identical financial entitlements. The estate's endurance is an institutional achievement, while the historical experience of its beneficiaries illustrates how vulnerable resource wealth can become when financial and legal systems are controlled by outside actors.

Section 136.10

Petroleum and Natural Gas Development on Indigenous Lands

Petroleum development occurred on many Indigenous lands beyond the Osage. Oil and gas production took place on lands of the Southern Ute, the Jicarilla Apache, the Ute Tribe of the Uintah and Ouray Reservation, the Wind River Reservation, the Navajo Nation, and others, and in Alaska, on the North Slope, under arrangements shaped by the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act (Chapter 115). Development patterns changed after the 1970s oil shocks, and again after hydraulic fracturing and horizontal drilling made previously uneconomic shale formations productive in the 2000s.

The Fort Berthold Reservation in North Dakota, home to the Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara Nation (also known as the Three Affiliated Tribes), became a leading example. The context is the Garrison Dam, built under the 1944 Flood Control Act, which created Lake Sakakawea and flooded the most fertile bottomlands of the reservation, displacing communities such as Elbowoods and forcing relocation (Chapter 114 and earlier chapters treat federal policy of the period). The loss of that land left the nation with a fractured landscape and a continuing sense of injustice. After 2008, Bakken shale oil development brought rapid growth, and Fort Berthold became one of the most productive areas of the formation.

The MHA Nation developed institutions to manage the boom. Its Energy Division provides an institutional example of an Indigenous government building regulatory and administrative capacity in connection with petroleum production, including permitting, tribal employment rights, and coordination with federal agencies. The nation negotiated a tax-sharing arrangement with the State of North Dakota for production on trust lands, and revenue supported government operations and community services. Employment and business opportunities grew, and so did infrastructure demands: roads, housing, public safety, and water systems strained under rapid population increases, and the reservation experienced social effects associated with energy booms, including increases in traffic and concerns about crime.

Petroleum revenue's benefits come with risks. Because oil revenues depend on commodity prices, government budgets can swing sharply, and the price declines of 2014 to 2016 and 2020 reduced income and drilling. Dependence on a single resource concentrates economic risk. Environmental liabilities include spills of crude oil and produced water, which is salty and can contain contaminants, as well as disposal of drilling waste, flaring of natural gas, and air emissions. Regulatory authority on trust lands is split among tribal, federal, and sometimes state agencies, creating gaps in enforcement that tribes have sought to fill by developing their own regulatory capacity.

Petroleum production at Fort Berthold illustrates how industrial growth can create new governmental functions in a short period. Production on a reservation with multiple land and mineral ownership categories requires a system for identifying the applicable interests before royalties can be paid. A tribal government may also receive taxes under applicable arrangements and may charge for services or pursue commercial participation, but each revenue source rests on a different legal basis. A government must distinguish receipts accruing to individual mineral owners from revenues available to support public services. The timing of production and changes in market prices may affect both. A boom can therefore generate impressive aggregate income while creating simultaneous demands for roads, emergency response, housing and inspection capacity. Growth increases the need for public administration rather than replacing it.

An effective resource department must reconcile physical and financial information. Production records can be compared with sales statements and royalty calculations; permits and inspections can be tracked against the location and duration of operations. These functions require expertise and dependable access to records. The existence of an Indigenous energy department demonstrates institutional development, but does not establish that the department possesses every power exercised by federal or state agencies. Jurisdiction may vary with land status and the type of activity. An agreement allocating taxation responsibilities, for example, does not automatically settle authority over spill response, worker safety or water protection. Fort Berthold is therefore an especially useful case for applying the chapter's five dimensions: mineral ownership, authorization, regulation, benefits and accountability must be examined separately, even when all are implicated by a single industrial development.

The fiscal risk is not simply that oil prices fall. Resource revenues can also decline as individual wells age, new investment slows or costs change. A government may have committed to recurring obligations such as salaries and services during a period of unusually high receipts. Historical analysis should compare the duration of revenue with the duration of those obligations. Infrastructure built to serve an industrial boom may remain useful, but it also creates maintenance costs. Saving a portion of temporary receipts, investing in other economic activities and maintaining reliable public accounts are means of turning finite production into durable institutional assets. They are not automatic outcomes of having a valuable deposit.

Section 136.11

Canadian Petroleum Development and the Indigenous North

In Canada, the largest Indigenous encounter with petroleum has been in northern Alberta, where bitumen deposits in the Athabasca region have been developed by surface mining and in-situ methods since the 1960s, expanding sharply in the 2000s. The region lies within the territories of Treaty 8 nations, including the Fort McKay, Athabasca Chipewyan, and Mikisew Cree First Nations, and Métis communities. The treaties, signed in 1899, guaranteed rights to hunt, fish, and trap, and these treaty rights became the legal basis for claims that development infringed Indigenous livelihoods.

Indigenous responses diverged. Some communities, notably Fort McKay First Nation, became deeply involved in the industry through service companies, contracting, and employment, and in 2017 Fort McKay and Mikisew Cree First Nation jointly purchased a 49 percent interest in a Suncor tank farm for C$503 million, an example of Indigenous equity ownership. Other communities, including the Athabasca Chipewyan First Nation, raised concerns about water quality, wildlife, and the cumulative impacts of numerous projects, and about the health of downstream communities such as Fort Chipewyan, where a 2009 Alberta Cancer Board review found elevated overall incidence and certain cancer categories; a 2014 follow-up found no statistically significant overall excess over its longer study period, although certain categories remained elevated. Neither study established an industrial cause. Both strategies could coexist within the same nation or region, and communities weighed employment and revenue against effects on hunting, fishing, and water.

Cumulative effects became a central legal and political issue. Beaver Lake Cree Nation, in Treaty 6 territory, brought a lawsuit beginning in 2008 alleging that the combined effect of many authorized projects violated treaty rights. In British Columbia's northeast, a 2021 decision in Yahey v. British Columbia held that the Province breached Treaty 8 by permitting cumulative development that unjustifiably infringed the Blueberry River First Nations' treaty rights to hunt, fish, and trap; the Province later negotiated an implementation agreement with the nation. These cases show that treaty rights can limit the volume of development even when each project has been individually approved.

Northern Canada followed a different path. The Mackenzie Valley Pipeline Inquiry of the 1970s, led by Justice Thomas Berger, gave Dene, Métis, and Inuit communities a national platform and fed demands for land-claim settlements (Chapter 137 treats pipelines in detail). The Gwich'in and Sahtu Dene and Métis agreements of the early 1990s, and the Tłı̨chǫ Agreement later, created Indigenous-owned lands, co-management boards, and royalty shares in the Northwest Territories, where oil production at Norman Wells and, later, diamond mining developed under these regimes. Environmental assessment boards created by the settlements include Indigenous membership, giving northern communities a more formal role than many southern First Nations had. The comparison between northern settled-claim areas and the Treaty 8 region illustrates how different legal foundations produce different degrees of authority.

The Canadian oil sands illustrate the difference between economic involvement and the authority to approve development. The 2017 East Tank Farm transaction gave Fort McKay and Mikisew Cree First Nations a combined 49 percent interest in a specific infrastructure business, with Suncor retaining 51 percent. This was an equity investment, not a transfer of Alberta's subsurface petroleum rights or of the province's general regulatory powers. Unlike wages, equity may provide a continuing share of business returns; unlike a royalty, its value depends on the assets and contractual arrangements acquired. The transaction demonstrates the growing ability of Indigenous institutions to negotiate and finance investments, while the distinction between infrastructure and the mineral estate prevents an exaggerated interpretation of what was acquired. Corporate securities disclosures are useful for the transaction's terms, but Indigenous sources are needed to understand how each nation evaluated the investment in relation to its broader priorities.

The Fort Chipewyan health controversy illustrates the difficulty of evaluating cumulative effects. The 2009 study and the 2014 follow-up used different observation periods, and differences in the statistical significance of their results cannot be read as a simple reversal of community experience. A rare cancer in a small population may generate a large change in a calculated rate when only a few cases are added. Health records can establish patterns, but identifying industrial sources requires evidence of exposure, timing, potential confounders and plausible pathways. Continuing concern about water quality and the availability of traditional foods may justify environmental and health monitoring even in the absence of demonstrated individual causation. Effective governance involves the ability of affected people to participate in the formulation of research questions and receive information about the results, not simply the ability to respond after an industrial regulator has made its decisions.

Section 136.12

Hard-Rock Mining and the Industrial Mineral Economy

Hard-rock mining extracts metals and industrial minerals from solid rock, including copper, gold, silver, nickel, zinc, lead, iron ore, and diamonds. The two main methods are open-pit mining, which removes overburden to reach ore near the surface, and underground mining, which uses shafts and tunnels. Ore is crushed and processed, often by flotation or chemical leaching, to separate valuable minerals from waste. The processing produces tailings, typically stored in impoundments behind dams, while the rock removed to reach ore, waste rock, is piled on the surface. Mining uses large quantities of water and can create acid mine drainage when sulfide minerals in waste rock or tailings react with air and water, producing acidic runoff that can carry metals into streams for centuries unless managed.

Hard-rock mining has affected Indigenous territories across the continent. In Alaska, the Red Dog zinc and lead mine in northwest Alaska operates on lands owned by NANA Regional Corporation, an Alaska Native corporation formed under ANCSA (Chapter 115), under an arrangement that gives NANA a royalty and has supported employment of shareholders in the region, while also provoking concerns about subsistence resources, dust, and water. The proposed Pebble copper-gold project in the Bristol Bay watershed, home to Yup'ik, Dena'ina, and Alutiiq communities and the world's largest wild sockeye salmon fishery, drew sustained opposition from tribes and fishing interests, and its federal regulatory status has remained contested in litigation. In British Columbia, the Tulsequah Chief mine on the Taku River raised questions of acid drainage and consultation and produced the 2004 Supreme Court of Canada decision in Taku River Tlingit First Nation v. British Columbia. In the Northwest Territories, the Ekati and Diavik diamond mines operated alongside Indigenous agreements, and in Labrador, Voisey's Bay (Section 136.13) became a major nickel producer.

Not every Indigenous response was acceptance or opposition. In Wisconsin, the Forest County Potawatomi Community and Sokaogon Chippewa Community purchased the site of the proposed Crandon mine in 2003 to prevent a sulfide mine upstream of the tribe's land and wild rice waters, an example of using property ownership to exercise refusal. Elsewhere, Indigenous governments and corporations have taken equity stakes, formed contracting businesses, or negotiated agreements. A recurring theme is the long time horizon: a mine may operate for decades, but closure, water treatment, and monitoring may extend for much longer, and unclear responsibility for those costs has been a major source of conflict.

Critical-mineral demand now adds new pressure. Governments designate minerals such as lithium, copper, nickel, cobalt, and rare-earth elements as critical for energy and security, and the expansion of battery and renewable technologies has increased exploration in Indigenous territories. Section 136.19 treats this development; here it is enough to note that the new demand layers on a history in which mine closure and long-term liabilities were frequently neglected.

A mine's financial life and environmental life do not necessarily coincide. A deposit is developed when investors expect sales to cover the cost of exploration, construction, operations, financing and closure. The resulting waste may remain chemically active after revenues cease. Closure planning accordingly involves estimates of future treatment costs and arrangements to secure the funds needed if an operator becomes insolvent. These costs are especially consequential for Indigenous nations when a site affects harvesting areas or water used by neighboring communities. A bond or other financial assurance is intended to reduce the risk that the public will inherit a private liability, but the adequacy of assurance depends on its amount, enforceability and the assumptions underlying cost estimates. The presence of a reclamation plan is not evidence that its obligations have already been fulfilled.

The Crandon case demonstrates that the instruments of property law can sometimes be used by Indigenous nations in ways quite different from conventional mineral development. In 2003 the Forest County Potawatomi Community and Sokaogon Chippewa Community jointly acquired the proposed mine property; the permit applications were subsequently withdrawn. The outcome followed a lengthy dispute in which the potential effects on water and wild rice were central. Acquisition was not equivalent to securing a general regulatory veto over mines throughout Wisconsin. Rather, it altered the legal position of two nations in relation to a particular site. The distinction matters in comparative history: an Indigenous government may assert jurisdiction, challenge a permit, purchase a property interest, or negotiate a binding agreement, and the historical effectiveness of any of these strategies depends on the surrounding legal and political conditions.

Section 136.13

First Nations and Mining Agreements in Canada

In Canada, the dominant mechanism for negotiated participation is the impact and benefit agreement, a private contract between a mining company and affected Indigenous communities. Such agreements, which are not required by a single national law but arose from negotiation, litigation risk, and regulatory expectation, typically address employment targets, training, contracting preferences for Indigenous businesses, revenue or profit sharing, environmental monitoring, cultural protection, and sometimes equity participation. Early examples include the Raglan agreement of 1995 between Inuit of Nunavik and a nickel mining company in northern Quebec. Their terms are often confidential, and critics note that confidentiality can limit accountability to community members. Negotiation capacity differs: well-resourced communities may hire technical and legal advisors, while smaller communities may negotiate at a disadvantage.

Voisey's Bay in Labrador is a major case. Nickel, copper, and cobalt were discovered there in 1993, in territory claimed by the Innu of Labrador and the Labrador Inuit, whose land claims were unresolved. The Innu Nation protested exploration activity in the mid-1990s, and both the Innu Nation and the Labrador Inuit Association participated in an environmental assessment and demanded that development not proceed without addressing their rights. After years of negotiation involving the company, the province, and the Indigenous organizations, agreements were concluded around 2002, and the mine began production in 2005. The Labrador Inuit Land Claims Agreement took effect in December 2005, creating the Nunatsiavut Government, and the agreements provided for training, employment targets, contracting, environmental oversight, and payments. Natural Resources Canada documentation confirms substantial Indigenous employment during construction. Indigenous participation in subsequent operations and expansion has varied, and the Innu land claim remained unsettled, so the agreements operated alongside unresolved questions about title.

Three concepts must be separated. Negotiated economic benefits are contractual commitments by a developer. Consultation is a government obligation, under Canadian law, to engage with Indigenous peoples when its decisions may affect their rights. Consent is the Indigenous community's authority to approve or refuse. An impact and benefit agreement can reflect consent if it follows a real opportunity to refuse, but it can also be an accommodation reached after approval is likely. In the Voisey's Bay case, the Innu and Inuit leverage came from unresolved land claims, public attention, and the prospect of litigation, rather than from a legal veto. Historians therefore treat such agreements as evidence of negotiated participation and bargaining power, not as unconditional proof of community approval.

Voisey's Bay is particularly valuable because it illustrates several institutional mechanisms operating at the same time. The impact and benefit agreements negotiated in 2002 by the Innu Nation and the Labrador Inuit Association concerned commitments by the developer. They are not interchangeable with the environmental management arrangements through which Indigenous representatives participated in oversight, nor with the agreements between the developer and provincial authorities concerning industrial development. Each mechanism allocates particular responsibilities to different parties. The eventual Labrador Inuit land-claims settlement and establishment of the Nunatsiavut Government provided a further, distinct basis for territorial and governmental authority. Historians should use the name Labrador Inuit Association for the organization that participated in the 2002 negotiations and avoid retroactively identifying it as the later government.

Employment commitments themselves raise questions of implementation. Hiring an Indigenous worker for construction is different from providing a durable career after the mine enters production. The occupational mix changes as construction ends and operations begin; skilled trades, technical work, transport and administrative jobs may have different training requirements. Procurement commitments can help local firms build capacity, but a contractor's dependence on one industrial customer can expose it to the same cycle as the mine. Training, contracting and workforce retention should therefore be treated as historical processes, rather than as benefits that can be fully measured by the date an agreement was signed. Public documentation of the agreements is necessarily selective, because some contractual terms are confidential. Students should distinguish published commitments, aggregate employment data and independently assessed outcomes. They should not infer private payment formulas or undisclosed consent provisions.

Section 136.14

Mining Accidents, Tailings, and Indigenous Environmental Protection

Tailings storage facilities hold the finely ground waste from ore processing, usually as a slurry behind an engineered dam. When such dams fail, the consequences can be severe because they release both solids and process water into watersheds. Risk assessment depends on geotechnical investigation of foundations, design of dams, monitoring, and regulatory oversight, and failures have prompted reviews of each. For Indigenous communities that harvest fish and rely on surface water, a tailings failure threatens food systems, cultural practices, and trust in regulators.

On August 4, 2014, the tailings storage facility at the Mount Polley copper and gold mine in British Columbia's Cariboo region, operated by Imperial Metals, breached. Roughly 24 million cubic metres of water and tailings flowed into Hazeltine Creek, Polley Lake, and Quesnel Lake, scouring the creek channel and depositing material in the lakes. The affected area lies in the territories of Secwépemc communities, including Xatśūll (Soda Creek) and Williams Lake First Nations, whose members use the watershed for fishing and other purposes; the downstream Quesnel Lake supports sockeye salmon populations important to regional fisheries. Authorities issued water-use restrictions in the immediate aftermath, and subsequent monitoring examined effects on water, sediment, and fish.

An independent expert engineering panel reported in January 2015 that the dam failed because of an unrecognized weakness in the foundation soil and design that did not account for it, and it recommended changes in tailings practice, including more rigorous design and review and greater attention to using less water in storage. British Columbia revised its Health, Safety and Reclamation Code for mines in 2016, adding tailings-related requirements, and the Auditor General of British Columbia later reviewed compliance and enforcement. The Xatśūll and Williams Lake First Nations entered a partnership arrangement with British Columbia concerning the response to the disaster, including participation in monitoring and environmental review, an example of Indigenous nations being incorporated into post-incident governance, though only after the failure had occurred. Questions of accountability remained debated, including the absence of prosecution under provincial mining law and the adequacy of penalties. This must be distinguished from the federal Fisheries Act prosecution initiated by direct indictment on December 6, 2024, against Imperial Metals Corporation, Mount Polley Mining Corporation and Wood Canada Limited: the 15 charges are allegations, not findings of guilt.

The case connects directly to Chapter 135. Fisheries and habitat protected through treaty stewardship or Indigenous management can be endangered by upstream industrial accidents, and the capacity to monitor and respond to such events becomes part of stewardship. Environmental restoration of the creek and lake began soon after the breach, and long-term monitoring of sediments and fish continues. Indigenous participation shows that sovereignty is exercised not only in approving or refusing projects but also in oversight, restoration, and the demand for accountability.

Post-disaster governance at Mount Polley involved more than determining the immediate cause of dam failure. Independent engineering review addressed the physical foundation and design; environmental monitoring investigated the movement of material through waters and sediments; regulatory authorities considered whether operating rules had been followed; affected Indigenous nations assessed implications for the use of their territories. Each inquiry had its own evidentiary standard. A finding of engineering failure is not automatically a criminal conviction, and a measurement of water chemistry at one time and place is not a determination that a whole watershed has recovered. The distinction between these forms of evidence is especially important where the health of fisheries matters to communities with long-standing harvesting relationships. Monitoring must consider the movement of sediment and ecological effects over time, not solely whether an immediate water advisory has been lifted.

The later federal prosecution also demonstrates the time separating a disaster from legal accountability. Canada announced Fisheries Act charges against the three defendants in December 2024, a decade after the breach. According to Imperial Metals' November 2026 financial disclosure, previously reserved January 2027 trial dates had been vacated following an adjournment related to Crown disclosure. The proceedings remained unresolved in that account, and the defendants' guilt had not been established. This dated procedural information should not be confused with findings from the independent engineering panel or with decisions to fund restoration. Indigenous participation in monitoring and response is similarly distinct from prosecutorial authority. A central question for students is whether a partnership created after a failure can also change the institutions that assess and regulate risk before future projects are approved.

Section 136.15

Mining, Indigenous Communities, and Resource Rights in Mexico

Mining has shaped Mexico since the colonial era, when silver from Zacatecas, Guanajuato, and other districts underwrote the Spanish empire and employed Indigenous and enslaved labor (Chapters 39 and 42). After the Revolution, Article 27 of the 1917 Constitution declared that the nation owns subsurface resources, and the land reform created ejidos and recognized comunidades as agrarian communal forms. Indigenous peoples, as holders of ejido or communal land, could control the surface but not the minerals. Reforms in the 1990s, including the 1992 Mining Law and the opening of ejido land to market transactions, encouraged foreign investment, and Canadian companies became prominent. Concessions were often issued by the federal government without community participation, which became a major source of conflict.

Indigenous communities across Mexico have contested concessions on grounds of water use, contamination, damage to sacred places, and lack of consultation. The Wixárika (Huichol) opposition to concessions in Wirikuta, a sacred territory in San Luis Potosí, drew national attention. Mexico ratified International Labour Organization Convention 169 in 1990, and its Constitution recognizes the rights of Indigenous peoples, but implementing consultation in mining remained contested. Violence and intimidation have accompanied some mining disputes, and many communities have faced difficulty securing legal remedies.

The Nahua community of Tecoltemi, in the municipality of Ixtacamaxtitlán in the Sierra Norte of Puebla, brought a landmark case. Two mining concessions in the area had been granted to a Mexican subsidiary of the Canadian company Almaden Minerals, which planned an open-pit gold and silver mine. In 2015 members of the community, supported by the Consejo Tiyat Tlali and the organization Fundar, challenged the concessions in court, arguing that they were granted without prior consultation and posed risks to water and territory. The case reached the Supreme Court of Justice of the Nation. On February 16, 2022, the Court's First Chamber, in Amparo en Revisión 134/2021, ruled in favor of the community and ordered that the challenged concession titles be set aside, recognizing the community’s rights to prior consultation and free and informed consent under the applicable constitutional and international framework. The Court rejected the argument that consultation could occur after concessions were granted.

The scope of the judgment must be stated precisely. The ruling applied to the two concessions affecting Tecoltemi and required that they be canceled and, if reissued, only after proper consultation. The Court did not declare the provisions of the Mining Law challenged by the community unconstitutional, as the plaintiffs had hoped, and commentators noted that it did not by itself invalidate other concessions or establish a general rule for all cases. It did establish an influential judicial statement that consultation must precede concessions affecting Indigenous communities, and the Mining Law amendments adopted in 2023 introduced consultation requirements. In 2024 a constitutional reform recognized Indigenous and Afro-Mexican peoples as subjects of public law, as noted in Chapter 1. Implementation remains an ongoing question, and Chapter 141 treats Indigenous Mexico more broadly.

The Tecoltemi litigation illustrates the separation of a concession from the surface rights of an agrarian community. A federal mining concession can affect the community's future use of its territory even before a proposed mine begins production. Requiring Indigenous consultation only when construction is imminent may therefore leave the earlier disposition of mineral rights outside the community's influence. The First Chamber's 2022 judgment addressed that earlier administrative decision. The official case record, Amparo en Revisión 134/2021, recognizes the relevant rights under the Mexican Constitution and ILO Convention 169, and the remedy set aside the contested concession titles. The judgment did not invalidate the entire Mining Law. This distinction is necessary when comparing the case with Indigenous legislation such as CAP-18-05: the former was a remedy in a particular judicial dispute within Mexican law, whereas the latter was an exercise of an Indigenous legislature's own asserted authority.

Students should also distinguish the legal categories of an ejido and an Indigenous community. Ejido membership and communal agrarian landholding arise from Mexico's agrarian institutions, while Indigenous identity and collective rights have distinct constitutional and international foundations. These categories can overlap, as at Tecoltemi, but neither should be used as a universal synonym for the other. A community's participation in consultation must be understood through its own representative institutions and applicable legal requirements. The availability of judicial review creates an avenue for contesting a concession, but it does not remove the financial, linguistic and organizational barriers involved in pursuing litigation. Nor does success in setting aside a particular concession guarantee that a community will face no future proposal. The significance of the case lies in the timing and substance of participation and in the enforceability of collective rights when government decisions threaten territorial interests.

Section 136.16

Greenland, Inuit Self-Government, and Mineral Development

Greenland's mineral history begins in the Danish colonial period. From the mid-nineteenth century until 1987, cryolite, a mineral used in aluminum production, was mined at Ivittuut in southwestern Greenland by a Danish company, and the mine took on strategic importance during the Second World War. Profits flowed largely to Danish interests, and the economic structure of Greenland remained dependent on fisheries and Danish subsidies. Home Rule, introduced in 1979, gave Greenland elected authority over many domestic matters, and mineral resources became a major issue because the economy depended on a block grant from Denmark. The 2009 Self-Government Act recognized the Greenlandic people as a people under international law; Greenland assumed mineral-resource responsibilities on January 1, 2010, with revenue arrangements under which above a threshold, mineral income reduces the Danish block grant.

Greenland's governmental framework differs from those of Indigenous nations within the United States, Canada, or Mexico. Inuit Greenlanders form the majority of the population, and the Greenlandic Parliament and Government exercise public authority over minerals as a self-governing territory, rather than a minority nation negotiating with a settler state. At the same time, Greenland remains within the Danish realm, which retains responsibility for foreign affairs and defense, and its small population and limited domestic capital make it dependent on outside investors. Greenland's leaders have treated mining as one route to economic independence, and have sought to diversify beyond fishing and tourism, while debating environmental risks to fisheries, sheep farming in the south, hunting, and tourism.

The Kuannersuit (Kvanefjeld) project near Narsaq in southern Greenland became the central controversy. The deposit contains rare-earth elements together with uranium, so that processing would have produced uranium as a byproduct and radioactive tailings. Greenland had maintained a policy against uranium mining from 1988, then lifted it in 2013 by a narrow parliamentary vote. Opposition grew among local residents concerned about dust, water, and effects on farming and fishing, and the project became a decisive issue in the April 2021 election, which brought to power a government opposed to uranium mining. In December 2021 the Parliament enacted legislation restricting uranium prospecting, exploration, and exploitation, and the project’s development path was halted. The law permits qualifying exploration and exploitation for other minerals where the average uranium concentration in the total resource is below 100 parts per million by weight, subject to other legal requirements. As of November 2026, the developer, Energy Transition Minerals, reported that development remained halted and that its legal efforts focused on litigation in Greenland, while preserving its related arbitration and Danish claims. This is the company’s account of ongoing proceedings, not a judgment authorizing development. Greenland has meanwhile pursued other minerals, and the island has attracted renewed external geopolitical interest in recent years, which makes questions of self-government and consent especially salient.

The Greenland case requires a different measure of resource authority. A government with jurisdiction over mineral licensing may still rely on overseas companies for capital, equipment and access to markets. It must therefore evaluate the relationship between prospective revenue and public expenditures for roads, ports, environmental oversight and services. Under Greenland's self-government arrangements, mineral income can also affect the Danish block grant under statutory revenue-sharing provisions. A projected increase in gross mineral sales is not equivalent to an equal increase in the resources available for public expenditure. This helps explain why mining has been debated in relation to economic independence as well as environmental protection. Neither a project approval nor a prohibition establishes independence by itself.

The 2021 uranium law illustrates a policy choice directed at a particular type of mineral resource. Its threshold for other minerals shows that the distinction between uranium mining and mining a deposit that contains uranium is legally significant. The continuing dispute over Kuannersuit concerns both the application of the new law and the developer's asserted rights under earlier arrangements. Company claims are not judicial determinations; the fact that a claim has been brought does not establish that a license must be granted or compensation awarded. Greenlandic voters and institutions have made decisions within a political framework distinct from the federal trust or provincial Crown arrangements discussed elsewhere in this chapter. The comparison is illuminating precisely because the power to legislate on minerals and the practical dependence on outside financing coexist.

Section 136.17

Indigenous-Owned Energy and Mineral Enterprises

An Indigenous nation can occupy several distinct roles in a resource economy, and a single government may occupy more than one at once. It may be a mineral owner, a lessor that grants leases, a recipient of royalties, a regulator that issues permits and enforces standards, a taxing authority, a commercial operator, a joint-venture partner, an equity investor, an environmental monitor, and a provider of public services funded by revenue. These roles imply different responsibilities and sometimes conflicting interests. A government that both regulates and profits from an industry may face pressure to relax standards; for this reason Indigenous governments increasingly separate regulatory agencies from commercial enterprises and create governance structures that distinguish governmental, regulatory, commercial, and fiduciary functions.

The institutional record shows growing capacity. The MHA Nation's Energy Division developed regulatory and administrative functions around petroleum. The Navajo Nation created the Navajo Transitional Energy Company to hold and operate coal assets and to pursue diversification. The Osage Minerals Council administers a mineral estate. Alaska Native regional corporations, such as NANA and Arctic Slope Regional Corporation, are business corporations rather than tribal governments, a distinction central to Chapter 115, and they have developed substantial resource enterprises. In Canada, Indigenous-owned development corporations have grown in contracting, services, and equity investment. Building these enterprises required technical expertise, financial management, legal advice, and training, and many nations invested in education to develop their own geologists, engineers, lawyers, and regulators.

Resource revenue can support education, health care, housing, infrastructure, and government administration, and in some nations it has funded long-term investment funds. The record, however, also shows hazards. Commodity prices fluctuate and can leave governments with budget shortfalls. Debt financing of enterprises can create risk. Revenue concentration in one resource leaves communities exposed when markets or regulation change, as in the case of coal on the Navajo Nation and Hopi Tribe. Distribution of benefits may be unequal among individuals, families, and communities; conflicts of interest arise when officials have personal ties to enterprises; and transparency about agreements varies. The central challenge is post-extraction economic development: preparing for a time when the resource is exhausted, closed, or unmarketable. Chapter 121 treats Indigenous economic development generally, while this chapter's concern is how extractive revenue relates to resource authority.

An Indigenous-owned resource company has a different institutional mandate from a government department. A company's directors must manage assets, contracts, staff and business risks; a government must also consider citizens' rights, public services, territorial responsibilities and the distribution of costs and benefits. A regulator may need to enforce rules against a company that is owned by the same nation. If those responsibilities are not clearly allocated, short-term revenue pressures may conflict with environmental enforcement. Independent technical review, transparent reporting and defined procedures for conflicts of interest can help preserve institutional integrity. These principles do not require a particular corporate form, and different nations may organize their institutions differently. The important historical question is whether a nation's chosen form allows it to carry out its own priorities while maintaining accountability to its citizens.

The Navajo Transitional Energy Company is one example of an Indigenous-owned enterprise established in a changing coal economy. Its commercial activities cannot simply be equated with the Navajo Nation's legislative decisions about uranium. The two involve distinct resources, environmental concerns and institutional mandates. Likewise, Alaska Native regional corporations created under the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act hold assets and operate businesses, but their shareholder-based governance is not the same as the political authority of a tribal government. In a single region, corporate mineral ownership, tribal environmental concerns, federal permitting and community subsistence interests can therefore be represented by separate Indigenous institutions. Recognizing these differences prevents the mistaken claim that every decision by an Indigenous-owned corporation is the decision of an Indigenous government.

Resource income also differs according to its economic form. A royalty depends on the mineral lease's defined payment base; a tax depends on a government's legal power and the applicable tax rules; a dividend depends on the performance and distribution decisions of a company; wages and procurement receipts accrue to workers and suppliers. These streams can contribute to community well-being but cannot be added indiscriminately as though all were public revenue. A development strategy can prioritize local procurement to strengthen small businesses, or invest in training to broaden the occupations accessible to citizens, even when government receipts are comparatively modest. Conversely, a government receiving substantial royalties may face pressure to allocate them immediately, while a longer-term investment strategy requires sustained political support. Analysis of Indigenous economic development should identify the actual beneficiaries and the timescale of the benefit.

The closure of coal operations described earlier provides a reason to distinguish current income from lasting wealth. A resource enterprise might finance durable assets, education or businesses in other sectors; it might also leave debt and obligations that persist after a commodity downturn. The appropriate measure is not simply the enterprise's gross revenue but its ability to serve community priorities after costs and liabilities are considered. An Indigenous government that establishes an independent environmental agency and funds it from general revenue may strengthen its ability to hold operators accountable even when those operators are Indigenous-owned. Institutional separation does not guarantee good outcomes, but it creates mechanisms by which commercial success and environmental responsibility can be assessed against distinct standards.

Institutional Study

The Organizational Structure of an Indigenous Resource Enterprise

A typical modern structure separates functions:

  • Legislative body (council or assembly): sets policy, approves major agreements, and enacts environmental law.
  • Regulatory agency (energy or environmental department): issues permits, inspects, enforces; ideally insulated from commercial interests.
  • Mineral administration (minerals council or department): manages leases, audits royalties, interacts with federal or provincial agencies.
  • Commercial entity (corporation or development company): operates, partners, or invests; reports to a board with defined duties.
  • Revenue and trust funds: allocate income to services, savings, and future generations.

The separation of governmental, regulatory, commercial, and fiduciary roles is what distinguishes a tribal government from a tribal corporation and helps manage conflicts of interest.

Section 136.18

Environmental Assessment, Consultation, and Free, Prior, and Informed Consent

Decisions about extractive projects pass through legal and administrative processes whose design determines how much influence Indigenous nations have. Environmental impact assessments, required in the United States under the National Environmental Policy Act and in Canada under federal and provincial statutes and land-claim regimes, examine a project's likely effects. Cultural impact assessments and consideration of cumulative effects broaden the review, though practice varies. In the United States, federal agencies engage in government-to-government consultation with tribes under executive orders and statutes such as the National Historic Preservation Act, which requires consultation concerning historic properties. Consultation in this sense is a procedure in which the agency must listen and consider; it does not by itself give a tribe veto power over a project on federal land.

Canadian law has developed the most elaborate judicial doctrine. In Haida Nation v. British Columbia (2004), the Supreme Court of Canada held that the Crown has a duty to consult, and where appropriate accommodate, Indigenous peoples when it has knowledge of a potential right or title claim and contemplates conduct that might adversely affect it, with the depth of consultation varying with the strength of the claim and the seriousness of the potential impact. In Tsilhqot'in Nation v. British Columbia (2014), the Court for the first time declared Aboriginal title to a specific territory, and indicated that where title is established, the Crown generally needs the title holder's consent or must justify infringement under the constitutional test. The duty to consult is therefore distinct from consent: it requires meaningful process, not agreement. Later decisions, such as the 2017 Clyde River case concerning seismic testing in Nunavut, examined the adequacy of consultation in regulatory contexts.

International law adds another standard. The United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, adopted by the General Assembly in 2007, addresses consultation and consent in Article 32 (examined below). The Declaration is not a treaty, and its standards are implemented, if at all, through domestic law. Canada enacted legislation in 2021 to align its laws with the Declaration, and British Columbia did so in 2019; the United States announced support in 2010 while stating that it would implement the Declaration within its existing legal framework; and Mexico, which ratified ILO Convention 169, has developed consultation jurisprudence, as the Tecoltemi decision shows. Free, prior, and informed consent therefore does not operate as an identical legal rule in every jurisdiction, and the gap between the Declaration's standard and domestic practice remains a central issue.

In practice, Indigenous participation takes several forms: community representation in assessment panels; Indigenous environmental monitoring programs staffed by community members; project approval conditions that incorporate community concerns; negotiated agreements; and litigation or administrative appeals. Each has limits. Monitoring can be underfunded; conditions can be unenforced; agreements can be confidential; and litigation is slow and costly. A key analytic distinction, introduced in Section 136.13, is between process, which gives a voice, and authority, which gives a decision.

The timing of consultation is as consequential as its formal existence. Consultation conducted before a mineral concession or major project commitment gives communities a different range of choices from consultation conducted after financing and construction arrangements have been settled. Meaningful consultation requires access to information in a form that participants can use, enough time to deliberate, and a process for addressing concerns. Indigenous representative institutions may have their own procedures for reaching decisions, and a developer cannot assume that a single meeting or signature represents every affected nation. Where several communities assert interests in the same watershed or territory, an agreement with one does not necessarily resolve obligations toward the others. These practical issues explain why consultation is more than a count of meetings.

Environmental assessment also requires attention to the scale of the question being asked. A project-level assessment may examine the additional disturbance caused by one mine or well field; cumulative-effects analysis considers how it interacts with previous and foreseeable development across a wider landscape. A harvesting community may experience impacts at the watershed scale even where individual sites satisfy particular permit conditions. Indigenous participation can contribute knowledge of seasonal use and culturally significant places that are not readily captured by standard industrial indicators. At the same time, not all such knowledge should be publicly disclosed. The procedures for handling sensitive cultural information, deciding who can access it and incorporating it into a decision are part of the institutional capacity necessary for meaningful participation.

Article 32 of the UN Declaration provides a comparative standard for judging the design of these processes. Its language directs consultation and cooperation toward obtaining consent before approval and calls for effective mechanisms of redress and mitigation. It does not make the domestic legal authority of every government identical. Canadian constitutional doctrine, Mexican consultation jurisprudence, United States statutes and the laws of Indigenous nations each require analysis in their own terms. Even where courts have recognized a particular procedural or substantive right, implementation can remain contested. Comparing the legal standard, the process actually followed and the resulting decision enables students to evaluate the exercise of authority without confusing recognition on paper with its practical operation.

Section 136.19

Critical Minerals, the Energy Transition, and Indigenous Environmental Authority

Reducing greenhouse gas emissions requires technologies such as batteries, electric vehicles, wind and solar installations, and expanded electrical grids, and these depend on minerals: lithium, copper, nickel, cobalt, graphite, and rare-earth elements, and in some national strategies uranium. Governments describe many of these as critical minerals and have adopted industrial policies to secure supply, citing energy security and competition. Analyses commonly find that a significant share of known deposits of some of these minerals lies on or near Indigenous lands, though estimates depend on the methods used. The energy transition therefore creates both an opportunity and a risk: low-carbon technologies can create environmental benefits at the global scale while generating new demands for land, water, and minerals at the local scale, where Indigenous communities may bear the burden.

Two cases illustrate the tensions, and their statuses should be checked at the time of reading. Thacker Pass in Humboldt County, Nevada, is the site of a lithium project in a clay deposit, located in territory called Peehee Mu'huh by Paiute and Shoshone peoples, who associate it with a massacre of 1865. The Bureau of Land Management approved the project in 2021. The Reno-Sparks Indian Colony, Summit Lake Paiute Tribe, and Burns Paiute Tribe challenged the approval, arguing inadequate consultation, and a federal district court ruled against them in 2023; construction began that year. The Fort McDermitt Paiute and Shoshone Tribe, whose reservation lies north of the site, secured a community benefit agreement with the developer, Lithium Americas, showing that tribes near the same project took different positions. As of 2026 the project was under construction with a federal loan, and the developer’s November 11, 2026 update projected mechanical completion in late 2027; a projection is not an assured completion or production date; the project was therefore not yet an operating mine.

Resolution Copper at Oak Flat in Arizona concerns copper in a deposit beneath land that the Western Apache call Chí'chil Biłdagoteel, a place sacred to many Apache people and listed on the National Register of Historic Places as a traditional cultural property. Congress authorized a land exchange in 2014, transferring the federal land to Resolution Copper, a venture of Rio Tinto and BHP. Apache Stronghold and the San Carlos Apache Tribe pursued litigation, including a religious freedom claim, and the Supreme Court declined to review the case in 2025. In March 2026, following the Ninth Circuit ruling, the land, about 2,400 acres, was conveyed to the company; the Forest Service signed its record of decision on March 16, according to its project updates; the Ninth Circuit declined rehearing en banc on September 29, 2026, and opponents have continued to pursue new and remaining lawsuits. The proposed block-cave mine would, according to the environmental review, eventually produce a large subsidence crater at Oak Flat. The project is not an operating mine, and its legal and development status may change. The case illustrates that sacred sites outside reservations are protected unevenly (Chapter 119 treats sacred landscapes) and that consultation did not result in consent.

The chapter must consider both dimensions. Low-carbon technologies do reduce emissions, and some Indigenous nations see opportunities in lithium, renewables, or mineral partnerships; others see a repetition of earlier patterns in which national priorities set aside Indigenous objections. Neither view is inevitably right. Presenting critical mineral development as inherently sustainable overlooks its water use, waste, and land impacts; presenting it as inevitably harmful overlooks the choices of nations that have decided to participate on negotiated terms. Chapter 138 examines the climate dimension in more detail.

The demand for critical minerals poses a problem of historical comparison. The Cold War procurement system made uranium a strategic resource; contemporary industrial policy seeks secure supplies for batteries, electricity networks and other technologies. In each period governments identified mineral supply as a national priority, but the technologies, legal frameworks and opportunities for Indigenous participation differ. The earlier uranium experience provides a reason to evaluate the current distribution of environmental risks before development, not a basis for assuming that every new mine will produce identical outcomes. Responsible comparison asks which protective mechanisms exist, whether they can be enforced, and who will bear the consequences if the project performs differently from its environmental or financial projections.

The Thacker Pass and Oak Flat cases also demonstrate why project status matters. A land transfer changes ownership; an environmental review evaluates anticipated effects; a record of decision identifies an agency's chosen action; construction establishes physical works; and commercial production generates saleable output. A proposed project can be the subject of community agreements, court proceedings and public expenditure long before it yields mineral revenue. The legal challenges described in this section are consequently part of the development history, not simply reactions to an operating mine. Where Indigenous communities disagree about a project, their positions should be described with reference to the specific community, agreement or proceeding. A benefit agreement is evidence of the position of its parties under its terms, not proof of the consent of every nation with an interest in the area.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Can the Energy Transition Advance Indigenous Sovereignty Without Reproducing Historical Patterns of Resource Dispossession?

The question: As governments expand mining of critical minerals, will Indigenous nations gain authority and benefits, or will transition projects repeat older patterns in which national priorities overrode Indigenous objections? This is an evolving question, and evidence is still accumulating.

Interpretation A — Partnership is possible: Proponents argue that stronger legal frameworks, equity ownership, benefit agreements, and Indigenous regulatory capacity allow nations to shape projects, share profits, and support local employment, and that the transition cannot succeed without Indigenous participation.
Interpretation B — The pattern repeats: Critics argue that urgency and national-security framing compress consultation, that sacred sites and water are put at risk, and that agreements concluded after approval do not constitute consent, citing cases such as Oak Flat.
Interpretation C — Sovereignty means the capacity to choose: A third view holds that the decisive issue is authority rather than outcome: nations that retain the ability to accept, condition, or refuse can pursue either path legitimately, and uniform positions are neither expected nor desirable.

Evidence: Legal records of approvals and litigation; benefit agreements where public; environmental assessments; statements by Indigenous governments; economic analyses of supply and demand.

Points of disagreement: Interpretation of consultation versus consent; weighing global climate benefits against local harms; assessment of how much leverage Indigenous nations have under current law; and prediction, which the evidence cannot settle.

Why it matters: How historians frame the transition shapes understanding of whether Indigenous sovereignty is expanding or being circumvented, and affects policy design.

Student task: Using Thacker Pass and Oak Flat, identify which dimensions of resource sovereignty each Indigenous party exercised, and propose evidence that would show which interpretation fits each case better.

Section 136.20

Extraction, Environmental Responsibility, and the Future of Indigenous Nationhood

The chapter's cases support a consistent conclusion. The first is that possession and authority differ. The Osage hold a mineral estate and have received substantial revenue, yet exercise authority within a federal approval system. The Navajo Nation received royalties and employment from uranium and coal, yet for decades lacked the regulatory capacity and legal authority to control hazards, and ultimately exercised sovereignty most sharply by prohibiting uranium. The MHA Nation built regulatory institutions for petroleum. Fort McKay invested in oil sands infrastructure while other Treaty 8 nations litigated. Innu and Inuit leverage in Labrador produced benefit agreements, and Tecoltemi's victory in Mexico established that consultation must precede concessions without granting a general veto. Greenland, by contrast, controls minerals through self-government and used that authority to restrict uranium.

Resource sovereignty therefore cannot be measured by mineral wealth extracted from Indigenous territories. It must also be evaluated through the authority to determine whether extraction occurs, to negotiate its conditions, to distribute its benefits, to enforce environmental protections, and to hold responsible parties accountable for its consequences. A nation that receives large royalties but cannot enforce cleanup has less resource sovereignty than the sum of its revenue suggests, and a nation that foregoes revenue to protect water may exercise more.

The chapter also demonstrates that economic development and environmental stewardship are not necessarily opposing objectives. Indigenous governments have pursued both, sometimes in the same decade and sometimes through the same institutions, and they have disagreed among themselves about balance. Intergenerational responsibility figures in Indigenous legal and ethical traditions, as the Diné Natural Resources Protection Act's reference to Diné fundamental law illustrates, and it provides a standard for evaluating projects that extend beyond a single generation's economic interests: closure, remediation, restoration, and the preservation of water and land for descendants.

Extracted resources must be transported, and this leads to the subject of Chapter 137. Pipelines, rail lines, transmission corridors, and roads cross Indigenous territories far from mines and wells, and conflicts over infrastructure have become a principal arena of Indigenous land defense. The authority questions examined here, including approval, regulation, benefits, and accountability, recur in those conflicts, often with the added dimension that the infrastructure may cross the territories of nations with no ownership of the resource being carried.

Across the chapter's case studies, the durability of institutions is a more informative historical measure than the size of a mineral deposit. The Osage retained a collective estate but operated within a federal administrative framework. At Voisey's Bay, negotiated commitments created routes to employment and economic participation, while separate mechanisms addressed environmental and territorial responsibilities. At Mount Polley, the disaster revealed the importance of oversight and the long duration of accountability. Tecoltemi and the Diné uranium law show different means of resisting unwanted development: judicial review in one case and Indigenous legislation in the other. Greenland illustrates the possibility of setting mineral policy through public self-government while facing external capital constraints. These are not positions on a single ladder of development. They are historically different arrangements of authority whose strengths and limitations can be compared through the five dimensions introduced at the beginning of the chapter.

The decisive question for intergenerational responsibility is what remains after the project changes or ends. Economic institutions may remain stronger through skills, infrastructure and investments; land and waters may remain altered or require treatment; unresolved liabilities may constrain future choices. A society's ability to decide what it wants to preserve is part of its political capacity. This chapter therefore connects directly to the treaty stewardship discussed in Chapter 135: industrial decisions can affect the fish, wildlife and habitats on which those institutions depend, even when mineral ownership lies elsewhere. Chapter 137 extends that analysis to infrastructure crossing the territories of communities that may have no financial interest in the resource being transported.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: Indigenous Authority Over Mineral Resources

What changed?

  • From community-regulated quarrying and exchange to corporate extraction administered by states.
  • From externally managed leasing toward negotiated agreements, tribal regulation, and, in some places, ownership and equity.
  • From little recognition of environmental and health risks to legislative refusal, litigation, and international standards.

What remained continuous?

  • Indigenous knowledge of land and water, and cultural responsibilities toward both.
  • Settler-state retention of ultimate legal power over subsurface resources in several jurisdictions.
  • Pressure on Indigenous territories whenever demand for a resource rises.

When and how: Change was gradual and uneven, often contested, and strongly shaped by Indigenous political mobilization after the 1970s. Who experienced it most directly: mining communities, workers, and residents near waste sites experienced the earliest and heaviest burdens; tribal governments and enterprises experienced expanding institutional opportunities. Why it matters: The pattern shows that authority, not simply wealth, is what has changed over time.

Chapter Summary

This chapter argued that control over mineral and energy resources has become one of the principal arenas in which Indigenous sovereignty, territorial authority, economic independence, and environmental responsibility are contested and exercised. Its central distinction is between possessing a resource and exercising authority over it, an argument developed through the five dimensions of ownership, decision authority, regulation, benefits, and accountability.

Indigenous societies were mineral users long before industrial extraction. Great Lakes copper, obsidian, turquoise, salt, pipestone, pigments, and quarried stone supported technology, ceremony, and exchange networks, and rested on detailed geological knowledge. Industrial extraction differed in scale, organization, and legal context, not in the sophistication of the societies it affected.

Colonial and settler mining transformed those economies. Silver mining in New Spain, and gold rushes in Georgia, California, Colorado, the Black Hills, and the Yukon, drew settlers into Indigenous territories and strained treaty guarantees. Discoveries did not automatically produce dispossession, but they weakened the commitments of settler states and created towns, roads, and legal claims that favored extraction.

Legal separation of the surface and the subsurface structures every later conflict. In the United States, trust lands, allotments, and fee lands carry different mineral rules and generally require federal approval of leases. In Canada, the Crown generally holds minerals, with reserves and modern treaties creating exceptions. In Mexico, the nation owns subsurface resources and grants concessions. In Greenland, a self-governing territory controls minerals. Ownership of minerals does not equal unrestricted authority to develop them.

Federal leasing law evolved from the 1938 Indian Mineral Leasing Act to the 1982 Indian Mineral Development Act and the 2005 tribal energy provisions, moving tribes from passive lessors toward negotiators and sometimes regulators, while retaining federal approval. Tribal litigation, intertribal organization, and political mobilization drove those reforms.

Uranium mining on Navajo lands from 1944 to 1986, driven by Cold War procurement and later by nuclear power, provided jobs and royalties but left more than 500 abandoned mines and a record of occupational and environmental exposure. The Church Rock mill spill of 1979 illustrated the hazards of milling and waste storage, distinct from mining. Epidemiological evidence supports population-level risk, while individual causation is difficult to establish.

The Diné Natural Resources Protection Act of 2005 demonstrated that the authority to refuse extraction is as significant as the authority to approve it. Grounded in Diné fundamental law and in findings about past harm, it prohibited uranium mining and processing in Navajo Indian Country, although jurisdictional disputes and remediation obligations continued.

Coal at Black Mesa and Kayenta delivered revenue and employment to the Navajo and Hopi nations, tied them to the regional electricity economy, and generated water and land concerns before closure in 2005 and 2019. Indigenous responses diverged, from lease signing to traditionalist opposition to the Northern Cheyenne's cancellation of leases, which shows that no single Indigenous position existed.

Petroleum cases showed different institutional models. The Osage Mineral Estate preserved collective ownership but required defense against fraud and violence. The MHA Nation at Fort Berthold developed regulatory capacity during the Bakken boom, with exposure to price volatility and environmental risks. In Canada, Treaty 8 and Treaty 6 communities pursued partnership, equity ownership, and litigation over cumulative effects, while northern land-claim regions used co-management and royalty arrangements.

Hard-rock mining and the case studies of Voisey's Bay, Mount Polley, Tecoltemi, and Greenland showed that negotiated benefits, consultation, and consent are distinct. Voisey's Bay agreements reflected leverage from unresolved claims; Mount Polley highlighted tailings risk and post-incident partnership; Tecoltemi established consultation before concession in a narrowly defined judgment; Greenland used self-government to restrict uranium.

Indigenous-owned enterprises, consultation doctrine, and international standards expanded Indigenous participation but did not establish a uniform right of consent. Article 32 of the United Nations Declaration states consent as an objective of consultation, but domestic implementation varies by jurisdiction. Contemporary critical-mineral projects, such as Thacker Pass and Oak Flat, show both the opportunities and the risks of the energy transition.

The chapter concludes that resource sovereignty is a profile of authority across five dimensions, not a measure of mineral wealth, and that economic development and environmental stewardship are not necessarily opposed. The decisive historical question is who holds the authority to decide the relationship between them. Chapter 137 turns to the infrastructure that carries extracted resources through Indigenous territories.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Extractive industries
Enterprises that remove nonrenewable resources such as metals, coal, oil, gas, and uranium for sale or industrial use.
Resource sovereignty
An Indigenous nation's authority to decide whether and how resources in its territory are used, protected, or left undeveloped.
Mineral estate
The legal interest in minerals beneath land, which in U.S. law may be separate from the surface.
Surface rights
Legal rights to use the land surface, which do not necessarily include minerals beneath it.
Subsurface rights
Legal rights to minerals or resources below the surface; ownership differs by jurisdiction.
Split estate
Land whose surface and subsurface interests are held by different owners.
Trust land
In the United States, land with title held by the federal government for a tribe or individual Indian.
Mineral lease
A contract allowing extraction in return for payment, often needing government approval on Indigenous lands.
Mining concession
In Mexico, a state grant allowing a private party to explore or exploit minerals owned by the nation.
Royalty
A payment to a mineral owner based on production value or volume.
Headright
An Osage share of the mineral estate's income, distinct from tribal ownership of the estate.
Resource rent
Income exceeding the costs of production, arising from the scarcity or quality of a resource, which governments and owners seek to capture.
Uranium mining
Extraction of uranium-bearing ore, with hazards including radon and dust for workers.
Uranium milling
Chemical processing of ore into concentrate, generating tailings with residual radioactivity.
Tailings
Finely ground waste remaining after valuable minerals are extracted, usually stored in impoundments.
Waste rock
Rock removed during mining that contains little recoverable ore.
Acid mine drainage
Acidic, metal-bearing water produced when sulfide minerals contact air and water.
Radioactive contamination
Presence of radioactive materials in soil, water, structures, or air at levels that may pose risk.
Occupational exposure
Contact with hazards, such as radon, through work.
Environmental justice
The principle that environmental burdens and benefits should be fairly distributed, and the movement advancing it.
Environmental remediation
Actions to clean up or reduce contamination.
Mine reclamation
Restoring mined land to a stable condition, which may differ from its original state.
Abandoned mine
A mine left without responsible operator or completed reclamation.
Impact and benefit agreement
A negotiated contract between a developer and affected communities on benefits and commitments, used chiefly in Canada.
Revenue sharing
Arrangements by which resource income is divided among governments, communities, or entities.
Indigenous procurement
Contracting preferences or requirements favoring Indigenous businesses.
Joint venture
A business arrangement sharing ownership, risk, and profit between parties.
Environmental impact assessment
A formal review of likely environmental effects before project approval.
Cumulative effects
The combined impacts of multiple developments over space and time.
Duty to consult
In Canada, the Crown's constitutional obligation to consult and, where appropriate, accommodate Indigenous peoples when its conduct may affect their rights.
Free, prior, and informed consent
An international standard of the UN Declaration, whose legal effect varies by domestic jurisdiction.
Indigenous environmental jurisdiction
An Indigenous government's authority to regulate environmental matters within its territory, whose scope is defined variably by federal, provincial, and Indigenous law.
Critical minerals
Minerals that governments designate as essential to economies or security and vulnerable to supply disruption.
Energy transition
The shift from fossil-fuel-based energy toward lower-carbon systems.
Economic diversification
Development of multiple sources of income so that a community is less dependent on a single resource.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that sovereignty is exercised in concrete decisions about land, water, and the ground beneath them. Whether a mine opens, who inspects it, who profits, and who pays for cleanup are decisions that reveal whether a nation's authority is real. The cases from the Navajo Nation, Osage Nation, Fort Berthold, Labrador, British Columbia, Puebla, and Greenland show that the answers vary with law, history, and political strength.

The chapter also reveals continuities across centuries. Where valuable minerals lay beneath Indigenous lands, whether silver, gold, uranium, coal, oil, or lithium, outside demand pressed on Indigenous territories, and legal systems often favored extraction. What changed was that Indigenous nations built institutions, litigated, legislated, negotiated, and invoked international standards, so that decisions once made largely by others are now contested by Indigenous governments with growing capacity.

It shaped later developments in this textbook. The authority questions examined here, approval, regulation, benefits, and accountability, recur in Chapter 137's conflicts over pipelines and infrastructure and in Chapter 138's treatment of climate change, where the same communities face both the effects of fossil-fuel use and the new demands of the transition. They also connect to Chapter 135, because extraction can threaten the fisheries and habitats whose stewardship Indigenous nations have worked to restore.

The chapter connects regions and periods by insisting on a continental view. Mexican concessions, Canadian agreements, Greenlandic self-government, and American trust law are different legal worlds, yet the same questions recur across them. Placing them side by side prevents the assumption that American tribal law describes the whole continent and allows students to see how institutional design affects practical power.

Finally, the chapter offers a way of thinking about the future. Indigenous economic development and environmental stewardship are not necessarily opposing goals, and Indigenous nations have pursued both. The decisive question is who has the authority to determine the relationship between them, and the history traced here suggests that this authority, more than any quantity of royalties or jobs, is the measure of resource sovereignty and a central dimension of Indigenous nationhood in the twenty-first century.
