Boarding Schools and Residential Schools

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

In October 1879, a small group of Lakota children arrived at a former army barracks in Carlisle, Pennsylvania, to begin a program whose founder believed that Indigenous identity could be removed through schooling. In the same year, a Canadian official returned from studying American industrial schools with a report urging that Indigenous children be educated away from their parents. Two governments, one border, and a shared assumption had begun to converge.

This chapter studies the boarding schools of the United States and the residential schools of Canada as institutions: how they were conceived, authorized, financed, staffed, regulated, criticized, and eventually transformed. Its subject is not a single kind of school. It is a family of institutions that included small mission day schools, church-run residential schools on the Canadian Prairies, large military-style industrial schools such as Carlisle, government boarding schools on reservations, and, in the later twentieth century, student residences and hostels in the Arctic. What linked them was a policy purpose that governments and churches stated openly in their own records: to change the languages, religions, family relationships, work habits, and political loyalties of Indigenous children.

That purpose requires a historian to distinguish education from schooling. Chapter 1 established that Indigenous nations possessed their own systems of teaching and learning long before any European-style school existed, and this chapter begins by describing those systems so that the later narrative is not mistaken for the arrival of education among peoples who lacked it. The institutions examined here did not introduce education. They sought to replace one educational order, rooted in kinship, community, land, and language, with another, controlled by outside authorities and designed to serve their objectives. This is the governing argument of the chapter: boarding and residential schools were components of broader assimilation policy, not conventional schools that happened to enroll Indigenous students.

The two national systems were related but not identical. The American system grew from missionary schooling, treaty obligations, and the 1819 Civilization Fund Act, and then expanded through the off-reservation model associated with Richard Henry Pratt. The Canadian system grew from church-operated institutions that predated Confederation, was redirected by Nicholas Flood Davin's 1879 report, and became a partnership in which the federal Department of Indian Affairs funded and supervised schools that churches largely operated. Legal authority, religious involvement, geography, funding, and the timing of reform all differed. This chapter keeps the two systems distinct and compares them explicitly, most directly in §87.7 and §87.20.

The chapter also insists on three further distinctions. The first is between compulsory schooling and compulsory residence: a law requiring school attendance is not the same as a law assigning a child to a distant institution. The second is between documented institutional history and the lived experience of children, which is treated more fully in Chapter 88. The third is between the historical operation of these schools and the modern investigations, burial-site research, and reconciliation processes that belong to Chapters 127 and 128. Counts of schools, students, and deaths are used here only with explicit attention to how each total was defined.

Indigenous peoples were never merely recipients of this policy. Parents refused enrollment, petitioned for the return of children, demanded better food and medical care, pressed for schools closer to home, and sometimes pursued literacy and formal education on their own terms while rejecting the suppression of their languages and religions. Students resisted, adapted, and wrote letters that survive in uneven form. Councils negotiated treaty provisions for schooling. From the 1960s and 1970s, Indigenous organizations demanded and won administrative control of educational institutions. This history of opposition and reclamation is part of the central narrative, not a postscript.

The evidence is substantial but uneven. Governments produced annual reports, inspection reports, regulations, appropriation acts, contracts, and correspondence. Schools produced registers, newspapers, photographs, and student files. These records were created by administrators with institutional purposes, and they often conceal as much as they show. Indigenous perspectives survive in petitions, letters, council minutes, oral histories, and later testimony. A historian must read official language critically, ask whose voice a source preserves, and recognize what the archive does not record.

Chapter 87 stands within Volume VIII's account of the assimilation era, alongside Chapter 85 on the Indian Act, Chapter 86 on allotment, and Chapter 88 on children, families, and cultural survival. Its task is to explain how the institutions worked. Chapter 88 turns to what their operation meant for Indigenous children and communities, and Chapters 127 and 128 take up the modern reckoning.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe Indigenous systems of education that existed before boarding and residential schools and explain why removal changed more than a child's schooling.
  2. Trace the origins of institutional assimilationist education in the United States, from missionary schools and the Civilization Fund Act of 1819 to Carlisle in 1879.
  3. Trace the origins of the Canadian residential school system from pre-Confederation mission schools through the Davin Report of 1879 and the expansion beginning in 1883.
  4. Distinguish compulsory school attendance from compulsory residence in a distant institution, using specific American and Canadian laws.
  5. Explain how governments, churches, contracts, and funding formulas divided authority over schools and sometimes weakened accountability.
  6. Compare boarding, industrial, mission, and day schools and Canadian residential, industrial, and day institutions, explaining why the differences mattered.
  7. Analyze language suppression, renaming, uniforms, religious instruction, and discipline as intentional components of an assimilationist curriculum.
  8. Evaluate industrial training and the Carlisle outing system as education, institutional maintenance, and labor, using documentary evidence.
  9. Assess the relationship among funding shortfalls, overcrowding, disease, and oversight, including P. H. Bryce's 1907 investigation, while distinguishing documented deaths from estimates.
  10. Interpret the evidence for Indigenous parental, student, and community opposition and explain the limits of school-produced sources.
  11. Explain the Meriam Report, the Indian New Deal, Canadian postwar reviews, the 1969 transition, and the movement for Indigenous control of education.
  12. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of the purposes of boarding and residential schools using evidence from this chapter.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did the United States and Canada create educational institutions intended to transform Indigenous children into members of settler society?
  2. What legal and administrative mechanisms sustained those institutions, and how did they differ between the two countries?
  3. In what sense were these schools components of a wider policy rather than isolated educational experiments?
  4. Why does the distinction between compulsory attendance and compulsory residence matter for historical accuracy?
  5. How did divided authority among governments, churches, and local administrators affect accountability for children's welfare?
  6. What can official reports, registers, photographs, and school newspapers establish, and what can they not establish?
  7. How did Indigenous parents, students, councils, and organizations contest school policies, and why did their choices differ?
  8. Why did reform of policy so often fail to end the institutions themselves?
  9. How should historians weigh the stated educational aims of schools against their assimilationist purposes and documented consequences?
  10. What does the movement for Indigenous control of education reveal about continuity in Indigenous political authority?

Historical Context

Students arriving at this chapter have already seen the transformation of Indigenous political life in the nineteenth century. Treaties, wars, removals, and the confinement of nations on reservations and reserves, discussed in Chapters 61 through 84, reduced the land base on which Indigenous economies and governments had rested. Chapter 84 compared the reservation and reserve orders; Chapter 85 described the Canadian Indian Act's administrative reach; and Chapter 86 examined allotment in the United States. Schooling belonged to this same field of policy. Officials who believed that Indigenous societies would disappear as distinct polities, or who wished to hasten that outcome, treated the education of children as a means of reshaping the next generation.

Two intellectual currents reinforced this approach. Many nineteenth-century Euro-American and Canadian policy makers held a hierarchical view of human societies in which "civilization," defined as settled agriculture, private property, Christianity, and the English or French languages, was the destination toward which others should be guided. Missionary organizations shared this view and added a religious purpose. At the same time, officials increasingly regarded adults as difficult to change and children as malleable. The conclusion that children should be separated from their parents' influence appears repeatedly in reports and speeches discussed in this chapter. It is a historical argument made by contemporaries, and the chapter examines it as such, not as an established truth about children or culture.

Financial and political considerations also mattered. Governments sought ways to administer Indigenous populations at lower cost, to prepare the ground for land sales and allotment, and to fulfill, minimally, treaty promises of education that Indigenous negotiators had often requested. In many treaty negotiations, Indigenous leaders did ask for schools and teachers, understanding literacy and new skills as tools for survival in a changing world. Governments later used treaty education clauses to justify institutions their negotiators had not envisioned. That tension between what Indigenous nations sought and what governments built runs through the chapter.

Finally, the institutions must be placed within the wider histories of schooling in the United States and Canada. Public education was expanding, industrial and vocational training was fashionable, and reformatories and orphanages were growing. Indigenous boarding and residential schools drew on these models. But they differed in decisive respects: they were designed for a population defined by Indian status or tribal membership, they aimed explicitly at cultural replacement, they were operated under special statutes and departments, and in many cases they removed children across long distances from families whose legal and political status differed from that of other citizens. Understanding both the resemblances and the differences is necessary for a precise historical account.

Indigenous Education and the Origins of Assimilationist Schooling

Foundations and the creation of the two systems.

Section 87.1

Indigenous Systems of Education before Boarding and Residential Schools

Any account of boarding and residential schools must begin with the education Indigenous children were already receiving. Indigenous nations did not lack educational institutions. They possessed systems of instruction that were embedded in family life, kinship obligations, ceremonial calendars, subsistence work, and political responsibility, and that were effective by their own standards: they sustained languages, economies, governments, and moral frameworks across many generations. Governments and missionaries in the nineteenth century frequently overlooked these systems because they did not resemble European classrooms. That oversight was partly misunderstanding and partly convenience, since it allowed policy makers to describe their institutions as bringing education to peoples who supposedly had none.

Instruction in most Indigenous communities was distributed across many people. Parents and grandparents taught daily skills and ethical expectations, and aunts, uncles, and clan relatives often carried defined responsibilities for particular kinds of teaching. Elders transmitted history, genealogy, and law through narrative, song, and ceremony. Children learned by watching, assisting, and gradually assuming tasks: planting and harvesting, preparing hides and food, building and repairing shelters, handling boats or horses, tracking animals, gathering plants, and caring for younger siblings. Apprenticeship to specialists, such as healers, ceremonial leaders, navigators, weavers, or craft workers, was common. Knowledge of the land was acquired in place, through seasonal movement and repeated experience, and was inseparable from language, since place names, kinship terms, and ceremonial vocabulary carried knowledge that translation could not easily preserve.

These systems differed widely among nations, and a single description would misrepresent them. A Diné child learned within a matrilineal clan system and a pastoral and agricultural economy in which herding, weaving, and ceremonial teachings were shared among kin. A Haudenosaunee child grew up in a society of clans and longhouses in which clan mothers held significant political responsibilities and in which the principles of the Great Law and the meaning of wampum belts were recited and interpreted by trained speakers. An Inuit child learned hunting, travel, and survival skills through close observation and participation, and was integrated into a kinship network that included the naming of children after relatives. A Kwakwaka'wakw child inherited names, rights, and responsibilities tied to a house and its crests and learned the songs, dances, and protocols of the potlatch and winter ceremonial. Each system conveyed technical competence, social obligation, and a sense of belonging to a particular people and place.

Indigenous education was also responsive to change. Many nations eagerly adopted new forms of literacy, new crops, new technologies, and, in some cases, formal schools. The Cherokee developed a syllabary in the 1820s that led to widespread literacy in their own language, a development treated in Chapter 64. Indigenous leaders who negotiated treaties often asked for teachers and schoolhouses, understanding that literacy in English or French could help protect land and rights. The historical question is therefore not whether Indigenous people accepted schooling. It is who controlled it, what it was meant to accomplish, and whether children's languages, families, and communities would remain part of their education.

For that reason the removal of children from their families and communities changed more than where lessons took place. It interrupted the transmission of language at its source, since children who spent most of the year in institutions where their language was discouraged could not learn it fully from relatives. It separated children from the elders and relatives who carried specific teaching responsibilities, and from ceremonial and economic cycles in which children had defined roles. It also shifted authority over children from families and nations to outside governments and churches. This is why the boarding and residential schools are treated here as part of a transformation of Indigenous societies, and not merely as an educational reform that proved harsh in practice.

Historical Vocabulary
Indigenous Education, Cultural Transmission, Assimilation, and Residential School

Indigenous education refers to the many systems by which Indigenous communities taught children knowledge, skills, values, language, and responsibilities. It was usually integrated into daily life and administered by kin and community, and it varied among nations. Cultural transmission describes the passing of language, knowledge, ceremony, and social practice across generations, which in Indigenous societies depended heavily on family and community relationships.

Assimilation, as used by nineteenth- and twentieth-century policy makers, meant the absorption of Indigenous people into the dominant society by replacing distinct languages, religions, political loyalties, and social practices. It was a stated goal of government policy and is a historical term of art here, not a description of voluntary cultural exchange. A residential school is the Canadian term for a school at which students lived for much of the year, operated under federal authority and usually by a church; the American analogue is the boarding school, though the legal arrangements differed, as §87.7 explains.

Potential misunderstanding: Describing these institutions as bringing "education" to Indigenous communities implies that education was absent. The accurate description is that they replaced community-controlled education with institutions controlled by others.

Section 87.2

Missionary Schools, the Civilization Fund Act of 1819, and Early American Indian Education

European colonial schooling of Indigenous children predates the United States. Catholic missionaries in New Spain and New France, and Protestant clergy in the English colonies, established schools as part of conversion efforts. In the eighteenth century Eleazar Wheelock's Moor's Charity School trained a small number of Indigenous students in Connecticut and later led to the founding of Dartmouth College, while in the early nineteenth century missionary societies opened schools among the Cherokee, Choctaw, and other southeastern nations. These early efforts were usually small and depended on Indigenous consent or tolerance. Many Indigenous leaders valued literacy, and some nations funded schools themselves, as the Choctaw did when they supported an academy for their students in Kentucky in the 1820s.

The federal government entered the field more systematically through the Civilization Fund Act, which Congress passed in 1819. The act authorized the President to employ suitable persons to instruct Indigenous communities in agriculture and to teach children reading, writing, and arithmetic, and it provided an annual appropriation of ten thousand dollars. The sum was small, but the principle was significant. The federal government acknowledged education as a lever of policy and created a mechanism for supporting it, while leaving the actual operation of most schools to missionary organizations that applied for and received a share of the money. The act reflected the premise, widely shared among officials, that Indigenous peoples would survive within the republic only by adopting settled agriculture and Christian practice.

The arrangement tied schooling to the evolving administrative apparatus. The Office of Indian Affairs, created within the War Department in 1824 and transferred to the Department of the Interior in 1849, handled the applications, accounts, and reports of mission schools. Missionary societies, including those affiliated with Protestant denominations and later Catholic orders, contributed their own funds and personnel. The result was a partnership in which religious organizations delivered instruction and the government supplied money and legitimacy. Treaties added further obligations. From the early nineteenth century, many treaties contained provisions for teachers, schoolhouses, or education funds, sometimes at Indigenous request and sometimes as part of the terms imposed by negotiators. The Fort Laramie treaty of 1868, for example, contained an education article that pledged the Lakota and allied signatories to send children between certain ages to school and committed the United States to provide schoolhouses and teachers. Such clauses created a treaty basis that later officials invoked in support of attendance enforcement.

Federal policy shifted again after the Civil War. Under the so-called Peace Policy of the late 1860s and 1870s, the administration assigned many agencies to religious denominations, giving churches responsibility for agency personnel and schools. Congress began appropriating modest sums directly for Indian education in 1870 and increased them substantially over the following decades. By the 1880s the number of federally supported institutions was growing rapidly, and the federal government, not missionary societies alone, became the dominant sponsor of Indian schooling. The extent of this expansion is one of the reasons the U.S. Department of the Interior, in its twenty-first century investigation, used the 1819 act as the starting point for its research period.

Students should distinguish the early schools from the later system. Early mission and day schools were typically small, located in or near communities, and often reached children who lived with their families. They frequently taught reading and religion alongside agriculture and domestic skills, and their influence was limited by their size and by Indigenous choices. The large off-reservation boarding school, which separated children from their communities for years at a time and was designed to produce a cultural break, was a later development associated with the 1870s and 1880s. The Civilization Fund Act authorized something less than that, but it established the administrative and financial foundations on which that later system was built.

Primary Source Window 1

The Civilization Fund Act of 1819 — Federal Education and the Language of Civilization

Creator: United States Congress · Date: March 3, 1819 · Type: Federal statute (appropriation and authorization)

Read the document: Original 1819 statute. The study below is editorial analysis unless quoted fragments are explicitly identified.

Context: Congress passed the act during the administration of President James Monroe, at a time when the government was pursuing a policy of encouraging agriculture and Christian instruction among Indigenous nations while also pressing for land cessions. The act authorized the President to employ people to teach agriculture and to teach children elementary subjects, and it set aside an annual sum of ten thousand dollars. Its reference to introducing instruction “with their own consent” must be read as a condition in the 1819 statute, not as proof that later boarding-school placements were consensual.

The statute stated a purpose “for introducing them among the habits and arts of civilization” and conditioned the proposed instruction on whether it could be introduced “with their own consent.” These are short verified statutory fragments, not an uninterrupted quotation.

Analysis: The phrase reveals the policy assumption that the education being funded was not neutral instruction. The word "civilization" in this usage referred to a particular set of practices and beliefs that officials associated with settled farming, private property, and Christianity. The act therefore shows that federal education policy was framed from the beginning as a means of cultural change. It also shows the administrative method: the government funded and authorized, while missionary organizations and other agents carried out the work.

Limitations: The statute is a short legislative text and says little about how schools actually operated, who attended, or how Indigenous communities responded. It does not indicate that the later boarding school system was foreseen, and it did not authorize compulsory attendance or removal of children from their communities. Historians must turn to annual reports of the Indian Office, missionary correspondence, treaty records, and Indigenous sources to see how the policy functioned in practice. The text also represents only the viewpoint of the federal legislature; no Indigenous nation participated in its drafting.

Perspective: The source represents the policy assumptions of federal lawmakers. Missing are the perspectives of Indigenous nations, many of which already had their own educational systems and some of which sought new schools on their own terms.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the statute authorize, and what does it not authorize?
  2. Who created it, and for what audience and purpose?
  3. What does the phrase "habits and arts of civilization" suggest about the aims of federal education policy?
  4. What can historians learn from the act about how the federal government and missionary organizations shared responsibility?
  5. What can historians not establish from the text alone, and what additional sources would help?
Section 87.3

Richard Henry Pratt, Fort Marion, Hampton, and the Establishment of Carlisle in 1879

The most influential model for American assimilationist boarding schools emerged from the career of Richard Henry Pratt. Born in 1840, Pratt served in the Union Army during the Civil War and afterward in the regular army on the Southern Plains, where he commanded Indigenous scouts and came to hold firm views about the capacity of Indigenous people and the failure, as he saw it, of the reservation system. Those views were tested in 1875, when the army sent Indigenous men and a few women, taken as prisoners after the Red River War of the Southern Plains, to Fort Marion, an old Spanish fort at St. Augustine, Florida. The prisoners included Kiowa, Comanche, Cheyenne, Arapaho, and Caddo people, and Pratt was assigned to guard them.

At Fort Marion, Pratt developed practices that he later carried into schools. He introduced military routine and drill, replaced prisoners' clothing with army-style uniforms, had their hair cut, and arranged English lessons with the help of local volunteers. He also placed prisoners in paid work for St. Augustine residents and relaxed some guard arrangements, giving the prisoners responsibilities within the structure he imposed. The prisoners, for their part, were not passive. Many produced drawings in a ledger-book style that survive as important historical records of their own perspectives, and individuals made their own choices about how to respond to the conditions of captivity. Historians continue to debate how much the Fort Marion program reflected genuine Indigenous accommodation and how much it reflected the constraints of imprisonment.

When the prisoners were released in 1878, some chose to continue schooling at the Hampton Normal and Agricultural Institute in Virginia. Founded in 1868 by Samuel Chapman Armstrong, Hampton had been established to educate formerly enslaved people and combined academic instruction with manual labor in a model that emphasized industrial training and discipline. Hampton began enrolling Indigenous students in 1878, and Pratt observed the program closely. The combination of vocational labor, moral instruction, and a regimented routine shaped his thinking, though the differences matter: Hampton's Black students were part of a population seeking education after emancipation, whereas Indigenous students were members of nations with their own political status and, in many cases, their own treaties with the United States.

Pratt persuaded the War Department to let him use the abandoned Carlisle Barracks in Pennsylvania for a school, and the Carlisle Indian Industrial School opened in October 1879. The first students came largely from Lakota communities at the Rosebud and Pine Ridge agencies, and recruitment among other nations followed. Carlisle's early program reflected Pratt's military background. Students lived in barracks, wore uniforms, followed bugle calls and drills, received English-language instruction, and divided their days between classroom work and industrial training in trades such as carpentry, blacksmithing, tailoring, and shoemaking for boys, and sewing, laundry, and housekeeping for girls. Students were also given new names and were discouraged from using their own languages. The school became a showpiece. Visitors toured its facilities, and its photographs, newspapers, and annual reports presented a carefully managed image of transformation.

Carlisle was not an unopposed institution. Indigenous parents and leaders raised objections, and the experience of some leaders illustrates the tension between curiosity and suspicion. The Brulé Lakota leader Spotted Tail, who had sent children to Carlisle, visited in 1880 and withdrew some of them after disagreeing with the school's practices. Carlisle also experienced the effects of disease and homesickness, matters discussed in §87.14, and some students ran away. The school operated until 1918, when the army reclaimed the site, and Pratt himself had left in 1904 after disputes with the Indian Office. Its legacy, however, was established: it demonstrated a model that the federal government replicated at other off-reservation schools in the 1880s and 1890s.

In 1892 Pratt delivered an address to a national conference of charities and corrections that stated his philosophy plainly. His most quoted sentence, "Kill the Indian in him, and save the man," has become a shorthand for assimilationist education. Historians emphasize that the sentence must be read within Pratt's argument, which combined a belief in the equal capacity of Indigenous individuals with an insistence that Indigenous cultural identity should disappear. He opposed the view that Indigenous people were inherently inferior, but he also treated their languages, religions, and tribal relationships as obstacles to be removed. The belief in individual capacity did not make the policy less coercive. It gave it a particular rationale: that sending children away would produce citizens integrated into the dominant society.

Section 87.4

The Canadian Origins: Mission Schools, the Davin Report of 1879, and the Expansion of Residential Education

Canadian residential schooling developed from its own institutional history. In the seventeenth century, Catholic religious communities in New France, including Récollets and Ursulines, had attempted to educate Indigenous children, with limited and uneven success. In the nineteenth century, Protestant and Catholic missionary organizations established new institutions in the colonies that became Upper and Lower Canada. The enterprise later called the Mohawk Institute began in 1828 near Brantford in what is now Ontario, as a Mechanics’ Institute and day school associated with the Anglican New England Company and Six Nations of the Grand River. By 1834 it included boarding accommodations for boys and girls. Some later historical chronologies also refer to an intermediate development around 1831; the key distinction is between its early day-school origins and its later residential role.

Colonial officials began advocating an industrial and boarding model in the 1840s. A government commission in the early 1840s criticized existing schools and recommended manual labour institutions, and Egerton Ryerson, an influential Methodist educator, prepared a report in 1847 that urged industrial schools under religious management with substantial government support. These proposals drew on the assumption that Indigenous children should be trained in agriculture and trades and instructed in Christianity, and that the schools should be operated by churches with financial support from the government. After Confederation in 1867, and especially after the Indian Act of 1876, the federal government assumed responsibility for Indian education. Treaties negotiated in the western Prairies from 1871 included provisions for schools on reserves, which Indigenous negotiators often requested and which the government later interpreted broadly.

The decisive moment came in 1879. Prime Minister John A. Macdonald's government commissioned Nicholas Flood Davin, a journalist and former lawyer who had become a political supporter of the Conservatives, to examine American industrial schools for Indigenous children. Davin traveled to the United States, consulted officials, and visited institutions, and he submitted a report that recommended a system of industrial schools. His report concluded that day schools on reserves were ineffective at changing children, because the children returned each evening to the influence of their families. He recommended that Indigenous children be educated away from their home communities, in boarding institutions where they could be separated from parental influence and trained in trades and agriculture. He also recommended that the schools be operated by churches with government support, reflecting both his admiration for American approaches and his view that religious bodies could provide teachers at low cost.

The Canadian government accepted these recommendations and authorized industrial schools in the North-West in 1883. The first schools, at Battleford, Qu'Appelle, and High River, opened in 1883 and 1884 under church administration with federal funding. Over the following decades, the network grew to include dozens of institutions in Ontario, the Prairies, British Columbia, and the north. An 1892 Order in Council converted the federally supported industrial schools—including Qu’Appelle, Battleford, and High River—from direct operating support to per-capita grants, effective in July 1893. Boarding schools had already been funded through separate per-student arrangements, with different rates. The change intensified enrollment-based financial pressures in the industrial-school category; its effects on conditions are discussed in §87.13. The government also amended the Indian Act to increase its authority over school attendance, culminating in the compulsory-attendance provisions of 1920 examined in §87.5.

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission's historical research documents the connections among Davin's recommendations, American precedents, and the Canadian expansion. It is important, however, not to reduce Canadian residential schooling to an American import. Canada had earlier institutions, its own treaty obligations, its own church-state partnerships, and a legal framework in the Indian Act that differed from American law. The American example served as a model and a justification, but the Canadian system developed through decisions of Canadian officials, churches, and Parliament, and it came to differ from the American system in important ways, including its extensive reliance on church administration.

Separating these funding categories also changes how administrative responsibility should be assessed. Prior to July 1893, the three western industrial schools received unusually direct support; the 1892 order shifted more operating risk toward their church managers while setting a maximum number of funded pupils. The federal department still determined eligibility for grants and exercised policy authority. The change neither removed government responsibility nor placed all Canadian schools on a newly invented national formula. To measure its effects, historians must compare particular school contracts, enrollment returns, maintenance estimates, and inspection reports over time.

Section 87.5

Compulsory Attendance, Recruitment, Transportation, and the Removal of Children

Governments that built boarding and residential schools had to find students. In the early years, recruitment depended on persuasion, material inducement, and the willingness of some families to send children, often because they valued literacy, because food and clothing were scarce, or because they hoped to give children skills for an uncertain future. As the networks expanded and as many families declined to enroll children, both governments turned to compulsion. The two countries used different legal instruments, and a careful account must specify what each law actually authorized.

In the United States, Congress addressed attendance through appropriations legislation and delegated authority to the executive branch. A law of 1891 authorized the Commissioner of Indian Affairs to make and enforce rules for the compulsory attendance of Indigenous children at schools. A provision of 1893 went further by authorizing the Indian Office to withhold rations, clothing, or other annuities from parents or guardians who refused or neglected to send their children to school. Because many families depended on treaty-guaranteed supplies that the government itself had promised in exchange for land, withholding them gave Indian agents an economic lever over families who had little other source of support. Agents also relied on Indian police, on persuasion by missionaries and teachers, and in some cases on the dispatch of school personnel to collect children.

These practices provoked resistance that sometimes reached the national press and Congress. In 1894, Hopi leaders at Orayvi and elsewhere resisted the removal of their children to schools, and the dispute became entangled with disagreements over allotment and agency authority. The federal government responded with military force, and in early 1895 it imprisoned a group of Hopi men, nineteen by most accounts, at Alcatraz. The confrontation is important not only as a case of coercion but as evidence that Indigenous communities understood what removal threatened and organized to prevent it. Within the same period Congress reconsidered some of its own approaches. An 1894 appropriations provision prohibited the withholding of rations as a means of compelling attendance and required the consent of parents before children were enrolled in off-reservation schools. Such provisions did not end coercion, because enforcement was uneven and local officials had wide discretion, but they show that American law changed over time and that recruitment practices varied by period.

In Canada, the federal government expanded its powers through successive amendments to the Indian Act. By the 1890s, regulations and amendments allowed officials to commit children to schools under certain conditions, and the Department of Indian Affairs relied on Indian agents, church personnel, and the police to secure enrollment. The most significant statutory expansion came in 1920, when an amendment, associated with Deputy Superintendent General Duncan Campbell Scott, made school attendance compulsory for Indigenous children within a specified age range, generally given as seven to fifteen. The amendment authorized the government to compel attendance and to determine the kind of school a child would attend. It also provided penalties for parents who resisted, and it gave truant officers, agents, and others the authority to enforce the law.

A critical legal distinction must be kept in view. Compulsory school attendance did not always mean compulsory attendance at a distant residential institution. The 1920 Canadian amendment created an obligation to attend school, but the assignment of a particular child to a day school, a boarding school, or a residential institution was an administrative decision. In practice, many First Nations communities lacked adequate day schools, and officials often placed children in residential institutions because no alternative existed or because they judged residence preferable. In other communities, children attended day schools and lived at home. The American laws likewise required attendance at some school without always specifying an off-reservation placement, and the 1894 legislation restricted removal without parental consent. The reality was therefore a spectrum of arrangements, and accurate history requires asking, for each period and place, which law applied, who decided placement, and what alternatives were available.

Transportation and distance shaped the experience of removal. Children were sent by wagon, rail, boat, and eventually automobile and aircraft, sometimes in groups accompanied by school staff. Rail networks allowed American off-reservation schools to recruit across wide areas, and Canadian schools in the West drew from reserves that could be hundreds of kilometres away. Distance limited visits, made correspondence a primary means of contact, and made it difficult for parents to monitor conditions. Governments sometimes covered travel at the outset but did not always pay for return trips, and in some documented cases children remained at schools through summers, a practice that further limited contact.

Section 87.6

Federal Departments, Churches, Contracts, and Financial Responsibility

Who actually controlled the schools? The answer differed between the two countries and changed over time, and it is central to understanding why accountability was so difficult to assign. In both systems, authority was divided among several layers: legislatures that appropriated funds, departments that set policy and inspected, local officials who supervised communities, and school administrators who managed daily operations. In Canada, churches held a formal and extensive role; in the United States, religious involvement was substantial but more varied and, after 1900, increasingly limited in federally funded institutions.

In the United States, the Office of Indian Affairs within the Department of the Interior held primary responsibility. The Commissioner of Indian Affairs issued regulations, and from the 1880s a Superintendent of Indian Schools supervised the growing system. Individual schools were headed by superintendents who reported to Washington, while Indian agents supervised reservations and handled recruitment and local disputes. Missionary organizations operated many schools, and from the 1870s the government made contracts with religious bodies to educate students at government expense. These "contract schools" became controversial, particularly because the Catholic Church received a large share of contract funds, and Congress reduced and by the end of the 1890s ended direct appropriations to sectarian contract schools. After that, government-operated boarding schools, staffed by civil service employees, became more prominent, though mission schools continued with private and treaty-fund support. Funding came from appropriations, treaty obligations, and sometimes Indigenous nations' own funds held in trust.

In Canada, the Department of Indian Affairs, headed by a Superintendent General who was a cabinet minister and administered by a Deputy Superintendent General, oversaw the schools. Local agents supervised reserves, and school inspectors reported on conditions. Churches, however, actually ran most schools. Roman Catholic institutions were often operated by orders such as the Oblates of Mary Immaculate and women's religious congregations. The Church of England, through its missionary bodies, administered Anglican schools. Methodist and Presbyterian schools were later joined, after the formation of the United Church of Canada in 1925, by the United Church, which took over most of their institutions. The government funded construction in many cases and paid operating grants, and under an 1892 order converted industrial schools to a per-capita grant scheme effective July 1893, while boarding schools already received different per-capita payments. The churches appointed principals and staff, subject to departmental approval, and managed daily life.

This division of labor made responsibilities ambiguous. Principals hired teachers and staff, but salaries were limited by grants that were often inadequate. The government set curricula and regulations, but local practice varied. Buildings were sometimes owned by churches and sometimes by the government, and maintenance responsibilities were disputed. Food, clothing, and heating were funded by per-capita payments that churches regularly argued were too low, while the department regularly argued that churches should make better use of their funds and farm labor. When inspectors identified deficiencies, remedies required negotiation among the department, the church headquarters, and the principal. The Bryce report discussed in §87.13 illustrates how documentation of problems could fail to produce reform when responsibility was shared.

American schools faced analogous difficulties, though the structure differed. Superintendents of government boarding schools were government employees, but funding shortfalls, political patronage, and distance from Washington often limited oversight. Congressional appropriations were set annually and rarely matched the needs of expanding institutions, and schools commonly compensated by relying on student labor for farm production and maintenance, a practice examined in §87.11. In both countries, the combination of limited funds, fragmented authority, and a population of children with little political power produced chronic problems of accountability.

Section 87.7

Boarding Schools, Industrial Schools, Day Schools, and Student Residences

The vocabulary of institutional schooling can mislead because the same words were used differently in different places and periods. A historian must therefore specify the kind of institution being discussed, since differences in organization had consequences for family separation, labor, transportation, instruction, and government oversight.

In the United States, several institutional forms coexisted. Day schools, usually small and located on or near reservations, allowed children to live at home. On-reservation boarding schools housed children at or near agency headquarters, so that students could in some cases visit relatives and families could visit them. Off-reservation industrial boarding schools, of which Carlisle was the model, recruited students from many reservations and housed them at a considerable distance for years at a time. Mission schools operated by religious organizations ranged from small day schools to substantial boarding institutions. Public schools also enrolled some Indigenous children, particularly after the 1930s, when federal policy encouraged contracting with states.

In Canada, the terminology shifted over time. In the early decades, the government distinguished industrial schools, which were larger, often located at a distance from reserves, and emphasized trades and farming, from boarding schools, which were typically smaller, located closer to reserves, and combined elementary instruction with agricultural and domestic labor. By the early 1920s departmental records increasingly used “residential school” as an overarching description, without evidence here of one formal 1923 merger order; Day schools, operated by the government or by churches on reserves, enrolled many First Nations children who lived at home. In the later twentieth century, student residences or hostels, particularly in the north, housed children who attended day schools or provincial and territorial schools during the week or the year. Many such residences, including those in the Arctic, were operated with church participation and are included in the later institutional accounting.

These distinctions matter for several reasons. First, family separation varied: a child at a day school lived at home, while a child at a distant industrial school might see parents only occasionally. Second, labor requirements differed, because schools with farms or workshops depended on student work more heavily than small day schools. Third, accountability differed: day schools were generally easier for parents to monitor, while distant residential institutions were harder to inspect and visit. Fourth, the definitions affect historical counts. Statements about the number of schools or students depend on which categories are included, and totals compiled under different definitions should not be treated as equivalent.

It is also essential to recognize that not every Indigenous child attended a residential or boarding institution. Government day schools, church schools, provincial or state public schools, and Indigenous educational initiatives were all part of the landscape of schooling. Many children never attended a boarding or residential institution, many attended for short periods, and others attended for most of their childhoods. Collapsing all of these experiences into a single category would obscure the historical record. It would also risk understating the importance of the specific institutions in which family separation and institutional control were most intense.

The changing labels also complicate historical counts. A school could move between day, boarding, industrial, and residential arrangements without changing its name or site; an institution might close its classroom operation while retaining a student residence. Later official lists often use definitions created for particular investigations or settlements rather than a uniform nineteenth-century census. Accordingly, comparisons of the two national systems should specify an institution’s function and years of operation, rather than infer a common history merely from its administrative label.

Historical Vocabulary
Industrial School, Boarding School, Day School, Outing System, and Per-Capita Grant

Industrial school referred to a school combining basic academic instruction with training in trades, farming, and domestic work. In Canada, the term was used for larger institutions as the categories were increasingly grouped under the broader description “residential schools” by the early 1920s. In the United States it described institutions such as Carlisle. A boarding school housed students for the school year; in the United States it could be on or off a reservation, while in Canada it often denoted a smaller institution closer to home communities. A day school enrolled students who lived with their families.

The outing system was a program, most associated with Carlisle, in which students were placed with families or employers outside the school for periods of work and supervised study, discussed in §87.11. A per-capita grant was a payment to a school based on the number of enrolled students, used at Canadian boarding schools before 1892 and extended through an 1892 order to industrial schools effective July 1893. It tied institutional income to enrollment.

Potential misunderstanding: These terms were not fixed categories. Institutions changed their names, functions, and funding arrangements, so historians must check how a particular source used each term.

Section 87.8

The Geography of Institutional Education: Reservations, Reserves, and Distant Schools

The locations of schools shaped their operation. In the United States, off-reservation schools were concentrated in the Midwest, the Plains, the Southwest, and the Pacific coast, often at sites chosen because of existing buildings, rail access, or political interest. Carlisle, in Pennsylvania, drew students from many nations across the country. Haskell, opened in 1884 at Lawrence, Kansas, and Chilocco, opened the same year in Indian Territory, in what became Oklahoma, served students from the Plains and Southwest. Schools such as Genoa in Nebraska, Flandreau in South Dakota, Pipestone in Minnesota, and Mount Pleasant in Michigan served the northern Plains and Great Lakes. In the Southwest, Phoenix Indian School opened in 1891, and on the Pacific coast, Chemawa in Oregon, which began in 1880 and moved to its present site near Salem in the mid-1880s, and Sherman Institute in Riverside, California, which opened in 1902, drew students from the Northwest, California, and the Southwest. Alaska had its own pattern, including mission schools and, from the 1930s, federal institutions such as the Wrangell Institute and, after the Second World War, Mount Edgecumbe near Sitka.

Reservation boarding schools outnumbered the famous off-reservation institutions. Numerous agency boarding schools operated in the West and Plains, often with enrollments of fewer than a few hundred, and these schools frequently kept children closer to home than Carlisle did, though they still limited daily family life. The Navajo Nation illustrates the effect of geography on schooling: for decades, the shortage of schools on a vast reservation meant that many Diné children attended no school, and in the mid-twentieth century the federal government expanded boarding and, later, day schools on and off the reservation in a major program. The varied geography meant that a Lakota child at a reservation school, a Diné child sent to a distant school, and a Native Hawaiian or Alaska Native student at a federally supported institution could have quite different experiences.

In Canada, the geography was shaped by the treaties and by the church missions. Ontario hosted early institutions such as the Mohawk Institute, Shingwauk at Sault Ste. Marie, and Mount Elgin. The Prairies contained the largest concentration of residential schools, extending across Manitoba, Saskatchewan, and Alberta, where the treaties of the 1870s and the industrial school program of the 1880s had been focused. British Columbia hosted schools including Kamloops, St. Mary's Mission, Kuper Island, Alberni, and Lejac, and its coastal geography meant that many children traveled by boat. In the north, the picture changed in the mid-twentieth century. The federal government and churches built schools and large student residences at Aklavik, Inuvik, Chesterfield Inlet, and elsewhere in the 1950s and after, drawing Inuit and northern First Nations children from communities that earlier had little contact with the residential system.

First Nations, Inuit, and Métis students require careful differentiation. First Nations children were the principal enrollees under the Indian Act. Inuit children were generally not covered by the Indian Act, and the federal government assumed greater responsibility for Inuit education only in the 1950s, when it built day schools and hostels in the Arctic as part of a rapid expansion of federal presence. For Inuit, residential education therefore came later than in the south, was often compressed into a few decades, and occurred in the context of relocation and settlement policy discussed in Chapter 100. Métis children were not generally eligible for federally funded places because Métis people were outside the Indian Act, although some attended residential schools, and provincial and church policies affected their access. The historical record on Métis attendance is therefore uneven and must be handled carefully.

Distance, climate, and transportation had practical effects. A school connected by rail to many communities could draw students widely, while an isolated school in the north might be reachable only by boat or aircraft for part of the year. Severe winters raised heating costs and constrained movement, and sparsely populated regions made inspection difficult. Isolation also limited contact with parents and outside observers, a point that connects geography to the problems of oversight analyzed in §87.15.

Photographs of school buildings and classrooms are important sources, but they require critical reading. Official photographs, including those taken at Carlisle, were often staged for visitors, reports, and fundraising, and some, such as paired images of students on arrival and after months at school, were designed to illustrate transformation. They are valuable evidence of how institutions wished to be seen, of architecture, uniforms, and classroom arrangements, and of the visual rhetoric of assimilation. They are not evidence that students consented to enrollment, were well treated, or shared the views the images suggest.

Division III

How assimilation was organized within schools.

Section 87.9

Language Suppression, Renaming, Uniforms, and the Curriculum of Assimilation

Boarding and residential schools did not merely happen to change children. Their founders and administrators intended cultural transformation, and their records state this intention in plain terms. Language policy, naming, dress, grooming, and drill were components of a curriculum designed to replace Indigenous identities with identities defined by the state and the churches. Explaining this as an intentional objective, not an incidental effect, is central to the chapter's argument. At the same time, the documentary record shows that practice varied from school to school, and claims about particular methods must be tied to evidence about particular institutions.

Language was the most direct target. In the United States, federal officials repeatedly emphasized English instruction. In 1887, Commissioner of Indian Affairs John D. C. Atkins issued an order that instruction in schools receiving federal support be conducted in English, and the Indian Office's rules and reports of the period described Indigenous languages as obstacles to civilization. In Canada, the language of instruction was English or, in some Catholic and Quebec institutions, French, and departmental regulations and inspectors' reports emphasized the importance of the official languages. The discouragement of Indigenous languages took forms ranging from instruction that simply ignored them, to rules against speaking them, to documented punishment of students for using them at certain schools. The strictness of enforcement varied by period, staff, and institution.

The picture is not uniform in every respect. Some missionaries, particularly in the nineteenth century, used Indigenous languages for religious instruction because it was an effective means of conversion. Syllabic writing systems for Cree and other languages were developed by missionaries and adopted by Indigenous communities, and Anglican missionaries in the Arctic used syllabics in Inuktitut. Policy shifted over time toward English or French, but individual schools and staff differed. Historians therefore distinguish between the stated policy of the governments, the more varied practices of missionaries, and the effects on children, which are examined further in Chapter 88.

Names and appearance were also regulated. Many schools assigned new English names to students or recorded them under altered names, and in some Canadian institutions administrative numbers appeared alongside or in place of names in records. Hair cutting is documented at Carlisle and many other schools, and the practice carried particular significance for nations in which hair held religious or social meaning. Students were issued uniforms or standardized clothing, and many schools, especially those modeled on Carlisle, used military drill and cadet organizations to teach discipline and posture. Standards of cleanliness, deportment, and appearance were prescribed by regulations and enforced by staff. These practices communicated a message that Indigenous markers of identity were to be replaced by markers the institutions defined as civilized.

The curriculum extended beyond language and appearance. Academic instruction typically covered reading, writing, arithmetic, geography, and history from a Euro-American or Canadian perspective, often emphasizing national symbols, patriotic observances, and citizenship ideals in the United States, and loyalty to the Crown and Christian morality in Canada. Instruction in agriculture, domestic science, and trades was explicitly designed to prepare students for particular roles in the settler economy. In the United States, the Indian Office issued a Uniform Course of Study for Indian schools in 1901, under Superintendent of Indian Schools Estelle Reel, which emphasized industrial and agricultural training. Academic achievement was often limited by the half-day system and by teachers' low expectations, and many students were taught only to the elementary level.

Taken together, language rules, renaming, dress codes, and curriculum content formed a coherent program. The objective was not simply to teach English or arithmetic. It was to replace the languages, values, and social practices that connected children to their families and nations. Historians who emphasize this point rely on the stated aims in reports and regulations, on the design of the institutions, and on the consistency of the policies across schools. They also caution that the effect on individual children varied, and that students and families found ways to preserve language and identity, a subject for Chapter 88.

Section 87.10

Religious Instruction, Discipline, Rules, and Institutional Authority

Religious instruction was integral to the curriculum in most schools. In Canadian residential schools, churches operated the institutions, and daily prayers, Sunday services, catechism classes, and religious holidays structured the school year. In the United States, mission schools had overt religious purposes, and many government boarding schools, even after the end of contract funding for sectarian schools, included Christian worship, Sunday school, and moral instruction as part of the routine. Officials and missionaries commonly saw conversion as part of civilization, and Indigenous ceremonies were discouraged or prohibited in both countries through laws and regulations that extended beyond schools. These include the Canadian Indian Act's restrictions on certain ceremonies and the American Code of Indian Offenses, discussed in Chapters 85 and 84.

The religious institutions were not uniform. Catholic, Anglican, Methodist, Presbyterian, and later United Church schools differed in doctrine, personnel, resources, and attitudes toward Indigenous languages and cultures. Orders of priests, brothers, and nuns staffed many Catholic schools, while Protestant schools relied on missionaries, lay teachers, and married couples. The relationship between churches and governments also changed over time. Churches were more dominant in the nineteenth century, when government oversight was limited, and the federal role increased in the twentieth century, culminating in the transfer of operations discussed in §87.18. Historians therefore avoid generalizations about "the churches" as a single actor.

Authority within the schools was hierarchical. A principal, or in American government schools a superintendent, exercised overall control and reported to a church or government superior. Teachers, matrons, disciplinarians, farm instructors, and other staff supervised academic work, dormitories, labor, and conduct. Many staff were poorly paid and untrained, and turnover was high. Older students were sometimes assigned supervisory roles over younger ones, creating internal hierarchies. Staff had wide discretion over children's daily lives, and children had few formal avenues of complaint.

Discipline was administered through rules governing nearly every aspect of daily life. Schedules, bells, and drill regulated movement, and staff monitored dormitories, dining halls, and workplaces. Rules prohibited unauthorized absence, speaking Indigenous languages in some schools, and various forms of perceived misbehavior. Punishments documented in records and correspondence included loss of privileges, extra work, confinement, restricted food, and corporal punishment such as strapping or whipping. Running away was a frequent disciplinary issue, and schools sought to retrieve runaways through staff, police, and agents, and sometimes punished those who returned. Correspondence was often supervised and could be read, censored, or restricted, limiting the ability of children to report problems and of parents to learn of them.

Corporal punishment was not unique to Indigenous schools, since it was widespread in schools generally in this period. Historians nevertheless distinguish the Indigenous institutions on several grounds. The children were separated from their families, which removed a protective check that most children in ordinary schools retained. The institutions operated with explicit cultural aims, and punishments were sometimes tied to enforcing those aims, as in punishment for speaking Indigenous languages. Oversight was weak, as §87.15 explains. The fuller account of what children experienced, including the testimony of survivors, belongs to Chapter 88. This section is concerned with the institutional structure of authority and discipline that made such experiences possible.

Section 87.11

Industrial Training, Student Labor, and the Carlisle Outing System

Work was central to the design of boarding and residential schools. The most common organizational model was the half-day system, in which students spent half the day in classrooms and half in labor. In both countries, the labor typically included farming, gardening, tending livestock, laundry, cleaning, food preparation, carpentry, blacksmithing, shoemaking, tailoring, sewing, printing, and the maintenance of buildings. Officials described this work as industrial training, intended to prepare students for self-support in a settler economy, and as moral training, inculcating habits of discipline and thrift.

The work was gendered. Boys were typically assigned to farm, garden, shop, and maintenance tasks, and girls to laundry, kitchen, sewing, and housekeeping. These assignments reflected the gender expectations of the dominant society, and they often differed from the economic roles that men and women held in the students' own communities, in which, for example, women were often responsible for agriculture in many nations. The training prepared girls for domestic service and boys for farm labor and trades, positioning graduates in the lower ranks of the settler economy.

Historians debate how to characterize this labor, and the question has analytical value: was it education, institutional maintenance, or economic exploitation? The evidence supports a combination. Some instruction was real, and some students acquired skills that they later used. At the same time, schools relied on student labor to produce food, clean and maintain buildings, make clothing, and run laundries, and records show that financial pressures encouraged administrators to rely on this labor to compensate for limited appropriations. In Canada, the per-capita funding arrangement made child labor a way of sustaining institutions on grants that churches described as inadequate. In both countries, the amount of labor could reduce instructional time, and inspectors repeatedly noted that academic progress was limited. Whether any particular school's labor program was predominantly educational or predominantly extractive depends on the evidence for that school.

The Carlisle outing system illustrates the model most vividly. Developed at Carlisle from 1880, building on Hampton's practices, the outing system placed students with families, farms, and businesses, primarily in Pennsylvania and New Jersey, for periods that could last from summers to a year or longer. Pratt presented the program as a means of teaching English, work habits, and the ways of the dominant society through immersion, and he argued that it demonstrated that students could succeed outside the reservation. Students lived in private homes, worked as farm laborers and domestic servants, and in some cases attended local public schools during the winter months.

The system was administered through records. The school kept registers of placements, collected reports from patrons, and communicated with students. Employers, called patrons, agreed to provide room and board, pay wages, and ensure a degree of supervision and religious observance. Wages were generally controlled by the school, with portions credited to student accounts and sometimes used for clothing and other expenses, and students' access to their money was managed by school officials. The school sought reports on student conduct and sometimes dispatched staff to visit placements. The program was not uniformly positive. Records show that some students were placed in situations of hard labor, that some experienced mistreatment, that some ran away from placements, and that the quality of supervision varied. Some students also described valuing the independence and income it provided. Historians use the outing registers and correspondence to study patterns of labor, wages, and supervision, but they must read these sources critically, since they were created by the school for its own purposes.

Section 87.12

The Institutional Day: Timetables, Meals, Dormitories, Lessons, and Correspondence

The daily structure of boarding and residential schools can be reconstructed from regulations, printed courses of study, annual reports, inspection reports, and surviving timetables. These sources show a day organized around bells, assemblies, and supervised movement among dormitory, dining hall, classroom, chapel, and workplace. Historians use them with care, because a timetable describes what administrators intended, while inspection reports and correspondence sometimes reveal how the schedule actually worked, or failed to work, in practice.

Where records survive, the typical American boarding school day began with a rising signal, followed by dormitory inspection, morning assembly, and breakfast. The day was divided between academic classes and industrial or domestic work, with meals, religious services, drill, and supervised recreation interspersed, and evenings devoted to study and further supervision. Carlisle's regimented routines, which included military drill and bugle calls, were widely publicized, and many other schools adopted versions of this pattern. The Canadian residential school day, as documented in departmental regulations and the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's historical research, followed a comparable structure: early rising, prayers, chores, meals, classroom instruction in the morning or afternoon, labor in the remaining half-day, evening prayers, and bedtime. The half-day system was formalized in Canadian regulations and remained common until the mid-twentieth century.

Dormitories were typically large rooms with rows of beds, supervised by matrons or other staff, and the separation of boys and girls, including siblings, was usual. Meals were eaten in common dining halls, and food was prepared partly by students. Inspection reports repeatedly document concerns about the quantity and quality of food, which §87.13 examines. Classrooms often held large groups of children of differing ages, and teachers' qualifications varied widely. Recreation, when provided, was supervised and could include organized sports, which in some American schools, notably Carlisle, became a prominent feature of the institution's public identity.

Correspondence between students and families is among the most revealing and most problematic records. Letters were a primary link between children and parents, but schools often required that letters be written in English, supervised the writing, and could read outgoing and incoming mail. Parents who could not read English depended on others to read letters to them, and letters from parents sometimes did not reach students. Some collections preserve letters from parents to school authorities asking about children's health or requesting their return, which show parental concern and are discussed in §87.16. Because correspondence was subject to supervision, historians treat its silence on problems cautiously.

The gap between schedule and practice is evident in inspection reports. Inspectors and medical officers noted that classes could be shortened by labor demands, that staff absences and turnover disrupted instruction, that overcrowded dormitories departed from regulations, and that the supervision envisioned in rules was not always carried out. For this reason, historians prefer to combine prescriptive documents, such as regulations and timetables, with evaluative documents, such as inspection reports, and with Indigenous testimony. The routines also had a broader significance. By regulating time and movement and by isolating children from the rhythms of family and community life, schools displaced the seasonal and kin-centered learning described in §87.1 with an externally imposed order.

Two dated records make the distinction between prescribed routine and inspected conditions concrete. Canada’s Annual Report of the Department of Indian Affairs for 1893, pages 173–174, printed a Qu’Appelle Industrial School schedule that began at 5:30 a.m., placed chapel at 6:00, academic lessons between 9:00 and noon, additional school and trade work in the afternoon, and evening prayer at 8:00. In the United States, Charles H. Thompson’s March 2, 1892 inspection report of Carlisle documented teaching, food, student health, industrial work, outings, and school conditions. These are different documentary genres: the Canadian timetable describes intended hours; the American inspection assesses observed or reported institutional operations. Neither record alone proves that every pupil followed the printed routine on every day.

Section 87.13

Funding Shortfalls, Overcrowding, Sanitation, and Peter Henderson Bryce's 1907 Investigation

The conditions in which children lived cannot be separated from how schools were financed. Both governments sought to operate institutions at low cost, and funding formulas, appropriations, and grants rarely matched the expenses of feeding, clothing, housing, heating, and staffing large numbers of children. Administrators responded by seeking to maximize enrollment, by relying on student labor, and by economizing on food, fuel, repairs, and salaries. The result, repeatedly documented in inspection reports, was chronic overcrowding, inadequate ventilation, limited sanitation, and uneven medical care.

In Canada, the 1892 order changed industrial-school funding to per-capita grants from July 1893. Smaller boarding schools already received per-pupil payments under a separate scheme; the two categories should not be treated as if they entered one uniform arrangement in 1892. This created an incentive to enroll and retain as many children as possible, including children who were unwell, and it left schools financially exposed when enrollment fell. Churches argued that grants were too low to cover real costs, and the department resisted increases. Buildings were often constructed cheaply and maintained poorly, with insufficient ventilation, a condition that mattered greatly in an era when tuberculosis spread through crowded indoor spaces. In the United States, annual appropriations were similarly limited, and the Meriam Report of 1928, discussed in §87.17, found that the diet and living conditions in many boarding schools were inadequate and that institutions depended on student labor to compensate.

Canadian medical officer Peter Henderson Bryce provides the best-documented example of an official investigation. Bryce, a physician who served as chief medical officer for the Department of the Interior and the Department of Indian Affairs, was assigned to inspect health conditions at Indian schools in Manitoba and the North-West Territories. His 1907 report documented serious deficiencies in sanitation, ventilation, and the health of students, and it raised particular concern about tuberculosis and mortality among children in the schools he examined. He attributed the problem substantially to the conditions of the buildings and the admission and retention of children who were already ill, and he recommended measures including improved construction and ventilation, medical examination of children before admission, and closer medical supervision.

The report is important as evidence that senior officials had been informed of the problem. It is also an example of how fragmented responsibility could limit reform. The Indian Department, the churches, the principals, and public health officials each held a part of the responsibility, and the recommendations required funding and cooperation that were not forthcoming. Historians have documented that the department did not implement most of Bryce's recommendations on the scale he proposed, and that the report received limited public circulation at the time. In 1922, after leaving the civil service, Bryce published a pamphlet criticizing the government's response, and that later publication and the 1907 report are distinct documents that should not be conflated. Whether the department's inaction reflected indifference, financial calculation, jurisdictional confusion, or a mixture is a matter of historical interpretation, though the continued operation of unsound schools after 1907 is documented.

The responsibilities of the various parties can be summarized as follows. The Indian Department set standards, paid grants, and inspected, and it had authority over building and funding decisions. Churches appointed principals and managed daily operations, and sought funds from the department. Principals controlled admissions and day-to-day conditions. Public-health officials such as Bryce could inspect and recommend but lacked power to compel changes. The system distributed authority so widely that each party could attribute shortcomings to another, and the children had no effective advocate within the structure.

Section 87.14

Infectious Disease, Student Deaths, and the Problems of Historical Recordkeeping

Infectious disease was a major cause of death in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and crowded institutions were especially vulnerable. Tuberculosis was the most frequently documented serious illness in the schools, and influenza, measles, and other contagious diseases spread through dormitories and classrooms. Contributing conditions, where records support them, included overcrowding, poor ventilation, limited nutrition, inadequate medical care, and the admission of ill children. Epidemics such as the 1918 influenza pandemic affected communities and institutions broadly, and historians must distinguish deaths attributable to conditions specific to the schools from the wider mortality of the period. They also note that Indigenous communities, because of poverty, confinement, and limited healthcare, often experienced disease more severely than other populations, so that school conditions and community conditions interacted.

Recordkeeping presents significant difficulties. Governments and schools recorded enrollments, illnesses, deaths, and burials in registers, annual reports, correspondence, and death certificates, but the records are uneven. Some schools kept careful registers and others did not. Cause of death was sometimes recorded vaguely or not at all. Burial locations were not always documented, and cemeteries were sometimes unmarked, relocated, or built over. Students who became seriously ill were in some documented cases sent home, so deaths may have been recorded in communities and not in schools. Deaths of children who were away from the school on outing placements or in hospitals may appear in other records. Transfers between schools and departures without notice further complicate the accounting. For all these reasons, any total of student deaths is a minimum count based on surviving documents and not a complete measure.

The methodological limits are illustrated by recent investigations. The U.S. Department of the Interior's Federal Indian Boarding School Initiative, which published its findings in 2022 and 2024, defined a federal Indian boarding school system for the years 1819 to 1969 and identified 417 federal schools within that framework. It identified at least 973 documented student deaths within that defined system and explicitly recognized that the figure was incomplete. This number should not be confused with a complete accounting of all Indigenous children who died in American schools, since it applies only to the federal system as defined, relies on surviving records, and covers only the stated period. It also does not include many church and other non-federal institutions. Similar cautions apply in Canada, where different bodies have compiled counts under different definitions and the Truth and Reconciliation Commission itself emphasized the incompleteness of records. The detailed history of gravesite investigations, and the figures compiled in Canada, belong to Chapters 127 and 128.

Historians therefore distinguish several categories of evidence: deaths documented by name and date in contemporaneous records; deaths documented in aggregate statistics; estimates based on partial data; and suspected or unlocated burials. They also distinguish the number of institutions under study, which varies with definitions, and the period covered, which differs among investigations. A statement that a given number of children died is meaningful only when accompanied by a statement of what was counted, from what sources, and over what period.

The incompleteness of the record is itself a historical subject. Registers and reports were produced by institutions with limited interest in documenting the deaths of Indigenous children, and many records were lost, destroyed, or dispersed. Families were often not informed promptly of illness or death, and in some documented cases they were not told where children were buried. Historians describe the gaps in the archive as an archival silence, and they treat the silence as evidence of the priorities and negligence of the institutions that created the records, while cautioning that absence of documentation is not proof of any particular conclusion.

Historical Vocabulary
Public-Health Inspection, Mortality Records, Institutional Accountability, and Archival Silence

Public-health inspection refers to examination of buildings, sanitation, food, and the health of residents by medical officers, as in Bryce's 1907 investigation. Mortality records are documents recording deaths, such as school registers, death certificates, and burial records, whose completeness varies widely. Institutional accountability describes the ability of authorities, the public, and affected families to hold an institution responsible for its conduct, which depends on clear lines of responsibility and effective oversight.

Archival silence describes gaps in the documentary record, whether caused by lost records, lack of recording, or the priorities of record-makers. A silence is a feature of the archive that historians must interpret, not evidence that nothing occurred.

Potential misunderstanding: A reported death total is not the same as the total number of deaths. Totals depend on definitions, sources, and time periods, and different totals may not be directly comparable.

Section 87.15

Punishment, Abuse, Institutional Oversight, and Failures of Accountability

Physical, emotional, and sexual abuse occurred in boarding and residential schools, and it is documented in sources that include inspection reports, correspondence, court records, government investigations, and survivor testimony. This section examines the institutional conditions that made abuse possible and sometimes allowed it to persist, and the kinds of evidence by which historians study it. The fuller account of children's experience, including survivor accounts, belongs to Chapter 88, and later investigations belong to Chapters 127 and 128. The present concern is with administrative structure and documented patterns.

The basic structural condition was the power imbalance between adults and children. Children lived under the near-total authority of staff, with limited ability to leave, to contact family, or to report mistreatment. Many children did not share a language with staff, especially when they first arrived, which further limited communication. The institutions' disciplinary systems gave staff wide discretion, and corporal punishment was normalized. Staff were often poorly screened, poorly paid, and poorly supervised, and the schools' remote locations limited outside scrutiny.

Oversight mechanisms existed but were weak. Inspectors visited periodically, but visits were infrequent and announced, and inspectors relied heavily on information from principals and staff. Reporting procedures usually ran through the institution's own hierarchy, so that complaints about staff were directed to the principals or church officials responsible for those staff. Parents who complained could be ignored, and children's letters could be read before being sent. Government officials and church authorities had interests in the reputation and continuity of the institutions and were sometimes reluctant to pursue complaints. Where abuse was identified, the documented responses included reprimand or dismissal in some cases, but historians and later investigators have also documented cases in which complaints were not acted on or in which staff members moved between institutions. The extent and frequency of such failures varied and is a subject of continuing research.

Different kinds of evidence carry different strengths and limits. Official inspection reports record what inspectors observed and what they chose to write, and they may understate problems. Criminal and civil court records document specific allegations and outcomes, but only for cases that reached courts, which were a small fraction of all incidents. Correspondence between staff, officials, and parents can reveal complaints and responses. Retrospective survivor testimony, which became extensive in the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, provides detailed accounts of experience that the contemporaneous record largely omits. Historians treat these categories as complementary, recognizing that each is shaped by who created it and why, and that the absence of contemporaneous documentation of abuse reflects the weakness of reporting channels as much as the absence of abuse.

The documented patterns vary between institutions and periods, and generalizations should be made carefully. Some schools had staff who were regarded by students as caring, and some students recalled positive relationships and valuable learning. Other schools were marked by pervasive mistreatment. A historian therefore cannot assume that every school or every child had the same experience, but the structural vulnerabilities were present throughout the system, and the evidence of abuse is extensive enough to be a central part of the institutional history.

Section 87.16

Indigenous Parents, Student Voices, Petitions, and Opposition to School Policies

Indigenous parents and communities were not passive in the face of these policies, and their responses must be central to the history. Opposition took many forms and arose from different motives. Some parents refused to enroll children, hid them, or arranged for them to avoid agents. Others enrolled children but protested school assignments, requested that children be returned, asked for improved food and medical care, or demanded that schools be located nearer to home. Communities held councils to discuss schooling, wrote petitions to agents, departments, and legislators, and in some cases sought legal remedies. Leaders used treaty obligations to argue for better schools, and some pressed for schools under community control.

The Hopi resistance of the 1890s, noted in §87.5, is one prominent example, and the Brulé Lakota leader Spotted Tail's visit to Carlisle in 1880, followed by the removal of some children, is another. In Canada, First Nations leaders and councils wrote to the Department of Indian Affairs protesting conditions, requesting day schools, and objecting to enforcement. Records show parents writing to principals to ask about sick children, to request permission for children to come home for family or ceremonial events, and to complain of mistreatment. Parents also used the options available to them, such as withholding consent where law allowed it, moving among reserves, or arranging enrollment at schools they judged less harmful.

Indigenous responses were not uniform. Some families pursued education and literacy actively, seeing them as tools for survival and political defense, while opposing the removal of children or the suppression of language and religion. Several nations established their own schools, and in the nineteenth century the Cherokee and Choctaw nations operated national school systems funded by their own governments, as discussed in Chapter 64. Some parents chose boarding or residential schools because food, clothing, or shelter were scarce, or because they valued the training. Others who initially cooperated later became critics. The different choices reflected differing circumstances, beliefs, and degrees of freedom, and historians avoid describing any single response as typical.

Student voices are also part of the record, but they require careful handling. Students wrote letters to parents, to school authorities, and to patrons, and some contributed to school newspapers such as Carlisle's The Indian Helper and The Red Man. These sources preserve students' words, but they were produced under institutional supervision, and school newspapers were edited to project a positive image. Letters might be written in English under instruction or review, and surviving collections were selected and preserved by the institutions. A student's expression of gratitude or contentment in such a source cannot be taken at face value, and a lack of complaint is not evidence of satisfaction. Nonetheless, the sources also preserve expressions of homesickness, requests, and implicit or explicit protest, and they show students shaping their circumstances through writing and through acts such as running away. Later memoirs and testimony by former students, some of whom became writers and leaders, supplement the record, with the caveat that they, too, are retrospective.

Opposition also had limits and costs. Parents who resisted could face withholding of rations, fines, imprisonment, or loss of standing with agents, and communities that depended on government supplies had limited leverage. Complaints often went unanswered, and some officials viewed resistance as evidence of the need for stronger measures. Yet opposition shaped policy. American legislation in 1894 restricting coercive recruitment responded in part to resistance and criticism, and in Canada complaints and organized advocacy helped build the pressure for change discussed in §87.18 and §87.19.

Multiple Perspectives

Parents, Students, School Administrators, Indigenous Leaders, and Government Inspectors

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns in the documentary record. They are not quotations from any single named individual.

From the perspective of parents: Responses varied from refusing enrollment, to negotiating placement, to accepting schooling as a necessity. Many sought information about their children's welfare and asked for their return, while operating under legal and economic constraints that limited their choices.
From the perspective of students: Experiences varied widely. Surviving sources, filtered by supervision and editing, show homesickness, adaptation, ambition, resistance such as running away, and in some cases appreciation for particular teachers or skills. No single account represents all students.
From the perspective of school administrators: Many principals and superintendents regarded the schools as beneficial and viewed parental opposition as ignorance or obstruction, and they relied on regulations and agents to secure attendance. Their reports emphasized success and often attributed problems to inadequate funding or to community attitudes.
From the perspective of Indigenous leaders: Leaders often supported education that respected community authority and objected to removal, suppression of language and religion, and unfulfilled treaty promises. Their positions varied by nation and over time, and some collaborated with school authorities while criticizing particular practices.
From the perspective of government inspectors: Inspectors varied. Some documented serious deficiencies and recommended reform, as Bryce did, while others endorsed schools or limited their reports to administrative matters. Their access to children's perspectives was limited by the formality of inspection.

Note on power: These perspectives did not carry equal authority. Administrators controlled institutions and records, while parents and students had limited formal power, which affected what was preserved and how it should be read.

Section 87.17

American Reform Criticism, the Meriam Report of 1928, and Changing Educational Policy

Criticism of American boarding schools was present from early in the system's history, and it came from several directions. Reformers questioned the quality of instruction, since the half-day system and the use of student labor left limited time for academic work. Physicians and inspectors raised concerns about health and diet. Some educators doubted that rigid vocational training prepared students for actual employment. Indigenous parents and leaders protested removal and cultural suppression. Congress periodically investigated, and the shift away from sectarian contract funding around 1900 reflected political conflict about the role of churches. These criticisms produced adjustments but not a fundamental change in the system's assimilationist orientation.

The most significant early twentieth-century critique was the Meriam Report of 1928. Commissioned by the Secretary of the Interior and prepared by a survey team led by Lewis Meriam of the Institute for Government Research, The Problem of Indian Administration examined health, education, economic conditions, and federal administration. Its education findings were severe. The survey team reported that boarding schools were often overcrowded and underfunded, that diets were inadequate, that much student labor was used to maintain institutions, that staff were often poorly qualified, and that curricula were poorly adapted to students' needs. The report argued that young children should not generally be removed from their families to attend distant boarding schools, recommended the development of community day schools, and urged that boarding schools, where retained, emphasize genuine academic and vocational preparation. It also recommended that education take account of Indigenous cultures and community conditions rather than seeking uniform assimilation.

The Meriam Report influenced the policy changes of the 1930s. Under Commissioner of Indian Affairs John Collier, appointed in 1933, the federal government promoted community-oriented schooling, expanded day schools, reduced some of the harshest practices, such as certain restrictions on Indigenous languages and religion, and sought to incorporate Indigenous cultural content into the curriculum. The Indian Reorganization Act of 1934, discussed in Chapter 97, shifted federal policy away from allotment and toward tribal organization, and the Johnson-O'Malley Act of 1934 authorized the federal government to contract with states for the education of Indigenous children, accelerating enrollment in public schools. Some boarding schools were closed or converted, and others changed their programs.

Policy reform must nevertheless be distinguished from the actual cessation of the boarding school system. Many boarding institutions continued to operate after 1934, and some expanded in the mid-twentieth century. The shortage of schools on large reservations, particularly the Navajo Nation, led to a substantial boarding school expansion after the Second World War. During the termination era of the 1950s, discussed in Chapter 104, federal policy again emphasized integration, and the boarding school remained a significant element of Indian education. In 1969 a U.S. Senate subcommittee report, commonly known by the title Indian Education: A National Tragedy, A National Challenge, again criticized federal Indian education and called for greater Indigenous participation. Some boarding schools have continued into the twenty-first century as part of the federal school system with changed missions and with Indigenous administration, an indication that the institutional form persisted even as its purposes were reconsidered.

From Cultural Assimilation toward Reform of Federal Indian Education

What Changed

  • Official policy shifted from explicit cultural replacement toward community schooling and, gradually, recognition of Indigenous cultures
  • Day schools and public-school attendance expanded relative to distant boarding schools
  • Reform reports such as Meriam's gave federal and public voice to criticism of conditions
  • From the 1960s, Indigenous communities began to assume administrative roles

What Stayed the Same

  • Many boarding schools continued to operate, and federal administration retained control over most Indigenous education
  • Funding limitations and uneven oversight persisted
  • Indigenous communities continued to seek control over curricula and administration

When and how: Change was gradual, uneven, and contested, and it varied by region. The 1928–1934 period was a turning point in policy, but practice changed more slowly. Who experienced it most directly: Students and families in regions where day schools replaced boarding institutions experienced the change most clearly, while those at schools that continued unchanged experienced greater continuity. Why the pattern matters: It shows that changes in stated policy did not automatically transform institutions.

Section 87.18

Canadian Residential School Reform, Integration Policy, and the 1969 Administrative Transition

In Canada, sustained government review of Indian policy followed the Second World War. A Special Joint Committee of the Senate and House of Commons examined the Indian Act between 1946 and 1948, hearing testimony from officials, church representatives, and Indigenous organizations. The committee criticized aspects of residential schooling, including conditions and the separation of children from communities, and favored the education of Indigenous children alongside non-Indigenous children in ordinary schools where possible. The Indian Act revisions of 1951 enabled the federal government to make agreements with provinces and school boards for the education of Indigenous students, formalizing a policy that came to be known as integration.

Integration changed where many children were taught but not necessarily how they were treated. Placing First Nations children in provincial schools could reduce separation from families, since children might attend while living at home. But integration did not by itself guarantee equality, cultural recognition, or parental control. Provincial schools often provided no instruction in Indigenous languages or histories, encouraged assimilation, and sometimes subjected students to racism. Federal agreements gave First Nations little voice in how their children were taught. Moreover, some integrated students lived in residences or boarding arrangements away from home, so integration sometimes coexisted with continued residential placement. Departmental inspectors in the 1950s criticized the residential model, and a 1958 recommendation by inspectors to end residential schooling is documented, but the closure of institutions proceeded slowly and unevenly.

A major administrative shift occurred in 1969, when the federal government began assuming operational responsibility for residential schools from the churches. Under agreements with the churches, the department took over administration, and in the years that followed many schools were closed or converted. This transition is often misunderstood. It was an administrative change in who ran institutions, and it did not close all residential institutions or end every form of separated schooling. Some schools continued under federal or, later, band operation, and student residences continued to house children who attended provincial schools. In the same year, the federal government issued its 1969 policy statement on Indian affairs, which proposed repealing the Indian Act and was vigorously opposed by First Nations organizations, an episode that intensified Indigenous demands for control over their own affairs.

The final closures extended into the 1990s. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission's historical chronology documents the transition and the prolonged closure of remaining institutions. Gordon's Residential School in Saskatchewan, often cited as the last federally operated residential school, closed in 1996, while some band-operated residential institutions and residences continued somewhat later. Which institution was "the last" therefore depends on definitions, including whether one counts federally operated or band-operated schools and whether student residences are classified as residential schools. This is an example of the definitional care required throughout the chapter.

The transfer of responsibility in 1969 also did not eliminate the tension between classroom integration and family residence. A student might attend a provincial public school during the day while continuing to live in a federally funded hostel or residence away from home. Statistics recording a decline in residential-school classroom enrollment therefore cannot, by themselves, establish that separation from family had ended. School records, residence records, and community accounts must be considered separately when reconstructing the pace and character of change.

Section 87.19

Indigenous Control of Education: Blue Quills, the 1972 Policy Statement, and Later School Transitions

The claim that Indigenous communities should control their own children's education had been made for generations, in treaty negotiations, petitions, and local protests. In the late 1960s and 1970s, it became an organized national demand. In Canada, one of the most visible expressions was the 1970 confrontation at Blue Quills, a Catholic residential school in Alberta. When the federal government moved to close the school and send students to provincial institutions, Indigenous families and leaders from the surrounding communities occupied the school buildings and demanded that they be transferred to First Nations control. After the July 1970 occupation and negotiations, federal officials agreed to the transfer in principle; Blue Quills’ own commemorative history records January 1, 1971 as the effective date for residence operations and July 1, 1971 as the target for school operations. The 1970 protest should therefore not be treated as the completed administrative transfer. It became widely regarded as the first school in Canada to be transferred to First Nations control, and it served as a model and symbol for later initiatives.

The institutional sequence matters politically. During the 1970 occupation, participating First Nations resisted a proposal to close Blue Quills in favor of sending children to other schools. Their demand was not simply for a continued facility but for control over decisions about staffing, curriculum, residence, and accountability while federal funding responsibilities remained. The negotiations and staged assumption of operations in 1971 therefore represented a claim to educational jurisdiction, not merely a change of contractor or principal.

In 1972 the National Indian Brotherhood, the national organization of First Nations that later became the Assembly of First Nations, issued a policy paper titled Indian Control of Indian Education. Drafted in response to the 1969 federal policy proposals, the paper argued that First Nations should have authority over their children's education, grounded in two principles: parental responsibility and local control. It called for education that would strengthen children's identity, teach their languages and cultures, and prepare them to live in both First Nations and non-Indigenous societies, and it criticized the integration policy for failing to recognize First Nations' rights. The federal government accepted the policy in principle in 1973, and it became a foundation for decades of work on First Nations educational authority, though implementation was uneven and funding disputes continued.

In the United States, parallel developments emerged. The Rough Rock Demonstration School, established in 1966 on the Navajo Nation, was an early experiment in community-controlled schooling, and Navajo Community College, founded in 1968, became the first tribally controlled college. The 1969 Senate report on Indian education added momentum. Congress passed the Indian Education Act of 1972, and in 1975 enacted the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act, which allowed tribes to contract with the federal government to administer programs that had been run by federal agencies, including schools. Further legislation in 1978 expanded tribal authority over schools and established funding structures for tribally controlled institutions. These measures reversed the long trend of federal control and marked a shift to tribal administration.

The transition was neither immediate nor complete. In both countries, communities faced limited funding, bureaucratic constraints, shortages of trained Indigenous educators, and the legacy of institutions that had weakened language transmission. Some boarding and residential schools continued to operate, in some cases under Indigenous administration, for years afterward. Others closed, and many communities established local schools, language programs, and cultural curricula. The movement for Indigenous control of education connected to broader political developments, including the era of self-determination in Chapter 114, Indigenous education and tribal colleges in Chapter 124, and language revitalization in Chapter 123. It also illustrates continuity in Indigenous political authority: the demand for control over education expressed the same claims to self-government that Indigenous nations had asserted throughout the period.

Section 87.20

Boarding and Residential Schools as Institutions of Colonial Assimilation: A Comparative Historical Synthesis

The American and Canadian systems shared assimilationist assumptions, drew on one another's models, and used similar mechanisms, but they differed in legal arrangements, religious involvement, geography, funding, institutional organization, and the chronology of reform. In both countries, governments held that Indigenous children should be reshaped through schooling, and in both, schools were part of a wider body of policy that included reservations and reserves, land allotment and the Indian Act, bans on ceremonies, and administrative supervision of Indigenous life. The institutions were therefore extensions of colonial policy, not isolated educational ventures.

The principal mechanisms were similar. Both relied on compulsory attendance backed by legal and economic pressure, though the laws differed and the distinction between attendance and residence mattered. Both separated children from families, usually by distance. Both restricted Indigenous languages, regulated names, dress, and behavior, and conducted religious instruction. Both depended on student labor and on limited funding, and both employed discipline that included corporal punishment. Both were administered through bureaucracies, with oversight that was fragmented and often ineffective. The differences were also significant. In Canada, churches ran most schools under federal contracts and per-capita grants, while in the United States the federal government operated most boarding schools directly after 1900. Canadian residential schooling was concentrated in the Prairies and the north, while American schools were distributed more widely. The timing of reform differed, with American reform beginning with the Meriam Report and the 1930s and Canadian reform following the Second World War and extending to the late 1990s.

Counts of schools illustrate the need for comparative care. The U.S. Department of the Interior's 2024 investigation identified 417 federal Indian boarding schools within its 1819–1969 framework. Canadian counts depend on whether one includes all historically operating institutions or only those recognized under a specific settlement framework, and the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's work addresses this question. These totals measure different categories and should not be compared as though they were equivalent. The same caution applies to enrollment and death figures, which differ in definition, period, and sources.

Indigenous resistance and the efforts to reclaim educational authority are central to the comparative picture. Parents resisted removal, students found ways to maintain identities and relationships, and communities and organizations pressed for control. The movements of the late 1960s and 1970s, from Blue Quills to Rough Rock, from Indian Control of Indian Education to the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act, show that the system's history cannot be told apart from the Indigenous political response to it. It is also important to avoid presenting modern educational outcomes as evidence of historical school success. The literacy, professional achievements, and later leadership of former students reflect their own abilities and efforts, and they do not justify the coercive assimilation policies under which they were schooled.

This chapter has documented how the institutions were created and administered. It has not attempted to capture fully what children and families lived through, which depends on Indigenous testimony and memory. It has also left to later chapters the modern investigations, burial-site research, apologies, and accountability initiatives. The distinction between documenting institutional history and examining the experiences of former students and their descendants is deliberate, and it leads directly to Chapter 88, which examines Indigenous children, families, and cultural survival.

Historical Debate

Were Boarding and Residential Schools Primarily Educational Institutions Misused for Assimilation, Instruments Deliberately Designed for Cultural Transformation, or Institutions in Which Education and Colonial Control Were Inseparable?

The question: Historians agree that assimilation was an aim of the policies that created these schools. They differ in how to characterize the relationship between that aim and the educational work the schools performed, and in how much weight to give variation among institutions.

Interpretation A — Educational institutions misused for assimilation: Some interpretations emphasize that many staff and families saw the schools as educational and that some students gained literacy and skills, so that the institutions are best understood as schools whose educational potential was distorted by assimilationist goals, neglect, and abuse. Proponents of this view point to variation among schools and to former students who valued aspects of their schooling.
Interpretation B — Instruments designed for cultural transformation: Other interpretations emphasize the stated aims in government documents, such as Pratt's address and Davin's report, and the deliberate design of institutions around removal, language replacement, and labor, to argue that cultural transformation was the purpose from the outset, and that education was the means.
Interpretation C — Education and colonial control inseparable: A third view holds that it is misleading to separate education from control, because the educational content, the legal authority to compel attendance, and the administrative structure all served the same colonial project. On this view the schools cannot be understood as ordinary schools with an added assimilationist purpose.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on policy statements, legislation, regulations, curricula, inspection reports, financial records, school newspapers, and Indigenous testimony and petitions.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and emphasis more than facts. Historians differ over how to weigh stated intentions against varied practice, how to treat individual accounts of positive experience, and what the term "education" should mean when applied to institutions with coercive aims. They do not substantially dispute that assimilation was a stated goal or that the schools used coercive mechanisms.

Why it matters: The characterization affects how the schools are placed within the history of education and of colonialism, what responsibility is assigned to governments and churches, and how later accountability is framed. It also affects how historians treat evidence of benefit, which should not be used to justify the system's coercive structure.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that boarding and residential schools were sustained institutional expressions of colonial assimilation policy, not isolated schools whose effects can be understood apart from the governments and churches that created and administered them. They replaced, or sought to replace, community-centered Indigenous education with institutions designed to transform children's languages, religions, family relationships, and loyalties. Understanding them requires attention to the education Indigenous nations already possessed, to the laws and bureaucracies that built the schools, and to Indigenous responses.

The American system grew from missionary schools, treaty education provisions, and the Civilization Fund Act of 1819, which established federal funding for instruction framed in the language of civilization. The off-reservation model emerged from Richard Henry Pratt's experience at Fort Marion and his work at Carlisle, which opened in October 1879 and combined military routine, English instruction, industrial training, and the outing system. Pratt's 1892 address summarized the assimilationist philosophy, joining a belief in individual capacity to an insistence that Indigenous cultural identity should disappear.

The Canadian system developed from church-operated institutions such as the Mohawk Institute, which began as a day school in 1828 and provided boarding by 1834, and from colonial proposals for industrial schools. Nicholas Flood Davin's 1879 report, based on his study of American schools, recommended that Indigenous children be educated away from their home communities in church-operated industrial schools, and the federal government began establishing such schools in the West in 1883. The 1892 per-capita funding arrangement and the Indian Act's compulsory-attendance provisions, notably the 1920 amendment, consolidated the partnership between the federal department and the churches.

Both governments moved from encouragement to compulsion. American measures of 1891 and 1893 authorized attendance rules and economic pressure, and were followed by restrictions in 1894, while Hopi resistance showed that families understood and opposed removal. The Canadian amendments expanded departmental power. A central distinction runs through the chapter: compulsory school attendance was not identical to compulsory residence in a distant institution, and historians must specify which law, which placement decision, and which alternatives applied.

Authority was divided. In the United States the Office of Indian Affairs and, after the end of sectarian contract funding around 1900, government superintendents ran most boarding schools, while in Canada churches administered schools under federal funding and supervision. Differences among boarding, industrial, mission, and day schools, and among Canadian industrial, boarding, residential, and hostel institutions, mattered for family separation, labor, and oversight. Geography shaped these differences, and First Nations, Inuit, and Métis students entered the system under distinct legal and chronological conditions.

Inside the schools, language rules, renaming, uniforms, religious instruction, and discipline formed a curriculum of assimilation, and the half-day system made student labor central to institutional operation. Whether this labor was education, maintenance, or exploitation depends on the evidence, and the evidence indicates that financial pressures encouraged reliance on children's work. The Carlisle outing system illustrates how labor and assimilation were joined and how institutional records both document and obscure experience.

Funding shortfalls, overcrowding, and poor sanitation contributed to illness and death, and P. H. Bryce's 1907 report showed that officials were informed about conditions in Canadian schools. Divided responsibility limited reform. Reported death totals, including the U.S. Department of the Interior's figure of at least 973 documented deaths in its defined federal system from 1819 to 1969, are partial minimums that rest on incomplete records and must not be treated as complete accountings. Institutional isolation, weak oversight, and the power imbalance between adults and children created conditions in which abuse could occur and persist.

Indigenous parents, students, councils, and leaders contested the schools throughout the period by refusing enrollment, petitioning, requesting children's return, demanding better conditions, running away, writing letters, and, in some cases, building their own schools. Their choices varied, and sources produced under institutional control must be read with caution. Their opposition influenced legislation, built pressure for reform, and ultimately fueled the movement for Indigenous control of education.

Reform came unevenly. In the United States, the Meriam Report of 1928 and the policies of the 1930s promoted community schooling, but many boarding schools continued. In Canada, postwar reviews, integration, and the 1969 transfer of operations from the churches changed administration without ending every residential institution, and the last closures occurred in the 1990s, depending on definitions. Blue Quills in 1970, the National Indian Brotherhood's 1972 policy paper, Rough Rock, and the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975 marked a shift toward Indigenous administration.

The chapter's comparative synthesis shows shared assumptions and different institutional arrangements, and it cautions against comparing school, enrollment, and death totals as though they measured equivalent categories. It also distinguishes documenting how the institutions operated from examining what they meant for children and families, the subject of Chapter 88, and from the modern investigations and reconciliation processes treated in Chapters 127 and 128.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because the boarding and residential schools were among the most sustained points of contact between Indigenous families and the state during the assimilation era. They show how a policy aim, cultural replacement, was turned into laws, budgets, buildings, regulations, and routines that touched individual households. Their history therefore links the large-scale processes examined elsewhere in Volume VIII, such as allotment and the Indian Act, to children's daily lives.

The chapter also shows how institutional history differs from the history of experience. Records of statutes, contracts, inspections, and schedules explain how the schools worked and who was responsible, yet they were produced by those who ran the institutions. Understanding the schools requires reading these sources critically, recognizing their silences, and turning to Indigenous testimony and memory, which Chapter 88 takes up and which Chapters 127 and 128 address in the context of modern investigations.

Finally, the chapter demonstrates continuity in Indigenous political life. Parents who petitioned, leaders who invoked treaties, students who wrote and ran away, and communities that occupied Blue Quills and drafted Indian Control of Indian Education were exercising the same claims to authority over their own children and futures that Indigenous nations asserted throughout this history. The reclamation of educational authority in the 1970s was not a new idea but the continuation of a long struggle, and it shaped the era of self-determination, language revitalization, and tribal education that follows.
