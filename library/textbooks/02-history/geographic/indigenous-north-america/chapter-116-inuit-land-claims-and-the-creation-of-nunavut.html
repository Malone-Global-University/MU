Chapter Opening

On April 1, 1999, a new Canadian territory came into legal existence. Its name, Nunavut, is an Inuktitut word usually translated as "our land." The territory did not appear overnight, and it was not simply conferred by Ottawa: it was the product of more than two decades of Inuit organizing, documentation, negotiation, and public argument.

Nunavut covers roughly two million square kilometres of the central and eastern Canadian Arctic, an area approximately one fifth of Canada's land surface, stretching from the mainland tundra west of Hudson Bay to the high islands of the Arctic Archipelago. At its creation it had a population on the order of 25,000 people, scattered across about two dozen communities, with Inuit forming the large majority of residents. Those communities are separated by great distances, linked by air rather than roads, and bound to one another by language, kinship, shared histories, and a political movement that took shape in the late twentieth century.

The history in this chapter is not the story of a single event. It is the story of two related but legally distinct achievements. The first was the Nunavut Land Claims Agreement, concluded in 1993, a negotiated settlement between Inuit of the Nunavut Settlement Area and the Government of Canada that defined collective Inuit rights to land, wildlife harvesting, resource revenue, and participation in environmental decision-making. The second was the Nunavut Act, a federal statute of the same year that provided for a new territorial government. The agreement was signed and ratified in 1993; the territory itself was established six years later. The interval between them was a period of institution-building that students should not collapse into a single moment.

That distinction matters because it shapes what Nunavut is. The Government of Nunavut is a public government serving all residents regardless of Indigenous identity. The organizations that hold Inuit treaty rights, most prominently Nunavut Tunngavik Incorporated, are separate bodies representing Inuit beneficiaries of the agreement. The two sets of institutions overlap, cooperate, and sometimes disagree. Neither is a sovereign state: Nunavut remains part of Canada, and its public government exercises powers established through Canadian constitutional and statutory law.

Historians reconstruct this history from a distinctive evidentiary base. It includes the 1976 Inuit Land Use and Occupancy Project, which converted Inuit testimony and geographical knowledge into maps and reports; proposals, position papers, and negotiating documents produced by Inuit organizations; the texts of the 1993 agreement and statute; plebiscite and ratification results; federal and territorial records; later commissions and reports; oral histories and memoirs by participants; and a substantial body of scholarship by Inuit and non-Indigenous writers. Each of these sources reflects particular interests and circumstances, and the chapter attends throughout to whose voices they carry.

The chapter's central argument is that the creation of Nunavut transformed Inuit land claims into a new arrangement combining constitutionally protected treaty rights, Inuit-owned lands, shared resource-management institutions, and a public territorial government. This was a major achievement of Inuit political self-determination. It did not create an independent Inuit state, and it did not remove the constraints of Canadian federalism, fiscal dependence, or the unfinished work of implementation.

Within the broader textbook, this chapter follows Chapter 115, which examined the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act and its corporate structure, and builds on Chapter 100, which traced Inuit experience of Canadian administration, settlement, and relocation. It anticipates Chapter 117 on First Nations constitutional politics, Chapter 123 on language revitalization, Chapter 135 on wildlife and treaty stewardship, and Chapter 139 on contemporary Indigenous governments. Where those chapters carry the larger story, this one stays focused on the movement and negotiations that produced the 1993 settlement and the territory of 1999.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the Inuit homelands of the central and eastern Canadian Arctic, including the Kitikmeot, Kivalliq, and Qikiqtaaluk regions, and the diversity of Inuit communities and dialects.
  2. Explain how twentieth-century Canadian administration changed Inuit relationships to land and why it prompted political organization.
  3. Distinguish between Aboriginal title, comprehensive land-claims policy, and a negotiated modern treaty.
  4. Trace the emergence of Inuit Tapirisat of Canada and the Inuit land-claims movement from 1971 through the early 1980s.
  5. Analyze the Inuit Land Use and Occupancy Project as a method of translating Inuit knowledge into evidence suited to negotiation.
  6. Assess the significance of linking a land-claims settlement to the creation of a new territory in the 1976 Nunavut proposal.
  7. Explain why the western boundary of Nunavut was determined through negotiation and plebiscites rather than by a fixed cultural frontier.
  8. Distinguish the 1993 Nunavut Land Claims Agreement from the 1993 Nunavut Act and explain the roles of each.
  9. Evaluate the agreement's provisions on land ownership, wildlife harvesting, shared management, and employment.
  10. Contrast Inuit treaty organizations with the public Government of Nunavut.
  11. Interpret competing scholarly assessments of whether Nunavut represents Inuit self-government.
  12. Connect the Nunavut experience to Canadian constitutional protection of treaty rights and to the comparative case of Alaska.

Historical Context

Inuit are the Indigenous people of the Arctic coasts and islands of northern Canada, Alaska, and Greenland, and they trace their ancestry to peoples who spread eastward across the Arctic over many centuries, a movement examined in Chapter 35. Within the Canadian Arctic, Inuit communities developed economies centered on seal, whale, caribou, fish, and birds, organized around seasonal movement between sea-ice, coastal, and inland camps. Authority rested in kinship networks, experienced hunters, and elders, and was shaped by deep environmental knowledge. The territory they used was not empty land awaiting administration; it was a landscape named, traveled, and harvested according to long-standing patterns.

Canadian claims over the Arctic expanded across the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, but for much of that period effective governance was thin. Whalers, traders, missionaries, and police established posts and relationships in the Arctic, and after the Second World War the federal government extended health, education, housing, and administrative services into Inuit communities with new intensity. Chapter 100 treats this transformation in detail, including settlement policy and the High Arctic relocations. For this chapter the essential point is that within a few decades many Inuit families moved from land-based camps to permanent communities, and decisions affecting their lives were made by officials working from Ottawa and later from Yellowknife.

Unlike much of southern Canada and parts of the western North-West Territories, the Arctic was not covered by the historic numbered treaties discussed in Chapter 82. No comprehensive land-cession agreement had addressed Inuit ownership of the lands they used. This gap would become politically decisive in the early 1970s, when Canadian courts and federal policy opened a new route for Indigenous claims. It also made the Arctic different from Alaska, where Chapter 115 examined a settlement enacted by Congress in 1971, and from other parts of Canada where earlier treaties had been signed.

Finally, the Inuit land-claims movement emerged in a period of wider Indigenous political mobilization across North America, described in Chapters 108 through 114, and in an Arctic context of resource exploration, northern development proposals, and growing national attention to the North. Inuit organizers also built ties across borders, including through the Inuit Circumpolar Conference founded in 1977, though this chapter concentrates on Canada. Students should read the Nunavut story as one product of that moment, shaped as much by the particulars of Arctic geography and Canadian law as by continental trends.

Section 116.1 · Division I — Inuit Homelands, Colonial Administration, and Political Awakening

Inuit Peoples and the Canadian Arctic Homeland

The region that became Nunavut is not a single environment. Its mainland is tundra and rock, cut by rivers, lakes, and bays; its islands include some of the largest in the world, ringed by sea ice for much of the year. Inuit living in this region organized their lives around the movement of animals and the condition of ice. Coastal communities depended heavily on ringed seal and other marine mammals, and on whales and walrus where available; inland groups relied more on caribou and fish. Seasonal travel carried families between camps by sled, boat, and on foot, and the knowledge needed for safe travel, including reading ice, weather, and animal behaviour, was learned in childhood and refined across a lifetime.

Three regions of the eventual territory are used in this chapter. The Kitikmeot region, in the west, includes communities such as Cambridge Bay, with Inuinnaqtun-speaking populations. The Kivalliq region lies along the western shore of Hudson Bay and its interior, with communities such as Rankin Inlet and Arviat. The Qikiqtaaluk region, historically called Baffin, includes Baffin Island and the High Arctic islands, with Iqaluit and Pond Inlet among its communities. These regional names are administrative as well as geographical, and they reflect real differences in dialect, history, economic opportunity, and relationships with outsiders.

Inuit authority over land and resources did not take the form of fixed state borders. Territories overlapped, families moved, and rights to use particular places were negotiated through kinship, reciprocity, and established practice. Experienced hunters and elders held influence, but this was not the same as hereditary rule. Language and place names carried territorial knowledge: a map of Inuit names and travel routes is itself a form of authority. Describing the Arctic as an uninhabited wilderness, as outside observers frequently did, erased this knowledge and made it easier to treat the land as available for administration or development.

Students should also avoid treating Inuit societies as uniform. Dialects of Inuktitut and Inuinnaqtun differ; subsistence emphases, contact histories, and religious affiliations varied; and communities have long had their own internal politics. The Nunavut movement was therefore an achievement of coalition-building among diverse communities, not the expression of a pre-existing single political body.

Section 116.2 · Division I

Canadian Sovereignty and the Transformation of Inuit Communities

For much of the early twentieth century, the Canadian presence in the Arctic consisted of trading posts operated by companies such as the Hudson's Bay Company, Anglican and Catholic missions, and detachments of the Royal Canadian Mounted Police. These institutions influenced Inuit economic life through the fur trade, introduced new religious and legal norms, and drew families toward posts, but many Inuit continued to live primarily on the land. The decline of fur prices and the strategic and political concerns of the postwar period changed this balance.

After the Second World War, and especially from the 1950s, the federal government extended services and control more intensively. Federal day schools and hostels brought children into formal education, often in English and with limited regard for Inuit language and knowledge, a subject connected to Chapters 87 and 88. Tuberculosis control involved removal of many patients to southern hospitals, separating families. Housing programs and administrative services encouraged settlement in permanent communities. Government programs also included relocations, notably the 1950s movement of families to the High Arctic, discussed in Chapter 100, and identification systems that assigned numbers to individuals before later surname projects. These measures varied by region and over time, and their effects remain contested and are examined in later inquiries.

The consequences for land use were complex. Many families remained deeply committed to hunting and harvesting, but the move to settlements meant that hunters travelled from a fixed community rather than living year-round at camps. Wage work, store-bought goods, and government services entered household economies, while country food continued to be central to nutrition and social life. Decisions about schools, housing, wildlife regulations, and development were taken by non-Inuit officials, and Inuit often had little formal say. The sense of being governed without representation, while still dependent on land-based resources that outsiders did not fully understand, provided a political foundation for the movement discussed in the following sections.

Inuit responses were not merely reactive. Families adapted, moved between community and land, maintained language and knowledge, and, as new forms of communication and organization appeared, began to articulate political demands. Inuit interpreters, teachers, and community workers acquired knowledge of both worlds and became key figures in the emerging movement.

Section 116.3 · Division I

The Inuit Land Question and Canadian Territorial Administration

Inuit in the eastern Arctic lived within the Northwest Territories, a vast jurisdiction whose government evolved from direct federal administration toward a partly elected territorial council. The seat of government moved to Yellowknife in 1967, within the Mackenzie Valley and far from most Inuit communities. For Inuit in the Qikiqtaaluk, Kivalliq, and Kitikmeot regions, decisions affecting schools, hunting, and local administration were therefore made hundreds or thousands of kilometres away. Representation existed, but the territory's population balance, languages, and geography made it difficult for Inuit voices to dominate decisions about the eastern Arctic.

The legal framework for Indigenous land questions in Canada also treated the Arctic differently from regions covered by earlier treaties. Where historic treaties had been signed, the central issue was often their interpretation and implementation. In much of the Arctic, no treaty of land cession existed. Inuit therefore stood in a position of asserting unextinguished Aboriginal rights over lands and waters that had never been formally ceded. This was a strong moral and legal claim, but its practical meaning was uncertain in the absence of clear judicial or statutory definition.

That uncertainty began to change in 1973. The Supreme Court of Canada's decision in the Calder case, brought by Nisga'a leaders in British Columbia, did not produce a single majority holding on whether Aboriginal title had survived, but it persuaded the federal government that unextinguished Aboriginal title could not be dismissed. In August 1973 Ottawa announced a policy for negotiating comprehensive claims in areas where Indigenous title had not been dealt with by treaty. Under this policy, negotiated settlements would clarify rights to land and resources in exchange for defined benefits and, at the time, a release of broader claims.

Historical Vocabulary
Comprehensive Land Claim

Definition. A comprehensive land claim is a negotiation between the Government of Canada and an Indigenous group over rights to land and resources in an area where no historic treaty or other legal instrument has dealt with Aboriginal title.

Historical context. The federal policy was announced in 1973 following the Calder decision and has been revised several times. Settlements produced under it, such as the James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement, the Inuvialuit Final Agreement, and the Nunavut Land Claims Agreement, are often called modern treaties. After 1983, Canadian constitutional law expressly recognized land-claims agreements as protected under Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982.

Why it matters. The comprehensive claims framework provided the legal channel through which Inuit land rights could be negotiated, but it also shaped the bargaining terms. In early settlements, Indigenous parties agreed to forms of release of broader claims; later policy debates have questioned that approach.

Potential misunderstanding. A comprehensive claim is not a lawsuit, and a modern treaty is not the same as a historic numbered treaty. It is a negotiated, constitutionally protected agreement concluded in the late twentieth century.

Section 116.4 · Division I

Inuit Political Organizing and the Founding of Inuit Tapirisat of Canada

Modern Inuit political advocacy in Canada took shape through the 1960s as Inuit gained experience in education, employment, and community councils, and as communication across distant settlements improved. Young Inuit leaders, many trained in English and in the institutions of the federal and territorial governments, connected with one another and with older community leaders. Interpreters and translators played a particularly important role, since they understood both the language of officials and the concerns of families.

In 1971 these networks produced Inuit Tapirisat of Canada, whose name is often translated as "Inuit will be united." It was created as a national Inuit organization and, in its early years, concerned itself with a broad agenda: cultural preservation, communications, education, social services, and political representation, as well as land. The organization's regional structure brought together Inuit from different parts of the Arctic, though regional differences would later lead some groups to pursue separate agreements. In 2001 the organization was renamed Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami.

The land question quickly became central. Resource exploration and northern development proposals underscored that decisions about Arctic lands were being made without a clear legal role for Inuit. The 1973 federal claims policy gave the movement a formal channel, and Inuit Tapirisat decided to prepare a claim based on Inuit occupancy and use. Leaders such as John Amagoalik and Tagak Curley became prominent, and in 1979 the election of Peter Ittinuar as the first Inuk Member of Parliament signalled the growing political presence of Inuit in national institutions.

The movement was therefore a convergence. Demands for land rights, for control over services, for language and culture, and for representation were not separate strands but aspects of a broader claim: that Inuit should have a decisive voice in the governance of the Arctic homeland. That claim of self-determination would be expressed in two parallel institutional forms, a land settlement and a government, which is why this chapter must keep them analytically distinct.

Section 116.5 · Division II — The Inuit Land-Claims Movement and the Vision of Nunavut

The Inuit Land Use and Occupancy Project

If Inuit were to negotiate under federal policy, they needed evidence that would be persuasive in a legal and political setting. Following a proposal by Inuit Tapirisat in 1973, a major research project was undertaken to document where Inuit had lived, travelled, and harvested. The Inuit Land Use and Occupancy Project, directed by anthropologist Milton Freeman in cooperation with Inuit organizations, collected information from Inuit hunters and elders in communities across the Arctic and produced a multi-volume report and map atlas published in 1976.

The method was both technical and communal. Researchers, working with Inuit interviewers and community members, asked people to describe and mark on maps where they had hunted, fished, trapped, camped, and travelled during their lifetimes, and to record place names and the seasonal patterns of harvesting. The resulting maps showed extensive and overlapping use across lands and sea ice, challenging the assumption that the Arctic interior and coastline were largely unused. The project also built a body of documented place names and travel histories that represented Inuit geographical knowledge in a form that outside officials could recognize.

The project's significance lay in its ability to translate knowledge from oral and experiential form into a document admissible in political and legal argument. It also had costs and limits. Maps compress movement and seasonality into lines and shaded areas, and the project's categories reflected the needs of negotiation. Some forms of knowledge, including relationships with particular places or beliefs about land and animals, do not map neatly. Nevertheless, the work demonstrated that Inuit occupation was continuous, extensive, and organized, and it provided a factual foundation for the 1976 proposal.

The project depended on evidence assembled across numerous communities, not merely on a map drawn by outside experts. Hunters described seasonal routes, locations of camps and caches, and places where harvests were obtained during different environmental conditions. An account of spring travel over sea ice required a different geographical vocabulary from an account of inland caribou movement. Inuit interpreters and local participants were therefore not incidental assistants: they helped translate the meaning of place names, travel histories, and practical knowledge into the conventions of research reports. The 1976 publication itself was arranged as a report on land use and occupancy, supporting studies, and an atlas, and the three parts should be read together.

A land-use map also changes the nature of a political claim. It can make widely dispersed patterns visible to a minister who has never visited the Arctic, yet it can imply that complex relationships are neatly bounded by a drawn line. Families might use the same area for different purposes in different years, while important locations could be known through memory rather than frequent recent visits. These limitations did not invalidate the evidence; they revealed why Indigenous testimony and documentary conventions needed to be interpreted together. The research process itself also trained participants and strengthened a cross-regional network that could support negotiations.

Section 116.6 · Division II

The Nunavut Proposal of 1976

In early 1976 Inuit Tapirisat presented a proposal to the federal government for a comprehensive settlement of Inuit land claims in the Northwest Territories. Its distinctive feature was that the settlement was to be linked to the creation of a new territory in the eastern and central Arctic. The proposal named this territory Nunavut, a word usually translated as "our land," and argued that a government located closer to Inuit communities and responsive to Inuit majorities would be a more effective guarantor of Inuit interests than a distant capital.

The proposal combined two ideas that could have been pursued separately. One concerned collective rights over land and resources: ownership, harvesting rights, a share in resource revenues, and a role in decisions about development. The other concerned governance: a new public territory in which Inuit would be the majority and could shape laws, services, and administration. The movement's leaders recognized that these goals were related but not identical. Land ownership belonged to Inuit as a collective of beneficiaries, while a territorial government would serve all residents, Inuit and non-Inuit alike.

The federal government did not accept the combined framework. Ottawa's claims policy treated land and resources as the proper subject of negotiation, and creating a territory involved constitutional, fiscal, and political questions affecting the whole Northwest Territories, including Dene and Métis residents with their own claims. By the late 1970s the territorial element had been separated from the formal claims negotiation, and the idea of Nunavut moved into other political arenas. Inuit leaders kept it alive through public campaigns, constitutional discussions, and later negotiations. This persistence is crucial evidence of Inuit agency: the territory was not offered but kept on the agenda against official reluctance.

The geographical ambition of the 1976 proposal was wider than the boundary ultimately enacted. Historical accounts of the proposal include western Arctic areas associated with the Inuvialuit and the Beaufort Sea. Subsequent decisions to pursue a separate Inuvialuit agreement, and extended negotiation over the eastern–western division, narrowed and reshaped the territory contemplated by Inuit organizers. Students should therefore avoid projecting the 1999 map backward onto the first proposal. The political idea survived, but its territory, beneficiaries, and institutional arrangements were not fixed at the outset.

The proposed public government was also a strategic response to a demographic reality. Inuit constituted a majority in much of the central and eastern Arctic, but Inuit land rights did not by themselves confer authority to administer every public service or to pass laws binding all residents. Conversely, a territorial legislature could address schools and housing without necessarily owning hunting grounds or minerals. By proposing both instruments, Inuit leaders sought to combine a durable legal foundation for collective rights with a practical government closer to the electorate. Ottawa's decision to separate the two negotiating tracks made the task longer and more complicated, but it did not persuade organizers to abandon their linkage.

Historical Vocabulary
Nunavut

Definition. Nunavut is an Inuktitut word usually translated as "our land." It is also the name of the Canadian territory established in 1999 and of the settlement area defined by the 1993 land-claims agreement.

Historical context. The word was used in the Inuit Tapirisat proposal of 1976 to designate a proposed homeland and government for Inuit of the eastern and central Arctic. It later became the name of the political movement, the territory, and the region under the agreement.

Why it matters. The name expresses a collective relationship to land. It also illustrates how a single word can refer to distinct institutional realities that students must keep separate.

Potential misunderstanding. "Our land" does not mean that all land in the territory is owned by Inuit. Inuit-owned lands cover a portion of the territory, while the rest is Crown land administered under Canadian law and subject to shared management institutions.

Section 116.7 · Division II

Different Inuit Regions and Different Settlement Strategies

The Nunavut movement was one of several Inuit efforts to secure land rights and political voice. In Nunavik, in northern Quebec, Inuit negotiated alongside the Cree and the Quebec and federal governments in response to the James Bay hydroelectric project, concluding the James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement in 1975. That settlement is often regarded as the first modern comprehensive land-claims agreement in Canada. It created regional institutions, including the Kativik Regional Government, and served as a precedent and a point of comparison for Inuit elsewhere.

In the western Arctic, the Inuvialuit pursued their own claim through the Committee for Original Peoples' Entitlement and concluded the Inuvialuit Final Agreement in 1984. The Inuvialuit settlement concerned the Inuvialuit Settlement Region within the Northwest Territories and did not involve the creation of a separate territory. In Labrador, Inuit negotiated a different settlement that was concluded later and created a distinct self-governing region. These cases show that Inuit political goals varied according to demographic position, environment, resource pressures, and relationships with provincial and territorial governments.

In the eastern Arctic the demographic situation was distinctive: Inuit formed a large majority in a region with a clear geographical coherence, which made the idea of a separate territory more feasible than elsewhere. In other regions Inuit were a smaller share of the total population or lived within provinces with established governments. The Nunavut model was thus shaped by circumstances as much as by principle, and it should not be treated as the only or inevitable Inuit path to self-determination. Comparisons with the Alaska experience described in Chapter 115 are valuable here: there, Congress created corporations in the absence of a Native-majority state government, while in Canada Inuit leaders sought a mixture of treaty rights and a public territory.

Section 116.8 · Division II

Building a Negotiating Organization: The Tungavik Federation of Nunavut

After the 1976 proposal, Inuit Tapirisat remained the national voice, but negotiating a detailed settlement for the eastern Arctic required a dedicated organization with authority to bargain on behalf of Inuit in the region. In 1982 the Tungavik Federation of Nunavut was formed for that purpose. It drew on regional Inuit associations from the Kitikmeot, Kivalliq, and Qikiqtaaluk areas and was responsible for negotiating the claim with the federal government.

Creating a unified position was difficult. Communities differed in their economic circumstances, with some close to mineral prospects and others dependent mainly on hunting. They had different priorities about land selection, wildlife management, financial arrangements, and the relative importance of the territory. Distance and the cost of travel made consultation expensive, and the need to translate technical legal and financial terms into Inuktitut and Inuinnaqtun was a demanding task. Delegates from communities, regional associations, and the federation had to reconcile local interests with the need for a coherent bargaining position.

The federation also faced a government that had its own institutional constraints. Federal negotiators operated within policy frameworks developed in Ottawa and under pressure to limit costs and preserve flexibility. Territorial officials had interests in the future of the Northwest Territories. The negotiations, which extended through the 1980s and into the 1990s, therefore combined technical bargaining with continued political pressure and public campaigning. The federation's persistence and its ability to hold together a diverse constituency were central to the eventual outcome.

A negotiating organization also had to establish whom it represented and how it could receive instructions. Elected and appointed delegates carried positions from regional and community discussions, while the negotiating team translated those positions into language suitable for legal schedules. The issue was more than obtaining a large land quantum: selecting land with access to particular harvesting areas or mineral resources could affect future livelihoods differently. The costs of consultation were unusually high because moving between many communities usually required aircraft and specialized transport. Reaching agreement meant returning repeatedly to explain difficult compromises and to hear objections.

The federation's work should not be confused with that of the eventual territorial government. Before Nunavut existed as a public jurisdiction, the Tungavik Federation represented Inuit in a defined claim. It could advocate, negotiate, and arrange ratification, but it did not operate a territorial legislature or serve non-Inuit residents as a public authority. This distinction became central to the 1992 political accord: the Inuit claimant organization could help secure the promise of a new territory, but Parliament would still have to establish the legal framework of public government. The movement thus required two kinds of institutional expertise—treaty negotiation and territorial construction.

Section 116.9 · Division III — Political Negotiations and the Nunavut Land Claims Settlement

The 1982 Northwest Territories Division Plebiscite

While the claims negotiation proceeded, the question of dividing the Northwest Territories was tested in a territory-wide vote. On April 14, 1982, residents of the Northwest Territories were asked whether the territory should be divided. Approximately 56.6 percent of those voting supported division, with support strongest in the eastern Arctic and weaker in parts of the western territory. The vote involved Inuit, Dene, Métis, and non-Indigenous residents, whose motivations varied.

Supporters in the east argued that a territory centred in the Arctic would be more responsive to Inuit communities, languages, and economies. Some non-Indigenous residents supported division for practical reasons, including administration and representation. In the west, opinion was more divided. Dene and Métis organizations, which were pursuing their own claims and constitutional goals, had concerns about how division would affect their negotiating position, the size and viability of the remaining territory, and the placement of the boundary. Residents of the Mackenzie Valley also differed over whether a smaller western territory would be viable or desirable.

The plebiscite established that a majority across the territory favoured division in principle, which strengthened the political case. It did not, however, determine where the line should be drawn, how the two territories would be governed, or how Indigenous claims would be integrated. Those questions required further negotiation. The vote thus marked a turning point without resolving the underlying problems: it confirmed political momentum while leaving the hardest issues for later.

The wording of the 1982 question mattered. Voters approved division in principle rather than a completed constitutional and administrative plan. There was no final western boundary, no settled distribution of territorial assets and personnel, and no agreed schedule for establishing the two governments. That explains why a clear result could be followed by a prolonged series of negotiations rather than by immediate legislation. The territories were not provincial governments with full constitutional powers; Parliament retained authority over the legal structure, and federal financing would remain indispensable.

The vote also cautions against treating a territorial majority as a single Indigenous judgment. Support was geographically uneven, reflecting different relationships to the existing administration in Yellowknife, distinct Inuit and Dene or Métis political projects, and practical concerns over economic viability. The eastern Inuit movement nevertheless gained an important public mandate: it could say that division had been submitted to the wider electorate and had received majority support. Subsequent negotiators had to convert that political expression into terms acceptable to multiple parties and to resolve the boundary problem without assuming that one plebiscite had extinguished overlapping land claims.

Section 116.10 · Division III

Territorial Boundaries and the Struggle over the Division of the Northwest Territories

The western boundary of the future Nunavut was among the most contested aspects of the process. The region where Inuit and Dene lands met was not a sharply defined frontier. Inuit, Dene, and Métis had used overlapping areas for hunting and travel, and their claims and interests intersected. Dene and Métis groups were also negotiating their own settlements, and a boundary drawn in the context of one negotiation could affect another. The geography of Nunavut was therefore the result of political negotiation, not of a timeless cultural or ethnic frontier.

In January 1987 negotiators from Inuit and Dene and Métis organizations reached the Iqaluit Agreement on principles for dividing the territory, including a proposed boundary and arrangements for overlapping areas. The agreement proved fragile. Dene and Métis leaders subsequently withdrew support, citing concerns about land rights and the relationship between boundary and claims. Negotiations continued, with federal and territorial participation, and the boundary question remained unsettled into the early 1990s. Meanwhile, Dene and Métis groups in the western Arctic concluded separate settlements, including those of the Gwich'in and the Sahtu, which helped clarify the territorial map.

A proposed boundary was eventually put to a vote. On May 4, 1992, residents of the Northwest Territories voted in a plebiscite on the boundary, and a majority, approximately 54 percent, approved it. Further negotiations produced the arrangements that were included in the political accord and implemented in the legislation. The boundary thus rested on repeated negotiations, public votes, and compromises, and some Indigenous leaders in the west continued to emphasize their concerns about overlapping interests.

A line on an administrative map could be drawn for governing convenience, but land-claims boundaries involved customary use and competing rights that could not be erased by a public vote. The 1987 Iqaluit Agreement attempted to reconcile these different questions by addressing proposed division principles and overlapping interests. Its breakdown showed the danger of treating a boundary settlement as final before all affected parties considered it acceptable. The negotiations were therefore not simply a disagreement between an eastern and a western electorate; they involved Indigenous rights-holders with positions that could diverge from those of the territorial administration.

The 1992 vote on the proposed boundary helped provide a basis for legislation, but the approval was not overwhelming and did not settle every question concerning shared use, representation, or public services. After division, the remaining Northwest Territories would continue to have its own Indigenous political claims and administrative needs. By placing the boundary issue next to the Nunavut treaty rather than subsuming one under the other, students can see why creating a territory required public-law negotiations alongside the resolution of Indigenous land rights.

Section 116.11 · Division III

Negotiating the Final Land Claims Agreement

Negotiations moved toward a formal agreement-in-principle during 1989 and early 1990, culminating in its signature at Igloolik on April 30, 1990 by the Tungavik Federation of Nunavut and Canada. The instrument established a detailed negotiating framework rather than completing the claim. Its provisions anticipated the transfer of Inuit-owned land, harvesting and participation rights, financial compensation calculated in first-quarter 1989 dollars, and the continued negotiation of final land selections. The remaining work involved drafting enforceable treaty language and securing agreement on the schedules and institutions that would give those commitments practical effect.

The major disputed subjects were land ownership and selection, including how much land Inuit would own and in what areas; mineral rights, including subsurface title on a portion of Inuit lands; wildlife harvesting rights and management; financial compensation and its timing; resource royalties; environmental assessment and planning; and the political reorganization of the territory. Inuit negotiators sought to secure strong protections for harvesting and for participation in decisions, while federal negotiators sought to limit costs and to retain public authority over Crown lands and resources. Compromises were reached on each of these issues, and the outcome reflected the relative bargaining power of the parties.

A key development occurred on October 30, 1992, when the federal government, the Government of the Northwest Territories, and the Tungavik Federation of Nunavut signed the Nunavut Political Accord. The accord linked the claims settlement to commitments concerning the creation of a new territory, setting a timeline and process for establishing Nunavut. It was a political solution to the problem that had divided the two goals in the 1970s: the territory would be created through legislation separate from the agreement but with a firm political commitment. This arrangement preserved the distinction between treaty rights and public government while ensuring that both would proceed.

The 1990 agreement-in-principle is important as a documentary stage between political demand and binding settlement. It was not itself the final modern treaty. The parties still had to identify parcels of Inuit-owned land, attach legally workable maps and schedules, establish public boards, and settle how decisions would be made when Inuit and government priorities diverged. Mineral ownership in particular required careful drafting because surface ownership and mineral title could be held separately. Wildlife management required a different approach, linking protected Inuit harvests to conservation and institutional review rather than merely assigning a single numerical entitlement.

The path from a negotiated framework to ratification also required a political bargain about the territory itself. Inuit leaders had consistently argued that ownership and harvesting protection would be incomplete if decisions about public administration remained concentrated far away. The political accord of October 1992 supplied a commitment from the federal and territorial governments to proceed with public territorial creation. Crucially, this was not the same legal document as the land-claims agreement. Article 4.1.3 of the subsequent treaty stated that the political accord and the associated territorial legislation were not intended to become land-claims or treaty rights under section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982. The distinction protected the conceptual separation between collective Inuit rights and the political institutions of all residents.

Section 116.12 · Division III

Inuit Ratification and the Nunavut Land Claims Agreement of 1993

An agreement negotiated by representatives required the consent of the people it affected. In November 1992 Inuit beneficiaries voted on the proposed final agreement, and roughly 85 percent of those voting approved. The ratification process involved community meetings, explanations in Inuktitut and Inuinnaqtun, and a vote across the settlement area, and the result gave negotiators a strong mandate.

The agreement was signed in Iqaluit on May 25, 1993, in a ceremony attended by Inuit leaders and by the prime minister of Canada. In June 1993 the federal Parliament passed the Nunavut Land Claims Agreement Act, which gave the agreement force of law, and the Nunavut Act, which provided for the new territory. The agreement came into force on July 9, 1993. From that date, it was protected as a treaty within the meaning of Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982, which recognizes and affirms existing Aboriginal and treaty rights and, since 1983, includes rights acquired through land-claims agreements. Chapter 117 examines this constitutional framework more fully.

The legal transformation was substantial. Before the agreement, Inuit relied on claims of unrecognized Aboriginal title whose content had never been defined. After it, they held defined treaty rights and obligations enforceable under Canadian law. The agreement also contained, as comprehensive settlements of the era did, provisions in which Inuit agreed to release other claims to lands in the settlement area in exchange for the rights and benefits it provided. This feature, and its relationship to the broader principle of continuing Indigenous rights, has been debated, and federal policy has moved away from such releases in more recent years.

Ratification was a significant political act in its own right. Negotiators could not simply presume that their agreement represented the consent of every person who would be affected. Public information and interpretation were necessary because the final arrangement dealt not only with land and money but also with future institutions, defined beneficiary rights, and the release of wider claims. The roughly 85% approval figure describes the share of ballots cast in favour; it should not be interpreted as 85% of every eligible Inuit person. The result nonetheless gave Inuit representatives a strong mandate to proceed.

The parliamentary process in 1993 followed two related but separate tracks. The Nunavut Land Claims Agreement Act brought the treaty into force on July 9, 1993, while the Nunavut Act created the legal machinery for a territory that did not begin operating until April 1, 1999. A treaty beneficiary might have rights recognized immediately in 1993 even while remaining a resident of the undivided Northwest Territories for several more years. These layered dates mattered for implementation: wildlife, land conveyance, and other treaty obligations had to be administered while the future territorial government was still being designed. The legal story thus cannot be compressed into a single date of independence or transfer of authority.

Section 116.13 · Division IV — Landownership, Treaty Rights, and Inuit Institutions

Inuit-Owned Lands and Financial Compensation

The agreement's land provisions provided Inuit with title to approximately 356,000 square kilometres of land within the Nunavut Settlement Area, an area comparable to a large country but a minority of the territory's total area. On approximately 38,000 square kilometres of these lands, Inuit also hold subsurface mineral title. On the remainder, Inuit hold surface title only, while minerals remain Crown property. These distinctions are crucial because subsurface rights determine who may authorize and profit from mining.

Financial compensation was negotiated as C$580 million valued in first-quarter 1989 dollars, with interest and staged payments over fourteen years producing approximately C$1.148 billion in total payments, as described in later federal settlement summaries. The 1989-dollar principal and the subsequent total are different measures and should not be conflated. The agreement separately established participation in resource royalties and provisions for implementation and training; those rights and expenditures are not simply additional annual dividends on the capital transfer. Funds were managed through Inuit institutions and trusts for the benefit of eligible Inuit, not distributed uniformly as individual cash payments.

Students should distinguish carefully between the Inuit-owned lands and the territory as a whole. Most of Nunavut's land is Crown land, administered under Canadian law and, in practice, through institutions of shared management created under the agreement. The agreement thus created layers of rights: ownership on Inuit lands, protected harvesting and participation rights on Crown lands, and public governmental authority over the territory. Unlike the Alaska settlement discussed in Chapter 115, the agreement did not make corporations the primary vehicle for holding land title and was accompanied by a public territorial government, though Inuit-owned land and development corporations also play an important role.

The land entitlement should be read geographically, not just numerically. The acreage selected had to include resources and sites valuable to Inuit communities, and specific parcels were identified through negotiated maps and schedules rather than handed over as one continuous block. Surface rights gave Inuit organizations an ownership stake and important choices about land access; ownership of subsurface minerals on a smaller portion created additional opportunities and responsibilities in relation to mining. Environmental regulation, water rights, and public interests could still apply. An owner of surface lands and a government regulator might therefore be different institutions with different legal functions.

The larger Nunavut Settlement Area is not identical in every respect to the later public territory. The agreement defines its own geographic reach, including marine spaces in which harvesting and resource-management rights matter; the Nunavut Act defines the public jurisdiction and its boundary. Treating both as the same shape would hide significant differences between treaty beneficiaries, Crown lands, offshore arrangements, and territorial residents. Nor does the financial transfer tell the whole story. Resource royalties, administration of Inuit-owned lands, funds for training, and public boards were separate mechanisms intended to make the settlement durable across generations.

Section 116.14 · Division IV

Wildlife Harvesting Rights and Subsistence Protection

Among the most important achievements of the agreement were its provisions protecting Inuit harvesting of wildlife. Caribou, seal, fish, whales, walrus, birds, and other resources are central to household nutrition, to the transmission of skills, to sharing networks, and to cultural identity. The agreement recognized a right of Inuit to harvest wildlife across the settlement area, subject to conservation requirements, and established that Inuit harvesting has priority after conservation needs are met.

To manage wildlife, the agreement created the Nunavut Wildlife Management Board, a public body with representation from Inuit organizations and from government. The board makes decisions and recommendations about wildlife management, including harvesting limits and conservation measures, and works with government departments and with community hunters and trappers organizations. This arrangement is often described as co-management: authority over wildlife is shared among Inuit organizations, the board, and governments. The system seeks to combine scientific research, Inuit knowledge, and regulatory responsibility.

The interaction of these elements has not always been smooth. Inuit knowledge of animal behaviour, ice, and weather can differ from the conclusions of scientific surveys, and disputes over quotas and population assessments have arisen. Nevertheless, the principle that Inuit participate in management and that harvesting rights are legally protected was a major departure from earlier arrangements, in which regulations were imposed by officials with limited Inuit involvement. Chapter 135 examines later developments in wildlife and treaty stewardship.

Wildlife rights require particular care because the agreement did not simply grant an unlimited right to take any species at any time. Conservation needs remained important, and the treaty provided institutions and procedures through which total allowable harvest and allocations could be considered. The Nunavut Wildlife Management Board brought Inuit-nominated and government-appointed participants into a structured process, while ministers retained specified roles in reviewing decisions. This design recognized Inuit participation as a legally protected component of management rather than treating it as an informal consultation offered at the discretion of an outside regulator.

Community hunters and trappers organizations supplied knowledge about local animal populations and the practical consequences of a management decision. A scientific survey might estimate population levels over a broad area, whereas hunters might emphasize seasonal movements, ice conditions, or recent observations near a community. These forms of evidence could reinforce or contradict one another; no serious treatment should assume that either is automatically infallible. For families, the consequences were immediate: access to country food affected household budgets, customary sharing, and the transmission of land-based skills. The settlement made participation and harvesting rights more secure, yet required continuing negotiation over conservation and administrative authority.

Section 116.15 · Division IV

Land-Use Planning, Environmental Assessment, and Shared Management

The agreement created a set of institutions to plan and review development. The Nunavut Planning Commission is responsible for preparing land-use plans that guide where and under what conditions development may occur. The Nunavut Impact Review Board screens and reviews proposed projects and assesses their potential effects on the environment and on communities. The Nunavut Water Board regulates the use of water and the deposit of waste. Each board includes members nominated by Inuit organizations and by governments, so that Inuit participation is built into the decision-making structure.

These institutions address a long-standing concern: that development of mines, transportation, and infrastructure could affect lands and animals on which Inuit depend without adequate Inuit voice. The agreement also provides for negotiated impact and benefit arrangements between Inuit organizations and developers for significant projects on Inuit lands, covering matters such as employment, training, and compensation. Mining, in particular, has been a focus: it can create employment and revenue while also raising risks for caribou, water, and harvesting.

The central analytical point is the difference between legally protected participation and unrestricted control. On Inuit-owned lands, Inuit hold ownership and have greater authority over access and development, especially where they own subsurface rights. On Crown lands, which cover most of the territory, Inuit participate through the boards but do not hold a veto in the way landowners do. Final decision-making authority often rests with governments, which may accept, modify, or reject board recommendations. This arrangement gave Inuit a significant institutional role while leaving authority over Crown resources shared and, in some respects, constrained.

The public institutions established under the agreement operated at different stages of decision-making. Land-use planning sought to set a broad framework for proposed uses of land; an impact review assessed the likely environmental and social consequences of particular projects; water regulation addressed water use and waste. None of these functions was interchangeable, and none could be reduced to an automatic approval or an automatic prohibition. A major project might pass through more than one process, creating opportunities for evidence from communities to shape its conditions before a final government authorization.

The boards reflected a negotiated compromise over authority. Inuit participation was built into their composition and procedures, but ministerial review and federal or territorial statutes continued to matter. Some development proponents viewed review as a source of delay and uncertainty, while hunters and community organizations could regard careful scrutiny as necessary protection of land, water, and animal movements. The most illuminating historical question is therefore not whether Inuit obtained an absolute veto across Nunavut: they did not. It is how the 1993 agreement altered the distribution of information, participation, and institutional accountability compared with earlier decisions made largely by distant officials.

Section 116.16 · Division IV

Nunavut Tunngavik Incorporated and the Continuing Treaty Relationship

The Tungavik Federation of Nunavut was succeeded, after the agreement came into force, by Nunavut Tunngavik Incorporated, the organization responsible for representing Inuit beneficiaries under the agreement. It works with three regional Inuit associations, one for each region, and with designated Inuit organizations that manage land and other responsibilities. Its functions include monitoring implementation, advocating for the fulfilment of obligations, managing certain settlement assets and funds, and participating in the appointment of members to the shared management boards.

The organization is distinct from the Government of Nunavut. Nunavut Tunngavik represents Inuit as a collective of beneficiaries of a treaty; the territorial government serves all residents of the territory, whether Inuit or not, through public institutions. The two interact constantly, for example when the territorial government implements policies that affect harvesting or education and when Inuit organizations assess whether government actions meet treaty obligations. They can cooperate on shared goals and disagree about priorities or responsibilities, and their relationship is a continuing feature of Nunavut's politics.

The agreement also addressed employment and contracting. Article 23 sets out an objective of a representative level of Inuit employment in government, reflecting the Inuit share of the population, and requires planning and training to achieve it. Article 24 addresses government contracting and encourages Inuit businesses to participate. These provisions respond to a long history in which Inuit were largely excluded from administrative and professional roles. They also reveal a central tension: a public government must serve all residents, while a treaty creates obligations specific to Inuit, and implementation has required ongoing negotiation and, at times, litigation.

Article 23's employment objective rested on the recognition that public institutions could not reflect Inuit priorities solely because Inuit formed a majority of residents. If skilled and managerial positions continued to be occupied mainly by people recruited from elsewhere, the new government would face limits in language capacity, local knowledge, and long-term accountability. The treaty therefore linked representativeness to planning, training, and monitoring rather than assuming that a new legislative boundary would produce an Inuit-majority civil service automatically. Article 24 similarly addressed government procurement, recognizing that contracts for construction, transport, and services could either reinforce or weaken local economic capacity.

These obligations also illustrate the complex relationship among the signatories and later governments. The federal Crown concluded the treaty with Inuit; the Government of Nunavut later inherited significant public-service responsibilities even though it was not a separate Inuit treaty organization. Nunavut Tunngavik Incorporated could monitor whether obligations were carried out and press governments to change practices, but it did not exercise the authority of a territorial legislature. This was an institutional strength, because Inuit rights were not contingent on the composition of a particular assembly, and a source of friction, because implementation could depend on cooperation among bodies with different constituencies and budgets.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Harvesting, and the Politics of Daily Life

The negotiations described in this chapter were conducted by leaders, lawyers, and officials, but their meaning was felt in households. For many families, daily life combined wage work, government services, store-bought goods, and the harvest of country food. Sharing networks, in which hunters distributed meat and fish among kin and neighbours, continued to link households, and skills such as sewing skins, preparing food, and navigating ice were transmitted across generations. Hunting required equipment, fuel, and time, and the cost of living in remote communities was high.

The land-claims process itself became part of community life. Meetings were held in community halls, often with interpretation, and radio and television in Inuktitut, including programming developed by Inuit broadcasters from the early 1980s, helped carry information across distances. Elders, hunters, women, and young people had different concerns, ranging from caribou and housing to schooling and employment. Ratification involved ordinary beneficiaries voting on a complex document, and the preparations for a new government raised hopes about jobs, services, and the use of Inuit languages in public life. At the same time, many residents faced practical problems, such as overcrowded housing and limited access to services, that no legal agreement alone could resolve.

Section 116.17 · Division V — The Creation of Nunavut and Its Historical Consequences

The Nunavut Act and the Design of a Public Territorial Government

The Nunavut Act, enacted by the federal Parliament in 1993, provided the legal basis for the new territory. It specified the territory's boundaries, established a legislature, an executive council, and a court system, and provided for a commissioner appointed by the federal government. Its powers were modelled on those of the existing territorial governments, delegated by Parliament, and its structure followed the patterns of Canadian territorial government with adaptations for Nunavut's circumstances.

Nunavut was designed as a public government serving all residents. Eligibility to vote and to hold office did not depend on Indigenous identity, and the legislature represents residents of electoral districts across the territory. This feature reflects the view that a territory with a large Inuit majority could ensure Inuit influence through democratic means, while also respecting the rights of non-Inuit residents. It also means that the Government of Nunavut is not an exclusively Inuit government and is not a sovereign state: it exercises powers delegated by federal statute, and the federal government retained significant responsibilities, including, at the outset, control over public lands and resources.

Inuit leaders regarded the arrangement as a meaningful achievement despite these limits. Control of a public government in which Inuit are the majority gave Inuit communities a decisive voice in education, health, housing, language policy, and many other areas, and allowed the government to reflect Inuit priorities and languages. The territory's finances, however, depend heavily on federal transfers, and its legislative authority remains within the Canadian constitutional framework. Evaluating Nunavut therefore requires holding together real gains in representation and persistent constraints.

Students must distinguish three legal layers operating side by side. First, the Nunavut Land Claims Agreement creates constitutionally protected collective rights and institutions. Second, the political accord of 1992 expresses commitments to the separate territory. Third, the Nunavut Act establishes the public government under federal law. Article 4.1.3 of the agreement explicitly states that the political accord and related territorial legislation were not intended to be treaty rights under section 35. This does not diminish the political significance of the new government; it prevents the false inference that every territorial power acquired the same constitutional status as an Inuit land-claims right.

The act's staggered commencement was also part of institutional design. An entire government could not be launched with one parliamentary vote. Parts of the statute entered into force to enable preparation and transitional arrangements, including Part III on December 10, 1993. Under section 79, the central establishment provisions would operate on April 1, 1999 unless brought forward. During the intervening years, officers, legal systems, employees, and legislative procedures had to be arranged in advance. Confusing those dates with the July 9, 1993 treaty effective date obscures how substantial the six-year transition actually was.

Section 116.18 · Division V

Preparing for Nunavut: Institutions, Capital, and Administrative Capacity

Between 1993 and 1999 the new government had to be designed and staffed. The Nunavut Implementation Commission, chaired by John Amagoalik, advised on the structure of government, the location of offices, training needs, and the transfer of programs from the Northwest Territories. Its recommendations emphasized a decentralized government, with offices distributed among communities rather than concentrated in a single capital, in order to spread employment and keep government close to people.

The choice of capital was contested. In a plebiscite in December 1995, residents voted between Iqaluit, on Baffin Island, and Rankin Inlet, in the Kivalliq region. Iqaluit, which already had considerable infrastructure and a larger population, won with roughly 60 percent of the vote. The decision reflected regional rivalries and the practical advantages of an established centre, but it also raised concerns in other regions about distance from the capital. The commission's emphasis on distributing government functions was in part a response to these concerns.

The practical challenges were significant. Few residents had formal training in public administration, and building a bilingual or multilingual civil service required education and recruitment. Housing and office space in small communities were limited, communications and transportation were expensive, and the government had to be established across vast distances. Training programs, partnerships with colleges, and recruitment from outside the territory all formed part of the effort. Questions of language were central: the new government was expected to use Inuktitut and Inuinnaqtun alongside English and French, a matter that connects to Chapter 123 on language revitalization.

Implementation required choices about where offices, schools, health services, and public employment would be located. A single capital could concentrate expertise and infrastructure but risk reproducing the remoteness that Inuit had criticized in government from Yellowknife. Decentralizing government offices offered a way to distribute payrolls and authority, though it increased the costs of travel, communications, training, and supervision. The Nunavut Implementation Commission's recommendations therefore involved a real trade-off between regional fairness and administrative efficiency, not simply a symbolic commitment to dispersal.

Preparing a public service also meant identifying which laws and services could continue unchanged during the transition. The new territory could not afford an administrative vacuum while it recruited staff, equipped offices, or organized its legislature. Recruiting Inuit employees at different occupational levels posed a long-term challenge given gaps in access to higher education and professional credentials. Bilingual and multilingual expectations required more than translated signs: they affected legal interpretation, public documents, training, and residents' ability to obtain services in their own language. The transition was thus simultaneously a constitutional project, an employment project, and a social-development project.

Section 116.19 · Division V

April 1, 1999: The Establishment of Nunavut

On February 15, 1999, voters in the new territory elected the first Legislative Assembly. Candidates ran as individuals rather than as members of political parties, in keeping with the consensus-style government used in the Northwest Territories. After the election, members of the assembly chose the territory's first premier, Paul Okalik, a young Inuk lawyer, and selected a cabinet. The legislature therefore began its work without party discipline, and the premier and ministers were accountable to the assembly as a whole.

On April 1, 1999, Nunavut was formally established, and the Northwest Territories was reduced to its western portion. Celebrations took place in Iqaluit and in communities across the territory, and Inuit and other residents marked the occasion as a historic moment. The event drew national and international attention, and for many Inuit it represented the realization of a goal pursued for a generation. The transfer of administration also had practical effects on relationships among the new territorial government, Inuit communities, the remaining Northwest Territories, and Ottawa.

Establishment, however, did not resolve the territory's challenges. It faced a shortage of housing, high living costs, limited infrastructure, health and education needs, and a public service still under construction. Revenues depended largely on federal transfers. The creation of the territory gave Inuit a new institutional framework for addressing these problems, but not the resources or the immediate capacity to solve them. Students should therefore treat April 1, 1999 as a beginning of a new period of governance rather than as a conclusion of the story.

The first election brought a significant test of the public-government concept. Residents elected representatives for geographic constituencies rather than selecting a parliament of treaty beneficiaries. The assembly used the consensus model inherited from the Northwest Territories, in which candidates stood without territorial political-party labels and elected members selected the premier and cabinet. That made the government's source of authority different from Nunavut Tunngavik Incorporated, whose mandate came from Inuit beneficiaries under the treaty. The first territorial cabinet had to serve all residents while operating in an Inuit-majority society whose political movement had made the new territory possible.

Establishment also redistributed governing responsibilities rather than automatically conferring provincial status. The new territory took over extensive public programs, but federal legislation and transfers continued to structure its finances. Inuit could now seek change through both treaty institutions and a legislature located in their homeland, yet that dual structure required coordination when government spending, employment, wildlife management, and land development intersected. April 1, 1999 was therefore the culmination of one phase—achieving a territory—and the beginning of another: demonstrating whether the institutions could deliver improved public services, protect rights, and maintain accountability across dispersed communities.

Section 116.20 · Division V

The Historical Legacy of Nunavut: Land, Government, and Self-Determination

The achievements of the process were substantial. Inuit secured recognition of a collective settlement area, constitutionally protected treaty rights, ownership of a significant area of land with mineral rights on part of it, protected harvesting and participation in wildlife and environmental management, financial resources and institutions to hold them, and a territorial government in which Inuit are the majority. The movement built durable Inuit organizations and demonstrated that sustained organizing and documentation could change the political map of Canada.

Tensions also emerged. The territory's finances depend on federal transfers, and its government faces demands that exceed its resources. Housing shortages, educational outcomes, health problems, and employment remain pressing, and the goal of representative Inuit employment in government has proved difficult to meet. Debates continue about language use in public services and schools, about the balance between a public government and Inuit-specific rights, and about the implementation of the agreement, which has led Inuit organizations to dispute the federal government's performance and, in some instances, to pursue legal action. Questions of jurisdiction over lands and resources have also been the subject of later negotiations. Chapter 139 takes up the longer history of Nunavut's governments.

Nunavut established a distinctive institutional model in Canada, one that combines treaty rights with a public territory, but it left Inuit self-determination as an evolving project. The model differs from the Alaska settlement discussed in Chapter 115, and from Inuit arrangements in Nunavik, the Inuvialuit region, and Labrador. A fair assessment recognizes both the extent of Inuit political achievement and the continuing limits on authority, resources, and implementation.

Nunavut is sometimes celebrated as an instance of the territorial map being redrawn in response to Indigenous political claims. That description is justified, but incomplete. The achievement had two legally different foundations: a modern treaty secured Inuit collective entitlements and institutions, while public legislation established a jurisdiction governed by all residents. The combination allowed Inuit influence both as rights-holders and as an electoral majority. It also created recurring questions about which institution should act when a policy touched both a collective right and a public service.

The comparison with Alaska is instructive because each settlement was an outcome of long political mobilization but took a different form. The Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act relied heavily on corporate ownership and extinguishment arrangements; Nunavut's modern treaty combined Inuit-owned lands, regulated harvesting, public co-management, and a separately established territory. Neither is a universal model. Both demanded continuing work to turn formal recognition into economic security, environmental protection, and institutions capable of responding to communities. The historical significance of Nunavut therefore lies not in closing the question of Inuit sovereignty or self-determination, but in expanding the practical instruments through which Inuit could pursue it.

Historical Debate

Did Nunavut Establish Inuit Self-Government?

The question: Scholars and commentators disagree about whether Nunavut should be understood as Inuit self-government or as a public territorial institution whose authority remains constrained by Canadian federalism. The debate turns partly on definitions and partly on how to weigh collective rights, democratic majorities, and actual governmental performance.

Interpretation A — Nunavut as a landmark of Inuit self-determination: Some interpreters emphasize that Inuit organized, negotiated, and won a territory in which they are a large majority, along with constitutionally protected treaty rights and shared management institutions. On this view, democratic control of a public government by an Indigenous majority is an effective and legitimate form of self-government, and the combination of land rights and territorial authority is a significant innovation.
Interpretation B — Nunavut as a public territory with limited authority: Others stress that the Government of Nunavut is a public government whose powers are delegated by federal statute, that it serves all residents, that it depends heavily on federal financing, and that early control over public lands and resources remained federal. On this view, the territory did not establish Indigenous self-government in the sense of an Indigenous-controlled jurisdiction, and Inuit-specific rights are held through the treaty organizations, not through the government.

Evidence: Both views draw on the text of the 1993 agreement and statute, on the composition of the electorate and legislature, on financing arrangements, on records of implementation, and on statements by Inuit leaders about their aims.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns terminology, since the meaning of self-government is contested, and interpretation, since scholars weigh democratic majority, legal authority, and performance differently. It does not primarily concern the basic facts of the institutional design.

Why it matters: How the question is answered affects how Nunavut is compared with other Indigenous arrangements, including those in Alaska and elsewhere, and how its achievements and shortcomings are evaluated.

Student task: Distinguish three things: Inuit collective rights under the treaty, the public authority of the territorial government, and the government's performance. Then evaluate which interpretation is more persuasive for each, and identify evidence that could clarify the problem.

Cause and Effect

Why Nunavut Was Established

Long-term causes

Continuous Inuit occupation and use of the Arctic; the absence of treaties of cession; postwar expansion of federal administration and settlement; and distance of decision-making from Inuit communities.

Short-term causes

The 1973 Calder decision and claims policy; the founding of Inuit Tapirisat; the land use and occupancy research; and the 1976 proposal.

Immediate context

The 1982 plebiscite, the formation of the Tungavik Federation, the boundary negotiations of 1987 to 1992, and the 1992 Political Accord.

Event or transformation

The 1993 agreement and Nunavut Act, followed by institutional preparation and the creation of the territory on April 1, 1999.

Immediate effects

Constitutionally protected treaty rights, Inuit-owned lands, co-management boards, and a new public government with an Inuit-majority electorate.

Long-term consequences

A distinctive Canadian institutional model, continuing disputes over implementation and funding, and an ongoing project of Inuit self-determination.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains Nunavut. It required Inuit organization, evidence, legal opportunity, favourable demographics, political persistence, and compromise by governments. It was not inevitable, and alternative paths were pursued elsewhere.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Inuit Authority Before and After 1999

What Changed

  • Authority moved from customary and kin-based leadership, and later from administration by non-Inuit officials, to a combination of treaty-based rights and a public government with an Inuit-majority electorate.
  • Inuit rights over land and wildlife became defined, written, and constitutionally protected.
  • Institutions such as co-management boards and Inuit organizations became formal bodies with legal standing.

What Stayed the Same

  • Harvesting, sharing, and knowledge of land and ice continued to be central to community life.
  • Inuit continued to assert a collective claim to the Arctic homeland and a voice in its governance.
  • Canadian federal authority and financing remained a defining framework.

When and for whom: Change was gradual and uneven, accelerating between 1971 and 1993 and again after 1999. Inuit leaders and beneficiaries experienced the shift most directly, while many families in small communities experienced greater continuity in daily life. The pattern matters because it shows that institutional change did not erase older practices, and that new institutions had to coexist with them.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how Inuit in the central and eastern Canadian Arctic organized to secure recognition of their rights, negotiated a landmark settlement, and obtained a new territory. It began with the Inuit homeland and with the expansion of federal administration in the twentieth century, which altered family life and territorial use and created the conditions for political mobilization.

The legal context was shaped by the absence of treaties of cession in the Arctic and by the 1973 federal decision to negotiate comprehensive claims following the Calder case. Inuit Tapirisat of Canada, founded in 1971, used this opening to launch a claim built on documented occupation and use.

The Inuit Land Use and Occupancy Project demonstrated how Inuit knowledge could be translated into evidence, producing maps and reports in 1976 that challenged assumptions about Arctic land use. Its significance lay in the way it allowed Inuit to present occupation as extensive and continuous.

The 1976 Nunavut proposal linked a land settlement to a new territory. The federal government separated the two, but Inuit leaders kept the territorial idea alive, demonstrating persistence and agency. Parallel agreements in Nunavik and the western Arctic showed that Inuit pursued different strategies in different regions.

The Tungavik Federation of Nunavut, formed in 1982, built a negotiating organization across dispersed communities. The 1982 plebiscite showed territory-wide majority support for division, though the boundary and related questions required years of negotiation, the 1987 Iqaluit Agreement, and a 1992 plebiscite. The boundary resulted from political compromise, not from a natural frontier.

Negotiations produced a signed agreement-in-principle at Igloolik on April 30, 1990, the Nunavut Political Accord in October 1992, an Inuit ratification vote in November 1992, and the signing of the final agreement in Iqaluit on May 25, 1993. Legislation passed in June brought the agreement into force on July 9, 1993, creating a modern treaty protected under Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982.

The agreement provided Inuit-owned lands, subsurface rights on a portion, and capital compensation of C$580 million valued in 1989 dollars (approximately C$1.148 billion with interest paid over fourteen years). It also established resource-royalty sharing, protected wildlife harvesting, co-management boards, and employment and contracting provisions. Nunavut Tunngavik Incorporated represents Inuit beneficiaries and is distinct from the public territorial government.

The Nunavut Act, which commenced in stages between 1993 and 1999, established a public government serving all residents, with powers delegated by federal statute. Preparation between 1993 and 1999, including the work of the Nunavut Implementation Commission and the 1995 choice of Iqaluit as capital, built the administrative foundations of the new government.

On April 1, 1999, after the February election and the selection of Paul Okalik as premier, Nunavut was established. The event was a historic achievement, but not an independent state and not a resolution of the territory's social and economic challenges.

The chapter's central argument is that Nunavut combined treaty rights, Inuit-owned lands, shared management, and a public territory, representing a major achievement of Inuit self-determination within the constraints of Canadian federalism. Debates about its meaning, and continuing issues of funding, implementation, and jurisdiction, show that Inuit self-determination remains an evolving historical project.

Constructing a Source Dossier on the Making of Nunavut

This project asks you to assemble and analyze a small dossier of primary and secondary sources that together explain how a land-claims settlement and a new territory came to be linked and then distinguished.

  1. Select three primary sources. Choose, for example, a section of the 1976 land use report, a provision of the 1993 agreement, and a provision of the Nunavut Act, or a documented plebiscite result.

Chapter Reflection

The creation of Nunavut matters because it shows how an Indigenous movement used documentation, negotiation, and sustained political organization to reshape both the legal and the geographical map of a country. Inuit did not receive a territory as a gift. They built it, over decades, from evidence of occupation, from organizations that held diverse communities together, and from a persistent claim that land and government had to be considered together.

The chapter also reveals how modern Indigenous settlements depend on distinctions that are easy to overlook. The 1993 agreement and the 1993 Act did different things; the treaty organizations and the territorial government have different constituencies; the Inuit-owned lands and the territory differ in size and meaning. Keeping these distinctions in view allows a more accurate assessment of what Nunavut achieved and what it left unfinished.

Nunavut's history connects to wider themes in this volume: the constitutional protection of treaty rights examined in Chapter 117, the comparative settlement model of Alaska in Chapter 115, the stewardship of wildlife in Chapter 135, the revival of Indigenous languages in Chapter 123, and the continuing development of Indigenous governments in Chapter 139. It also stands as an example of Indigenous self-determination pursued through negotiation and institution-building, rather than through separation from the state.

The story did not end on April 1, 1999. The territory's challenges, the continuing disputes over implementation, and the ongoing efforts of Inuit and their governments to shape their future show that self-determination is a process rather than a single achievement. Understanding how Nunavut came to exist is therefore a foundation for understanding what Inuit and their governments have done since.
