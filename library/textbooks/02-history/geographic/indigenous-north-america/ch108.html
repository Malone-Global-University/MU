The Beginnings of Red Power

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

In Oklahoma City in 1966, a small group of young Indigenous activists carried a banner reading Red Power into the parade of the National Congress of American Indians. The slogan was brief. The argument behind it, that Indigenous peoples were nations entitled to decide their own futures and not clients to be managed, had been taking shape for years.

A surviving photograph of the 1966 parade, preserved in the Stan Steiner Papers at Stanford University Libraries and exhibited online by the Albuquerque Museum, shows members of the National Indian Youth Council (NIYC) displaying a Red Power banner during the National Congress of American Indians (NCAI) annual convention in Oklahoma City. The museum's account identifies Clyde Warrior, Mel Thom, and Della Hopper Warrior among the participants. The image is valuable precisely because it is modest. It records neither a seizure of federal property nor a confrontation with police. It records a public declaration, made inside an Indigenous gathering, by younger activists who wanted a different tone of politics. Surviving accounts describe the NIYC contingent as acting on its own initiative; the museum describes a confrontational entry into the parade and disapproval from some established NCAI members; precisely who objected and how individual delegates responded would require additional contemporaneous records.

The scene opens this chapter because it shows that the movement later associated with the occupation of Alcatraz in 1969 was already publicly visible in the mid-1960s. It also introduces the chapter's central problem. The NCAI, founded in 1944, was the principal national institution of Indigenous advocacy, and Chapter 107 examines it, the NIYC, and related organizations in detail. The NIYC was formed in 1961 by younger people who admired the older organization's goals but wanted more assertive methods and a sharper political language. The parade episode captures that relationship: two Indigenous organizations, in the same city, in the same week, sharing many objectives and disagreeing about how those objectives should be pursued.

This chapter asks how Indigenous activists transformed longstanding struggles for sovereignty, cultural survival, and self-government into a more confrontational and nationally visible political movement during the 1960s. The answer has several parts. Federal termination policy, relocation to cities, persistent poverty, and jurisdictional conflict gave younger Indigenous people urgent grievances (Chapters 104 through 106). The Black freedom movement and the federal War on Poverty offered models, vocabularies, and, unevenly, resources. Indigenous thinkers, above all Clyde Warrior, articulated a critique of paternalism and a demand for community control. Fishing families in Washington State turned treaty rights into direct action. Indigenous educators in the Navajo Nation created schools and a college under tribal direction. Urban communities, women organizers, and university students built networks that the movement would draw on.

The chapter treats Red Power as a developing set of ideas and practices, not as a single organization with a membership roll and a program. Different activists emphasized treaty rights, national independence, economic opportunity, educational control, protection from police abuse, or cultural renewal. Some cooperated closely with existing tribal governments and national organizations. Others criticized them. Many Indigenous people who engaged in the activism of these years never used the slogan, and some rejected it. The chapter also resists a tidy opposition between conservative elders and radical youth. Elders taught, funded, and protected many young activists, and the generations frequently shared goals while differing on method.

Historians reconstruct these developments from a mixed body of evidence: photographs and museum collections; the testimony of activists before federal bodies; newsletters and periodicals produced within Indigenous communities; federal agency files and congressional records; contemporary newspaper coverage, which often misunderstood or sensationalized its subjects; statutes and court decisions; and, increasingly, oral histories and Indigenous-authored scholarship. Each carries limits. Newspaper reporters rarely understood the nations they described. Federal records reflect the priorities of the agencies that created them. Later memory, including the memory of participants, is shaped by the movement's later fame. The chapter distinguishes among these kinds of evidence throughout.

The chapter has deliberate boundaries. It ends at the close of 1968, when the foundations of Red Power were in place but its best-known national campaigns still lay ahead. The occupation of Alcatraz (Chapter 109), the history of the American Indian Movement (Chapter 110), the Trail of Broken Treaties (Chapter 111), Wounded Knee in 1973 (Chapter 112), and the full Pacific Northwest Fish Wars (Chapter 113) receive their own treatment. Here, the task is to explain why a new movement emerged, what it argued, how its methods evolved, and which networks and institutions made 1969 possible.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define Red Power as a developing set of ideas and political practices and explain why historians disagree about when it began.
  2. Trace continuities between earlier Indigenous treaty defense, reform, and anti-termination activism and the activism of the 1960s.
  3. Explain how termination, relocation, poverty, and jurisdictional conflict created conditions for a new political generation.
  4. Analyze how education, veteran experience, urban life, and intertribal contact shaped the outlook of younger Indigenous activists.
  5. Distinguish among sovereignty, self-determination, and Indigenous nationalism, and between political nationhood and minority status.
  6. Evaluate Clyde Warrior's critique of paternalism and his claim that freedom required community control over decisions.
  7. Interpret the 1966 NIYC parade photograph and the controversy surrounding the slogan Red Power as historical evidence.
  8. Describe how fish-ins, demonstrations, and publicity broadened the repertoire of Indigenous political action.
  9. Assess the War on Poverty and the Rough Rock and Navajo Community College experiments as tests of community control.
  10. Compare the roles of urban communities, women organizers, and student networks in building the movement.
  11. Analyze Indigenous participation in the Poor People's Campaign and the relationship between Red Power and Black Power.
  12. Assess the Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968 as an illustration of tension between individual rights and tribal political authority.

Historical Context

Students approaching this chapter should bring with them three earlier developments. The first is the long history of Indigenous nations asserting treaty rights and self-government against encroachment, discussed in Volumes VI through VIII. The second is the federal policy shift of the 1950s. Chapter 104 explains termination, the congressional effort to end recognized political relationships between the United States and particular tribal nations. Chapter 105 explains relocation, the federal program that encouraged movement to cities. Chapter 106 explains the struggles over fishing, hunting, land, and water rights that intensified as states and non-Indigenous interests pressed against treaty guarantees. This chapter summarizes those subjects only as needed.

The third development is institutional. Chapter 107 describes the NCAI, the NIYC, the Alaska Federation of Natives, and Canadian organizations. Those chapters narrate how organizations were built. This chapter concentrates on what changed in political ideas, methods, and social foundations: how a younger generation came to describe the conditions of Indigenous life as a political problem of dispossession and dependency, and how it experimented with new forms of action and new institutions of self-governance.

The wider environment mattered. The 1960s saw the civil-rights movement, student organizing, and anticolonial movements abroad. The federal War on Poverty, launched in 1964, created programs that reached some reservations and urban Indigenous communities for the first time. These influences did not create Indigenous political traditions, which were centuries old, but they provided occasions, tactics, funding, and vocabulary. The chapter's task is to show how Indigenous activists selected from, adapted, and sometimes rejected what the wider environment offered.

One terminological note is needed. Throughout this period, participants commonly used "Indian" in speech and organizational names, and the chapter follows period usage when quoting or naming organizations. The chapter otherwise prefers the names of specific nations, and uses "Indigenous" for the broader category. The chapter also uses "continental United States" when distinguishing the mainland states from Alaska, where Native peoples faced a distinct legal situation (Chapter 99 and Chapter 115).

Section 108.1

Defining Red Power and Its Historical Origins

Red Power is best understood as a cluster of ideas and political practices that crystallized in the 1960s, not as a single organization. It combined four elements: a claim that Indigenous peoples were political nations with inherent rights, not merely racial or ethnic minorities; a demand for self-determination, meaning real authority over decisions affecting Indigenous communities; a willingness to employ public, often confrontational, direct action alongside lobbying and litigation; and an affirmation of Indigenous cultures and histories as sources of political legitimacy. No single group controlled the label. Activists with quite different priorities, from treaty fishing to urban policing to curriculum reform, are grouped under it by historians, and some of them would have rejected the grouping.

The phrase itself echoes the contemporary slogan Black Power, which entered national discussion in 1966, and the parallel was deliberate in some uses. But the slogan was not a simple borrowing. Indigenous activists emphasized collective political status grounded in treaties and inherent sovereignty, a framework that differed from a claim to racial solidarity or inclusion within the United States. Section 108.7 returns to the slogan's circulation and the debates over it.

Dating the movement is a genuine historical problem. Some narratives begin in 1969 with the occupation of Alcatraz Island, the first demonstration to command sustained national attention (Chapter 109). That date marks the movement's arrival in national media, not its beginning. Others begin in 1961 with the founding of the NIYC, or in 1964 with the fish-ins, or in 1966 when the slogan appeared in public. Still others trace the movement to Indigenous resistance in the 1940s and 1950s, particularly the campaigns against termination. Each dating emphasizes a different aspect: organization, method, language, or political culture. This chapter adopts the view that Red Power had a prehistory in earlier Indigenous activism, an emergence in the early to mid-1960s, and a threshold moment at the end of 1968, while recognizing that the movement's later public identity was formed in the national campaigns that followed.

The relationship among key concepts requires care. Sovereignty refers to the inherent political authority of Indigenous nations, which predates and persists alongside the United States. Self-determination refers to the exercise of that authority in practice, through control of governments, programs, schools, lands, and resources. Indigenous nationalism refers to political identification with one's own nation and, in some usages, with a broader Indigenous solidarity. Direct action refers to public, nonroutine political action such as demonstrations, deliberate violation of regulations to assert a right, and occupations. These ideas existed separately before the 1960s. What was new was their combination in a generation that regarded petition and negotiation alone as insufficient.

Finally, historians must note who was and was not part of the movement as described. Red Power developed unevenly across regions and communities, and the most visible participants were often those in urban areas, universities, and places with access to media. Many reservation residents pursued sovereignty through their own tribal governments, councils, and legal strategies, with less public visibility. The chapter attends to both.

A study of the term must begin with the distinction between the history of a movement and the history of its name. The National Indian Youth Council existed before the best-known photographed use of Red Power, and Nisqually and Puyallup families defended fishing places before youth organizations recruited outside supporters. Meanwhile, Indigenous communities formed new educational and social institutions without necessarily regarding them as components of a movement. To identify all these efforts retrospectively as Red Power is useful for comparing political change, but it can make distinct choices look like coordinated decisions. The chapter therefore uses the expression as a historical interpretation and identifies particular organizations, nations, and actors whenever the evidence permits.

The scale on which power was contested also varied. A parent demanding a say in school instruction, a fishing family setting a net at a treaty-protected site, and an urban organizer challenging police practices were not performing interchangeable acts. Each disputed an authority that affected daily life and asked who should make the governing decision. Their convergence lies in that common problem of power, not in a shared national command structure. Students should assess a claimed turning point by asking what changed in ideas, methods, public visibility, organizational cooperation, and actual control, rather than assuming that publicity alone proved transformation.

Historical Vocabulary
Red Power

Definition. A slogan and, by extension, a broad political current of the 1960s and 1970s expressing Indigenous nationalism, demands for sovereignty and self-determination, and support for direct action and cultural affirmation.

Historical context. The expression appeared in public in the mid-1960s, with documented use in NIYC circles in 1966, and its circulation widened after 1968. It echoed Black Power while articulating distinct claims based on treaties and nationhood.

Why it matters. The term gave activists a shared language across nations and regions, and gave outside observers a label for the movement. Both effects were double-edged: the language could unify, but it could also obscure particular nations' histories and priorities.

Potential misunderstanding. Red Power was never one organization, and it did not mean the same thing to all who used it. The term also does not describe all Indigenous activism of the period.

Section 108.2

From Earlier Indigenous Resistance to Postwar Activism

Indigenous nations had defended their treaties, lands, and governments long before the 1960s. Volumes VI and VII describe armed and diplomatic resistance. In the early twentieth century, Indigenous intellectuals and reformers created national organizations, among them the Society of American Indians founded in 1911, and campaigned over citizenship, land, and the treatment of Indigenous children (Chapters 92 and 95). In the 1930s, many nations engaged the Indian Reorganization Act, adopting, amending, or rejecting its provisions, and debating among themselves the meaning of tribal government (Chapter 97). Indigenous soldiers and workers in the Second World War returned to communities that still faced restricted rights and poor services (Chapters 101 and 103).

The NCAI, founded in 1944, institutionalized this heritage. Its member tribal governments, lawyers, and staff opposed termination in the 1950s through lobbying, testimony, and the mobilization of tribal councils. Their work had measurable effects: the pace of termination slowed, and the policy lost credibility over the following decade. The campaign also taught a generation of Indigenous leaders how federal legislative and administrative systems worked, and it established the principle that tribal nations spoke for themselves in national forums. This is a point of continuity that the activists of the 1960s generally acknowledged, even when they criticized the caution of older leaders.

The 1961 American Indian Chicago Conference, convened at the University of Chicago with participants from many nations, produced a Declaration of Indian Purpose that emphasized continued Indigenous existence as distinct peoples, the importance of consultation, and the need for choice in decisions about change. The conference demonstrated the possibility of broad consensus. It also revealed generational and strategic differences. Many of the younger participants, as scholars commonly argue, concluded that consultation without power would produce little, and some of them formed the NIYC soon afterward. Chapter 107 follows these organizational developments.

What did earlier generations contribute? They preserved legal claims grounded in treaties and the trust relationship. They built networks among tribal governments. They created habits of intertribal cooperation, often through the NCAI, and they gave the younger generation experience in testifying, drafting, and negotiating. They also maintained languages, ceremonies, and community institutions through decades of pressure, a cultural continuity that the 1960s activists drew upon directly. In many communities, elders were the teachers, advisers, and critics of young activists.

What changed in the 1960s? The target audience widened beyond federal officials to include the national public and the media. The vocabulary shifted from appeals for fair treatment to claims of rights and of national self-governance. The methods expanded to include public demonstration and deliberate civil disobedience. And the social basis broadened, as younger people with college educations, urban experience, and exposure to other freedom movements joined older leaders. The change was thus a transformation of means and tone within continuing goals, not an abrupt break.

For many families, political knowledge was carried less through a national convention than through remembered dispossession, resistance to removal, and disagreements about how to manage reservation affairs. When a young organizer invoked a treaty, an elder might know where a disputed boundary lay, how a river had been used, or which promises had been repeated in negotiations. Such knowledge made later protest intelligible. It was not simply folklore added to legal claims: it provided practical information about places and a continuing account of obligations that official records often compressed into maps and administrative categories.

The contrast between lobbying and direct action also needs qualification. Indigenous petitioners had long used varied strategies, including courtroom appeals, public gatherings, refusals to comply with restrictions, and organized economic resistance. What changed in the 1960s was the deliberate linking of local controversies with youth networks and mass-media opportunities. A fish-in might be planned for cameras and still concern a precise treaty clause; a convention might use conventional procedure while endorsing demands for far-reaching reform. Dividing the political world into moderate institutions and militant streets would conceal the exchange of information and assistance among them.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Anti-Termination Advocacy to Red Power Activism

What changed

  • Tactics broadened from lobbying and testimony to demonstrations, arrests, and publicity campaigns
  • Political language moved toward explicit claims of nationhood and self-determination
  • The age and social composition of activists shifted toward students, urban residents, and younger workers
  • Audiences expanded to include national media and other freedom movements

What stayed the same

  • The defense of treaties, land, and tribal governments remained central
  • Intertribal cooperation continued as a source of strength
  • Elders and community institutions remained anchors for cultural and political legitimacy
  • Opposition to termination and to externally imposed policy persisted

Why the pattern matters: The change was uneven and contested within communities, and it was gradual in some regions and abrupt in others. Those who experienced the shift most directly were younger and urban activists; many reservation communities experienced greater continuity.

Section 108.3

Termination, Relocation, Poverty, and the Conditions of Protest

In the 1950s and early 1960s, Indigenous communities faced a combination of pressures that made protest both necessary and difficult. Termination, as expressed in the 1953 congressional resolution and in the terminations of particular nations such as the Menominee and the Klamath, announced a federal intention to end the special political relationship with some tribal nations (Chapter 104). Public Law 280, also enacted in 1953, extended certain state criminal and civil jurisdiction over Indigenous lands in several states, often without tribal consent, creating confusion and conflict over policing and courts. Relocation encouraged individuals and families to move to urban areas for employment, though its promises of work and housing were often unmet (Chapter 105).

Material conditions on many reservations and in many urban neighborhoods were severe. Housing was frequently inadequate, health services were limited, and schools, including federal boarding schools, had long been instruments of assimilation and often provided poor education (Chapters 87 and 88). Unemployment was high where local economies were thin and where federal policy had limited Indigenous control over land and resources. Rural nations often lacked capital and credit because of trust restrictions, while many urban migrants faced discrimination in housing and employment. These conditions varied widely among nations and regions, so generalization requires care, but protest leaders consistently pointed to dependence and disempowerment as the underlying political problem.

Treaty rights faced direct challenge. State governments sought to regulate off-reservation fishing and hunting guaranteed by treaties, and federal projects such as large dams submerged Indigenous lands. The Seneca Nation's loss of territory to the Kinzua Dam in the 1960s, despite a treaty dating from the 1790s, is one example that Indigenous commentators across the country cited as evidence of how readily the United States set aside its promises (Chapter 106). These episodes were not only about specific lands or waters. They raised the question of whether Indigenous nations possessed enforceable legal and political standing.

Jurisdictional conflict compounded the difficulties. Where state authority extended over reservations, residents might face unfamiliar law enforcement and courts; where it did not, tribal courts often lacked resources. Indigenous people in cities encountered local police and municipal institutions with little accountability to them. Discriminatory treatment by officials, merchants, and employers added further pressure. The sense that Indigenous people were subject to authorities they had not chosen, and could not hold to account, became a central theme of the new activism.

Not all signals were negative. The Kennedy and Johnson administrations expressed interest in new approaches, and the War on Poverty opened some channels for local participation. In March 1968, President Lyndon Johnson sent Congress a special message on Indian affairs that rejected termination and endorsed a policy of self-determination without termination, and an advisory National Council on Indian Opportunity was created. Activists welcomed the shift in rhetoric while questioning whether implementation would give Indigenous communities actual power (Chapter 114). Protest thus arose not only from despair but also from heightened expectations and from the perception of an opening.

Policy changes did not fall equally on all Indigenous communities. Federal termination laws addressed selected nations rather than every reservation, while Public Law 280 produced differing jurisdictional arrangements in different states. Urban relocation programs reached only a portion of those who moved to cities, and independent migrants often relied more on kin or urban Indian centers than on federal caseworkers. Activists encountered this uneven landscape and made political arguments from specific local experiences. A campaign against state police enforcement could coexist with a tribal council's negotiations over federal school funding, without one grievance canceling the other.

Federal antipoverty programs complicated the politics further by creating opportunities to work within the very system that activists criticized. Community-action grants could fund staff, meeting places, and locally proposed projects. Yet administrative regulations, reporting requirements, and disputes over representation could shift decisive authority away from the people ostensibly being served. For young leaders the choice was rarely a simple yes or no to government money. It was whether resources could be placed under accountable Indigenous direction, with control over aims, personnel, and evaluation. This tension connects the background of the movement to Warrior's testimony and to the institutional experiments examined later in the chapter.

Cause and Effect

Why a New Protest Movement Emerged in the 1960s

Long-term causes

Centuries of dispossession, treaty violation, and externally imposed administration; a long record of Indigenous political organizing; the legacy of assimilation policy and boarding schools.

Short-term causes

Termination and relocation in the 1950s; jurisdictional conflict under Public Law 280; threats to treaty fishing and land; persistent poverty and discrimination.

Immediate context

The civil-rights movement, the War on Poverty, expanded access to higher education, and a growing intertribal and urban population.

Transformation

Younger activists adopted direct action, nationalist language, and community-control demands while building on older legal and organizational traditions.

Immediate effects

Fish-ins, new national organizations, experimental institutions, student networks, and new urban organizations.

Long-term consequences

Shifts in federal policy rhetoric and practice toward self-determination, the national campaigns of 1969–1973, and new institutions in education, law, and community services.

Historical complexity: No single factor produced the movement. Hardship alone did not guarantee protest; networks, leaders, models, and political opportunities had to exist, and they did so unevenly across the continent.

Section 108.4

Indigenous Youth and the Emergence of a New Political Generation

The generation that moved to the foreground in the 1960s had distinctive experiences. Many had attended boarding schools or public schools where Indigenous languages and histories were absent or denigrated. Others grew up in cities, as relocation or economic migration had taken their families there. Some were veterans of the Korean War era, and growing numbers attended college, often with scholarship support unavailable to earlier generations. Higher education exposed them to other social movements, to academic debates about race and colonialism, and to peers from many nations.

The founding of the NIYC in August 1961 at Gallup, New Mexico, brought together college students and young workers from several nations. Among the founders were Mel Thom, Clyde Warrior, Herb Blatchford, and Shirley Hill Witt. The organization defined itself as a vehicle for young Indigenous people to engage in political life, and its early programs emphasized education, leadership training, and advocacy. It maintained a core commitment to the sovereignty of nations and to the preservation of Indigenous cultures. Chapter 107 provides the institutional account.

For this chapter, the important point is how such young activists learned politics. Family history mattered: many had grandparents who had known allotment and boarding schools and who transmitted ceremonial and historical knowledge. Encounters with discrimination, whether in border-town businesses or in urban housing markets, politicized them. Intertribal friendships formed at universities and workshops produced shared analyses across nations. Travel to meetings, to Washington, and to other movements' events broadened their perspective. And the experience of seeing programs designed in distant offices fail to meet local needs convinced many that the missing ingredient was power.

The relationship between generations deserves care. The common picture of conservative elders and radical youth does not fit the evidence. Many older leaders in the NCAI and in tribal governments were themselves forceful critics of federal policy, and had spent years in difficult negotiations. Younger activists often relied on elders for moral and financial support, and some grounded their politics in the teachings of grandparents. Where friction occurred, as in the 1966 parade episode, it concerned strategy, tone, and urgency more than the goals of sovereignty and treaty rights. Some elders worried that confrontation would provoke retaliation, while some young activists believed restraint had delivered insufficient results.

Young Indigenous people were also divided among themselves. Some pursued careers in tribal government or the federal service, hoping to reform institutions from within. Others favored independent organizing, and still others focused on cultural revival. The NIYC itself contained diverse views. The movement's strength lay partly in this variety, which allowed multiple strategies to operate at once, though it also produced tensions that later sections describe.

The paths into political work differed sharply. Students in southwestern colleges could meet members of other nations at workshops and conferences. A young person in a coastal fishing family might first encounter politics through a state officer's warning or the seizure of gear. A relocated worker might experience the authority of employers, landlords, and city police more directly than a federal agency. These differences influenced the issues each person viewed as urgent. The growth of intertribal networks did not erase these origins; it created places where participants could compare them and find a language adequate to more than one local struggle.

Participation also involved unequal costs. Travel to a meeting demanded money, time away from work, and arrangements for children or elders. Students were sometimes more mobile than relatives supporting households, but could face disciplinary proceedings, tuition insecurity, or loss of scholarships. An activist's public confidence might depend on quieter labor by parents, partners, community centers, and friends. Examining these conditions helps explain both why youthful leadership became visible and why a history written only through celebrated speakers fails to reconstruct the full social organization of protest.

The intellectual foundations of the emerging movement.

Section 108.5

Sovereignty, Self-Determination, and Indigenous Nationalism

The most fundamental claim of the emerging movement was that Indigenous peoples were nations. This claim distinguished Red Power from civil-rights movements seeking inclusion for racial minorities within existing institutions. Indigenous activists did support equal treatment and an end to discrimination, but they insisted that their peoples possessed a distinct political status based on prior existence, treaties, and the continuing government-to-government relationship with the United States. A demand for integration, in this view, could not by itself address the central issues of land, self-government, and cultural survival.

Treaties provided the legal and moral basis of the claim. Hundreds of treaties between Indigenous nations and the United States had recognized nations as parties capable of making agreements, and many contained guarantees of land, services, and rights to resources. Activists argued that the United States' obligations under these instruments remained in force, and that violations were breaches of promises, not merely administrative errors. This argument connected the 1960s movement to the treaty-based politics described in Volumes VI and VII, and to Indigenous legal thought across centuries. Alaska Native claims, which rested on aboriginal use and occupancy and not on treaties, followed a parallel logic (Chapter 115).

Self-determination translated sovereignty into practice. For activists, it meant control over community decision-making, including how programs were designed, who staffed them, how budgets were spent, and what schools taught. It also meant control over resources: land, water, fisheries, minerals, and the revenues they might produce. Economic opportunity mattered, but the activists held that employment or services imposed from outside would not remedy dependency. They argued that the centuries-long pattern of external administration was itself the cause of poverty, and that a measure of authority had to precede development.

Indigenous nationalism took several forms. Some activists emphasized the sovereignty of their own nations, such as the Ponca, the Navajo, the Haudenosaunee, or the Puyallup, and framed demands in terms of those nations' treaties and histories. Others developed a broader intertribal identity, speaking of Indigenous peoples across the continent as sharing a common situation under colonial rule. These approaches were not mutually exclusive, and many individuals held both. Tensions did arise, since a pan-Indian framework could appear to flatten distinct national histories. Section 108.7 and the debate in Section 108.8 return to the question.

The governing intellectual distinction of this period is that self-determination required control over decisions and institutions, not simply access to programs designed and administered by outside authorities. This distinction explains why activists could criticize a federal program even when it brought money to their communities, and why they treated formal participation, such as advisory seats, as insufficient. It also explains why the era's institution-building efforts, discussed in Division III, were as central to the movement as its protests.

The emphasis on nationhood altered the policy question itself. When an official offered equal treatment as citizens, Indigenous leaders could answer that citizenship did not extinguish the obligations arising from treaties or the powers retained by tribal governments. A person might possess U.S. citizenship and remain a citizen or member of a tribal nation; the two relationships operated in different political registers. This did not make struggles against discrimination irrelevant. It meant that eliminating discrimination in a municipal office could not, on its own, resolve conflicts over reservation jurisdiction, treaty harvest, or the federal trust relationship. Activists criticized a framework that treated every claim as a request for a better individual benefit.

The language of self-determination was also susceptible to appropriation. Federal administrators could promise consultation while keeping authority to approve budgets, contracts, personnel, or curriculum. In such arrangements an advisory committee might express its preference without being able to implement it. Indigenous reformers therefore asked which institution had the last word and whether local people could change a program once it began. The distinction between participation and control is measurable: who appoints the director, controls records, sets priorities, and decides whether a project has succeeded? These questions apply both to community-action agencies and the schools discussed in Section 108.12.

Neither nationhood nor self-determination supplied an automatic formula for resolving disputes inside a community. Tribal councils, hereditary leaders, religious authorities, young activists, and individual residents could disagree over what counted as legitimate representation. A call for collective sovereignty did not eliminate arguments over civil liberty, gender, or the allocation of resources. The later debate about the Indian Civil Rights Act demonstrates that the movement's political language contained real tensions. Its historical importance lies not in delivering one settled answer but in compelling public debate over who should exercise power and under what accountability.

Historical Vocabulary
Self-Determination

Definition. The exercise by a people of authority over its own political, economic, social, and cultural life. In United States Indigenous policy, it later came to name a set of federal laws and administrative practices that shifted program control to tribal governments (Chapter 114).

Historical context. In the 1960s, activists used the term to demand real decision-making power, a meaning broader than the later statutory usage. Presidential statements in 1968 adopted similar language, but activists disputed whether practice matched rhetoric.

Why it matters. The idea links the Red Power movement to both nineteenth-century assertions of sovereignty and later legal reforms.

Potential misunderstanding. Self-determination is not identical to sovereignty: sovereignty is an inherent status, while self-determination describes how that status is exercised and recognized in practice. It also does not mean separatism in every case, since activists differed on relations with the United States and Canada.

Section 108.6

Clyde Warrior and the Intellectual Foundations of Red Power

Clyde Warrior (1939–1968) was among the most influential thinkers of the early movement. Raised in the Ponca community of Oklahoma, he was shaped by the Ponca ceremonial and social life and by his relationships with elders, and he often spoke of the community as the source of his political values. As a college student he cofounded the NIYC in 1961 and quickly became one of its most recognized speakers and writers. He died in July 1968 at the age of twenty-eight, and his short career left a body of speeches, essays, and testimony that scholars and activists have continued to study.

Warrior's central argument concerned paternalism. He held that federal Indian policy, and the programs administered under it, treated Indigenous people as wards who needed guidance and protection, and that such treatment prevented the development of competence, responsibility, and political power. In his analysis, poverty was inseparable from powerlessness: communities denied authority over their own affairs could not build the institutions or the habits of self-government on which prosperity depended. This framing differed from approaches that saw poverty mainly as a shortage of income or skills.

His writings also addressed Indigenous society itself. In a widely read essay of the mid-1960s, "Which One Are You? Five Types of Young Indians," he described several attitudes among young Indigenous people toward their communities and toward assimilation, criticizing those who sought acceptance by the dominant society at the expense of their own people. Warrior was not simply a critic of outsiders. He urged Indigenous communities to take responsibility for their own decisions, including the right to make mistakes, a theme running through his arguments for community control. Students should consult the primary texts for his exact language, as paraphrases can flatten his tone, which combined sharp humor with moral seriousness.

Warrior drew on tribal tradition. He argued that Indigenous communities had long possessed their own forms of consensus, leadership, and social obligation, and that these forms, not externally designed substitutes, should anchor political life. In this respect his thought belongs with Indigenous nationalism: it grounded political legitimacy in the community's own institutions and history. It also accounts for his impatience with programs that bypassed those institutions. He was, in the language of the time, a "hell-raiser," committed to public challenge, but his challenges rested on a constructive vision of what Indigenous communities could govern.

Warrior's influence was amplified through his collaborators. Mel Thom, Hank Adams, Shirley Hill Witt, Della Hopper Warrior, and others worked beside him in the NIYC, in the fish-ins, and in the Poor People's Campaign. His death in 1968 prevented him from participating in the better-known protests that followed, but later activists repeatedly cited his arguments. The conclusion that his ideas anticipated the national campaigns should be tempered: the later movement drew on many sources, and Warrior was one of a number of thinkers.

Warrior's criticism should be placed in the circumstances of the federal War on Poverty. The Office of Economic Opportunity advertised participation and local initiative as ways to address entrenched deprivation. Warrior challenged a definition of participation in which an outside institution could design the rules and then invite Indigenous people to choose among approved options. His argument did not reject improvements in housing, schools, or work. It asked why communities that had their own political institutions were treated as incapable of deciding how those improvements should be pursued. In that argument, poverty and political dependence were linked without being identical: a program might increase material resources while leaving control unchanged.

The form of his testimony matters as much as its message. Warrior spoke to a federal advisory commission, using a channel conventionally associated with expert recommendations and administrative reform. The act of giving evidence there shows that confrontational politics and institutional engagement were not mutually exclusive. A critical speech inside a hearing could expose contradictions in official policy more effectively than a simple rejection of government forums. Students should compare the audience for this testimony with the audience for the 1966 parade image. One asked officials to reconsider the meaning of their programs; the other used public symbolism to announce a political position.

His essay 'Which One Are You?: The Five Types of Young Indians' gives another angle on the problem of political identity. It criticized postures young people might adopt while navigating assimilation, pride, and pressure to conform. The essay is cited by the Oklahoma Historical Society as appearing in Stan Steiner's 1968 volume The New Indians; that publication date should not be confused with the date of every earlier version or with a claim that Warrior wrote after his death. Its rhetorical categories were deliberately provocative, not a survey establishing five objectively measurable kinds of Indigenous people. Reading them alongside his testimony reveals an effort to turn self-examination into collective responsibility.

Warrior's early death in July 1968 also shaped his historical reputation. Later activists remembered him as a leading voice at a moment when Red Power became more visible, and later publications reprinted and interpreted his writing. Historians should distinguish the claims he made in identifiable documents from the meanings assigned to him afterward. His significance lies in the sustained connection he drew between inherited community traditions and the practical question of governing institutions.

Section 108.7

The Language of Red Power and the Politics of Identity

The emergence of the slogan Red Power cannot be pinned to a single moment or speaker. The Albuquerque Museum's documentation attributes an early public use of the expression to Clyde Warrior in 1966, and the museum's photographic record of the NIYC banner at the NCAI parade supports a mid-1960s date for its appearance in public. Historians should be careful, however, not to turn this into a claim that Warrior invented the movement or its philosophy. The words circulated in conversation, in organizational meetings, and in journalism, and later authors, including Vine Deloria Jr., helped to popularize it. The slogan crystallized ideas that were already in circulation.

The 1966 parade is a revealing episode precisely because of its ambiguity. As noted in the chapter opening, the photograph shows NIYC members with a Red Power banner during the NCAI convention parade in Oklahoma City. Accounts agree that the intervention was a deliberate public statement by younger activists in a gathering of established leaders. What the surviving evidence does not fully establish is how the NCAI leadership received it, how many conventioneers approved, and what each participant intended. Interpretations that cast the episode as a simple rebellion against elders should be set aside in favor of attention to its function as political theater, a way of making a generational argument visible.

The slogan raised questions of identity. Pan-Indian identification, meaning a shared political identity among peoples of different nations, was not new, since intertribal organizations, boarding-school friendships, and urban communities had fostered it for decades. In the 1960s it acquired sharper political content. Activists spoke of a common struggle against colonial domination while recognizing that nations had distinct treaties, languages, and governments. The slogan's color-based terminology was drawn from the racial vocabulary of the day, and some Indigenous people objected to it because it seemed to define Indigenous peoples by race rather than by nationhood, which contradicted the movement's own premises.

Other critics objected on different grounds. Some tribal leaders feared that a confrontational label would alienate officials and allies. Others felt that a slogan promoted by young people in cities did not speak for reservation communities. Some Indigenous people supported the goals while preferring different language, such as sovereignty, treaty rights, or self-determination, which carried clearer legal meanings. The terminological controversy thus reflected substantive disagreement about whether the movement's primary frame should be race, nationhood, or shared experience.

For historians, the slogan's fate illustrates how political language works. A term can unify activists across regions while hiding real differences; it can mobilize public attention while provoking resistance; and it can outlast the specific circumstances of its coining. The chapter uses Red Power as an analytical label for the movement, with the qualification that participants described their work in many other terms, and that national names and community-specific vocabularies often mattered more to them.

The Oklahoma City image documents a carefully chosen setting. Rather than addressing only the state, NIYC members entered a public event associated with the country's best-known national Indigenous advocacy organization. The display could be understood as an argument directed toward fellow Indigenous leaders as well as toward photographers and outside spectators. The Albuquerque Museum's interpretive text describes the intervention as disruptive and notes criticism among some NCAI members. That account is valuable evidence about how the episode has been interpreted, but it should not be transformed into a claim that every convention delegate condemned the demonstrators. The archival photograph establishes who and what appeared before the camera; correspondence or convention records would be required to reconstruct the full response.

The symbolic emphasis on 'power' also changed the question asked of an audience. A petition for program reform might invite officials to weigh competing requests; a demand for power asked why the official alone could decide. This rhetorical shift resonated with other freedom movements, although individual Indigenous activists drew different lessons from Black Power. The distinction between an intertribal slogan and a nation's particular political status remained unresolved. One could endorse solidarity across tribes while fearing that a broad label obscured the treaties or governing authority of one's own people. That productive tension helps explain why the slogan mobilized some activists and remained uncomfortable for others.

Section 108.8

Cultural Affirmation, Historical Memory, and Intellectual Resistance

Cultural affirmation was an essential part of the movement, not an adjunct to it. Assimilation policy had targeted Indigenous languages, ceremonies, and histories for generations, and boarding schools had punished their use (Chapter 87). Activists therefore regarded the maintenance and revival of culture as a political act. A people able to speak its language, practice its ceremonies, and tell its own history was, in effect, resisting a policy designed to make it disappear. This conviction linked the political demands of the 1960s to the cultural continuity described in Chapter 88.

Publications were important vehicles. The NIYC produced a newsletter whose title, ABC: Americans Before Columbus, asserted prior presence and carried political analysis. The Tundra Times, founded in Alaska in 1962 by Howard Rock, became a forum for Alaska Native concerns. The American Indian Historical Society, established in San Francisco in 1964 by Rupert Costo and Jeannette Henry Costo, published The Indian Historian and challenged textbooks that portrayed Indigenous people through stereotype. Such periodicals gave communities a means of discussing politics in their own voices, outside the mainstream press.

Arts and literature contributed. The Institute of American Indian Arts, opened in Santa Fe in 1962, trained a generation of Indigenous artists and writers. N. Scott Momaday's novel House Made of Dawn, published in 1968, brought Indigenous literature to wide attention; it won the Pulitzer Prize the following year (Chapter 126). Dance, drumming, and powwow culture spread widely among Indigenous communities, and ceremonial revivals, such as renewed attention to the Sun Dance in some Plains communities, reinforced community identity. These developments were uneven and varied by nation, and their relationship to political activism differed from place to place.

Historical memory was also contested terrain. Activists criticized school curricula and popular media for portraying Indigenous people as vanished or as hostile obstacles to progress. They asserted alternative narratives rooted in oral tradition, treaty histories, and community experience. This effort anticipated the later interventions treated in Chapters 125 and 132, and it relied on the methods discussed in Chapter 1: respect for oral tradition, critical use of documents, and insistence that Indigenous peoples were historical actors. Language preservation, though still small-scale in the 1960s, was an element of the same project (Chapter 123).

The relationship between cultural continuity and resistance should not be romanticized. Cultural revival was selective and contested, and communities disagreed about which practices to share publicly, which to protect, and who had authority to decide. Some elders opposed public display of sacred ceremonies; some activists adopted symbols from other nations in ways that other Indigenous people criticized. These disagreements show that cultural affirmation was an internal conversation as well as a public stance.

Schooling and popular culture were important targets because representations of the past influenced public judgments in the present. Standard classroom accounts often began Indigenous history with European arrival and treated Native peoples chiefly as obstacles to settlement. In contrast, community historians and organizers insisted on continuity: nations remained, governments functioned, and ancestral knowledge informed contemporary claims. Demanding instruction in Indigenous history was therefore not simply asking for more favorable images. It challenged a chronology in which Indigenous political agency disappeared before modern America began.

The 1960s were also a period of experimentation in Indigenous art and public expression. The Institute of American Indian Arts in Santa Fe created one setting in which students addressed both inherited forms and new media. Writers and artists could resist stereotypes without agreeing that tradition should be fixed in one approved style. N. Scott Momaday's House Made of Dawn appeared in 1968 and received the Pulitzer Prize for fiction in 1969, an important later marker of wider public recognition. Its literary impact should not be mistaken for evidence that all Indigenous activism had a single aesthetic program. Cultural affirmation was as plural as the nations and communities participating in it.

Historical Debate

Did Pan-Indian Identity Strengthen or Weaken Nation-Specific Sovereignty?

The question: The movement promoted both nation-specific claims and a broader Indigenous solidarity. Historians and Indigenous thinkers disagree about how the two related.

Interpretation A — Complementary: Some scholars argue that intertribal identification strengthened specific nations by providing shared resources, allies, and visibility, and that most activists maintained strong national and community loyalties alongside broader solidarity.
Interpretation B — In tension: Others stress that pan-Indian frameworks, particularly those built on racial or generalized symbols, could obscure the treaties, languages, and political institutions of particular nations, and could be shaped by non-Indigenous expectations of a single “Indian” identity.

Evidence: Historians draw on activist writings, organizational records, newspapers, and oral histories. They also consider how particular campaigns, such as fishing rights, were grounded in specific treaties even when supported by activists from many nations.

Points of disagreement: The debate concerns interpretation and emphasis more than chronology. Scholars differ over how much weight to give nation-specific and intertribal evidence, and over whether the same pattern held in all regions.

Why it matters: The answer affects how the movement is described: as a continental political identity, as a coalition of distinct nations, or as both. It also connects to later debates about citizenship and belonging (Chapter 144).

Student task: Choose one example from this chapter, such as the fish-ins or the Poor People's Campaign, and decide which interpretation its evidence better supports. Identify evidence that could change your assessment.

Section 108.9

The Fish-Ins and the Rise of Indigenous Direct Action

Treaty fishing in the Pacific Northwest provided the first sustained theater of Indigenous direct action in the 1960s. The treaties negotiated in the 1850s with Puget Sound nations had reserved the right to take fish at usual and accustomed places, and generations of Indigenous families had continued to fish for subsistence, ceremony, and income. By the early twentieth century, however, Washington State regulations, increasingly enforced by game and fisheries officials, restricted Indigenous fishing, particularly with nets, and fishers were repeatedly arrested. The legal history of these disputes belongs to Chapter 106, and the full Fish Wars to Chapter 113. This section examines how fishing became a vehicle for direct action.

Activists adopted the strategy of the fish-in, a deliberate exercise of the claimed treaty right in defiance of state regulation, undertaken publicly so that arrests would draw attention. The term and the method resembled the sit-ins of the civil-rights movement, and the fish-ins' organizers acknowledged the influence. Yet the claim differed: the participants were not seeking access to something denied on racial grounds, but asserting a treaty right they considered binding on the state. In March 1964, demonstrations on the Puyallup River brought arrests and publicity, and on March 2 of that year the actor Marlon Brando joined a fish-in on the Puyallup, drawing national press. The involvement of celebrities and of non-Indigenous supporters, including religious groups, expanded coverage but also raised questions about who controlled the message.

Indigenous participants were central. Billy Frank Jr., a Nisqually fisherman, was arrested repeatedly while fishing in the Nisqually River and became one of the best-known figures of the struggle. Janet McCloud of the Tulalip, working with the Survival of American Indians Association formed in 1964, organized support, raised funds, and mobilized communities. Hank Adams brought strategic and organizational skills. The NIYC, whose leaders participated in the early demonstrations, helped connect the Northwest struggle to a national audience and to activists from other regions. The cooperation of people from different nations in support of specific nations' treaty rights illustrates the movement's combination of nation-specific claims and intertribal solidarity.

The political significance of the fish-ins lay in several features. Arrests dramatized the contradiction between treaty promises and state enforcement. Media coverage, though often simplified, introduced treaty rights to a national audience. Participation by activists with experience in other movements brought new tactics, such as nonviolent civil disobedience and press strategy. And the campaign demonstrated that Indigenous people could sustain a confrontation with state authority over years. At the same time, the fish-ins were not unanimously supported even among Indigenous people in the region: some tribal governments favored litigation and negotiation, and some feared that confrontation would harm their legal position.

Legal developments intersected with the protests. In 1968, the United States Supreme Court's decision in Puyallup Tribe v. Department of Game held that the state could regulate off-reservation treaty fishing in the interest of conservation, so long as its regulations were reasonable and nondiscriminatory, while leaving unresolved questions about the allocation of the catch. Indigenous activists regarded the ruling as a setback and protested it, including at the Poor People's Campaign in Washington (Section 108.15). The unresolved questions would be addressed in the following decade, a story told in Chapters 113 and 135.

A precise chronology illustrates how local and national strands intersected. In January 1964 NIYC leaders publicized their support for Northwest fishing rights; on March 2 Marlon Brando joined Robert Satiacum and others during a Puyallup River fish-in near Tacoma. A Seattle Post-Intelligencer photographer documented Brando with Satiacum in a boat that day. The University of Washington's Museum of History and Industry collection preserves the image with its date and subject identification. Brando's presence drew newspaper attention, but the campaign's authority came from treaty-protected Indigenous fishing and the work of local families and organizers. Later demonstrations, including events in the autumn, must be distinguished from the March appearance.

Consider the distribution of work behind a deliberately public arrest. Indigenous fishers needed to know the river, the season, and the gear; organizers needed to coordinate legal assistance, witnesses, transportation, publicity, and family support. Some participants expected to challenge a particular regulation in court. Others believed that simply continuing to fish was the clearest statement that a nineteenth-century agreement remained binding. The public drama was brief, but those preparations and the ability to continue afterward made direct action sustainable. Students should therefore evaluate results beyond the immediate number of arrests or press articles, while leaving the later allocation of fishing rights to Chapter 113.

The Fish-Ins: Treaty Right, Conservation Rule, or Public Cause?

Indigenous fishers and organizers: Fishing was a treaty-guaranteed right and a foundation of family life, ceremony, and nationhood; state restrictions and arrests were violations of promises made by the United States. Public action was a way to make that violation visible.
State fisheries and game officials: The state regulated fishing to conserve declining fish runs and to apply uniform rules, and regarded unregulated net fishing as a threat to the resource. Enforcement was a legal duty.
Non-Indigenous sport and commercial fishers: Many believed that fixed rules should apply to everyone, and some were hostile to Indigenous claims, regarding treaty rights as special privileges. Others, including some church-affiliated and civil-rights supporters, sympathized with the Indigenous position.

These groups did not have equal legal authority or equal vulnerability; the fishers risked arrest and loss of equipment, while officials possessed enforcement power.

Section 108.10

From Petitioning to Confrontational Political Organizing

The fish-ins were part of a broader expansion in the repertoire of Indigenous political action. Earlier generations had relied heavily on lobbying, testimony, resolutions, and litigation. These methods remained vital, but activists added others. Public demonstrations, including marches and pickets, appeared at federal offices and at gatherings. Nonviolent civil disobedience, adapted from civil-rights practice, involved deliberately violating laws or regulations in order to assert rights and invite legal challenge. Political theater, as in the 1966 parade, communicated messages visually. Press releases, newsletters, and speaking tours aimed at publicity, and community meetings built local support.

The change should be described with precision. Evidence for organized boycotts by Indigenous activists in this period is limited, and the chapter does not treat them as a major tactic. Demonstrations in Washington, D.C., became more frequent in the later 1960s, particularly in 1968 with the Poor People's Campaign. Public criticism of federal agencies, notably the Bureau of Indian Affairs, grew sharper, as activists argued that the agency's structure perpetuated dependence. The tactical shift was thus a widening of options, not a wholesale replacement of earlier methods.

Tactical choices were debated among activists themselves. Some, including organizers connected with the NIYC, argued that confrontation was necessary because petitions had failed to stop violations of treaty rights. Others regarded public protest as risky, since it could provoke retaliation or damage relationships with legislators and agencies. Some tribal governments, responsible for the daily administration of communities and dependent on federal funding, favored quiet negotiation. Still others combined approaches: participating in demonstrations while also pursuing litigation, or protesting while serving on advisory bodies. The distinction between moderate and radical camps often collapsed on closer inspection, since individuals changed positions with circumstances.

The media environment shaped the repertoire. Television and national newspapers could amplify demonstrations, but they also imposed expectations. Journalists tended to seek dramatic images, often of conflict, and to treat Indigenous activists as exotic or as figures from an earlier era. Activists learned to anticipate these expectations, sometimes exploiting them and sometimes working around them through their own publications. The fish-ins, with their visual drama and clear moral stakes, were especially suited to national coverage.

Finally, the new methods required organizational capacity: people to coordinate logistics, raise funds, provide legal support, and care for families affected by arrests. Much of that work fell to women and to community volunteers whose labor is less visible in the record (Section 108.14). The expansion of tactics depended on social foundations more than on slogans.

Direct action did not replace the slow work of documenting a claim. Activists who publicly challenged an enforcement officer often depended on tribal legal histories, treaties, affidavits, and the advice of people who knew earlier court cases. Conversely, a lawsuit might gain political force from sustained public observation of what state officials did. A useful comparison is between testimony delivered in a hearing room and a protest staged where the disputed right was exercised. Both could be designed to produce a record. They differed in how they selected an audience and how much personal risk participants accepted.

Media strategy introduced another tension. A short photograph or television segment could make a confrontation vivid but could also turn a complicated conflict over political authority into a story about dramatic personalities. The presence of well-known supporters sometimes widened coverage while diverting attention from Indigenous organizers. Movement participants therefore debated when publicity served their objectives and when it subordinated them to journalists' preferences. The critical question was not whether to use mass media, but whether the public story preserved the community's account of why the action was taken.

Section 108.11

The War on Poverty and the Demand for Community Control

The Economic Opportunity Act of 1964 launched the Johnson administration's War on Poverty and created the Office of Economic Opportunity (OEO). Its community action programs were intended to involve poor people in planning and administering local antipoverty efforts, a principle often summarized as maximum feasible participation. For Indigenous communities, the programs opened new avenues, since they allowed tribal governments and, in some places, Indigenous organizations to receive funding directly, rather than only through the Bureau of Indian Affairs. Programs in the Navajo Nation, such as the Office of Navajo Economic Opportunity, and in other reservation and urban communities, offered job training, Head Start preschools, legal services, health programs, and other services.

These programs had real effects. They gave some communities their first experience in administering substantial budgets, trained local staff, and brought new services. In some places they also employed young activists, giving them skills and networks. Indigenous participants in community action programs frequently became leaders in tribal government and in later organizations. This part of the story contradicts any claim that federal antipoverty programs were uniformly harmful or irrelevant to Indigenous communities.

But conflicts emerged. Federal officials, state authorities, and tribal councils sometimes disagreed over who controlled the programs. Where federal agencies retained final decision-making authority, communities experienced limited participation. Some tribal governments viewed community action organizations as rivals, while some community groups viewed councils as unresponsive. Guidelines designed for general application did not always suit Indigenous nations, whose legal status differed from that of municipalities or poor neighborhoods. Delays, reporting requirements, and shifting priorities frustrated local administrators.

Activists increasingly interpreted these conflicts as evidence of a deeper problem. In their analysis, poverty was not only a lack of income but a consequence of dispossession of land and resources, and of dependency created by external administration. Programs that provided services without transferring authority therefore addressed symptoms. Warrior's 1967 testimony, discussed above, was addressed to this very contradiction: the rhetoric of participation coexisted with continued outside control. The demand for community control, which began as a critique of programs, became the foundation for institution-building.

It would be a mistake to see the War on Poverty as simply a failure or a betrayal. It was both an opportunity and a constraint. It gave Indigenous communities resources and experience while revealing the limits of participation without sovereignty. That ambivalence characterized the movement's relationship to the federal government more generally: activists engaged with federal programs while criticizing the structure of power that those programs embodied. Chapter 114 examines how federal policy in the 1970s responded.

The vocabulary of 'maximum feasible participation' in the War on Poverty suggested that poor people should help design efforts intended for them. Its implementation produced conflict over who was entitled to represent a community and who had authority to approve programs. On reservations, federal agencies, tribal governments, and new community-action bodies could all claim legitimate roles. An elected tribal council might regard a separately organized local program as an intrusion; younger organizers might regard a council controlled by established figures as insufficiently responsive. The choice between tribal sovereignty and community participation was not always simple, because both could be invoked in disputes within the same nation.

Warrior's criticism of paternalism can be tested against concrete administrative questions. Was the program director selected locally? Could residents decide whether to fund transportation, education, housing assistance, or legal aid? Did grant rules allow people to use their own language and teaching methods? Who could audit expenditures and remove a failing administrator? Such questions expose the difference between a project located in an Indigenous community and one controlled by that community. They also show why federal resources could be welcomed and contested simultaneously. Refusing all aid could deprive residents of services; accepting aid without governing authority could perpetuate the same dependency that reformers sought to end.

These disputes should not be resolved retrospectively by calling every War on Poverty project either emancipatory or colonial. Some organizations trained Indigenous staff and expanded their capacity to manage projects; some were weakened by unstable funding or interference. Evidence for evaluation includes budgets, board minutes, project correspondence, and the assessments of participants. A historical account that records only federal program goals would miss the everyday negotiations that determined whether participation became decision-making authority.

Section 108.12

Indigenous-Controlled Education and Institutional Independence

Education was a central arena of community-control experiments. Federal boarding schools and many public schools serving Indigenous students had emphasized assimilation, suppressed languages, and given communities little voice (Chapters 87 and 88). Indigenous parents and educators had long criticized this system. In the mid-1960s, with funding from federal sources including the Bureau of Indian Affairs and the Office of Economic Opportunity, the Navajo Nation became the site of influential experiments in local control.

The Rough Rock Demonstration School, opened in 1966 in the Rough Rock community of Arizona, was operated under a contract that gave a locally elected Navajo school board responsibility for administration. Robert A. Roessel Jr. served as its first director, working alongside Ruth Roessel and Navajo community leaders. The school sought to teach in ways that drew on Navajo language, culture, and community knowledge, and to employ local people as staff and advisers. Parents were engaged as participants and decision-makers. The school received national attention as a model of community-controlled education.

The educational philosophy rested on the principle that Indigenous communities should control curriculum, language use, administration, and institutional priorities. This did not mean rejecting modern education; Rough Rock students studied English and academic subjects. It meant that the framework, aims, and governance of schooling would be set by the community. Historians of the school note that it faced challenges, including funding uncertainty, disagreements within the community, and tensions with federal and state agencies, and that its record in practice was mixed and has been evaluated differently by different observers. Its significance lies partly in what it demonstrated was possible and in the precedent it created.

Higher education followed. In July 1968, the Navajo Nation established Navajo Community College, now Diné College, the first tribally controlled community college in the United States. The establishment of the institution in 1968 should not be confused with the start of instruction; classes began in 1969, initially in borrowed facilities, and the college's later growth belongs to Chapter 124. The college's founding mission emphasized Navajo language, history, and culture within a broader curriculum, reflecting the same principle as Rough Rock: that Indigenous nations could create and govern their own institutions.

These institutions complicate any portrayal of Red Power as solely a protest movement. They show it as a movement of institution-building, in which communities assumed practical responsibilities for education and services. They also show the relationship between symbolic politics and administrative authority. A banner at a parade proclaimed a position; a school board in Rough Rock exercised it. The later transfer of authority to tribal governments under federal legislation in the 1970s drew on these early examples (Chapter 114).

Rough Rock illustrates the institutional stakes of community control. Its 1966 opening gave a locally chosen Navajo board a central role in directing an educational program on Navajo land. The project sought to connect schooling to the language, community knowledge, and priorities of the families it served. That differed from the familiar pattern of distant agencies setting curricula and judging success chiefly by assimilation into mainstream institutions. Yet an innovative charter did not abolish administrative problems. Staffing, funding, certification, curriculum resources, and relations with the Bureau of Indian Affairs still required negotiation. Local control had to be built and maintained through decisions rather than declared once at the opening ceremony.

Navajo Community College offered a related but distinct example. The Navajo Nation created it in 1968, while instructional operations began in 1969. Keeping those dates separate matters because the decision to create a tribally controlled college belongs to the movement's 1968 foundation, but the institution's subsequent teaching history extends beyond this chapter. Higher education could serve national development needs—preparing teachers, administrators, and professionals—while retaining language and cultural responsibilities. The issue was not whether students should acquire modern technical knowledge; it was whether the nation could establish an institution in which that knowledge was taught under Indigenous direction.

Section 108.13

Urban Indigenous Communities and Grassroots Mobilization

By the 1960s, substantial Indigenous populations lived in cities, including Los Angeles, Oakland and San Francisco, Minneapolis and Saint Paul, Seattle, Chicago, and Denver. Chapter 105 explains how relocation and economic migration created these communities. This section concerns their politics. Urban Indigenous residents came from many nations, lived in neighborhoods that mixed Indigenous and non-Indigenous residents, and relied on institutions they built themselves: Indian centers, friendship houses, churches, sports leagues, powwow clubs, and informal networks of kin and neighbors.

These institutions served practical needs, such as finding housing and jobs, and also created spaces of intertribal community. Newcomers met people from other nations, shared news from home, and exchanged knowledge of ceremonies and languages. They also built habits of cooperation across tribal lines, since the community was by necessity intertribal. This experience helped to make the cities laboratories for pan-Indian identification, though national and family identities remained strong. Many urban residents maintained ties to reservations, traveling back for ceremonies and for family needs, so that cities and reservations formed connected rather than separate worlds (Chapter 140).

Discrimination and policing generated grievances. Urban Indigenous residents reported difficulties in employment and housing, and in some cities encountered police practices that they regarded as discriminatory, including disproportionate arrests. The evidence on policing comes largely from community testimony and later investigation, and it varies by city and period, so students should treat generalizations with care. But the perception that police and municipal institutions did not protect Indigenous people was widespread, and it contributed directly to the founding of the American Indian Movement in Minneapolis (Section 108.18).

Urban communities also became sites of cultural and social service organizing. Indian centers offered counseling, referrals, and legal and health assistance. Community members organized youth programs and meetings that gave attention to local needs. In California, the Bay Area's Indigenous community developed networks that would sustain student and community activism in 1968 and 1969. In the Pacific Northwest, urban residents in Seattle and Tacoma supported the fish-ins with funds and volunteers. The cities, in other words, provided the people, the meeting places, and the supply lines for activism that was often focused on rural issues.

The urban setting also posed dilemmas. Urban residents were sometimes criticized by reservation communities as having abandoned their homelands, and some resented the claim of urban activists to speak for all Indigenous people. Urban organizations often lacked the legal standing of tribal governments, and tribal citizens in cities faced questions about how to participate in the politics of their nations. These tensions are an important reminder that the movement's social base was not homogeneous.

Urban networks mattered because they joined people with different experiences under common conditions. A Bay Area newcomer might meet members of numerous nations through a friendship center, campus association, religious network, or political gathering. In Minneapolis, Ojibwe and Dakota residents who already had relationships with nearby homelands also met migrants from more distant communities. Such contacts made it possible to compare grievances and exchange methods. They did not create an urban community detached from reservations; letters, visits, remittances, ceremonies, and political obligations kept urban and homeland histories connected.

Local organizing took place in mundane settings as well as public demonstrations. A meeting room in an Indian center, a family apartment, a campus classroom, or a church hall could become a place to discuss legal assistance or distribute notices. Community gatherings supplied trust and practical knowledge that temporary protest coalitions could not invent. An organizer who knew where a family lived, who could translate an announcement, or which relative might host travelers was performing political work. These informal connections help explain how a small visible core could call upon a larger network when an event occurred.

Cities could also complicate claims of representation. Urban residents belonged to particular tribal nations but might live far from the election processes and services of their home communities. Municipal agencies rarely understood those relationships, and some tribal officials questioned whether urban associations could speak on matters affecting reservations. Organizers responded in different ways: seeking stronger ties to tribal governments, creating independent urban institutions, or building coalitions around shared issues such as housing and policing. A citywide Indigenous identity was therefore a political resource, not a substitute for every member's national belonging.

Section 108.14

Indigenous Women, Families, and the Organization of Resistance

Indigenous women were central to the movement, though their contributions have often been underrepresented in popular accounts and in the records produced by officials and journalists. In the Pacific Northwest, Janet McCloud was a principal organizer of the treaty-fishing struggle. Shirley Hill Witt, a cofounder of the NIYC, helped to shape its work and later wrote about it. Tillie Walker, Martha Grass, Della Hopper Warrior, and LaDonna Harris, who founded Oklahomans for Indian Opportunity in 1965, represent a larger group of women who organized meetings, managed finances, spoke publicly, and led community programs. These women worked in a variety of settings, from national campaigns to local councils.

The forms of labor were diverse. Women organized meetings in homes and community centers, prepared meals for gatherings and for those traveling to protests, and raised funds through community events. They cared for children and elders while others were away or in jail. They taught cultural knowledge, including languages, songs, and stories, to younger generations, an activity that was itself part of resistance to assimilation. They wrote, edited, and distributed newsletters, drafted statements, and handled correspondence with officials and supporters. In tribal governments and community action programs, women served as staff and leaders.

Gendered recognition of leadership was uneven. Journalists and officials frequently addressed male spokespeople, and national organizations, like their counterparts in other movements, were often led by men. Women's contributions were sometimes described as support, though many of them directed strategy and held authority. The historical record reflects these patterns, so reconstruction requires attention to sources beyond contemporary news reports, including organizational papers, oral histories, and later memoirs. Indigenous nations varied in women's formal political roles, and some, such as the Haudenosaunee, had long recognized women's authority in governance; the extent to which these traditions informed individual activists differed.

Family and household were also political spaces. Participation in protest carried risks for families, including arrest, lost income, and retaliation, and the willingness of families to bear these risks was a precondition for sustained action. Households hosted activists, and kinship networks provided the social trust on which organizing depended. Recognizing the family dimension prevents the movement from being portrayed solely in terms of public figures and speeches.

Janet McCloud's organizing in the Pacific Northwest illustrates how work often described as logistical could be politically decisive. A fishing demonstration needed places where supporters could gather, food for those who traveled, communication with families, and help when people were arrested. McCloud's advocacy connected these practical needs to a principled defense of treaty rights. Her example also cautions against depicting women only as wives or relatives of prominent male activists. Women argued publicly, built organizations, addressed government officials, and sustained their own political networks, even when photographs favored the men most often arrested or interviewed.

Shirley Hill Witt brought another form of leadership through writing, organizing, and the political education of younger people. Tillie Walker, whose Mandan and Hidatsa family history intersected with the Missouri River dam projects, helped carry the concerns of Indigenous poverty and federal control into the 1968 Poor People's Campaign. LaDonna Harris's work in Oklahoma shows a related emphasis on organizational capacity and locally responsive programs. These women did not form a single ideological faction; they worked in different places and institutional contexts. What links them is their refusal to separate practical community welfare from the question of political agency.

Gender also shaped what archives preserved. A meeting's minutes might record the chair's speech but not the people who arranged transport, childcare, or accommodations. Newspaper accounts might identify a male negotiator while neglecting the women who coordinated residents' participation. Oral histories, organizational correspondence, family photographs, and community newspapers can recover more of this work, although each source has limitations. Historians should resist solving the problem by inventing representative scenes. The appropriate method is to name documented people, identify their roles, and explain why otherwise ordinary labor had political consequences.

Care for children and elders was neither separate from activism nor uniformly borne by women. Families negotiated the costs of joining demonstrations, keeping businesses open, meeting ceremonial responsibilities, and returning safely from travel. People could support political objectives without attending a march, and those who did attend depended on others. This social history does not diminish formal leadership. It explains the conditions that allowed formal leadership to persist beyond a single rally.

Everyday Life and Social History

Organizing in Kitchens, Meeting Halls, and Fishing Camps

The public events of the movement, the arrests and demonstrations, depended on ordinary routines. A fish-in required people to arrange transportation, gather equipment, secure legal help, and supply food. Meetings in homes and community halls required that someone prepare a meal, mind children, and keep records. Newsletters had to be typed, copied, folded, and mailed. Fundraising relied on community suppers, donations, and the contributions of working families whose wages were modest.

Historians reconstruct these activities from oral histories, organizational records, photographs, and the recollections of participants, while noting that the surviving record is partial and that such tasks were often unrecorded. The scarcity of documentation should not be mistaken for scarcity of effort. Because the labor was frequently performed by women and by elders, attention to it corrects a view of the movement as the work of a few prominent men. It also illuminates how the movement's cultural commitments were lived: songs, food, and the teaching of children were part of political practice.

Section 108.15

Civil Rights, Black Power, and the Poor People's Campaign of 1968

The civil-rights movement influenced Indigenous activists in several ways. It demonstrated the power of nonviolent direct action and the importance of media attention. It created a national conversation about discrimination and inequality. And it produced alliances and organizations, some of which welcomed Indigenous participation. Indigenous activists adopted and adapted tactics such as sit-ins, marches, and arrests. Some, including leaders in the Northwest, openly acknowledged this debt.

Yet Indigenous activists also insisted on differences. The civil-rights movement's central goal, ending racial discrimination and securing equal participation, did not fully fit Indigenous claims. Indigenous nations held treaty rights, lands, and governments that no integration could provide, and many Indigenous people feared that a framework focused on individual equality could undermine collective sovereignty. The relationship between Red Power and Black Power illustrates the point. Both asserted pride, self-determination, and community control, and both criticized paternalism. But Black Power emerged from a population claiming full citizenship and equal protection within the United States, while Red Power rested on political nationhood and a distinct legal relationship. The overlap was real and the differences were real.

The Poor People's Campaign of 1968 made these relationships visible. After the assassination of Martin Luther King Jr. on April 4, 1968, the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, under Ralph Abernathy, carried forward the planned campaign for economic justice. In May and June, thousands of people, including African Americans, Latinos, poor whites, and Native Americans, converged on Washington, D.C., and established an encampment known as Resurrection City. Native participants included organizers associated with the NIYC and other groups. Tillie Walker, Mel Thom, Hank Adams, and Martha Grass were among the Indigenous organizers, and delegations from various nations traveled to the capital.

The Indigenous presence was distinctive. Participants joined the coalition's call for economic justice but advanced their own demands concerning sovereignty, land, treaty enforcement, and federal obligations. They publicly challenged the Department of the Interior and other federal agencies, and joined protests that followed the Supreme Court's Puyallup decision in May. Their participation shows how Indigenous activists could support broader struggles against racial discrimination and poverty while maintaining that tribal nationhood raised additional legal and political questions that a general antipoverty program could not address.

The campaign's results were mixed. It drew attention to poverty and produced some concessions from federal agencies, though the encampment ended amid difficult conditions, and many organizers considered its material achievements limited. For Indigenous participants, the experience strengthened networks, gave them experience in Washington, and introduced them to activists from other communities. It also revealed the difficulties of coalition: priorities and legal circumstances differed, and Indigenous demands were sometimes overshadowed by the larger groups' concerns. The campaign thus belongs among the formative experiences of the movement, a bridge between the early 1960s and the national campaigns that followed.

The Indigenous delegation entered a campaign designed to connect people affected by different kinds of poverty, but its representatives insisted that deprivation in Native communities had a history of land loss and externally imposed government. The National Park Service's documentary study reproduces an Indigenous statement presented by Mel Thom to Secretary of the Interior Stewart Udall and Commissioner Robert L. Bennett. It identified paternalism and the federal administration of Indigenous lands as central obstacles. The statement appears within a wider 'Statement of Demands for Rights of the Poor,' presented through the Southern Christian Leadership Conference and its Committee of 100 in late April and early May 1968. Locating the document in this sequence clarifies that an Indigenous political program was put before officials before the largest Washington encampment reached its climax.

Indigenous participants joined an interracial coalition and also encountered the limits of coalition language. A demand for jobs and income might unite African American, Latino, Appalachian, and Indigenous participants; a demand that a tribal nation govern its land or that federal officials honor a treaty required additional explanation. These were not competing claims so much as different layers of political argument. Indigenous organizers used the campaign to show that poverty could result from exclusion from decision-making about resources that nominally belonged to Indigenous communities. Their position drew attention to the inadequacy of reform programs that did not alter who controlled land, budgets, and agencies.

Martha Grass's public role on Solidarity Day and Tillie Walker's organizational work show that the campaign was not a story of one spokesman alone. The timing of the May 1968 Supreme Court ruling in the Puyallup fishing litigation also connected national demonstrations with the Northwest treaty struggle, although the 1968 decision did not conclusively determine the validity of every disputed fishing restriction. The campaign did not win its full legislative agenda. Its importance for this chapter lies in the relationships developed, the public presentation of a distinct sovereignty analysis, and the experience activists gained in coordinated national protest.

Section 108.16

Indigenous Students and the Struggle for Educational Representation

By the late 1960s, more Indigenous students attended colleges and universities, and campuses became sites of political formation. Students formed organizations, shared analyses across nations, and encountered curricula in which Indigenous peoples were absent or stereotyped. Indigenous students challenged those curricula, sought courses on Indigenous history and cultures, and demanded recruitment of Indigenous faculty and students. These demands drew on the same principle as community control: that Indigenous people should have authority over the representation of their own histories.

Campus organizing intersected with broader student movements. In California, the Third World Liberation Front, a coalition of student groups representing African American, Asian American, Latino, and other students, organized a strike at San Francisco State College beginning on November 6, 1968. The strike lasted until March 1969 and became one of the longest campus strikes in American history. Its demands centered on ethnic studies, admissions, and the voice of students of color in university governance. Indigenous students and members of San Francisco's Native communities took part and helped to shape the development of American Indian Studies at the institution.

The students involved included individuals who would later be associated with the Alcatraz occupation of 1969, whose story belongs to Chapter 109. This chapter treats the strike as the beginning of a student movement, one of the networks that linked campuses and the urban Indigenous community in the Bay Area. It should not be read backward as simply a preface to Alcatraz; the students pursued their own educational goals, and many of them continued that work in academic programs and community organizations.

The campaigns linked education, identity, and political authority. Students argued that who teaches and what is taught shape how a people is understood and how it understands itself. They sought not only inclusion in existing courses but also programs controlled by Indigenous people and responsive to Indigenous communities. Their efforts parallel Rough Rock and Navajo Community College, though in a different institutional setting. The relationships between campus programs and tribal nations, including debates about accountability and community benefit, would continue to evolve (Chapters 124 and 145).

The San Francisco State dispute helps distinguish the chronology of organization from that of outcome. Indigenous students and their allies participated in the Third World Liberation Front, whose demands included educational programs responsive to communities excluded from the existing curriculum. The major strike began on November 6, 1968, and continued into March 1969. This chapter examines the demands and the start of the mobilization; it does not treat institutions established afterward as though they were already operating at the end of 1968. The difference matters when evaluating the power of a political demand and the negotiations required to turn it into a department or curriculum.

University records identify the participation of American Indian students and community members, including Richard Oakes, alongside people who helped shape the objectives and early teaching of American Indian Studies. Their goals included presenting Indigenous histories from Indigenous perspectives and increasing the authority of Native people over what was taught about their own nations. A university program could not substitute for a tribal government. It could nevertheless become a place where students found historical materials, developed organizing skills, connected with community elders, and contested the portrayal of Indigenous people as subjects rather than authors of history.

The coalition's demands must be read with attention to authorship. The published lists include overlapping but distinct positions advanced by the Black Student Union and the Third World Liberation Front. Attributing each demand to every participating Indigenous student would erase real differences in priorities. The demand for ethnic studies, however, provides evidence of a shared strategy: students sought institutional changes in teaching, recruitment, and governance rather than only symbolic recognition. This was one arena where political solidarity operated without requiring the participants to abandon separate histories or objectives.

Section 108.17

Indigenous Mobilization across North America

Red Power as a label belongs chiefly to the continental United States, but similar concerns animated Indigenous politics in Alaska and Canada. Comparing these regions clarifies both the shared and the distinctive elements of the period. In each, Indigenous peoples confronted settler states seeking to administer their lives, extract resources, and assimilate their communities; in each, a new generation challenged paternalism and demanded recognition of rights and self-government. The legal and political contexts differed substantially, and the chapter preserves those differences.

In Alaska, Native peoples, including Iñupiat, Yupik, Athabascan, Unangax̂, Tlingit, Haida, and others, faced the consequences of statehood in 1959, which authorized the new state to select large amounts of federal land without resolution of Native claims. Native leaders organized in response. The Tundra Times, begun in 1962, gave expression to Native concerns, and the Alaska Native representatives met in October 1966, with the Alaska Federation of Natives taking more formal shape in 1967 to unite regional organizations in pressing land claims. A federal freeze on land transfers and the discovery of oil at Prudhoe Bay in 1968 increased the urgency. The resulting struggle, which led to the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of 1971, is the subject of Chapter 115. The Alaskan movement emphasized aboriginal land claims and regional organization more than the treaty-based arguments of the continental United States.

In Canada, First Nations, Métis, and non-status communities faced the administrative framework of the Indian Act (Chapter 85), which defined legal status and gave the federal government extensive control. Indigenous organizing in the 1960s included provincial and national bodies. The federal government commissioned a major survey of Indigenous conditions in the mid-1960s, often called the Hawthorn report, and the National Indian Brotherhood was formed in 1968 as a national organization of First Nations political bodies (Chapter 107). Canada's centennial in 1967 prompted Indigenous commentary: at a celebration in Vancouver on July 1, Chief Dan George delivered an address that offered a pointed Indigenous reflection on the nation's history. The federal White Paper of 1969, which proposed to end the legal distinctiveness of Indian status, and the strong Indigenous response to it, lie just beyond this chapter's boundary but grew out of the debates described here (Chapter 117).

Métis and non-status communities had their own concerns. Métis political organizations in the western provinces were developing in this period, seeking recognition and services, and non-status people who had lost or never had legal status under the Indian Act pursued separate advocacy. Their circumstances show that the Canadian situation included diverse legal categories, with distinct histories and claims (Chapter 52 and Chapter 83).

The comparison reveals common features: concern over land and resources, resistance to externally imposed administration, cultural survival, and demands for self-government. It also reveals contrasts: treaty-based claims in much of the continental United States and parts of Canada, aboriginal-title claims in Alaska and in parts of Canada without treaties, and different legal frameworks for status and recognition. Contacts across borders also existed, through shared organizations, speakers, and publications, and through peoples whose homelands crossed international boundaries (Chapter 142). Mexican Indigenous peoples faced their own political contexts, which this chapter does not examine.

In Alaska, the struggle centered especially on land ownership and the consequences of statehood. Native associations sought to prevent the transfer or disposal of contested lands before their claims had been addressed. The Alaska Federation of Natives grew out of an October 1966 statewide meeting and developed a more formal federation in 1967. Its work involved regional negotiations, communication over vast distances, and the political problem of coordinating nations with distinct languages and territories. Those concerns were comparable to Red Power's interest in collective authority, but the legal setting was not identical to that of treaty-protected reservations in the lower forty-eight states.

In Canada, the centennial celebrations of 1967 created opportunities for public Indigenous critique. Chief Dan George's July 1 address in Vancouver contrasted the celebration of Confederation with the experience of peoples whose territories and political standing had been transformed without their equal consent. The speech was not an American Red Power declaration, and Canadian First Nations and Métis organizations followed their own histories of association and political demands. The comparison illustrates the value of seeing contemporaneous Indigenous resurgence across borders while avoiding a story in which all developments are copied from one U.S. movement.

Section 108.18

Minneapolis and the Founding of the American Indian Movement, 1968

The founding of the American Indian Movement in Minneapolis in July 1968 is a concluding milestone of this chapter. The city had a substantial and growing Indigenous population, many of them Ojibwe and Dakota people from nearby reservations, and many living in neighborhoods with limited resources. Residents reported problems with employment, housing, and treatment by police, and community members sought ways to protect themselves and each other. Some of the early organizers had personal experience of incarceration and of the criminal justice system, which shaped their view of its inequities (Chapter 130).

George Mitchell, Dennis Banks, and Clyde Bellecourt were central organizers of the July founding meeting; Eddie Benton-Banai and other women and men, including Mary Jane Wilson, also figured in accounts of AIM’s early organization. The new organization focused initially on local concerns, particularly policing and community protection. It organized patrols to observe police activity in Indigenous neighborhoods, an effort intended to deter abuse and to document incidents. The patrols were a grassroots response to a specific urban condition, which later accounts have sometimes obscured by emphasizing AIM's national campaigns. The early AIM was also concerned with housing, employment, and education, and it linked its work to a broader assertion of Indigenous pride and rights.

AIM's founding connected to wider political change. Its organizers were aware of the civil-rights and Black Power movements, which offered examples of community self-defense and assertion, and of the NIYC and other Indigenous organizations. At the same time, the new organization drew on the particular networks of Minneapolis, including churches, community centers, and relationships among Ojibwe and Dakota families. Its origins illustrate how urban Indigenous communities could generate political organization in response to local conditions.

This chapter stops at the founding. AIM's subsequent growth, leadership, programs, controversies, and national campaigns are examined in Chapter 110, and its role in later occupations in Chapters 111 and 112. Students should be careful not to read AIM's later prominence back into 1968, when it was a local organization of limited resources. They should also note that AIM was one among many Indigenous organizations of the time, and that its approach was neither universally welcomed nor typical of all Indigenous activism.

The organizational setting in Minneapolis deserves particular precision. Historical accounts describe a July 28, 1968 meeting attended by a large group of local Indigenous residents, initiated by organizers including George Mitchell, Dennis Banks, and Clyde Bellecourt. Eddie Benton-Banai contributed to AIM's early development, as did additional women and men whose roles are sometimes neglected in compact founding lists. The presence of Mary Jane Wilson in published accounts is one reminder that the movement was not produced by three names alone. There is no reason to make these accounts compete as though they describe identical kinds of participation: organizing the initial meeting, helping define the program, and building the subsequent institution are related but different activities.

Police monitoring and practical assistance were especially important to residents who experienced unequal treatment by city authorities. The early patrol concept sought witnesses and accountability; it did not confer policing authority on AIM. The organization drew on an urban network that already existed, just as NIYC had drawn on earlier youth and treaty-rights networks elsewhere. Recognizing these local origins makes it possible to understand why a later national movement could begin with quite specific concerns. The subsequent evolution of AIM is reserved for Chapter 110.

Section 108.19

The Indian Civil Rights Act and the Tensions within Self-Determination

The Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA) of 1968 was enacted as part of broader civil-rights legislation signed by President Johnson on April 11, 1968. It followed congressional hearings, led by Senator Sam Ervin of North Carolina, that examined complaints about the treatment of individuals by tribal governments and by federal and state authorities. The Act extended to people subject to tribal authority many protections resembling those of the Bill of Rights, including protections of speech, religion, due process, and equal protection. It did not apply all constitutional guarantees: it omitted, for example, some provisions concerning establishment of religion and the right to counsel at tribal expense. It also initially limited tribal criminal penalties, and it made federal habeas corpus review available to test the legality of detention by tribal order. A separate title provided that states could assume jurisdiction under Public Law 280 only with the consent of the tribes affected.

The statute thus did several things at once. It responded to genuine concerns that individuals might be subject to abuse by tribal officials, and it extended familiar protections to people living under tribal governments. It also imposed federal standards on tribal governments without the consent of the nations involved, an assertion of what Congress regarded as its plenary authority. And it, at the same time, reduced a significant threat to tribal jurisdiction by requiring tribal consent for future state assumption of jurisdiction. The Act was therefore not simply restrictive or protective; it was a compromise containing both limits and protections.

Indigenous reactions were diverse. Some tribal leaders and governments objected that the Act intruded on self-government and that imposing a foreign legal framework ignored distinct Indigenous legal and cultural traditions. Others noted that Indigenous communities themselves had concerns about arbitrary exercises of power, and that individual rights were not alien to Indigenous values. Activists critical of federal paternalism could oppose congressional imposition while supporting accountability of governments to their people. Those who supported protection of civil liberties could still criticize the manner of enactment. The disagreement concerned means, not opposition to colonial domination, which all shared.

The longer consequences unfolded afterward. The statute required tribal courts and councils to attend to new standards, and the question of how it would be enforced, and by whom, was central. In 1978, the Supreme Court's decision in Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez held that the Act did not create a federal cause of action for most claims against tribal governments, leaving enforcement largely to tribal institutions, a development discussed in Chapter 118. The ICRA's history thus illustrates how legislation framed as protection of rights could simultaneously alter the balance of authority between tribal nations and federal government.

The Act's internal architecture is important. Title II of Public Law 90-284 introduced limitations on tribal governmental action and a federal habeas-corpus remedy to test detention; other provisions addressed questions of state jurisdiction. Yet the original 1968 language must not be replaced silently with today's amended U.S. Code. Students examining an original statute should ask what it required in 1968, what it left to tribal institutions, and which questions were not settled until later litigation. Comparing the original text with a current codification can itself show how legal frameworks evolve over time.

The controversy also warns against treating 'tribal sovereignty' and 'individual rights' as opposites that can never be reconciled. Indigenous political communities have their own laws, responsibilities, and practices for holding leaders accountable. Federal legislation may protect against certain abuses while overriding the authority to define procedures locally. The tension involves institutional design: who articulates a right, who judges a dispute, and what remedies are available? Rather than importing the entire later history of Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez, the chapter should identify the unresolved stakes visible to people confronting the statute in 1968.

Historical Vocabulary
Plenary Power

Definition. In United States federal Indian law, the doctrine that Congress holds broad authority over Indigenous nations and their affairs.

Historical context. The doctrine developed through nineteenth-century court decisions and congressional practice and was invoked to justify measures such as allotment and termination, as well as protective legislation. Indigenous nations have long contested its legitimacy.

Why it matters. Measures like the ICRA were enacted under this asserted authority, which is why even rights-protecting legislation could be understood as an exercise of external power.

Potential misunderstanding. The doctrine is a legal construct of United States courts and Congress and does not describe the source of Indigenous sovereignty, which activists and many scholars regard as inherent.

Historical Debate

Did the Indian Civil Rights Act Protect Individuals or Undermine Tribal Sovereignty?

The question: Historians and legal scholars disagree about how to evaluate the Act's significance for Indigenous self-government.

Interpretation A — A protection of individuals: The Act addressed real abuses, extended important liberties to people living under tribal authority, and recognized tribal governments while holding them to standards of fairness. Its consent requirement for state jurisdiction also protected tribal authority.
Interpretation B — An intrusion on sovereignty: The Act imposed federal standards without tribal consent, rested on assertions of plenary power, and risked substituting external legal concepts for Indigenous legal and political traditions, with litigation expanding federal influence over tribal institutions.

Evidence: Historians examine congressional hearings and debates, tribal responses, case law, and the experience of tribal courts. Evidence about outcomes varies by nation.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement is largely interpretive and concerns the weighing of values and of effects, not the basic facts of the statute. Scholars also differ on how much the Act changed actual tribal practice.

Why it matters: The debate bears on how sovereignty is understood and on how individual rights and collective authority relate in Indigenous governance (Chapter 118).

Section 108.20

The Foundations of Red Power by 1968

By the end of 1968, the intellectual and organizational foundations of Red Power were largely in place. Its intellectual arguments rested on the claim that Indigenous peoples were nations whose self-determination required control over decisions and institutions. Clyde Warrior and others had articulated a critique of paternalism and a vision of community-based self-government. A slogan had appeared in public and begun to circulate, with controversy attached. Cultural affirmation, through publications, arts, and ceremonial life, had become an element of political resistance.

Its networks had grown. The NIYC and other organizations linked young activists across nations. Urban communities provided social foundations and, in Minneapolis, a new organization. Women organized the labor on which sustained action depended. Students built campus networks and joined coalitions, as in the San Francisco State strike. Alliances with other movements were tested in the Poor People's Campaign. In Alaska and Canada, parallel organizations were forming around land and status.

Its methods had diversified. Fish-ins had demonstrated sustained direct action. Demonstrations in Washington and public criticism of federal agencies had become more common. At the same time, institution-building at Rough Rock and in the Navajo Nation's new college had shown that community control could be put into practice. Federal policy, meanwhile, was shifting in rhetoric toward self-determination, while legislation like the Indian Civil Rights Act showed continuing federal assertions of authority.

Unresolved tensions remained. Activists disagreed about strategy and language. Reservation and urban communities had different priorities. Tribal governments and grassroots organizations sometimes competed. Questions of individual rights and tribal authority, of pan-Indian identity and national distinctiveness, and of cooperation with federal agencies versus confrontation were open. The movement should not be read as a unified force heading toward a predetermined climax. Many different outcomes were possible in 1968, and the national campaigns that followed were shaped by contingency, including the choices of particular people and the responses of governments.

The chapter closes at a threshold. A generation of Indigenous activists had inherited long traditions of sovereignty and resistance, transformed them through new methods and a new political language, and begun to build institutions that would outlast any slogan. Their work set the stage for the occupation of Alcatraz in November 1969, the subject of Chapter 109, and for the broader developments traced in the chapters that follow.

Taken together, the developments of the decade show several routes into political power. Treaty-based direct action forced public attention onto the gap between legal promises and state enforcement. Youth and urban networks enabled people of different nations to coordinate action. Community-controlled schools and colleges demonstrated the practical meaning of institutional authority. Women's organizations and the Poor People's Campaign connected everyday welfare with sovereignty, while student coalitions made control over historical knowledge a matter of public contest. These routes were not coordinated by a single headquarters; they converged around a growing insistence that Indigenous people should govern the decisions most affecting them.

The threshold of 1968 should not be confused with completion. The Indian Civil Rights Act remained contested, local programs remained financially dependent, and Indigenous nations disagreed about political methods and priorities. Still, the movement now possessed recognizable ideas, experienced organizers, visible tactics, and institutions that could support further action. The occupation of Alcatraz in the following year would make these currents more visible, not create them from nothing. The historical problem carried forward is how broad solidarity could produce effective action while preserving the political claims of particular nations.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has explained how Red Power emerged as a developing set of ideas and practices during the 1960s, not as a single organization and not suddenly with the occupation of Alcatraz in 1969. It began with the 1966 NIYC parade intervention in Oklahoma City, a modest public act that revealed a generational argument over strategy within a shared commitment to sovereignty.

The movement grew from older Indigenous traditions of treaty defense, reform, and anti-termination campaigning, carried forward by the NCAI and by tribal governments. The generation of the 1960s inherited legal claims, intertribal networks, and cultural continuity, and transformed the tone, audience, and methods of political action. The relationship between generations was one of overlap and mutual dependence as much as friction.

The conditions of protest included termination, relocation, jurisdictional conflict, poverty, and threats to treaty rights, together with the opening created by the civil-rights movement, the War on Poverty, and expanded higher education. No single cause produced the movement, and its emergence depended on leaders, networks, and political opportunities that existed unevenly.

The movement's intellectual foundations were the claim that Indigenous peoples were political nations, the demand for self-determination as control over decisions and institutions, and an affirmation of Indigenous cultures and histories. Clyde Warrior's critique of paternalism, expressed in his 1967 testimony, was a central statement, though one voice among many. The slogan Red Power, publicly displayed in 1966, unified and divided, since it raised questions about race, nationhood, and pan-Indian identity.

New methods appeared. The fish-ins of the Pacific Northwest turned treaty rights into sustained direct action, bringing together Indigenous fishers, organizers, and outside supporters, while state officials and others disputed their claims. The repertoire expanded to include demonstrations and public criticism of federal agencies, though lobbying, litigation, and negotiation remained vital and many activists combined approaches.

Institution-building was equally important. The War on Poverty gave some communities new resources and experience while revealing the limits of participation without authority. Rough Rock Demonstration School, opened in 1966, and Navajo Community College, established in 1968, demonstrated that Indigenous communities could control education, and showed that Red Power was a movement of governance as well as protest.

Urban communities, women, and students supplied the social foundations. Cities generated intertribal networks and grievances about discrimination and policing. Women organized, funded, taught, and led, though their labor is less visible in the record. Students challenged curricula and joined coalitions such as the San Francisco State strike beginning in November 1968.

The relationship with other movements was complex. Indigenous activists adapted civil-rights tactics and sympathized with Black Power's assertion of community control, but insisted that nationhood, treaties, and land raised distinct questions. The 1968 Poor People's Campaign displayed both coalition and distinctiveness. Parallel developments in Alaska and Canada shared concerns over land, status, and self-government within different legal frameworks.

The year 1968 brought several milestones: the presidential message rejecting termination, the Indian Civil Rights Act with its tensions between individual rights and tribal authority, the Puyallup decision, the founding of AIM in Minneapolis as a local response to policing and discrimination, and the loss of Clyde Warrior. These events illustrate unresolved disagreements within Indigenous politics and between Indigenous nations and governments.

The chapter's argument is that Red Power transformed existing traditions of sovereignty and resistance through more confrontational methods, cultural affirmation, community control, and national mobilization. By the end of 1968 its foundations were established while its best-known campaigns lay ahead, a threshold that leads to Chapter 109.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it corrects a common picture of Indigenous activism as a sudden outburst in 1969. The movement's roots lay in generations of treaty defense and community organizing, and its emergence in the 1960s involved argument, experiment, and organizing at many levels, from a parade in Oklahoma City to a school board in Rough Rock and a patrol in Minneapolis.

It reveals larger processes. Indigenous politics changed through the interplay of long-standing sovereignty claims with new opportunities and models, and the new generation did not abandon older goals but changed the methods and language through which they were pursued. The chapter also shows the importance of ordinary labor and local institutions in sustaining political change, and the reality of disagreement within Indigenous communities about strategy, identity, and the meaning of rights.

Later developments drew on these foundations. The occupations and marches of 1969 through 1973, the legal victories and policy changes of the 1970s, the growth of tribal colleges and community-controlled programs, and the continuing debates about sovereignty and individual rights all trace connections to the years examined here. At the same time, continuities persisted: treaty rights, cultural survival, and self-government remained the central concerns of Indigenous nations.

Within this textbook, the chapter connects the policy history of termination and relocation with the sovereignty era that follows. It shows that the era of self-determination was not simply granted by government but shaped by Indigenous initiative. The next chapter turns to Alcatraz, where these networks, ideas, and experiences became visible to a national audience.
