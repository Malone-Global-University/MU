Indigenous Children, Families, and Cultural Survival

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

Government policy could build a school, set a calendar, and write a rule about language. It could not decide what a grandmother would say to a child at the end of a long absence, or whether that child would listen. The history of assimilationist schooling is therefore a history of institutions, but it is also a history of families.

In the autumn of 1884, a girl of about eight years old left her mother's home on the Yankton Agency in present-day South Dakota and travelled east to White's Indiana Manual Labor Institute in Wabash, Indiana. She would later be known to the public as Zitkála-Šá, a writer, musician, teacher, and political activist. In 1900 she published a short account of her arrival at the school in which she described the cutting of her hair and what that act meant to her. Her account is one of the best-known first-person records of an Indigenous child's entrance into an institution designed to change her. It is also an adult's interpretation of a childhood memory, written sixteen years later for a national magazine audience, and historians read it with both of those facts in mind.

Eighty-nine years later, in 1973, a six-year-old girl named Phyllis Webstad, a member of the Stswecem'c Xgat'tem First Nation in British Columbia, prepared for her first day at St. Joseph's Mission residential school near Williams Lake. Webstad has described how her grandmother bought her a new orange shirt for the occasion, and how the shirt was taken from her when she arrived. The two scenes are separated by nearly a century, by an international border, and by the different legal and administrative systems of the United States and Canada. They are linked by a shared pattern: a family prepares a child for school, an institution removes a possession or a feature that marked the child as belonging to that family, and the child must decide what to carry forward.

Chapter 87 explained how the American boarding school and Canadian residential school systems were created, funded, governed, and operated. This chapter turns from the institutions to the people they affected. Its governing question is how assimilationist schooling and related government interventions disrupted Indigenous childhood, family relationships, and cultural transmission, and how children, parents, grandparents, elders, and communities preserved, reconstructed, and transmitted Indigenous identities across generations. The chapter does not describe the school systems a second time. It asks what separation, schooling, letter-writing, resistance, homecoming, and renewal looked like from the perspective of the children and families who lived through them.

Four propositions organize the chapter. First, family separation was an intentional or foreseeable consequence of policies that sought to move authority over children from Indigenous communities to outside institutions. Second, children and families experienced these policies differently depending on their nation, community, school, age, gender, and period. Third, children and families exercised agency through correspondence, resistance, mutual support, adaptation, and continued participation in cultural life. Fourth, cultural survival depended not simply on individual resilience but on the continuing work of families, communities, elders, and Indigenous political institutions. These propositions can be held together only if the historian refuses two simplifications: that the schools were benign instruments of opportunity, and that Indigenous families were merely their casualties.

The evidence for this history is unusual and requires care. For the United States, the most valuable sources include student and family correspondence preserved in school files, now partly digitized by the Carlisle Indian School Digital Resource Center and the National Archives, together with student writing, school newspapers, and later autobiography. For Canada, the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC) gathered thousands of survivor statements and published selections in The Survivors Speak in 2015; these are retrospective recollections recorded decades after the events they describe. Government and church records, by contrast, were written by administrators and often say little about what children felt or what families said. A central skill of this chapter is therefore the ability to tell apart a letter written at the time, a memoir written years later, an administrator's reply, and an interview recorded in the twenty-first century, and to ask what each can and cannot establish.

The chapter moves through five divisions. The first examines Indigenous childhood and the circumstances of separation, from family decisions to the first days at school. The second reconstructs everyday life inside the institutions from the perspective of language, siblings, correspondence, food, and labor. The third considers student voices, resistance, and the most severe consequences of institutional life, including the documented case of Chanie Wenjack. The fourth follows children home and into adulthood and compares experiences across regions and genders. The fifth traces cultural continuity and the growth of family and community demands for control over education, ending with a historical synthesis.

Two cautions run throughout. Survivor testimony and family letters describe real harm, including homesickness, punishment, illness, and loss, and nothing in the discussion of Indigenous resilience should be read as diminishing that harm or the responsibility of the governments and churches that created the conditions. At the same time, Indigenous families were never only damaged institutions. Parents petitioned, children wrote home, grandmothers taught, communities organized, and by the 1970s Indigenous political bodies were demanding and in some cases obtaining control of schools. That combination of documented injury and continuing agency is the subject of this chapter.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Indigenous families, through kinship networks and elders, functioned as institutions of education, cultural transmission, and political continuity.
  2. Analyze how recruitment, treaty provisions, compulsory attendance, poverty, and administrative pressure constrained the choices of Indigenous parents, and why apparent agreement does not necessarily indicate free consent.
  3. Describe children's journeys, arrival experiences, renaming, hair cutting, and uniform clothing, and explain why identical practices could carry different meanings for school officials and for children.
  4. Distinguish language loss, language shift, bilingualism, and the choice not to transmit a language, and avoid treating them as interchangeable.
  5. Evaluate letters, petitions, memoirs, institutional rebuttals, and oral testimony as different kinds of evidence, including the difference between the date of an experience and the date it was recorded.
  6. Trace forms of resistance and mutual support among children, including refusal, concealment, flight, and the maintenance of friendships and sibling bonds.
  7. Interpret the case of Chanie Wenjack as documented history, with attention to his family and community and to the limits of what the record establishes.
  8. Assess the experience of homecoming, the roles of elders and caregivers, and the intergenerational effects of schooling without assuming identical outcomes.
  9. Compare experiences among American Indian, First Nations, Inuit, and Métis families by region, institution, gender, and period.
  10. Explain the concept of survivance and distinguish cultural continuity from the unchanged preservation of earlier traditions.
  11. Trace the movement from individual family petitions to collective political demands for Indigenous educational control, including the 1972 National Indian Brotherhood policy paper and the 1975 Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act.
  12. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of whether schooling destroyed, transformed, or produced varied outcomes for Indigenous family continuity.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. In what ways was the Indigenous family itself an educational and political institution, and why did assimilationist policy treat it as an obstacle?
  2. How should historians interpret parental consent when the parent faced poverty, treaty obligations, or the threat of punishment?
  3. Why could the same school practice, such as cutting hair or issuing uniforms, mean hygiene to an administrator and loss to a child or a parent?
  4. What can a letter written in 1890 tell historians that an interview recorded in 2012 cannot, and what can the interview reveal that the letter cannot?
  5. How did children and families sustain relationships across distance, institutional rules, and language barriers?
  6. What distinguishes resistance aimed at a specific grievance from opposition to assimilation as a policy, and why does the distinction matter?
  7. How did experiences differ among American Indian, First Nations, Inuit, and Métis children, and among girls and boys, and what explains the differences?
  8. How did Indigenous communities keep languages, stories, and ceremonies alive, and what part did deliberate reconstruction play alongside unbroken continuity?
  9. Why did control over children's education become a central political demand of Indigenous families and organizations in the twentieth century?
  10. How can historians recognize documented harm and Indigenous agency in the same account without minimizing either?

Historical Context

The family-centered history in this chapter rests on foundations laid in earlier chapters. By the 1880s, the United States and Canada had each concluded treaties and established reservation and reserve systems that limited Indigenous mobility, land use, and political authority (Chapters 82 and 84). Canada's Indian Act gave the federal government extensive administrative control over First Nations communities (Chapter 85). In the United States, allotment legislation was pressing on communal landholding (Chapter 86). Both governments came to regard education of children as a central instrument for changing Indigenous societies. Chapter 87 examined the resulting institutions in detail: the American boarding schools, with the Carlisle Indian Industrial School opening in 1879 as a model, and the Canadian residential schools operated largely by Christian churches under federal funding and oversight.

Earlier nineteenth-century developments provide background. The United States Civilization Fund Act of 1819 authorized federal money for missionary and other efforts to instruct Indigenous people in agriculture and literacy, linking education to a broader policy of cultural change. Treaty negotiations in both countries sometimes included provisions for schooling, which Indigenous negotiators in some cases sought and later found being used for purposes they had not intended. Indigenous communities also had earlier experience with mission schools, and some families, particularly those who had already adopted literacy or Christianity, regarded formal education as a resource. This chapter does not retell those developments but uses them to explain why a family's attitude toward schooling could differ from one community to another.

The central context for this chapter is the Indigenous family as an institution. Before and during the assimilation era, children's upbringing was distributed among households and kin. Parents, grandparents, siblings, aunts, uncles, clan relatives, and elders each contributed to instruction in language, conduct, subsistence skills, and the responsibilities of belonging to a particular nation. Education was not separate from daily life but embedded in it, taking place in seasonal work, ceremonies, storytelling, and the ordinary rhythms of household and community. Assimilationist policymakers understood this and drew a conclusion from it: if the family transmitted Indigenous languages, beliefs, and political loyalties, then removing children from the family for long stretches would weaken those connections. Pratt's often-cited formulation, associated with Carlisle, about killing the Indian in order to save the man, has been quoted in many sources and should be read as one articulation of that logic rather than the whole of it.

Historians must also resist two opposite errors when studying this period. The first treats Indigenous families as passive victims whose cultures were simply erased by institutions. This ignores a large body of evidence that parents wrote, travelled, petitioned, withdrew children, and continued teaching those who returned, and that students formed friendships, kept their languages, and resisted in many ways. The second error treats resilience as proof that harm was limited. This ignores documented testimony of homesickness, illness, punishment, loss of language, and bereavement, and the foreseeable consequences of removing children from their families. The chapter's approach follows recent scholarship, including work by Brenda J. Child on family correspondence, K. Tsianina Lomawaima on student life at Chilocco, and Celia Haig-Brown on Kamloops survivors, in presenting separation as coercive and harmful while recognizing Indigenous children and families as historical actors.

Division I — Indigenous Childhood, Family Authority, and the Experience of Separation

Section 88.1

Indigenous Childhood, Kinship, and the Family as an Institution of Cultural Transmission

A history of what separation meant must begin with what children were separated from. Indigenous childhood in the late nineteenth century varied enormously, from Arctic hunting camps to Pueblo villages, from Plains reservation households to Northwest Coast fishing communities. Generalizations therefore require caution. Yet across this diversity, historians and Indigenous writers have described a shared feature: children were raised within a web of relationships that extended well beyond the parent-child pair, and instruction in how to live was embedded in the daily activities of that web.

Kinship systems differed. Some peoples traced descent through the mother's line, as among the Haudenosaunee and many Pueblo nations, so that a child's clan, name, and obligations came through maternal relatives. Others emphasized the father's line, and still others, such as many Great Basin and Subarctic communities, used bilateral systems in which both sides mattered. Extended households could include grandparents, aunts and uncles, and cousins who were addressed with terms that English renders as "mother" or "brother." The Lakota and Dakota tiospaye, an extended family band, and the Diné system of clans are examples of relationships that carried duties of care and instruction. This textbook uses these examples to illustrate variety rather than to describe any single "Indian family."

In such systems, many adults had responsibilities toward a child. Grandparents were often especially important. They had accumulated knowledge, often had time to teach, and in many communities were expected to tell the stories, explain the names, and show the skills that belonged to the family. An aunt or uncle might discipline or instruct in ways that a parent did not. Older siblings looked after younger ones and learned responsibility in doing so. Because the household and the community were intertwined, a child could learn from many people over the course of a day or a season.

Childhood activities reflected the work and ceremonial calendars of each community. Children collected plants, tended animals, carried water, fished, gathered, and learned to hunt or process food, with responsibilities increasing with age and often differing by gender. Play involved games that taught skills and strategies. Seasonal movements, such as camps for berry picking, fishing, or bison hunts where those still occurred, were occasions of instruction. Stories were told in particular seasons or settings in many communities, and ceremonies marked stages of growth. Language was learned in all of these settings, so that a language carried not just vocabulary but the manners, humor, and values of the people speaking it.

Children also learned who they were politically. To be a Mohawk child or a Diné child or a Cree child was to belong to a nation with its own history, relationships with land, and obligations to others, as Chapter 1 emphasized in discussing sovereignty and belonging. Learning one's clan, one's relatives, one's place of origin, and the expectations of one's community was part of becoming a member of that nation. The family was thus one of the institutions through which political continuity was maintained, not only a unit of affection and care.

The family was not static or idealized. Late nineteenth-century Indigenous families were already under pressure from epidemic disease, loss of land, reduced access to game and other resources, and the administrative restrictions of reservation and reserve systems. Some families were disrupted by death or hardship. Many had already incorporated Christianity, literacy, or farming. Historians should not suppose that boarding and residential schooling struck intact, unchanging households. They should recognize, however, that policymakers targeted the family relationships that remained.

Government officials and missionaries commonly described Indigenous child-rearing as inadequate, because it did not match their expectations about schooling, religion, or domestic order. Their writings are therefore poor evidence for what families actually did, and they must be read alongside Indigenous sources. Family and community oral traditions, later memoirs, linguistic documentation, and ethnographic accounts provide a more reliable picture, though each carries its own limitations. The picture that emerges is of children who were not unschooled but educated in different ways, by different people, toward different ends.

Historical Vocabulary
Kinship, Cultural Transmission, Intergenerational Knowledge, and Indigenous Childhood

Kinship refers to the system of relationships, by descent, marriage, adoption, and naming, that organizes who is related to whom and what they owe one another. In many Indigenous societies kinship was a framework for social, economic, and political life, not only a description of family trees.

Cultural transmission is the process by which language, knowledge, values, and practices are passed from one generation to the next. It occurs through teaching, observation, participation, ceremony, and storytelling.

Intergenerational knowledge is knowledge held and taught across generations, such as place names, ecological understanding, genealogies, or ceremonial protocols. It depends on continuing contact between elders and young people.

Indigenous childhood is used here to describe the diverse ways Indigenous children were raised within their own communities before and alongside colonial schooling.

Why it matters: These terms explain why removal of children for long periods threatened more than individual relationships: it interrupted the channels through which communities reproduced themselves.

Potential misunderstanding: Kinship should not be read as merely "tradition" opposed to modern life. Kinship systems changed over time, and families adapted them to new conditions, including reservation and reserve life.

Section 88.2

Parents, Consent, Coercion, and the Contested Decision to Send Children Away

How children came to be in school is a question about families. Officials sometimes described enrollment as voluntary, and in some instances parents did request schooling. In many others the decision was shaped by pressures that make the language of consent inadequate. Understanding the range between those cases is central to this chapter.

Recruitment took several forms. In the United States, agents and school recruiters visited reservations to enlist students, sometimes offering assurances about care and opportunity. Reservation agents controlled rations and other resources, which gave their recommendations weight. Treaties negotiated in the nineteenth century often contained education provisions, and the federal government interpreted these as authority to provide schooling on its own terms. Later federal law and regulation introduced attendance requirements, and agents could use withheld rations or other penalties against families who refused. In Canada, church missions and Indian agents enrolled children, the 1920 amendments to the Indian Act expanded compulsory attendance powers, and officials could use fines, threats of legal action, and the involvement of police against parents (Chapter 87 describes the legal framework).

Families responded in different ways. Some parents wanted their children to learn English or French, arithmetic, and skills they hoped would help them deal with the settler economy and the administrative world around them. Treaty negotiators in some cases had sought education as protection against dispossession. Parents who valued schooling often distinguished between instruction and the other features of the institutions, such as long absences, religious conversion, labor, or suppression of language. Wanting a child to be literate was not the same as consenting to the loss of a child's language or years of separation. Historians who read school records for evidence of parental approval must therefore ask what exactly was being approved.

Other families resisted. They hid children, refused to sign enrollment papers, kept them home during recruiting visits, or argued with agents. Their resistance was not costless. Poverty and hunger were constant pressures. When ration distribution or relief depended on the goodwill of an agent, a family's refusal could have material consequences. In remote communities, geographic isolation meant that parents had little means to learn what happened at a distant school or to intervene. Parents who complied in order to protect their food supply or their other children were not freely consenting in any ordinary sense.

The unequal relationship between officials and parents is documented in the sources. Letters and agent reports show administrators arguing that Indigenous parents were unfit judges of their children's interests, and administrators often treated parental objections as obstacles to be overcome. Family testimony, by contrast, frequently emphasizes that children were taken, that the decision was not the parents' to make, or that the family felt it had no alternative. Both kinds of source are partial. Official documents tend to understate coercion, and later testimony reflects the memory of the people who experienced it. Comparing them is more reliable than relying on either alone.

The historian must also avoid the opposite mistake of assuming that every placement occurred without family involvement. Letters in school files show parents asking that a child be admitted, requesting that a child be transferred to a closer school, or seeking permission for a child to return home. These requests show families attempting to use the system for their own purposes, even as they operated inside constraints they had not chosen. The existence of such requests does not alter the fact that the institutions were created to shift authority over children from the family and community to outside bodies.

Cause and Effect

How Colonial Education Policies Transferred Authority over Children from Families to Institutions

Long-term causes

Assimilationist ideology that treated Indigenous languages, religions, and family forms as obstacles to settler-state aims; confinement of Indigenous communities on reservations and reserves; and earlier mission schooling and treaty education provisions.

Short-term causes

Federal and church expansion of boarding and residential schools in the 1880s and 1890s, per-student funding that rewarded enrollment, and growing administrative reach through agents, ration systems, and attendance regulations.

Mechanisms

Recruitment by agents and missionaries; compulsory attendance laws and regulations; withholding of rations or threat of legal penalty; the practical impossibility of parental oversight at a distance.

Effects

Children spent months or years under institutional authority; parents lost day-to-day influence over language, religion, and discipline; families developed strategies of petition, correspondence, resistance, and negotiation.

Historical complexity: Transfer of authority was not total or uniform. Families retained influence through letters, visits, summer returns, and withdrawal, and some communities negotiated for closer, day, or mission schools. The pressures differed by country, period, and community.

Section 88.3

Leaving Home: Recruitment, Journeys, Distance, and the First Experience of Separation

The journey to school was the first stage of separation, and its character depended on distance, period, and institution. Some children attended schools located on or near their reservations or reserves and could in principle be visited by relatives. Others travelled hundreds of miles or more. Carlisle, in Pennsylvania, drew students from the Plains, the Southwest, the Great Lakes region, and elsewhere. Zitkála-Šá travelled from Dakota country to Indiana. In Canada, children from northern or remote communities were transported to schools far from their homes, and by the mid-twentieth century air travel and bush flights were part of the story for some families, especially in the North.

Modes of transport reflected the technology of each period. Wagons carried children from villages to agencies, and railways took many students from agency towns to eastern schools in the late nineteenth century. Steamboats and, later, aircraft and buses shaped Canadian experiences. These journeys could be organized by agents, missionaries, or school employees, and children of different ages might travel together. Younger children could be separated from older siblings or friends, and the group might include children who did not share a language.

For parents, the departure was a moment of decision and uncertainty. Many did not know exactly where the school was, how long their child would stay, or when they might see him or her again. Official assurances about return dates were not always honored. Families in remote places often had no practical way to visit. Parents' own feelings about departure varied, from reluctance and grief to hopeful anticipation in cases where a family had sought the school. The sources for these emotions are mostly later testimony, and historians should avoid supplying feelings that the evidence does not record.

For children, the journey could be understood differently from how adults framed it. A young child told that she was going to school might imagine a short visit or a new experience, and might not understand that the stay would last months or years. Language barriers meant that explanations might not be understood at all. Survivor accounts mention curiosity, excitement about riding on a train or boat, fear, and confusion, and many describe homesickness developing quickly. These accounts come from retrospective testimony and vary widely, so the chapter does not generalize about how every child felt.

Distance had practical consequences. Writing a letter took days or weeks to reach its destination. Parents needed money or transport to visit. Illness at school could develop faster than a family could respond. Children who were far from home could not easily leave, as §88.11 will show. Distance therefore functioned as an instrument of institutional control: it reduced the capacity of families to observe, intervene, or retrieve children. At the same time it created conditions in which letters, photographs, and visits acquired great importance.

Closer schools were not necessarily gentler. Day schools and nearby residential institutions allowed more contact and sometimes allowed children to return home on weekends or at midday, and some families preferred them for that reason. But proximity did not remove the regime of discipline, language restriction, or labor inside the school. It did, however, alter how families could respond, because a parent who lived within walking distance could appear at the door.

Section 88.4

Arrival, Renaming, Hair Cutting, Clothing, and the Loss of Personal Autonomy

The first days at school were often the point at which the institution's aims became visible to children. Across the United States and Canada, arrival rituals typically included changes of clothing, haircuts, bathing and inspection, the issuing of numbers or new names, assignment to dormitories, and the removal of personal possessions. These practices were normal features of institutional intake, and administrators often described them in the language of cleanliness, discipline, and preparation for a new life. For children, they could mean a sudden loss of control over their own bodies and identities.

Clothing and hair carried meanings that school officials often ignored or discounted. In many Indigenous communities, hair had cultural, spiritual, and social significance. The way it was cut could mark mourning, status, or age. Clothing could indicate community, family, and personal history. Uniforms identified students as members of the institution and erased visible distinctions among them, which was part of the purpose. Renaming was also common. Students were given English or French names, or numbers, in place of names given by families, which cut official records off from kin naming practices and made it harder for families to trace children.

Administrators defended such procedures on hygiene grounds. They pointed to lice, disease, and the need to manage large groups of children. Historians should take these claims seriously as claims, while noticing how they fit with a broader policy of cultural change. A haircut can control lice and also remove a cultural marker. Both purposes could coexist, and the effect on a child depended in part on whether the child or the child's family understood the practice as a mark of loss. A parent who did not know how long the school would keep a child could not be sure what the cutting of hair meant for the child's place in the community.

Zitkála-Šá's account of her first days at White's Institute, published in 1900, offers an unusually detailed record from a former student. She entered in 1884, at about eight years old. Her essay describes the strange sounds and sights of the school, the restrictions on her movements, and the moment she learned that her hair was to be cut. In her account, she resisted by hiding, then was found and held, and the cutting took place against her will. She wrote that in her own community short hair was associated with mourning or with cowardice, and her recollection of the event links it to a sense of lost dignity and lost spirit. These details come from her own retrospective narrative, not from a contemporary record, and her words should be read as an adult's literary rendering of a childhood memory.

The case concerns White's Indiana Manual Labor Institute, which was a mission-affiliated manual labor school, not Carlisle. Zitkála-Šá later worked at Carlisle as a teacher in the late 1890s, and her criticism of Carlisle (§88.9) arose from her adult experience there combined with her earlier childhood experience. Keeping the two institutions distinct prevents misattributing details of one to the other.

Other students described similar experiences with different emphases. Some remembered clothing as uncomfortable or unfamiliar, others as exciting. Some accepted haircuts without distress, and others recalled them as humiliating or frightening. These differences reflect age, community background, and the manner in which the practices were carried out. A single account such as Zitkála-Šá's cannot stand for all students, but it indicates what the same procedure might mean to a child for whom hair held cultural significance and whose family was far away.

Section 88.5

Language Loss, Language Preservation, and the Struggle over Cultural Belonging

Chapter 87 examined the official language policies of the schools. This section asks what those policies meant for children and their families. In most schools, students were expected to speak English, or in some Canadian schools French, and the use of Indigenous languages could be discouraged, punished, or simply denied time and space. For children who arrived speaking only their own language, the first months could be a period of isolation in which they could neither understand instructions nor express their needs.

Survivor accounts collected by the TRC and other bodies connect these restrictions to later difficulties in family communication. Some former students recalled that after years at school they struggled to speak with grandparents who spoke little or no English, or that parents and children found themselves unable to share a language. Others described being ashamed of their language, having internalized the school's view of it, or deciding not to speak it to their own children for reasons of protection or shame. These testimonies, recorded in the early twenty-first century, describe experiences from the 1930s through the 1970s and should be read as survivor recollections, not as contemporary records.

It is important to distinguish different outcomes. Losing fluency means a person's ability in a language declined, sometimes only partially. Acquiring another language means adding English or French, which could be an asset in negotiating with governments, finding work, or advocating for a community. Choosing not to transmit a language to one's children is a decision with its own reasons, sometimes made in the belief that a child would face fewer obstacles speaking English, sometimes made out of shame or fear. These are not the same thing. Treating them as equivalent obscures how individuals and families made choices under pressure.

Children also kept their languages. Many spoke them quietly with siblings, cousins, or trusted friends, in dormitories at night, in the fields, or on the way to and from classes. In schools with large numbers of students from one community, a language could survive within peer groups. Some teachers and staff, including Indigenous employees, tolerated or even used Indigenous languages. Students from different nations sometimes shared English as a common language while retaining their own in smaller circles. Letters home were another site of language contact: children wrote in English because of school requirements or because supervisors read their letters, while parents may have dictated replies through interpreters or relied on literate relatives.

Language transmission after school depended on families and communities. Where grandparents and other elders remained fluent and children returned regularly, languages could continue to be used in households. Where whole generations had experienced long residential schooling, and where English or French dominated outside the home, language shift sometimes accelerated. The outcomes differed by nation, region, and period, and many communities retained high levels of fluency despite intensive schooling. These variations are why the chapter avoids saying that every student permanently lost an Indigenous language, and why it avoids treating bilingualism as itself a sign of cultural destruction. Bilingualism could be a resource. The harm lay in the compulsion, the punishment, and the stigma, and in the interruption of family transmission.

The history of language is linked to later revitalization efforts, examined in Chapter 123. For the purposes of this chapter, the main point is that language was held within family relationships, and the disruption of those relationships affected its transmission. The recovery of languages has therefore also been a family and community project.

Section 88.6

Brothers, Sisters, Friendships, and the Separation of Siblings

Siblings were often the closest relatives a child had in school. Their presence could comfort, and their separation could deepen isolation. Institutional organization often divided children by sex and age, with separate dormitories, play areas, dining arrangements, and sometimes classrooms. Brothers and sisters might live within sight of each other and still be forbidden to speak, or might see one another only at set times and under supervision. Siblings attending different schools, or whose enrollment dates differed, might not see each other for years.

Survivor testimony published by the TRC records these experiences. Daniel Nanooch, who attended a Canadian residential school, recalled being separated from his sister and the difficulty of that separation. Inez Dieter recalled restrictions on her interactions with her brother within the school. Both accounts appear in the section of The Survivors Speak on separating siblings. These testimonies describe childhood experiences from decades before the TRC's work, and they are retrospective, though each speaker is identified by name and the accounts can be compared with those of other survivors who described similar rules.

The two published accounts permit a more precise comparison than a generic description of sibling separation. In the Wabasca setting, Nanooch remembered a schoolyard fence dividing boys from girls and reported seeing his sister only about four times during a school year, despite their attending the same mission. Dieter recalled turning to smile at her brother in class and being punished when caught; she also remembered using signs to communicate. These memories record particular rules and responses, not a universal regulation for every Canadian school. They also reveal that a sibling's presence could remain emotionally significant even when ordinary interaction was prohibited. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission placed both recollections in its chapter on separating siblings, alongside other accounts of brothers and sisters trying to maintain contact. Read together, they document institutional limits on children's family relationships and the ingenuity children used to preserve them.

Separation had varied consequences. Some siblings maintained their bond by exchanging glances, passing messages through other children, or meeting briefly at allowed times. Others recalled that they were not able to rebuild the closeness they had before. The rules also affected the ability of older siblings to look after younger ones, a responsibility many children had carried at home. Being physically near but forbidden to speak could in some accounts be as painful as being far away.

Friendships among students formed within and across lines of nation, language, and background. At boarding schools such as Carlisle and Chilocco, children from many nations lived together, and the relationships among them contributed to what historians call intertribal or pan-Indian identities, as well as to personal friendships that lasted for decades. K. Tsianina Lomawaima's study of Chilocco emphasizes how students built their own social world, including friendship groups, informal rules, and shared strategies for dealing with staff. Students also protected one another, shared food, covered for rule-breaking, and comforted younger children. In Canada, survivors have likewise described friendships that sustained them.

These friendships were not simple escapes from the institution. They developed within conditions of control and unequal power, and student peer hierarchies could also include bullying and exploitation. Older children sometimes enforced rules or participated in discipline, and some students acted as monitors or informants. Historians should not romanticize peer networks. The evidence suggests that they were important sources of support, as well as arenas of conflict, shaped by the institution's own structures.

Section 88.7

Letters Home, Family Visits, Parental Petitions, and the Struggle to Maintain Contact

Correspondence was the main channel through which many separated families maintained contact, and it is also among the best contemporary evidence for family relationships. Because letters were written at the time, they allow historians to see what children and parents said to one another and to officials while events were unfolding, rather than relying only on later memory. They also have distinctive limitations, which this section makes explicit.

Letters from students were often shaped by school conditions. In many institutions, staff read outgoing mail, set times for letter writing, required that letters be in English, or corrected students' writing. Students might write what they thought supervisors would approve, or they might find ways to include signals of distress or requests for help. Parents who did not read English relied on interpreters, missionaries, or literate relatives to read letters to them and write replies. That intermediary role meant that family correspondence passed through other hands and could be altered, delayed, or lost. Postal communication to remote communities was irregular. A letter might reach a family weeks after it was written, or not at all.

Parents' letters and petitions to school authorities are particularly valuable because they record families addressing the institution. Parents wrote to ask about their children's health, to request that a child be allowed to come home for a visit or permanently, to report a death in the family, or to ask for money, clothing, or food to be sent. They also wrote to protest decisions and, in some cases, to demand a child's return. Brenda J. Child's study of Ojibwe families draws extensively on this correspondence to show how parents continued to act as parents from a distance.

Administrative responses reveal how schools handled such requests. Rules often required that a student remain for a set number of years, that travel costs be paid by the family, or that approval come from the superintendent or an Indian agent. Schools sometimes granted requests, especially when a child was seriously ill, and sometimes refused. Officials commonly framed refusals in terms of the child's benefit or the need to follow regulations, and some reserved the right to decide whether a family's reasons were adequate. The documents therefore record not only family wishes but the institutional machinery that governed them.

The 1890 Cornelius case illustrates these dynamics. On April 28, 1890, Elijah J. Cornelius wrote to Carlisle asking that his sons Jesse and James be returned to him because of his concerns about their health. On May 17, the Carlisle administrator Alfred John Standing replied that an Office of Indian Affairs ruling meant the boys were not then eligible to return for another year, with the question of their return subject to a later health assessment. The letters, preserved in the Carlisle Indian School Digital Resource Center, show a parent asserting concern and an institution responding in terms of its own policy. They also show the limits of what the documents say. They do not record the boys' own views, the nature of the health concerns beyond what the letters state, or what ultimately happened, which students may be able to trace through the digital collection's other files.

The archive adds details easily lost in a general summary. Cornelius wrote that James and Jesse had already been at Carlisle nearly five years, and that they themselves wished to return. Standing's response referred specifically to an Office of Indian Affairs ruling under which they were not yet eligible to leave; he proposed assessing their health when arrangements were made for students going home. The difference matters. The letters do not demonstrate that Standing invented the requirement, nor do they show that the boys' father had accepted it. The first document records a family trying to act on its assessment of two children's welfare; the second records an administrator explaining how a rule constrained or justified the answer. To determine whether the boys eventually returned, a researcher would need subsequent correspondence, student registers, and transportation records rather than infer an outcome from these two letters alone.

Survival of correspondence is itself uneven. Carlisle's centralized files provide dates, recipients, sometimes names and identification numbers, and the Office's description of the request. An Indigenous family's messages, however, may be represented only by the items an administrator chose to preserve. The parent who travelled in person, spoke to a local agent, or used an interpreter could leave a much thinner record. Comparing letters therefore requires attention not only to their words but also to the recordkeeping system that collected them. A series of letters from the same family can show changing circumstances and repeated advocacy; a solitary letter rarely establishes what happened next. The most careful family history connects correspondence with enrollment and attendance registers, community records, and, where available and appropriate, family-held accounts. It preserves the distinction between an observed administrative decision and conclusions about private feelings that the surviving papers cannot prove.

Section 88.8

Food, Labor, Recreation, Illness, and Children's Everyday Strategies of Survival

Everyday life in the schools was made up of routines: waking, chores, classes, meals, prayers, recreation, and sleep. Children experienced these routines in ways that official reports rarely recorded. This section draws on letters and testimony to reconstruct ordinary experience, while keeping in view that the setting was coercive.

Food was a recurrent concern in the documents. Students might miss familiar foods and write home to ask for them. Many survivor accounts mention hunger, institutional meals, or unfamiliar diets, and children sometimes found ways to obtain extra food. Boarding school administrators were frequently under budget pressure, which Chapter 87 examines. For families, packages of food, or money to buy it, were ways to care for a child at a distance, and requests for such packages appear in letters.

Labor occupied a large share of children's days. Boys often worked in farm, shop, and maintenance tasks, and girls in laundry, sewing, kitchen, and domestic work, reflecting both the schools' training goals and their need for labor. Some programs placed students with families or employers outside the school during summer, an arrangement known at Carlisle as the outing system. Chapter 87 describes the institutional uses of labor. From a family's perspective, children's work meant that time they might have spent learning at home was spent in institutional service, and parents sometimes objected when children seemed overworked or when schooling appeared secondary to labor.

Recreation and friendship also formed part of the record. Students played games and sports, took part in music and performance, celebrated holidays, and created their own entertainments. Some remembered these activities fondly. Historians should treat such memories as real, without taking them to show that the schools were benign. Enjoyment and friendship could develop within coercive conditions, and children made the best of the circumstances they had.

Health appears frequently in the letters. Families asked about illness, and students wrote about being sick or about other children who were sick. Parents' fears were grounded in the actual prevalence of tuberculosis and other diseases in the schools, documented in government reports such as the 1907 Bryce report on Canadian schools and the 1928 Meriam Report on American Indian education. Chapter 87 treats the institutional public-health history. Here the question is how illness looked to children and parents, who wrote of fever, cough, injury, and worry, and who sometimes requested that a sick child be sent home.

One accessible example is the collection of letters from Fort Mojave Indian School held by the U.S. National Archives. In his January 16, 1929 letter to his father, the student Curtis Etsitty combined ordinary school news with a request for familiar food from home. Such letters show a child performing the routine of writing home while expressing a personal need. They are small, but they illustrate the intimacy of family connection that the institution could not fully control.

The National Archives identifies Etsitty's letter as written on January 16, 1929, while reproducing a second letter from February 25 with a variant spelling of his surname in its caption. The January letter refers to pictures and books made at school, basketball, and a wish for mutton from home. These details matter together. They show the standardized school-writing assignment alongside a child's selection of topics that linked the classroom to his family. The same archival account describes correspondence from the sisters Eunice and Donna Mae Vanderbilt in November 1930, including inquiries about relatives and holiday activities. Some of their letters were returned as unclaimed. That postal outcome cannot establish why a reply failed to arrive, but it reveals the practical obstacles to maintaining contact even when children took the initiative to write. Such records allow a history of agency and uncertainty without pretending to know every child's interior life.

Section 88.9

Zitkála-Šá, Indigenous Student Writing, and the Contest over Historical Memory

Section 88.4 used Zitkála-Šá's account of her arrival at White's Institute as a record of a child's experience. This section returns to her as an adult author. The difference matters. An autobiographical essay is a work of memory shaped by the writer's later life, audience, and purposes, and historians must read it as both a source about the past and a political act in the present of its writing.

Zitkála-Šá, born Gertrude Simmons in 1876 at the Yankton Agency, attended White's Institute from 1884, later studied at Earlham College, and taught at Carlisle in the late 1890s. In 1900 The Atlantic Monthly, one of the leading literary magazines in the United States, published three of her essays: "Impressions of an Indian Childhood," "The School Days of an Indian Girl," and "An Indian Teacher Among Indians." Together they describe her early life with her mother, her departure for school, her experiences there, and her return to teach among Indigenous people. Their literary quality and their willingness to criticize assimilationist institutions made them unusual in a periodical aimed at a general readership.

The essays also demonstrate the use of autobiography as a form of Indigenous political expression. Zitkála-Šá wrote in English, in a literary style, for an audience that was overwhelmingly non-Indigenous. She presented a child's perspective on institutions that most readers knew only through official descriptions. Her choice to emphasize the loss and disorientation she experienced was therefore also a choice about how to influence public understanding. She later became a prominent political figure and was involved in reform organizations, so her writing belongs both to the history of childhood and to the history of Indigenous political advocacy.

Her account did not go unanswered. The Carlisle school's magazine, The Red Man, published a response in February 1900 that challenged her presentation of school life. The school had its own reputation to defend, having been the most prominent off-reservation boarding school, and the Carlisle administration used its publications to present its work in a favorable light. The response, preserved among Carlisle resources, represents an institutional voice contesting the account of a former student and teacher. Students can read it through the Carlisle Indian School Digital Resource Center.

The February 1900 editorial is particularly revealing because it did not simply deny that Zitkála-Šá had literary ability. Its editors acknowledged her skill in describing childhood and the force of her satire, but argued that the account left out happier experiences and might mislead readers unfamiliar with schools. The response came from a publication devoted to defending and promoting the Carlisle educational project; its institutional purpose shaped what it treated as a fair account. Nor should the school's objection be mistaken for independent evidence that the episodes she described were false. The disagreement was partly about what a public narrative should foreground: a former student's memories of constraint or the school's asserted accomplishments. Students can place the editorial beside her February 1900 essay and identify both the concessions the editors made and the reasons they gave for disputing her interpretation.

Comparing the two texts requires attention to authorship, audience, and interests. Zitkála-Šá wrote as an individual with personal experience, addressing a national readership, and reflecting on events from years earlier. The school's magazine wrote as an institution, addressing its supporters, and defending a program. Neither text is neutral. The institutional response is valuable as evidence of how the school understood and protected its image, and of how the school responded to critique, but it is not a reliable account of what Zitkála-Šá experienced as a child. Her essay is valuable as evidence of a child's experience as remembered and shaped by an adult, but it is not an administrative record of White's Institute or Carlisle.

The exchange also shows that former students were not silent. They wrote, published, and argued with institutions in public. Other Indigenous writers of the period used similar forms. Treating student writing as a source of historical memory, rather than only as an expression of personal feeling, allows historians to see how Indigenous people contested the meaning of boarding school history while the institutions were still operating.

Section 88.10

Canadian Survivor Testimony, Childhood Memory, and the Reconstruction of Experience

Official records of Canadian residential schools consist largely of administrative correspondence, inspection reports, enrollment and financial documents, and annual reports. They generally record what officials thought worth recording, and they say little about how children experienced daily life. Survivor testimony fills part of that gap. This section explains how historians use it and how to avoid common errors in doing so.

The largest body of such testimony was gathered by the TRC between 2008 and 2015. Survivors spoke at national events, community hearings, and private statement-gathering sessions. The Commission's 2015 volume The Survivors Speak organizes selected statements by theme, including the decision or compulsion to attend, arrival, siblings, family contact, school life, and resistance. Other collections of testimony include interviews conducted by researchers such as Celia Haig-Brown, whose work with former students of the Kamloops school in British Columbia was published in the 1980s, and many community-based oral history projects.

Testimony describes specific experiences: the first day of school, loneliness, separation from relatives, language restrictions, punishment, friendships, and the return home. It also describes experiences in the decades after school. Because speakers attended school at different times and in different places, the testimony covers a range of periods and institutions, from the early twentieth century through the 1970s. The experiences of a child entering a school in the 1930s were not identical to those of a child entering in the 1960s, and testimony from different communities can show that variation.

Historians should distinguish the date of an experience from the date of the telling. A statement given in 2012 about a school year in 1958 is a primary source for the speaker's recollection and understanding in 2012 of events in 1958. It is not a document produced in 1958. Memory can be vivid and reliable on some points and vulnerable on others, such as exact dates or the sequence of events. The setting of the telling matters, too: a public hearing, a private interview, or a legal process may shape what a person chooses to say. These are features of all oral evidence, not reasons to dismiss it.

The report provides an example of how precise source identification improves interpretation. The testimony of Inez Dieter, identified in the commission's notes as a January 16, 2012 statement in Regina (SP035), and that of Daniel Nanooch, recorded July 4, 2013 in High Level, Alberta (2011-1868), appear together under the editorial heading 'Separating siblings.' These are two individual recollections gathered in different places and more than a year apart, subsequently arranged by a reporting commission. The thematic arrangement helps readers see a recurring issue, but it does not make the interviews interchangeable or establish their schools, childhood dates, and personal circumstances from the published excerpts alone. When writing a historical account, students should attribute each statement to its speaker and cite the report's pages or notes rather than describe the collection as a single anonymous survivor voice.

Researchers can also compare the kinds of silence in school and survivor records. Administrative files may describe a child's transfer, attendance, health, or discipline but omit how parents understood the event. A recorded recollection may explain that personal meaning but not reproduce the exact wording of an official order issued decades earlier. The evidentiary solution is not to rank one source type as automatically reliable and the other as suspect; it is to ask each what it was produced to communicate. The date of the event, the date of writing or recording, the identity of the speaker, and later editorial selection are separate variables. When communities establish access protocols or survivors limit the use of their statements, historians must take those conditions seriously. Attribution, respectful quotation, and acknowledgement of uncertainty are part of the scholarly method rather than obstacles to it.

Testimony should be treated as evidence requiring attribution and contextualization, not as an emotional supplement to official records. That means naming the speaker where permitted, stating the school and period when known, and comparing statements with other sources. Where multiple survivors from different schools describe similar practices, such as restrictions on sibling contact, the convergence gives added weight to each account. Where accounts differ, historians should explain the difference by reference to institution, period, and individual circumstance rather than by assuming that one account must be wrong.

Care for the speakers is part of the method. Many survivors gave their statements under protocols about consent, privacy, and use. Some asked that their names not be published, and others asked that their words be shared widely. This textbook uses published testimony in accordance with those conditions and encourages students to read the original statements in their full context. The chapter distinguishes survivors' words from editorial paraphrase and avoids inventing detail.

Historical Vocabulary
Survivor Testimony, Oral History, Autobiography, and Archival Silence

Survivor testimony is an account given by a person who experienced institutional schooling, describing events and their consequences. It is a primary source for the speaker's memory and interpretation, recorded at the time of speaking.

Oral history is the practice of recording and interpreting spoken recollections. Oral histories differ from oral traditions, which are community-held bodies of knowledge transmitted over generations (Chapter 1).

Autobiography is a self-authored account of one's own life, shaped by the author's later perspective, audience, and aims.

Archival silence describes the absence from records of certain people, relationships, or experiences, whether because they were not recorded, were not preserved, or were not considered worth noting.

Why it matters: These terms help students separate kinds of evidence and recognize that gaps in the record do not mean gaps in experience.

Potential misunderstanding: Retrospective testimony is sometimes called "less reliable" than contemporary records. It is better described as a different kind of evidence, with distinct strengths and limits.

Section 88.11

Refusal, Flight, Concealment, and Children's Efforts to Reclaim Authority

Children and families resisted institutional regimes in many ways, and the sources document more of this than older histories acknowledged. Resistance ranged from small acts of refusal to flight, and its forms depended on age, distance, relationships, and risk.

Within schools, students resisted by speaking their languages when forbidden, by slowing work, by refusing orders, by hiding food or possessions, and by keeping or creating objects and practices that connected them to home. K. Tsianina Lomawaima's study of Chilocco shows that students developed informal norms and networks that allowed them to manage discipline and sustain relationships. Writing letters, sending messages through other children, and passing news among siblings were ways of reclaiming connection. Older children sometimes helped younger ones, sharing food or advice. Students also kept contact with relatives by finding ways to send letters that staff did not read or by asking others to carry messages.

Running away was a more dramatic form of resistance and appears frequently in school files. Administrators used terms such as "absconding" or "truancy" and treated departures as disciplinary problems, often organizing searches and retrieving students. Children left for many reasons: homesickness, mistreatment, fear of punishment, illness, or a wish to see a relative. Some succeeded in reaching home. Others were caught and returned, with punishment in some cases. The risks were serious, especially for children far from home who had to cross unfamiliar country, travel in cold weather, or lacked food and money. Chanie Wenjack's case (§88.12) is the best-documented example of how dangerous such journeys could be.

Historians must distinguish different motivations. A student who ran away after a particular punishment was responding to a specific grievance. A student who left to see a dying relative was responding to a family obligation. A student who repeatedly refused to speak English or to follow religious instruction may have been opposing assimilation more broadly. These categories overlap and cannot always be separated from the sources. Treating every act of resistance as political opposition would overstate what the evidence supports, and treating it as mere misbehavior would adopt the administrators' framing.

Parents and relatives took part in resistance. They hid children from recruiters, refused to return them after summer visits, wrote petitions, travelled to schools to retrieve children, and argued with agents and missionaries. Some families kept a child at home during an illness or an important ceremony and risked consequences in doing so. Such actions are documented in correspondence, agent reports, and later testimony. They show that the boundary between family and school was contested, and that families continued to assert claims over their children.

Resistance did not mean that institutional power was weak. Authorities had resources that families lacked, including legal power, control of rations or funding, and the ability to punish. In many cases resistance succeeded only in limited ways, and its costs could be severe. The evidence for resistance should be used to show that children and families acted, not to suggest that the system was ineffective. Its effects on families remained serious even where individuals resisted.

Cause and Effect

How Separation, Discipline, and Institutional Isolation Produced Resistance

Long-term causes

Strong family and community bonds; Indigenous expectations about children's care and instruction; and a policy aimed at replacing those relationships with institutional authority.

Short-term causes

Homesickness, strict discipline, language restrictions, illness, and restrictions on contact with relatives.

Triggers

Specific events such as punishment, news of a relative's illness, a denied request to go home, or the loss of a possession or a friend.

Effects

Everyday refusal, concealment, mutual aid, and, in some cases, flight, with outcomes ranging from successful return to severe harm.

Historical complexity: Resistance was shaped by age, distance, and risk, and not every child had the same options. Acts of resistance often responded to immediate conditions rather than to a general program of opposition.

Section 88.12

Illness, Death, Bereavement, and the Case of Chanie Wenjack

The most severe consequences of institutional schooling included the illness and death of children. Chapter 87 treats the public-health record of the schools and Chapters 127 and 128 address modern investigations. This section considers how loss affected children, siblings, parents, and communities, and examines one carefully documented case.

Illness was a real fear for families, and government reports, such as the 1907 Bryce report, confirmed that it was warranted. When a child fell ill or died at a distant school, a family might receive news after days or weeks, if at all. Letters in school files show parents asking about their children's health and, in some cases, learning of a death by correspondence. A family's grief could be compounded by the distance that prevented them from attending the child, travelling with the body, or conducting the ceremonies their community expected. Surviving siblings and fellow students also experienced these losses, and survivor testimony includes recollections of children who became ill or died.

Chanie Wenjack was a twelve-year-old Anishinaabe boy from Marten Falls First Nation in northern Ontario. He was a student at the Cecilia Jeffrey Indian Residential School, in northwestern Ontario, hundreds of kilometres from his home. According to Parks Canada, which has designated him a National Historic Person, he left the school on October 16, 1966, trying to return to his family, and his body was found on October 23 along a railway line. He died during the journey. The school's records and early press accounts used the name Charlie; his family's name for him, Chanie, is the one used today.

Several features of the case illustrate broader patterns. His home was far from the school, a distance that made visits and communication difficult and made the journey he attempted dangerous. He tried to go home, which shows the importance of family attachment to a child in his circumstances. An inquest followed his death, and its jury is reported to have made recommendations concerning the care of Indigenous children in residential schools; students should consult Parks Canada and the other sources in the Source Register for the details of the investigation and its findings. The case drew public attention in the late 1960s, including through journalism and later through artistic and educational works whose history Chapter 127 treats.

The later official record adds a limited but important public response to his death. The coroner's inquest, held in November 1966, directed attention to the residential-school arrangements that put children far from their homes. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission's historical study reports that most jurors' recommendations criticized weaknesses in the system and favored alternatives such as day schools; where residences continued, it reports recommendations for fewer students and more staff. These findings should be attributed to the inquest as summarized by the commission, not treated as proof of everything Chanie experienced personally. Parks Canada's account also explains that his family sought the return of his body to Ogoki Post and that his mother and sisters ultimately accompanied him home. That family dimension is essential to understanding the case as more than an episode in institutional policy or subsequent national remembrance.

The historian's responsibilities in discussing the case are specific. Chanie should be presented as a child with a family and community, not only as a symbol of tragedy. His relatives, his home community, and the people who searched for him and mourned him are part of the story. His death occurred in 1966, within the lifetimes of many people alive today, which is a reminder that residential schooling was not confined to the distant past. The record does not allow confident statements about his thoughts or what he experienced in his last days, and this textbook does not speculate about them. Graphic or imagined descriptions would go beyond the evidence and would be disrespectful to the family.

Broader bereavement associated with institutional schooling shaped community memory. Families who lost children and did not learn the circumstances, or who could not bring a child home for burial, carried those losses for decades, and in many communities these memories have fed present-day demands for investigation and accountability, as later chapters explain. Treating loss as part of family and community history, and not only as a statistic of institutional mortality, shows what the system cost those whose relatives it harmed.

Section 88.13

Returning Home: Reunions, Language Barriers, and Changes in Family Relationships

Children returned home at different times and in different ways. Some came back each summer, some at the end of a term of years, some because of illness or family request, and some because they had finished or left school. For many, the return was an emotional event, anticipated for months or years. The sources describe a wide range of responses.

Reunions with parents, siblings, grandparents, and communities could bring joy. Survivors and students recalled the pleasure of seeing relatives, eating familiar food, and returning to places they knew. At the same time, differences could be apparent. A child who had spent years in an institution might have changed in dress, speech, work habits, or expectations. Language was often central: a child who had been discouraged from speaking an Indigenous language, or who had had little chance to use it, might have difficulty speaking with relatives, and parents might find a child's English or French unfamiliar. Some returning students struggled to resume tasks that their families expected of them, such as hunting, farming, or household work, because they had been trained for different work or had missed the seasonal instruction that would have prepared them.

Returning children also encountered a community that had changed. Family members might have died, moved, or taken on new responsibilities. Land, economic conditions, and local leadership could have shifted. Returning students sometimes found themselves between worlds, trusted fully by neither the school-oriented outside nor the community they had left. Others re-entered community life with little difficulty. The evidence shows that outcomes depended on the length of absence, the strength of family ties, the community's response, and the individual.

The pace and meaning of return were also shaped by the age at which a student left and came home. A child who spent one school year away might have missed a season of work, ceremonies, and ordinary conversation; another who remained for several years could return to a changed household or to siblings who had themselves grown older. These possibilities must be established from particular recollections rather than generalized into a single pattern. Letters written before departure, school enrollment registers, and records of return travel can sometimes reconstruct the chronology, while testimony from students and relatives can illuminate the relationship after return. A student could use new skills acquired at school and simultaneously feel anger about the circumstances in which they were taught. Such complexity does not negate coercion. It explains why the history of homecoming is not adequately represented as either effortless reintegration or permanent separation in every case.

The chapter's source base includes both students who remained strongly connected to family traditions and those who encountered difficulties reestablishing those connections. In Brenda J. Child's study of Ojibwe families, letters and family histories show students and parents maintaining connections across separation, and many returning students resumed their place in family and community life. In testimony collected from Canadian survivors, some describe estrangement from parents or grandparents, shame about language, or conflict at home, while others describe being welcomed and gradually relearning what they had missed. These accounts do not cancel one another. They show that return was a process, not a single event.

Homecoming was often a process rather than an event. Relatives might restore everyday routines through shared meals, conversation, work, travel, and time with younger children. A returning student might first participate by listening or observing and later resume speaking a language or undertaking responsibilities learned earlier. In other families, prolonged absence had made the practical work of reconnection more difficult. Historians should be careful about the phrase 'forgotten culture': a person might retain knowledge but hesitate to speak, lack opportunities to practice, or need assistance to recover a skill. The distinction matters because it keeps the agency of the returning child and the teaching work of relatives visible. Where testimony describes estrangement, the account should remain specific; where it documents renewed connection, that history deserves equal precision and attention.

Elders and relatives actively shaped the process. Grandparents taught language and stories to returning children. Parents reintroduced them to subsistence work and ceremonies. Siblings who had stayed at home helped bring returning children back into community life. These roles are discussed further in §88.14. The experience of return demonstrates that children's identities were not determined by schooling alone, but by continuing relationships that could be rebuilt or strained.

Not every return was voluntary or lasting. Some students returned and were sent back, and others left school and later reentered. Some did not return home at all, whether because they stayed in the school's region, entered employment, or died. Historians should avoid treating "return" as a uniform category. The chapter's concern is with how those who did return, and the families who received them, experienced the change.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Home Before School, Home After School, and the Reconstruction of Belonging

What Changed

  • Many returning children had different language abilities, habits, and expectations than before.
  • Family roles and teaching responsibilities could be disrupted by long absence.
  • Some students and families felt estranged or uncertain, and some communities faced altered local conditions.

What Stayed the Same

  • Kinship ties, obligations, and affection continued, and many relationships were resumed.
  • Elders, parents, and communities continued to teach and to include returning children in ceremonial and everyday life.
  • Many students retained or relearned languages, names, and knowledge.

When and how change occurred: Change was gradual and uneven, depended on the length of absence and the school, and was reversible in some respects. Who experienced change most directly: children who had been away longest and families with few intermediaries; those with strong local kin networks and continuing language use often experienced greater continuity. Why the pattern matters: It shows belonging as something rebuilt through family and community effort, and it counters both the claim that return erased the effects of school and the claim that return necessarily completed estrangement.

Section 88.14

Parents, Grandparents, Elders, and the Persistence of Cultural Education

Cultural education did not stop when children went to school. It continued in the families and communities that remained, and it resumed in new forms when children returned. Explaining cultural survival requires identifying who did the teaching, what they taught, and in what circumstances.

Elders and grandparents played central roles. In many communities they were the primary holders of language, stories, genealogies, ceremonial knowledge, place names, and ecological understanding. They taught younger relatives through conversation, demonstration, and participation in tasks. When parents had been in boarding or residential schools themselves, grandparents sometimes took on more of the teaching of language and tradition, a pattern that survivors and community members have described. Where grandparents were no longer living, other relatives or community members filled the role in some cases.

Specific kinds of knowledge passed through specific practices. Agricultural knowledge was taught in the fields and gardens; food practices, such as gathering, hunting, fishing, preparing, and preserving, in seasonal work; spiritual teachings in ceremonies and in personal instruction; kinship responsibilities in daily interactions and in the obligations of clan and family. Summer returns allowed children to participate in the seasonal round, and household activities and ceremonies became occasions for reconnection. Where schools allowed summer visits or where children lived near home, these opportunities were more frequent.

Women and other caregivers had an important place in maintaining networks of kinship and cultural knowledge. Mothers, grandmothers, aunts, and older sisters often cared for children, taught language, and preserved family histories, and they maintained correspondence with absent children, sent food and clothing, and arranged visits. The letters studied by Brenda J. Child show mothers and other relatives actively participating in the lives of children at school. The labor of maintaining family connections across distance was substantial and often fell to women.

Family teaching also depended on repeated access to places and activities. Place names carried accounts of travel, relationships, and earlier events; work at a fishing station, garden, or gathering place could bring together older and younger generations in a setting where instruction was practical and social at once. The educational task was not confined to a formal lesson. It might involve recognizing when to harvest, how to distribute a catch, or why a particular story was connected to a landscape. Returning children could participate in such activities even if their school experience had reduced their fluency in the community language. The survival of knowledge therefore depended partly on continued access to land and on the relatives who made time and space for participation. This connects family history to the earlier chapters on reserves, reservations, and land dispossession without retelling their institutional histories.

Some Indigenous communities retained their languages at high levels despite intensive residential schooling. Their experience shows that outcomes were not uniform. Reasons for retention included the community's size and isolation, the number of speakers in each generation, the presence of language-supporting institutions such as ceremonies and church services in the Indigenous language, the age at which children entered school, and families' decisions about what to speak at home. Historians have not identified a single explanation, and this textbook avoids claiming one. Studying such communities helps clarify how transmission worked and what supported it.

Cultural survival in these accounts is explained by concrete practices and institutions: households that spoke the language, elders who told the stories, ceremonies that continued, communities that gathered. It is not an abstract quality of resilience. Treating survival as a matter of personal strength alone can obscure the work of families and communities and can imply that those who lost knowledge lacked strength, which is not an accurate or fair conclusion. The continuation of Indigenous knowledge depended on many people doing particular things over many years.

Section 88.15

Parenthood, Family Relationships, and the Complexity of Intergenerational Effects

Former students grew up and many became parents. Historians and survivors have asked how institutional childhood influenced later family life. The topic is sensitive and the evidence needs careful handling, because generalizations can wrong both survivors and their descendants.

Survivor testimony describes a range of experiences. Some former students recalled that years of institutional life, with limited opportunities for close family relationships, had made parenting difficult. Some described difficulty expressing affection, uncertainty about how to raise children, or the influence of institutional discipline on their own parenting. Others said they had deliberately parented differently from how they were treated, protecting their children, teaching them languages and traditions, or insisting on family closeness. Some described healing through community, ceremony, counselling, and later education. These accounts, recorded in many cases decades after the events, are attributed testimony and should be treated as such.

Scholars have developed concepts to describe how the effects of institutional schooling may have passed across generations, often called intergenerational effects or intergenerational trauma. These interpretations draw on survivor testimony, community research, and, in some work, psychological and health studies. They provide frameworks for understanding patterns that communities have described. They are later scholarly interpretations, however, and must be distinguished from direct testimony. They do not show that all descendants experienced identical outcomes. The chapter avoids deterministic claims and does not assign clinical diagnoses to historical individuals whose medical histories are unknown.

Documented mechanisms can be described without overgeneralizing. Separation limited the opportunities children had to observe and learn parenting within family settings, a point survivors themselves make. Language loss in some families reduced communication between generations. Institutional discipline and, in documented cases, abuse, affected children's later lives. Fear, silence, and shame about school experiences kept some families from speaking about them. These mechanisms operated differently in different families and communities, and many families also built strong relationships and transmitted knowledge. The evidence supports both the existence of harm and the diversity of outcomes.

The surviving evidence also warns against treating a family's later difficulties as automatic proof of a single cause. Poverty, compulsory schooling, land loss, discriminatory administration, and ordinary changes in household circumstance could operate together. A former student's account may explicitly connect a parenting difficulty to school separation, while another may describe care learned from an aunt, grandfather, or other relative after returning. Those distinctions should be retained instead of merged into a general diagnosis. The historical question is how a specific person's opportunities to observe, practice, and receive support in family relationships were changed. Webstad's published account, for example, identifies the influence of her own residential-school experience and names her aunt Agness Jack as someone who helped her raise her son. Her account offers an attributed example of both disruption and family assistance, not an empirical rule about all survivors.

Former students also became community leaders, teachers, writers, political advocates, and parents who sought to protect their own children from similar experiences. Some, like Zitkála-Šá, used their education in Indigenous political causes. Others were among the parents who petitioned for community schools or who supported language programs later in the twentieth century. Treating former students solely as damaged would ignore that agency and would reduce Indigenous families to damaged institutions, which the evidence does not warrant.

Healing has been a recurring theme in survivor accounts and community initiatives. Families and communities have used ceremonies, support groups, and cultural programs to address the legacies of schooling. These developments belong largely to later chapters, but they show that the history of family relationships continued after the schools. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission documented some of this work, and later chapters examine its political consequences.

Section 88.16

Regional and Gender Differences: First Nations, Inuit, Métis, and American Indian Families

Family experiences varied widely, and this section compares selected cases to show how distance, institutional type, region, language, gender, and age shaped them. The comparisons are illustrative, not exhaustive.

In the United States, American Indian families in the Plains, Southwest, and Great Lakes encountered boarding schools of several types: off-reservation institutions such as Carlisle and Chilocco, reservation boarding schools, and day schools. Students from Plains nations, such as the Lakota and Dakota, could be sent east to distant schools or to nearby agency schools. Southwestern families, such as the Mohave near Fort Mojave, sometimes had schools within a day's travel but under federal control. Great Lakes families, including Ojibwe families studied by Brenda J. Child, maintained connections through letters and visits despite separation and sometimes used the schools' seasonal calendars to arrange return. The regional pattern was not uniform and changed as federal policy shifted after the Meriam Report.

In Canada, First Nations families on the Prairies and the Pacific Coast faced residential schools operated largely by churches under federal funding. Prairie families often lived within reach of schools but under a system in which compulsory attendance and agent authority were strong. On the Pacific Coast and in the British Columbia interior, schools such as Kamloops and St. Joseph's Mission served many nations with distinct languages, and survivors from those schools, as in Haig-Brown's study, described both hardship and strong peer relationships. The Anishinaabe families of northern Ontario, such as those of Marten Falls, often faced very long distances to school, as Chanie Wenjack's case shows.

Inuit experiences differed in timing and distance. Sustained federal and church schooling in the Canadian Arctic expanded mainly from the 1950s, and Inuit children were often sent to hostels, residences, or schools in settlements far from their home camps, sometimes travelling long distances by air or sea. Families who lived seasonally on the land could be separated from children for months, and language and subsistence knowledge were especially at issue. Chapter 100 discusses the Inuit twentieth-century context in more detail. Because the period of distant schooling began later than in southern Canada, many Inuit survivors' experiences fall in the 1950s through 1970s.

For northern families, the geography of contact could involve practical constraints not always present at southern schools. A child sent to a distant hostel might be unable to return for a short visit, while a change in transportation service or weather could alter a planned journey. Inuit family life and the knowledge of travel, animals, seasonal conditions, and language varied among communities, so it is inaccurate to treat all northern experiences as identical. Histories of Inuit schooling must also account for the later period of institutional expansion and for differences between day schooling, settlement hostels, and distant residences. A comparative study should identify the school, community of origin, route, and years of attendance before generalizing. Otherwise geography becomes an evocative description of distance rather than a demonstrable explanation of how family communication and return were affected.

Métis children occupied a distinctive position. As Chapter 52 explains, Métis are distinct Indigenous peoples, and their relationship to the federal Indian Act system differed from that of First Nations. Many Métis children nonetheless entered residential institutions, sometimes because of local circumstances, church arrangements, or family hardship, and others attended provincial or other schools. The recognition and documentation of Métis experience has followed a different path from that of First Nations experience, and Chapter 127 considers the later accountability process.

Gender categories in school files were usually administrative labels defining dormitories, work assignments, and sometimes sports or visiting rules. They do not explain all the identities or family responsibilities children held in their own nations. Nor can historians infer from a general girls' or boys' curriculum that every student performed the same work or endured the same discipline. Comparative evidence must connect regulations to what a particular institution did and, where possible, to what students later recalled. Sibling-separation testimony shows one way the division affected relationships. School records about labor or training reveal another. Reading them together helps distinguish the educational goals officials announced from the patterns of care, solidarity, and hardship children experienced within sex-segregated institutions.

Gender shaped daily life. Schools commonly assigned boys and girls to different labor, dormitories, and expectations, and sex segregation affected sibling contact (§88.6). Girls were frequently trained for domestic work, and boys for farm and trades work. Girls and boys faced different risks and pressures, and survivors have described gendered forms of discipline and harm. Women and girls also performed much of the labor of maintaining family relationships, as seen in letters and testimony. The sources do not allow a single account of gender differences, but they show that gender mattered.

The relationship between residential schooling and child welfare changed over time in Canada. From the 1950s, provincial child-welfare systems expanded, and the TRC documented that residential institutions increasingly served child-welfare purposes as well as educational ones, with children placed because of family circumstances as determined by officials. The removal of Indigenous children into foster care and adoption, often called the Sixties Scoop, developed alongside this change and is a distinct subject with its own legal and administrative arrangements. This chapter introduces it only as a transition, and Chapter 131 treats it fully. Students should not conflate the two systems, even though their histories overlapped and affected some of the same families.

Division V — Cultural Survival, Community Renewal, and Indigenous Control of the Future

Section 88.17

Language, Storytelling, Ceremony, and the Continuity of Indigenous Knowledge

Indigenous languages and knowledge survived assimilationist policy because people continued to use and teach them. The mechanisms were varied: household conversation, storytelling, naming, songs, ceremonies, relationships with land, and community gatherings. This section examines those practices and the people who sustained them.

Storytelling carried history, ethics, humor, and knowledge of place. In many communities particular stories were told at particular times and by particular people, and children learned by listening and, in time, by retelling. Names, whether given in ceremonies or passed from relatives, tied individuals to families and histories. Songs and ceremonies maintained relationships among people and with the land and other beings. These practices could be carried on in homes, at community gatherings, and, in some places, in defiance of prohibitions. In the United States and Canada, governments at various times restricted ceremonies, as earlier chapters document, and some communities continued them privately or adapted them. The result was that ceremonial life survived, but sometimes in altered or reduced form, and in some cases was later revived from the memory of elders.

Older and younger generations maintained knowledge through direct participation. A child who accompanied a grandmother to gather plants learned names, uses, places, and protocols in a way that a classroom could not provide. A young person who sat with elders at a gathering learned how to listen, when to speak, and how disagreements were handled. This is why interruption of contact was so consequential, and why the renewal of contact, even for short periods, mattered. It is also why teachers, writers, parents, and community leaders who made time and space for these practices played central roles.

Boarding and residential school experiences also produced new relationships. Students from different nations lived together, often for years, and formed friendships, shared English, and in some cases developed a sense of shared experience as Indigenous people. Historians sometimes call this intertribal or pan-Indian identity. It contributed to political and cultural networks that later supported advocacy, as when former students became leaders in organizations that addressed Indigenous rights. Such networks did not replace national identities. A person could be Dakota, Ojibwe, or Cree and also part of wider relationships, and the evidence shows that students often held both.

Survival involved adaptation and, at times, deliberate reconstruction. Communities adopted new materials, forms, and technologies, and re-created practices that had been interrupted, sometimes relying on written records, recordings, or the memory of a few elders. Revival is not inferior to unbroken continuity, and it should not be dismissed as inauthentic. At the same time, historians should not claim that everything was preserved unchanged, since loss was real. Where knowledge was lost with the death of elders or the interruption of transmission, communities faced real constraints in recovering it. Chapter 123 and Chapter 146 take up these later efforts.

The concept of survivance, associated with the Anishinaabe scholar Gerald Vizenor, provides a way of describing this combination of continuity, adaptation, and active presence. It is a useful analytical term, but, as the safeguards for this chapter note, it must not be used as an excuse for abuse or to minimize the scale of documented harm. Indigenous continuity and institutional responsibility are separate historical questions, and an account can affirm both.

Historical Vocabulary
Survivance, Community Continuity, and Self-Determination

Survivance is a term used by Gerald Vizenor and other scholars to describe Indigenous continuity as more than survival: an active presence, creative adaptation, and refusal of narratives of disappearance or victimhood alone.

Community continuity refers to the persistence of communities as political, cultural, and social entities across generations, including through the work of families, elders, and institutions.

Self-determination is the principle that Indigenous nations and communities should make decisions about their own affairs, including education and child-rearing. In the United States it is also the name of a policy era and of the 1975 Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act (Chapter 114).

Why it matters: These terms help describe how families and communities preserved identity and increasingly regained authority.

Potential misunderstanding: Survivance does not mean that harm was minor or that survivors should be expected to be resilient. It describes what communities did in conditions they did not choose.

Section 88.18

Family Advocacy, Political Organization, and the Demand for Indigenous Educational Control

The individual petitions and letters examined earlier were acts of family advocacy. Over the twentieth century, this advocacy grew into collective political demands that Indigenous communities control the education of their own children. Chapter 87 treats the institutional and legislative history of school reform. The question here is why control of children's education became a family and community priority.

The answer lies in the earlier experiences described in this chapter. Parents had learned that decisions about their children were made by others, that distance and rules limited their influence, and that schooling affected language, belonging, and health. Former students who had become parents and leaders brought their own memories into political discussion. When governments began to reconsider policy, as in the Meriam Report's critique of American Indian education in 1928 and the Canadian parliamentary review of Indian affairs in 1946 to 1948, Indigenous people were among those arguing for change, though their influence was limited at first. After the Second World War, expanding provincial schooling and shifts in federal policy changed the options, and Indigenous organizations became more active.

In the United States, a movement for tribal participation in education developed through the mid-twentieth century. The Rough Rock Demonstration School on the Navajo Nation, which opened in the 1960s, is often cited as an early example of community-controlled schooling. Federal legislation in the early 1970s and the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975 provided a legal basis for tribes to contract to administer federal programs, including education, in their communities. Tribally controlled schools and colleges expanded in the following years (Chapters 114 and 124).

In Canada, Blue Quills in Alberta became a landmark. In 1970, families and leaders, including community members from the surrounding First Nations, occupied the former residential school facility after the federal government announced plans to close it and shift students to other schools. The protest led, in 1971, to the transfer of the school's administration to a First Nations education authority, and Blue Quills is widely regarded as one of the first schools in Canada under First Nations control. In 1972, the National Indian Brotherhood issued its policy paper Indian Control of Indian Education, which argued that parents should have responsibility for their children's schooling and that local control and language and cultural programs were essential. The federal government accepted the policy in principle in 1973, though implementation was uneven and continued to be contested.

The Blue Quills chronology gives concrete form to that demand for authority. The University of Calgary's historical timeline identifies a seventeen-day sit-in in 1970 organized by the Blue Quills Native Education Council and places its effective assumption of school control in July 1971, with federal funding continuing. That distinction avoids compressing political protest, negotiation, and actual administration into a single event. The 1972 National Indian Brotherhood policy was a separate national statement, and its acceptance in principle in 1973 did not transfer every school or implement every demand immediately. At the community level, the issue remained whether parents and Indigenous leaders could decide the language, curriculum, staffing, and daily relationship of the school to families. This was a reversal of the direction of authority examined at the beginning of the chapter, even where fiscal or regulatory constraints remained.

The central argument of the 1972 paper was grounded in family. It insisted that parents' authority over their children's education was a basic right, and it linked education to cultural identity and community development. This view connects directly to earlier family complaints about separation, loss of language, and distance. It also shows continuity between a father's 1890 letter requesting a son's return and a national organization's demand that communities decide how their children are taught: both asserted that children belonged first to families and communities.

Reclaiming educational authority was simultaneously a political, cultural, and family-centered objective. It was political because it demanded recognition of Indigenous nations' authority. It was cultural because it aimed at protecting and teaching language, history, and values. It was family-centered because it responded to the experience of parents and grandparents who had seen children removed or changed. The chapter's account of this movement is brief by design, since Chapters 87, 114, and 124 treat the legislative and institutional details.

Section 88.19

Phyllis Webstad, Family Memory, and the Continuing Significance of Childhood Experience

Phyllis Webstad's account connects a child's first day at residential school to a family relationship and to later public memory. A member of the Stswecem'c Xgat'tem First Nation in the British Columbia interior, she attended St. Joseph's Mission near Williams Lake during the 1973–1974 school year, when she was six years old. Her case shows that residential schooling operated within living memory and that its effects were remembered within families.

Webstad has described how her grandmother bought her a new orange shirt for her first day at the school, and how the shirt was taken from her when she arrived. She has explained that she was excited to go to school and proud of the shirt, and that its removal came to represent the loss of what had been hers. The account is her own public narrative, published by the Orange Shirt Society, and this chapter relies on it rather than on invented dialogue or third-person psychological speculation.

The grandmother-granddaughter relationship is central to the account. In Webstad's telling, her grandmother, a woman of modest means, chose the shirt as a gift for a child going to a new place. The gift expressed care, pride, and preparation, and many Indigenous families in similar situations made comparable efforts to equip children for school. A seemingly ordinary possession could carry the family's affection and the child's sense of herself. Its removal, a procedure that was routine in school intake, took on the meaning of loss, which is why Webstad's narrative has resonated with other survivors and families.

Webstad's own account includes a further family chronology relevant to the chapter's central question. She identifies her grandmother and mother as former residential-school students and describes how family experience affected her understanding of parenthood. In the same narrative she credits the assistance of her aunt, Agness Jack, when she raised her son. These details should not be read as an invitation to diagnose relatives or supply emotions they did not record. They instead show how one person's retrospective testimony connects several generations and gives credit to a specific caregiver. The grandmother's purchase of the orange shirt is an act of preparation and affection; the aunt's later assistance is another instance of family support. The two moments belong to different stages of her life, and the link between them is established by her own public narration, not by an invented model of a uniformly damaged or uniformly resilient family.

The account also illustrates the difference between a childhood event and its later significance. The events took place in 1973 and 1974. Webstad's telling of them, and the subsequent development of Orange Shirt Day, occurred decades later. Orange Shirt Day began in 2013 and became a broader commemoration, a development that Chapter 127 treats in full. This chapter uses her narrative as a source for a family relationship and a child's arrival, not as a history of the commemoration.

Her account has limits that students should recognize. It is one person's recollection, told in a public setting for a purpose that includes education and remembrance, and told more than forty years after the event. It does not describe all children's arrivals, and it does not establish what school staff intended with respect to each child's possessions. It is nevertheless valuable evidence of how a child and a family understood the experience, and it shows the long reach of childhood events in family and community memory.

A Grandmother's Gift, a Child's Expectations, and the Meaning of Personal Possessions

Clothing, toys, and small belongings were part of how families prepared children for school and how children carried home with them. Parents and grandparents often had limited resources, and a new item of clothing represented a significant choice. For children, such objects could be tied to relatives, to pride, and to the sense of a new beginning. When institutions removed personal possessions, as was common at intake, children lost these links at the very moment they entered an unfamiliar place.

Section 88.20

Indigenous Children and Families across the Assimilation Era: Separation, Survival, and Renewed Authority

The chapter's central question was how government interventions altered Indigenous childhood and family relationships, and why Indigenous identities and institutions persisted. The evidence supports a combined answer. The United States and Canada built and enforced systems that removed children from their families for long periods and placed them under institutional authority, with foreseeable consequences for language, kinship, health, and belonging. Children and families nonetheless acted: they wrote, petitioned, resisted, taught, remembered, and eventually organized politically to reclaim authority over education.

Comparing the two countries reveals similarities and differences. In both, schooling was linked to broader policies of assimilation and administrative control, and in both, distance, discipline, and language restrictions affected families. Canada's system relied heavily on church administration under federal funding and extended later into the twentieth century, with residential schools operating into the 1990s, while the American system shifted earlier toward day schools and reform after the 1920s. Canadian compulsory-attendance powers, and the later overlap with child welfare, shaped family options differently from American regulations. Documentary evidence is also different: the American record includes extensive digitized student and family correspondence, while the Canadian record is especially rich in survivor testimony gathered by the TRC. Each evidentiary base illuminates different features, which is why this chapter uses both.

The relationships among family separation, language transmission, school discipline, resistance, reintegration, and political organization are best understood as connected. Separation reduced opportunities for transmission, and discipline reinforced language restrictions. These conditions generated resistance in many forms and made letters and visits precious. Reintegration after school depended on families and elders. Memories of separation fueled the political demand for community control, and success in that demand supported cultural renewal. None of these links was automatic, and each varied by community and period, but together they trace a historical pattern.

Survivor-authored histories and family archives are essential for understanding a past that governments documented largely from their own point of view. Zitkála-Šá's essays, family letters, TRC statements, and public narratives like Webstad's allow historians to hear voices that official records suppress. These sources require critical reading, but they are not supplementary. They are central evidence for the history of childhood and family in this period.

The chapter has avoided framing Indigenous history solely through loss. It has also avoided using cultural survival to minimize documented harm. Both statements need to be made together. Children experienced homesickness, punishment, illness, and bereavement, and some died. Families endured separation and uncertainty. Languages and knowledge were interrupted. Governments and churches bore responsibility for creating and operating these systems. At the same time, parents, grandparents, elders, and children maintained relationships and knowledge, and Indigenous political institutions asserted authority over education and community life. A responsible account holds these facts together.

Historical Debate

Did Boarding and Residential Schools Primarily Destroy Indigenous Family Continuity, Transform It, or Produce Different Outcomes Depending on Community, Institution, and Historical Circumstances?

The question: Historians and communities disagree about how to characterize the long-term effect of institutional schooling on Indigenous families. The disagreement concerns emphasis and interpretation more than the underlying facts of separation and harm, which are well documented.

Interpretation A — Destruction and rupture: Some scholars and many survivors emphasize that policies were designed to break family transmission of language and identity and that they caused severe, lasting disruption of kinship, language, and community life, with consequences extending across generations.
Interpretation B — Transformation and adaptation: Other historians, drawing on family correspondence and student-centered studies such as those by Brenda J. Child and K. Tsianina Lomawaima, emphasize that students and families adapted, used schools for their own purposes, built new networks, and retained or rebuilt cultural identities, so that institutions changed Indigenous families without destroying them.
Interpretation C — Varied outcomes: A third approach stresses that outcomes differed by nation, school, period, gender, age, and family circumstance, and that generalization risks erasing important differences.

Evidence: Interpretations draw on family and student letters, school records, memoirs, survivor testimony, community histories, and demographic and linguistic evidence. Letters of the sort studied by Child show persistent ties. TRC testimony documents severe harm and loss. Language retention varies by community.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns emphasis and interpretation more than evidence: how much weight to give documented harm compared with documented adaptation, and whether a single term such as "destroy" or "transform" can capture varied experience. The positions are not mutually exclusive and many scholars combine them.

Why it matters: How the question is answered affects how histories of Indigenous communities are told, whether loss or agency is foregrounded, and how responsibility is understood. A purely adaptive account can understate coercion and harm, and a purely destructive account can understate Indigenous action.

Student task: Select two communities or two sources from this chapter and argue which interpretation best fits the evidence for each, identifying what additional evidence would test your conclusion.

Indigenous children and families were not simply the objects of state policy. They were historical actors whose decisions and relationships shaped the continuation of Indigenous communities. Their experiences set the stage for the later chapters of Volume VIII and for the political and legal developments of the twentieth century, including the movements for self-determination examined in Volumes IX and X, the investigations treated in Chapters 127 and 128, and the contests over child welfare in Chapter 131.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined how assimilationist schooling and related interventions affected Indigenous childhood, family relationships, and cultural transmission in the United States and Canada, and how Indigenous children, parents, elders, and communities responded. It complemented Chapter 87's institutional history by focusing on people: the children who were sent or taken to school, the families who made or contested those decisions, and the communities that carried on.

Indigenous childhood was embedded in kinship networks that included parents, grandparents, siblings, aunts, uncles, clan relatives, and elders. Through work, play, ceremony, storytelling, and language, these relatives taught children how to live and who they were as members of particular nations. The family was therefore an institution of cultural and political continuity, which explains why policymakers targeted it and why removal mattered.

Children entered schools through recruitment, treaty provisions, compulsory attendance, and administrative pressure. Parental decisions were constrained by poverty, hunger, isolation, and the threat of penalties, so apparent agreement cannot be equated with free consent. At the same time, some families sought education and used the system for their own purposes. The transfer of authority from families to institutions was a foreseeable consequence of the policies, not a side effect.

Journeys to school and arrival rituals marked the first experience of separation. Distance, language barriers, uncertainty, haircuts, uniforms, renaming, and the removal of possessions affected children's autonomy and identity. Zitkála-Šá's account of her 1884 entry to White's Institute, written in 1900, showed how an adult author interpreted the meaning of such practices, and the chapter emphasized that school officials and children could understand the same practices very differently.

Within schools, children confronted language restrictions, sibling separation, labor, illness, and discipline, while also forming friendships and finding strategies of survival. Letters and petitions, such as the Cornelius exchange of 1890 and Curtis Etsitty's 1929 letter, document how families sought to maintain contact and how institutions responded. These sources, written at the time, differ in kind from the retrospective survivor testimony published by the TRC, and students must distinguish between the date of an experience and the date it was recorded.

Children and families resisted through refusal, concealment, mutual aid, flight, petition, and continued use of Indigenous languages. Resistance had costs and risks, and it varied in motive. The documented death of Chanie Wenjack in 1966 after he left the Cecilia Jeffrey school showed the danger of distance and separation and was treated with attention to his family and community and without speculation.

Return home was a process of reunion, adjustment, and sometimes estrangement. Elders, grandparents, parents, and women caregivers continued cultural education through language, stories, work, and ceremony, and some communities retained their languages at high levels. Intergenerational effects were real but varied, and the chapter distinguished direct testimony from later scholarly interpretation and avoided deterministic or diagnostic claims.

Experiences differed among American Indian, First Nations, Inuit, and Métis families by region, institution, period, and gender. Canada's expanding child-welfare administration from the 1950s overlapped with residential schooling, but the Sixties Scoop is a distinct subject treated in Chapter 131. The comparison showed why generalization about "the Indian family" or "the school experience" would be inaccurate.

Cultural survival depended on continuing practices and institutions rather than on abstract resilience, and it included adaptation and deliberate reconstruction. Survivance, as a concept, describes active continuity without minimizing harm. Family advocacy grew into collective demands for Indigenous control of education, from the Blue Quills transfer and the 1972 National Indian Brotherhood policy statement to the 1975 U.S. Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act.

Phyllis Webstad's account of her 1973 arrival at St. Joseph's Mission connected a grandmother's gift to a child's experience and to later memory, and it showed that residential schooling was within living memory. The debate about whether schools destroyed, transformed, or produced varied outcomes for family continuity illustrated that documented harm and documented agency must be held together. Indigenous children and families were historical actors whose decisions shaped the continuation of their communities.

Children and Families in the Boarding and Residential School Era: A Comparative Documentary Study

This project asks you to analyze one American and one Canadian family-centered source collection and to compare what contemporary correspondence and retrospective testimony can establish.

  1. Choose collections. For the American study, use the Carlisle family correspondence or the Fort Mojave letters. For the Canadian study, use an identifiable survivor account from the National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it moves the history of assimilationist schooling from institutions to relationships. Policies, statutes, and budgets are necessary parts of that history, but the lives they changed were lived in households, dormitories, letters, journeys, and homecomings. Seeing the system through children and families shows both its coercive aims and the ways Indigenous people continued to act within and against it.

The chapter also reveals how historians work with unequal evidence. Governments recorded administrative decisions, while families and children left letters, memoirs, and, later, testimony. Reading these sources together requires attention to who created them, when, and why. Distinguishing a contemporary letter from a retrospective account is not a technical detail but a condition of honest interpretation.

Continuities survived beyond the chapter. Family advocacy led to community control of education, language revitalization, and the later accountability processes treated in Chapters 123, 124, 127, and 128, and the question of Indigenous authority over children returns in Chapter 131. The ideas that the family is a political and cultural institution, and that communities should decide how their children are taught, run through the history of the later twentieth century and the present.

Within the larger textbook, Chapter 88 establishes that Indigenous children and families experienced significant, historically documented harm under assimilationist policies and remained agents in the preservation, reconstruction, and transmission of Indigenous cultures and political identities. Holding both truths together is the chapter's central contribution and a model for the volumes that follow.
