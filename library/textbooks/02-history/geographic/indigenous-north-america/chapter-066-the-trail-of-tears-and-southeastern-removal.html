The Trail of Tears and Southeastern Removal

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

Between 1831 and the early 1840s, tens of thousands of Choctaw, Muscogee, Chickasaw, Seminole, and Cherokee people were compelled to leave homelands that had sustained their ancestors for centuries. They did not leave in a single column, at a single time, or under a single set of conditions. Their removals were many separate events, and what followed them was not an ending.

In the winter of 1831, a French visitor named Alexis de Tocqueville watched at the Mississippi River crossing near Memphis as a party of Choctaw people waited for ferries in bitter cold. His brief, much-quoted account describes elders, infants, and the sick, and an extraordinary silence among those who crossed. The scene is valuable because it is one of the few contemporary descriptions of the first mass removal by an outside observer. It is also limited. Tocqueville did not speak Choctaw, he saw a single crossing on a single day, and he recorded what an astonished foreign traveler could see rather than what the people crossing felt, said to one another, or planned. The historian's task in this chapter is to move from such glimpses toward a fuller reconstruction, drawing on Indigenous testimony, tribal histories, government records, and the physical landscape of the routes themselves.

Chapter 65 explained how removal became federal policy: through the ideology of expansion, the economic pressures of cotton and land speculation, the extension of state law over Indigenous territories, the Indian Removal Act of 1830, the contested role of the federal courts, and a series of treaties negotiated under intense coercion. This chapter begins where policy ended and implementation began. Its subject is the lived history of removal: how treaty deadlines turned into military orders, how families were gathered, detained, transported, and resettled, how each nation resisted or negotiated within the narrowing options it faced, and how survivors rebuilt governments, households, and religious and cultural institutions in a new country.

The chapter treats five nations, and it insists that their experiences were distinct. The Choctaw were the first to move in large numbers, under a treaty whose promises to families who wished to remain were poorly honored, and a substantial community remained in Mississippi. The Muscogee (Creek) people faced a collapse of land security through fraud and intrusion, a violent crisis in 1836, and a military roundup in which many were shackled. The Chickasaw sold their lands and negotiated, at great expense, for a place within Choctaw territory, and organized much of their own emigration. The Seminole rejected a treaty they said their representatives had no authority to make, and their removal, entangled with the Seminole Wars and the status of Black Seminole people, was never fully accomplished. The Cherokee, who had built a constitutional republic and spent years in legal and political resistance, were removed by the army in 1838 after a treaty signed by a faction without the consent of the national government, and their journey produced the most documented and most widely remembered mortality of the era.

The phrase "Trail of Tears" is today most often associated with the Cherokee, and the National Park Service's Trail of Tears National Historic Trail is organized primarily around Cherokee routes. But the removals of the other southeastern nations were also journeys of exile, disease, and death, and Indigenous communities have used the phrase for their own histories. This chapter therefore uses the term with care, and examines in a Historical Debate feature how its meaning has been shaped by memory, commemoration, and scholarship. It also avoids the opposite error of dissolving distinct national histories into a single generic narrative. Comparison, rather than substitution, is the method of the chapter.

The evidence is uneven, and students should read it critically. Removal generated an enormous administrative record: treaties, correspondence among the War Department, army officers, and contractors, muster and emigration rolls, physicians' reports, and financial accounts. These records tend to reflect the concerns of those who produced them: costs, schedules, supply, and the management of crowds. Indigenous voices survive in letters, petitions, council statements, newspaper writings, missionary diaries that recorded Indigenous words, and, later, in oral histories and interviews, some collected generations after the events. Tribal nations maintain their own historical resources and have shaped how this history is told. No single kind of source is sufficient.

The chapter's governing sequence is removal and resistance, then displacement and suffering, then survival and reconstruction. That sequence is not a story of inevitable defeat. Indigenous leaders made difficult, contested decisions under pressure, often disagreeing among themselves about whether to resist, negotiate, or relocate on the best available terms. Their communities were separated, but they were not erased. The Choctaw, Muscogee, Chickasaw, Seminole, and Cherokee nations in Indian Territory, the Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians, the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians, and the Seminole Tribe of Florida and Miccosukee Tribe all trace continuous histories through this era. Removal changed the history of these peoples profoundly. It did not end it.

This chapter links backward to Chapter 64, which examined the Cherokee Republic, and to Chapter 65, which examined the creation of removal policy. It points forward to Chapter 67, which treats the military and political history of Seminole resistance in detail, and to later chapters on citizenship, tribal governance, and historical memory, including Chapters 93, 118, 132, and 139. Its central question is how forced removal transformed the families, governments, homelands, and economies of southeastern Indigenous nations, and how those nations preserved their identities and political institutions despite displacement.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how removal treaties and federal deadlines were converted into the practical machinery of detention, transportation, and resettlement.
  2. Compare the removals of the Choctaw, Muscogee, Chickasaw, Seminole, and Cherokee nations in terms of chronology, treaty arrangements, degree and form of coercion, transportation, and long-term outcomes.
  3. Distinguish among voluntary migration under duress, coerced treaty emigration, military removal, and continued resistance, and explain why these categories overlap.
  4. Trace the principal routes of removal on a map, identifying water, overland, and mixed routes and the western destinations in Indian Territory.
  5. Analyze how logistical failure, weather, disease, contractors' practices, and confinement contributed to mortality, rather than attributing deaths to a single cause.
  6. Assess the choices Indigenous leaders and families made, including negotiation, petition, armed resistance, flight, remaining in place, and organizing their own emigration.
  7. Evaluate primary sources, including treaties, letters, military correspondence, and administrative records, for authorship, purpose, and perspective.
  8. Evaluate mortality estimates and explain why historians cannot treat any single figure as an exact documented total.
  9. Describe the experiences of women, children, elders, and enslaved Black people during removal, including the dangers faced by Black Seminole people.
  10. Explain how communities that remained in the Southeast, including Mississippi Choctaw, Eastern Cherokee, and Florida Seminole people, sustained continuity.
  11. Interpret how Indigenous nations rebuilt governments, kinship networks, and religious and cultural institutions in the West.
  12. Distinguish the history of removal itself from later commemoration, and assess debates over the meaning and scope of the phrase "Trail of Tears."

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  3. In what sense can an emigration be called "voluntary" when the alternative was loss of land, legal protection, or physical safety?
  4. Why did similar federal policies produce such different levels of violence, confinement, and mortality across nations?

Historical Context

Students should enter this chapter with a clear but compact understanding of the removal regime established in Chapter 65. Several elements are essential. First, the Indian Removal Act of 1830 authorized the president to negotiate exchanges of land with Indigenous nations east of the Mississippi River for territory in the West; it did not itself compel any nation to move, and it did not provide a plan for how removal would be carried out. Second, the southern states, notably Georgia, Alabama, and Mississippi, had extended state law over Indigenous territories and sought to eliminate Indigenous jurisdiction, creating conditions in which remaining in place meant living under laws that the Indigenous nations had not made and that denied them standing in state courts. Third, federal officials, especially under President Andrew Jackson and his Secretary of War Lewis Cass, pursued removal chiefly through treaty negotiations, often with factions or small groups of signatories, backed by threats, bribes, and the withdrawal of federal protection against intruders.

The practical administration of removal was shaped by decisions made in Washington but implemented by the army, civilian agents, and private contractors. Early removals were organized under the War Department with considerable improvisation. Congress appropriated money, officers were assigned to supervise, and contractors bid to supply food, wagons, and boats. After the Jackson administration ended in March 1837, President Martin Van Buren oversaw the great military removal of the Cherokee in 1838, and the Seminole removal remained entangled in war.

The five nations at the center of this chapter occupied a broad belt of the Southeast that stretched from the Atlantic coast of Georgia and Florida westward across Alabama and Mississippi to the Mississippi River valley, with Cherokee lands rising into the southern Appalachians of Georgia, Tennessee, Alabama, and North Carolina. Their societies had changed considerably over the preceding half century. Many had adopted selective features of the market economy, including cotton, livestock, mills, and, among some wealthier families, the ownership of enslaved Black people. Some had embraced written laws, missionary schools, or Christianity, while others maintained older religious and political structures, and many combined elements of both. These changes were Indigenous choices made under pressure, not signs of assimilation in the sense that removal advocates claimed. As Chapters 28 and 64 describe, these nations were politically organized, economically diverse, and deeply attached to particular landscapes.

A final piece of context concerns the western destination. The territory that became known as Indian Territory, roughly the region of present-day Oklahoma, was not empty. It was home to other Indigenous peoples, including the Osage, Caddo, and, further west, Comanche and Kiowa peoples, and some earlier migrants from the Southeast, including Western Cherokee and Western Muscogee communities. The newcomers arrived in a country with its own politics, resources, and rivalries, and their resettlement required negotiation with the peoples already there as well as with the United States. The chapter's narrative therefore moves back and forth between East and West, between the displacements and the reconstruction.

Section 66.1

The Southeastern Nations on the Eve of Removal

By the early 1830s the Choctaw, Muscogee, Chickasaw, Seminole, and Cherokee together numbered several tens of thousands of people, and they lived in a region of fertile river valleys, pine uplands, hardwood forests, coastal plain, and southern mountains. Population figures from this period are estimates drawn from agents' reports, censuses, and later rolls, and they differ. The Choctaw are commonly estimated at roughly twenty thousand people, concentrated in what is now central and eastern Mississippi with some communities in Alabama and Louisiana. The Muscogee, whose towns lay along the Chattahoochee, Tallapoosa, Coosa, and Alabama river systems in Alabama and Georgia, are often estimated at over twenty thousand. The Chickasaw, a smaller nation of several thousand people, held northern Mississippi and adjoining lands in Tennessee and Alabama. The Seminole, a people formed in Florida over the preceding century from Muscogee-speaking migrants, Miccosukee-speakers, and other Indigenous people, were joined by Black communities living in close association with them. The Cherokee, numbering somewhat more than sixteen thousand in the East by the mid-1830s, held mountainous and valley lands across Georgia, Tennessee, Alabama, and North Carolina.

Each nation had an established political life. The Choctaw were organized in three regional districts, each with its own chief, and had in the 1820s modified their government in ways that strengthened a written legal order. The Muscogee were a confederacy of towns (talwa), divided between Upper Towns and Lower Towns, with a national council that struggled in the 1820s and 1830s to hold together a divided polity under external pressure. The Chickasaw maintained a government in which leading families, notably the Colbert family, played a prominent role in diplomacy. The Seminole lived in a number of loosely linked towns under recognized leaders, and relied on consensus among those towns rather than centralized authority. The Cherokee Nation, as Chapter 64 described, had a written constitution, a bicameral legislature, a newspaper, and a national court system.

Their economies were varied. Women had long been the principal cultivators of maize, beans, and squash across much of the Southeast, and in many communities they continued to manage household agricultural production. Men hunted and, in the early nineteenth century, increasingly raised livestock and engaged in trade. In the Cherokee and Muscogee countries, and among some Choctaw and Chickasaw families, cotton, mills, ferries, inns, and commercial farms had appeared, and a minority of wealthy families held enslaved Black laborers. Seminole households practiced agriculture, kept cattle and horses, and maintained commercial ties with Spanish, and later American, traders. This variety is important. The federal narrative that portrayed Indigenous people as nomadic hunters who neither used nor improved the land was false for all five nations, and, as Chapter 65 showed, it served political purposes.

The social worlds of these nations were organized around kinship, town and district, clan, and religion. Clans, which in these largely matrilineal societies passed through the mother's line, structured marriage, obligation, and, in many places, the settlement of disputes. Towns were centers of ceremony, such as the Muscogee Busk, and of governance. Missionary schools and churches had taken root in some communities, especially among the Cherokee and Choctaw, and Christianity co-existed with older practices. The loss of land therefore meant much more than a change of address. It meant separation from burial grounds, ceremonial grounds, and landscapes associated with origin accounts and community memory. The Choctaw, for example, trace important origin traditions to the mound at Nanih Waiya in present-day Mississippi, and the Muscogee and Cherokee similarly associate sacred places with their homelands.

The scale of the coming displacement can be summarized, with caution, in the following way. Over roughly a dozen years, from 1831 into the early 1840s, tens of thousands of people from these five nations were moved west of the Mississippi, along with enslaved people held by Indigenous households. Smaller numbers had emigrated earlier, and smaller numbers remained. Removal was therefore not a single act but a process that unfolded in different places at different times, and that left a significant Indigenous presence in the Southeast that persists to the present. Understanding the nations on the eve of removal is the first step in grasping both what was taken and what was preserved.

Section 66.2

From Removal Treaties to Deportation: The Machinery of Displacement

The practical history of removal begins with a gap between the treaty and the journey. A treaty could cede land and promise a western home, but it did not move a single family. Removal required an administrative system, and that system was assembled hurriedly, with limited planning, from the existing apparatus of the War Department, the army, Indian agents, and private contractors. Congress appropriated funds, and in the early 1830s the Office of Indian Affairs, which was then part of the War Department, assigned officers to supervise emigration. These officers, many of whom had little experience in organizing the movement of thousands of civilians, depended on contractors to supply provisions, wagons, teams, and river transportation.

That dependence on contractors shaped the quality of removal. Contractors were paid by the number of people supplied and the quantity of provisions delivered, creating incentives to cut costs. Spoiled corn, inadequate or poor-quality meat, insufficient wagons, and overcrowded boats were recurrent complaints in the removal records of several nations. Not every contractor failed, and some conductors and officers sought to provide for the people in their charge. But the system as a whole rewarded economy over care, and in a period when the people being moved were walking or riding hundreds of miles in all seasons, economy often meant hunger, exposure, and illness.

The mechanism by which families were brought to the point of departure varied. In some cases, notably among the Choctaw and Chickasaw, leaders and agents organized gatherings at designated places, often called emigration depots, from which parties set out. In others, particularly the Cherokee in 1838 and the Muscogee in 1836, soldiers went into communities, seized families from their homes, and marched them to stockades. Where treaties allowed it, families sold or abandoned livestock, crops, furniture, tools, and houses, often at a fraction of their worth. Intruders, who in many places had already settled on Indigenous land, sometimes seized property as families were being led away.

Students should distinguish among several forms of removal, recognizing that the boundaries are porous. Some people emigrated early and relatively voluntarily, believing that conditions in the East would worsen, though even this was voluntary only in a limited sense, given the pressures behind it. Others emigrated under treaty provisions that their leaders had negotiated or signed under coercion, and conducted their own movements within a federal framework. Others were removed by military force against their will. And some resisted, through legal action, political petition, flight, or armed opposition. The Choctaw, Chickasaw, and early Cherokee emigrants experienced the first or second of these categories; the Muscogee and Cherokee of 1836 and 1838 experienced the third; and the Seminole combined all four over a decade.

Historical Vocabulary
Detachment and Emigration Depot

Definition. A detachment was a party of emigrants organized for travel under a conductor, typically numbering from several hundred to well over a thousand people. An emigration depot was a designated assembly point, often a fort, a riverside landing, or an agency, where people were gathered, counted, and issued provisions before setting out.

Historical context. The term "detachment" was used in official correspondence and in Cherokee conductors' accounts. It reflects the administrative character of removal: people were organized into units with a numbered or named leader, a roll, wagons, and a supply arrangement. In the Cherokee case in 1838, many detachments were conducted by Cherokee leaders under contract with John Ross and the Cherokee National Council, while the earliest were supervised by army officers.

Why it matters. Using the specific term helps students avoid the image of a single column of marchers. In the Cherokee case alone there were many detachments that left at different times, followed different routes, and met different conditions, and the same is true of the other nations.

Potential misunderstanding. "Detachment" is a military-sounding word, but most detachments were composed of civilian families, and many were conducted by Indigenous leaders rather than soldiers.

Five Homelands, Many Routes: The Geography of Southeastern Removal

Using a map of the Southeast and Indian Territory without modern state boundaries, students should consider the following:

  • Choctaw homelands in central and eastern Mississippi, with routes converging on river points such as Vicksburg and Memphis and continuing through Arkansas, including Little Rock and Fort Smith, to Choctaw territory along the Red and Arkansas rivers
  • Muscogee homelands along the Chattahoochee, Tallapoosa, and Coosa rivers, with concentration at Fort Mitchell and movement by road and river toward Mobile, the Gulf and Mississippi River systems, and the Tennessee River, then westward into Arkansas and Indian Territory
  • Chickasaw homelands in northern Mississippi, with emigration through Memphis and across Arkansas to Choctaw territory near Fort Towson
  • Seminole homelands in peninsular Florida, with captives gathered at coastal posts such as Fort Brooke at Tampa Bay and shipped by sea to New Orleans before moving up the Mississippi and Arkansas rivers
  • Cherokee homelands in the southern Appalachians, with stockades such as Fort Cass; a water route down the Tennessee, Ohio, Mississippi, and Arkansas rivers; and several overland routes across Tennessee, Kentucky, Illinois, Missouri, and Arkansas. The National Park Service's national historic trail describes routes through nine states
  • Western destinations in what is now eastern Oklahoma, organized around Fort Gibson, Fort Smith, and Fort Towson, in a region already inhabited by Osage and other peoples and by earlier Cherokee and Muscogee migrants

Section 66.3

The Choctaw Nation and the First Great Removal

The Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek, signed in September 1830 and ratified by the Senate in early 1831, was the first major removal treaty negotiated after the Indian Removal Act. Under it the Choctaw Nation ceded its remaining lands in Mississippi, approximately ten and a half million acres, in exchange for territory in the West and various payments and provisions. The negotiations were conducted under strong federal pressure, including the extension of Mississippi's laws over Choctaw territory, a policy that abolished Choctaw governmental authority in the state's view and exposed Choctaw people to state courts and legal liabilities. Choctaw leaders disagreed deeply over how to respond, and the treaty reflects a division, not a consensus.

The three district chiefs took different positions. Greenwood LeFlore, chief of the northwestern district, was a leading advocate of negotiation, and he subsequently chose to remain in Mississippi, where he became a planter and a figure in state politics, though with limited long-term protection for his place there. Mushulatubbee, chief of the northeastern district, and Nitakechi, chief of the southwestern district, signed the treaty and led parties west. Many ordinary Choctaw people opposed it, and there were bitter divisions within communities over whether the better course was to accept removal on negotiated terms, to hope for protection under federal law, or to refuse. The anger of those who opposed the treaty, and the alleged use of liquor and pressure in the negotiations, are well documented in the accounts of contemporaries, though the details of what occurred at the council remain debated.

One feature of the treaty distinguished it from many later agreements: Article XIV. This article provided that Choctaw heads of families who wished to remain in Mississippi could register their intention with the federal agent within six months of ratification, and, upon doing so, receive an individual allotment of land, calculated by the size of the household, and become citizens of the state if they remained on the land for five years. In principle, this was a mechanism for continued residence and citizenship. In practice, as later sections will show, it was administered so poorly, and the obstacles to using it were so numerous, that most claims failed.

The problems began with the registration process. The federal agent charged with accepting claims, William Ward, faced a short timeline, a widely scattered population, and a population largely unaware, in many cases, that the mechanism existed or how to use it. Those who did register faced further obstacles, including disputes over the location of their claims, the opposition of white settlers eager to acquire the land, and the unwillingness of some officials to enforce the provision. Many claims were never approved, many families who had registered were later dispossessed, and for decades after the treaty Choctaw people in Mississippi litigated, petitioned, and negotiated for the land the treaty had promised. This history is examined in Section 66.5.

Federal preparations for removal proceeded in the winter of 1831. Army officers, among them George Gaines, who had long experience in the region as a trader and Indian factor and who was placed in charge of aspects of the first removal, planned assembly points and arranged contracts. The plan envisioned that the Choctaw would be moved in stages, in the late fall and winter, and by a combination of river travel and overland movement. The timing was fixed in part by the treaty, in part by the desire of Mississippi's officials to open land for settlement, and in part by the administrative convenience of moving large groups when agricultural work was slack. It did not take into account the likelihood of bitter weather, as the winter of 1831–1832 proved to be exceptionally severe.

The choices facing Choctaw families were difficult. A family could register under Article XIV and attempt to remain, risking dispossession and the hostility of neighbors. It could prepare for removal, which meant selling or abandoning property, burying the dead, and leaving for an unfamiliar country. Or it could attempt to hide, resist, or delay. Each choice carried costs. The next section considers the journeys of those who departed.

Historical Vocabulary
Commutation and Remainer

Definition. In treaty language, commutation referred to the conversion of an entitlement, such as rations, provisions, or transportation, into a cash payment or other substitute, and was a source of both flexibility and abuse. A remainer is a modern historian's convenient term for an Indigenous person or family who stayed in the homeland rather than emigrating, whether under a treaty provision such as Article XIV, by evading removal, or by obtaining legal permission.

Historical context. Remaining was possible for some individuals and families under particular treaty provisions, such as Article XIV of the 1830 Choctaw treaty; Article 12 of the December 1835 Cherokee treaty briefly proposed pre-emption rights to some eastern remainers, but the March 1, 1836 supplementary articles voided those rights. Remaining was also possible outside any surviving treaty entitlement, through flight or the tolerance of neighbors. Remaining was often precarious, and remainers faced legal discrimination, land loss, and pressure to leave.

Why it matters. The concept of remainers corrects the idea that the Southeast was emptied of Indigenous people. Several of today's federally recognized nations in the East descend from communities that remained.

Potential misunderstanding. "Remainer" is a modern analytical term, not a term used in the sources, and it should not suggest that staying was a free or easy choice.

Section 66.4

The Choctaw Journeys, 1831–1833

The first parties of Choctaw emigrants set out in the autumn and early winter of 1831, assembling at points on the Mississippi River and in the interior, and proceeding by a mix of steamboat, wagon, and foot. Two principal gathering places were Memphis, in Tennessee, and Vicksburg, in Mississippi, from which people were to cross the river and travel through Arkansas. Departures continued in 1832 and again in 1833, and the removal is conventionally described as occurring in these three waves. But Choctaw migration did not end in 1833, and later sections note that smaller groups continued to move west in the following years and decades.

The winter of 1831–1832 was bitterly cold, with heavy snow and freezing rain in places along the route, and the river, in parts, was clogged with ice. Parties were forced to wait at crossings, sometimes for days, without adequate shelter. Food supply, organized through contractors, proved erratic. Records of the removal and later recollections describe shortages, spoiled provisions, and delays in the arrival of supplies, and the exposure and hunger weakened people already burdened by the stress of displacement. Illness spread, particularly among children and the elderly. The record of how many people died in the first wave is incomplete and varies by source, a problem Section 66.19 addresses.

Routes ran through Mississippi, Arkansas, and Louisiana, with river transport on the Mississippi, the Ouachita, and the Arkansas rivers, and overland travel across swampy and wooded country. Arkansas was then a territory with few roads, and heavy rain and flooding made large stretches of the route nearly impassable. The Arkansas Gazette, a Little Rock newspaper, reported on the passage of Choctaw parties through the territory, and its coverage is one of the few contemporary press records. A Choctaw leader's description of the removal as a "trail of tears and death," as commonly cited from the Gazette's reporting, is among the earliest recorded uses of a phrase later associated chiefly with the Cherokee, though scholars continue to discuss the precise wording and attribution.

The second wave, in 1832, was complicated by a cholera epidemic that spread along the Mississippi River system and the Arkansas River that year and again in 1833. Cholera, a disease spread through contaminated water, was exacerbated by crowded conditions, poor sanitation, and exhausted bodies, and it struck both emigrants and the people around them. The administration of the removal changed too. Responsibility for the emigration was transferred among officers and agencies, and some of the early improvisation gave way to more structured contracting, though the quality of provisioning remained inconsistent. By the third wave, in 1833, a more settled system was in place, but disease and hardship remained.

Behind these accounts are the individual experiences of families. People left behind farms, cattle, orchards, and graves. They carried what they could, often little. Women gave birth on the road, and children and elders died and were buried along the way, often hurriedly and without the ceremonies appropriate to their communities. Parties organized by district and leader tended to stay together, and kin and neighbors cared for the sick and the orphaned. Some people, alarmed by the news from the first parties, hid or delayed. Others arranged to travel with relatives. Historians reconstruct these experiences from contemporary letters, from missionary accounts, from tribal oral histories, and from later recollections, and they must read each carefully for its perspective.

Estimates of the total number of Choctaw moved in this period, and of deaths, are uncertain. Commonly cited figures place the number removed in the three waves at roughly twelve to thirteen thousand people, and deaths from exposure, disease, and related causes in the thousands, often given as about two to three thousand, though some estimates run higher and others lower. These figures should be treated as approximations, drawn from official counts, agent estimates, and later scholarship, rather than as precise totals. Section 66.19 examines why.

Section 66.5

Choctaw Resettlement, Survival, and Communities That Remained

The Choctaw who reached the West arrived in a region of rivers, prairies, and timbered hills between the Red and Arkansas rivers, in what is now southeastern Oklahoma. They were assigned lands under the treaties of 1820 and 1830, and the federal government maintained posts, including Fort Towson and Fort Smith, from which agents distributed rations and annuities. The first task of resettlement was survival. People arrived in winter or in epidemic seasons, often without sufficient seed, tools, or shelter, and the first growing seasons were precarious. Federal rations, administered through agents and contractors, were essential in the early years but were irregular, and complaints about delays and quality continued.

Choctaw families rebuilt households with the materials at hand. They cleared fields, built log houses, and replanted maize and other crops. They brought with them knowledge of agriculture, livestock, and craft, and they reestablished churches and schools; missionaries who had worked in Mississippi followed them west, and mission schools became important institutions. Disease remained a persistent danger: the combination of exposure, unfamiliar environment, and the earlier stresses of the journey meant that mortality did not end on arrival. Historians of the Choctaw West have emphasized that the first decade or so of resettlement was in many respects as dangerous as the journey itself, although the precise toll is hard to measure.

Political reconstruction was parallel. Choctaw leaders in the West, who retained the district structure, adopted written laws and constitutions in the 1830s, drawing on earlier Choctaw practice and responding to the new circumstances of a western homeland. They established a council, courts, and a law-enforcement system, and negotiated relations with the United States and with neighboring peoples. In 1837 the Choctaw concluded the agreement with the Chickasaw described in Section 66.9, accepting the Chickasaw as a district within the Choctaw Nation's lands. The decision showed the capacity of Choctaw leaders to exercise sovereignty over western territory, and it also brought tensions that would continue until the Chickasaw separated their government in the 1850s.

Not all Choctaw went west. Several thousand people remained in Mississippi, some under Article XIV of the treaty and many without the protection of any formal land claim. Those who had registered claims faced a long struggle. Many claims were rejected or not processed, and families who had settled on allotments were dispossessed by speculators and settlers who used fraud, intimidation, and the state's legal system to take their land. Choctaw people who remained lived on the margins of Mississippi society, often as tenant farmers, sharecroppers, or laborers on lands that had once been Choctaw territory, and they faced discrimination under state law and social custom. The federal government's attention to their claims was sporadic, and in the 1840s and later it sponsored further, partial removal efforts, which drew some additional families west.

Despite these pressures, the Mississippi Choctaw preserved community life. Families maintained kinship ties, language, ceremonies, stickball, foodways, crafts, and community networks, often in clusters of settlement in east-central Mississippi. They kept contact with relatives in Oklahoma, and some individuals moved between the two. In the twentieth century these communities formally organized, and the Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians was federally recognized in the 1940s, establishing a government-to-government relationship that continues today, as discussed in later chapters on tribal governments. The history of the Mississippi Choctaw underscores that removal was never total, and that continuity in the homeland was achieved through sustained community effort under adverse conditions.

The Choctaw case therefore establishes a pattern that later sections will see repeated in different forms: removal produced a western nation, rebuilt through hard work and political reorganization, and an eastern community, which survived through a quieter and more precarious continuity. Both were Choctaw. Both have their own histories, and neither can be understood without the other.

Section 66.6

The Muscogee Nation after the Treaty of Cusseta

The Muscogee faced a different sequence from the Choctaw. Their lands in Alabama and Georgia had been reduced by earlier cessions, including those following the Creek War of 1813–1814, and by the 1820s Muscogee leaders were struggling to defend what remained against the pressure of the states and the settlers who followed. The Treaty of Cusseta, concluded in March 1832, was the culmination of that struggle. Under it, the Muscogee ceded all their remaining lands east of the Mississippi, but the treaty did not require immediate emigration. Instead, it provided that individual Muscogee people would receive allotments, and that those who chose to remain could stay for a period, with the expectation that most would eventually move west.

The allotment arrangement is critical to understanding what followed. Under its terms, a number of leaders received larger tracts, commonly cited as full sections, and heads of families received smaller allotments, commonly cited as half sections. The treaty allowed allottees to sell their tracts with the approval of the president, and it promised protection for those who remained against trespass. In the intention of its negotiators, this arrangement may have offered a path to continued residence for some, but it created in practice a system in which Muscogee land passed into private hands. The treaty converted a collective territorial right into individual properties that could be bought and sold, and it opened them to a land market in which Muscogee families were at a severe disadvantage.

Speculators and settlers took advantage of that market. Land speculation companies and individuals, many based in Alabama and Georgia, acquired allotments through fraud: by bribing or deceiving allottees, by using impersonators who falsely claimed to be the legitimate holders, by procuring signatures from people who did not understand what they were signing, and by obtaining the approval of certifying agents who were themselves complicit or negligent. Contemporary investigations, including federal inquiries, documented widespread frauds, though the remedy they provided was limited, and many families never recovered their land. The scale of the loss, and the degree to which federal officials failed to prevent it, are central facts of Muscogee history in these years.

State authority compounded these abuses. Alabama extended its laws over the Muscogee territory in 1832, and its courts and officials treated Muscogee people as subject to state jurisdiction, while excluding them from effective legal protection. Settlers poured onto Muscogee land, claiming allotments, seizing crops and livestock, and harassing families. Federal troops, who were supposed to protect the Muscogee against intruders under the treaty, were often absent or ineffective, and the federal government's reluctance to confront Alabama left the nation increasingly exposed. Muscogee leaders protested to federal agents, to the War Department, and to the president, and Opothleyahola, among others, traveled to Washington to press their case. These appeals met limited success.

By 1835 and 1836, many Muscogee families faced destitution. Having lost their land, or lacking secure title to it, they were unable to plant, and hunger spread. Some moved into the woods and swamps to avoid settlers, and others lived on the charity of neighbors. Leaders disagreed about how to respond: some, including Opothleyahola, hoped to retain land and, failing that, to negotiate the best terms for emigration; others, especially among the Lower Towns, urged resistance. These pressures, combined with the continued intrusion of settlers, set the stage for the crisis of 1836.

Section 66.7

The Muscogee Crisis of 1836 and Forced Removal

The events of 1836 are sometimes described as the "Second Creek War," a label that should be used with caution. The term suggests a conventional war between two parties, but the events had the character of a crisis in which a dispossessed and starving population, under siege by settlers and speculators, was met by military force. Some Muscogee people did take up arms, and violent incidents occurred, including the burning of the town of Roanoke, Georgia, in May 1836. But the broader context was one of land fraud, intrusion, and hunger, and the military response was directed not only against those who had fought but against whole communities.

The violence began in the spring of 1836 and drew in the Georgia and Alabama militias and federal troops. Leaders of the resistance included figures from the Lower Towns, among them Eneah Emathla, though the movement was not centralized, and many Muscogee people did not take part. Other Muscogee leaders, including Opothleyahola, cooperated with federal authorities, and many Muscogee warriors, in the summer of 1836, volunteered for service against the Seminole in Florida under the command of General Thomas Jesup, on the understanding that the United States would protect their families. The divisions within the nation were therefore deep, and the distinction between "friendly" and "hostile" Muscogee, a distinction used in the official records, obscures a more complicated reality in which individuals and towns made choices under rapidly shifting pressures.

Federal and state forces moved rapidly. General Winfield Scott, who initially commanded the campaign, and then Jesup, who took over, organized columns that marched through Muscogee territory, confronted armed groups, and rounded up large numbers of people, including many who had not taken part in the fighting. Resistance collapsed within a matter of weeks, with the surrender or capture of leading figures in the summer. The military did not stop at those who had resisted. Under the logic of the campaign, Muscogee communities were treated as a population to be controlled and removed, and the roundup extended to families and towns that had remained at home.

Those taken into custody were concentrated at points including Fort Mitchell, Alabama, and other posts and camps. Men identified as hostile were in many cases placed in restraints, and the caravan of prisoners that set out from Alabama in the summer of 1836 included men bound in chains or shackles, a fact documented in contemporary reports, with women and children following in the same convoys. The conditions of confinement were crowded, with limited food and sanitation. Illness, particularly among children and the elderly, spread in the camps.

It is important to keep the chapter's focus on removal. The military history of the 1836 campaign, with its columns and engagements, has been written elsewhere, and this chapter does not rehearse it. What matters here is the transformation of a dispossessed people into captives and then into emigrants. The sequence from fraud to hunger to violence to confinement illustrates how removal could be accomplished less through a single act of treaty than through a cumulative process in which the loss of land, the failure of federal protection, and military force combined to eliminate the possibility of remaining.

Section 66.8

Muscogee Removal, 1836–1837: Captivity, Transportation, and Loss

From the summer of 1836 and through 1837, thousands of Muscogee people were moved west. Routes varied, and the records describe movements by road, by river, and by a combination of the two. Some parties were taken to Gulf and river ports and transported by steamboat across the Gulf and up the Mississippi and Arkansas river systems; others went by way of the Tennessee River and overland across Mississippi, Tennessee, and Arkansas. The records show crowded vessels, delays at stopping places, and inadequate provisioning. Students should consult the map in Section 66.2 to see how these routes ran.

The experience of captivity and transportation was harsh. People who had been seized from their homes were marched for long distances, often on foot, under guard. Men in shackles walked alongside women carrying infants and the belongings that soldiers allowed them to take. The records describe disease, notably fevers, dysentery, and respiratory illness, spreading through the crowded steamboats and camps. Children and the elderly died in large numbers. Violence occurred as well, and guards sometimes used force against those who tried to leave the line or to return home.

A particularly severe episode occurred on October 31, 1837, when the steamboat Monmouth, carrying several hundred Muscogee people under forced removal, collided on the lower Mississippi River with the Trenton, which was being towed by the steamboat Warren, and sank near present-day Profit Island Bend, Louisiana. Historical accounts differ on the exact toll: 311 deaths is a frequently cited count, while other contemporary and modern accounts report roughly 300–400. The disaster illustrates the extreme danger of crowded river transport; the precise responsibility for the collision and allegations about the vessel’s seaworthiness should not be treated as settled facts.

They reconstituted their talwa, naming new towns for those left behind, and reestablished ceremonial grounds, including the fires of the ceremonial grounds that were carried west. Leaders including Opothleyahola, who settled with his followers in the western country, resumed the political life of the nation. The rebuilding was difficult, and divisions between earlier emigrants and later arrivals and between Upper and Lower Town communities persisted. But the Muscogee Nation, reconstituted in the West, preserved its town structure, its council, and its ceremonial life, a continuity that would sustain the nation through later crises.

Not all Muscogee descendants were removed from the Southeast. Some families in southwest Alabama, including ancestors of today’s Poarch Creek Indians, remained on or near land recognized through individual arrangements and maintained community networks despite dispossession around them. The Poarch Creek Indians’ own history documents this continuity, which prevents treating Muscogee removal as a total evacuation of Alabama. Their later federal recognition belongs to a subsequent period, not to the 1830s.

Section 66.9

Chickasaw Negotiations, Land Sales, and the Search for a Western Homeland

The Chickasaw experience of removal differed from that of the Choctaw and Muscogee in a crucial respect: the Chickasaw sold their lands under an arrangement that placed the proceeds under the nation's own account, and they sought, with considerable determination, to control the terms of their departure. The Treaty of Pontotoc Creek, concluded in October 1832, ceded the Chickasaw homeland in northern Mississippi. Under it the United States was to survey and sell the land, and the proceeds, after expenses, were to be held for the benefit of the nation. Individual Chickasaw families could receive allotments, which could in some circumstances be sold, and the treaty was later modified by an 1834 agreement that eased the terms for selling those allotments.

Chickasaw political leadership was shaped by a small number of prominent families and individuals. Levi Colbert, a leading negotiator and a figure of great influence until his death in 1834, and other members of the Colbert family had long managed relations with the United States. Ishtehotopa, recognized as the principal leader, and elders such as Tishomingo, whose very old age at the time of removal became part of the record of the journey, participated in the councils. The leadership was not unanimous, and there were differences over how much to concede, but the Chickasaw as a whole appear to have concluded that resistance would not prevent the loss of the land, and that the best course was to obtain the highest possible payment and a secure destination.

The difficulty lay in the destination. The treaty promised a western home but did not secure one. The lands of the West that might have been assigned to the Chickasaw were already ceded to the Choctaw, and the Chickasaw had to negotiate with a nation that had its own interests. Exploring parties went west in the 1830s, but agreements with the Choctaw proved elusive, and for several years the Chickasaw sold land, waited, and negotiated. The delay had costs. The government's management of the proceeds of the land sales was criticized, as funds were invested in state bonds and securities, some of which later lost value, and the nation's ability to secure its financial position was constrained. Settlers and speculators meanwhile pressed into the ceded area.

The breakthrough came with the Treaty of Doaksville in January 1837, concluded between the Chickasaw and Choctaw nations with the participation of United States officials. Under its terms the Chickasaw received the right to settle within Choctaw territory in the West, in a district that would be organized with Chickasaw representation in the Choctaw government, and they agreed to pay the Choctaw a substantial sum, commonly cited as $530,000, for the right. The agreement reveals Indigenous diplomacy at work: two nations negotiated, with federal oversight but also on their own terms, the sharing of a homeland. It also reveals the cost to the Chickasaw, who paid with their own funds for the privilege of entering a territory already held by another nation.

Section 66.10

Chickasaw Removal and the Reconstruction of Community

Chickasaw migration began in earnest in 1837, after the Doaksville agreement, and the principal movement occurred in 1837 and 1838. Unlike the Muscogee and Cherokee removals, it was not carried out through a military roundup. The Chickasaw used funds from the sale of their lands to hire contractors and organize their own transportation, and the movement proceeded in parties that gathered at Memphis and traveled across Arkansas to the Choctaw country. Distinguishing this process from the military removals is important for understanding the differences among the nations, but it would be a serious error to portray it as painless or wholly voluntary. The Chickasaw left because the alternative was the loss of their land and legal protection, and the choice was forced by the same system of state pressure and federal policy.

The journey, which usually lasted weeks, crossed the Mississippi and then ran across the swampy and forested lowlands of eastern Arkansas toward the Arkansas River valley and the Choctaw country. Families traveled with wagons, horses, cattle, and the enslaved people held by wealthier households, which, according to contemporary estimates, numbered more than a thousand people. The records of the movement show that the emigrants were not spared from hardship. The quality of supplies varied, and delays occurred. Most seriously, smallpox, a disease to which many of the emigrants lacked immunity, broke out among Chickasaw parties in 1837 and 1838, and deaths, commonly estimated in the hundreds, occurred on the route and in the weeks after arrival. The disease struck a population that was already weakened by the strain of travel.

The elderly leader Tishomingo died in 1838. Older accounts often place his death on the journey, but Chickasaw Nation historical material reports newer research indicating that he died after reaching the Choctaw Nation in Indian Territory. The difference matters: a loss related to removal must not automatically be described as a death on the route. His death, near the end of a long life of leadership, nevertheless symbolized the generational disruption of resettlement. Many other elders, along with infants and children, died, and communities lost the knowledge, authority, and care they had provided.

Resettlement brought further difficulties, many of them stemming from the arrangement with the Choctaw. Chickasaw families settled in the western portion of Choctaw territory, near Fort Towson and along the Red River, in an area with fertile bottomland but also with disease and flooding. They had to rebuild homes and farms, and the nation's funds, partly spent on the Choctaw purchase and removal, were depleted. The Chickasaw participated in the Choctaw council as a district, but tensions arose over representation, jurisdiction, and the use of resources. Chickasaw leaders wanted greater autonomy, and these pressures persisted until the 1850s, when a new treaty with the United States and the Choctaw allowed the Chickasaw to establish their own separate government. The Chickasaw adopted a constitution in 1856, reasserting an independent national structure.

The Chickasaw migration did not end in 1838. Tribal histories describe continued removals through 1851 and later family arrivals; smaller groups continued to move west in the following years, and some Chickasaw people remained in the East, in small numbers, in Mississippi and elsewhere in the Southeast. The mixture of negotiation, financial arrangement, and hardship in the Chickasaw case shows that removal was shaped by the choices and resources of the nations themselves, but also that those choices were made within a coercive framework that left no good options.

Section 66.11

Seminole Sovereignty and Resistance to Removal

The Seminole case differs from the others in that the treaty on which removal was based was disputed from the start, and a substantial part of the population refused to comply. In May 1832, at Payne's Landing on the Ocklawaha River in Florida, a number of Seminole leaders signed an agreement with the United States that provided for the nation's removal west of the Mississippi within three years, subject to the condition that a delegation of Seminole leaders should first inspect the western lands and find them acceptable. In March 1833, at Fort Gibson in Indian Territory, a delegation of seven chiefs who had gone west to inspect the land signed a document stating that the territory was acceptable and that the Seminole would move.

Interpretations of what happened diverge sharply, and the dispute is central to the history. Seminole leaders, on returning to Florida, asserted that the delegation had not been authorized to commit the nation to removal, that they had been pressured or misled in signing the Fort Gibson document, and that the Payne's Landing agreement itself had been signed by only a portion of the leadership and under duress. Federal officials maintained that the treaties were binding and that the Seminole were bound to leave by the deadline of early 1836. The question of consent is a historical question about Seminole political organization as well as about law. The Seminole did not have a centralized government capable of binding all towns to the decisions of a few leaders, and the notion, built into the treaty, that a handful of chiefs could commit the whole people did not correspond to Seminole political practice.

Differences within the Seminole communities also shaped the response. Some leaders, among them Micanopy, the principal figure, wavered in the face of federal pressure, and a group of Seminole expressed willingness to emigrate. Others, including figures who would become prominent in the war, such as Osceola and Jumper, opposed removal firmly. The Miccosukee-speaking communities of northern and central Florida and the Muscogee-speaking Seminole communities did not always agree, and the nation's decentralized structure meant that consensus was difficult to achieve. Those who opposed removal were not simply recalcitrant; they argued that Florida was their homeland and that the land to the west was unfamiliar, already occupied, and unsafe.

The place of Black Seminole people in this history is crucial. Many Black people lived in or near Seminole communities, in relationships that ranged from close alliance and kinship to a form of tributary dependence, in which Black families farmed their own land, kept their own livestock, and owed the Seminole a portion of their produce, a relationship that differed fundamentally from chattel slavery as practiced in the plantation South. Some were descendants of people who had escaped from slavery in Georgia and the Carolinas, and others were born in Florida. Black Seminole leaders, notably Abraham, served as interpreters and counselors, and played an important part in Seminole diplomacy. For Black Seminole people, removal to the West, where they might be claimed by slaveholders or placed under the authority of other Indigenous nations that practiced slavery, posed a distinctive threat. Their opposition to removal was therefore among the strongest, and their alliance with Seminole leaders strengthened the resistance.

For all these reasons, removing the Seminole proved far more difficult than removing the other nations. The population was dispersed across a large, thinly settled peninsula of swamp, hammock, and pine land, much of it poorly known to the army. The people were determined, and the alliance with Black communities gave them additional strength and knowledge of the terrain. The federal government, which had assumed that the treaties would lead to a relatively quick migration, found itself instead drawn into a prolonged war. The detailed history of that war, including its campaigns, strategy, and the roles of Seminole and Black Seminole fighters, belongs to Chapter 67. This chapter follows its consequences for removal.

Section 66.12

Seminole Deportations, Black Seminoles, and Communities That Remained

Seminole removal differed from that of the other nations in that most people were moved not as emigrants who responded to a treaty deadline but as captives, prisoners of war, or surrendering families, over a period of years. From 1836 through the early 1840s, groups were gathered at coastal posts, notably Fort Brooke at Tampa Bay, and were shipped by sea to New Orleans, from which they traveled up the Mississippi and Arkansas rivers toward Fort Gibson and the Indian Territory. The captures and surrenders occurred at different times, often under varied circumstances, and the experience of any one group depended on when and how it left.

Captivity involved the separation of families and the loss of leaders. Individuals were captured separately, held in different posts, and sent west on different transports, and families were sometimes divided by the army's decisions about who to detain and when to ship. Military officers, concerned with security, held men in close confinement, and conditions on the posts and ships were crowded. Disease took a toll in the camps and in transit, and deaths, among them those of children and elders, occurred in holding areas, on ships, and on the river and overland legs of the journey. The records of the movement are fragmentary, and mortality figures are uncertain.

For Black Seminole people, deportation carried the additional danger of re-enslavement. Slaveholders in Georgia, Florida, and elsewhere claimed that many Black people living among the Seminole were their escaped property, and efforts to seize them were a recurrent feature of the conflict. Federal commanders, including General Thomas Jesup, sometimes offered Black people who surrendered assurances that they would be allowed to go west with the Seminole, and these assurances were not always honored, since the status of Black Seminoles was contested, and some were claimed by slaveholders or sold. The uncertainty, the threat of seizure, and the legal ambiguity made the journey especially perilous. Those who reached Indian Territory did not necessarily find safety. They lived under the authority of the Muscogee Nation, whose lands the Seminole were initially assigned to share, and the presence of slaveholding interests and of slave raiders in the region endangered Black Seminole families. Some leaders, including Black Seminole figures, later led groups out of Indian Territory toward Mexico in search of greater security, a movement in the late 1840s that falls outside this chapter's main chronology.

In Indian Territory, Seminole communities, with Black Seminole neighbors, struggled to reorganize. They were settled under the authority and, initially, the jurisdiction of the Muscogee Nation, an arrangement the Seminole resented, and political tensions over land and autonomy persisted until a separate Seminole territory was established in the 1850s. They rebuilt towns and farms, drawing on kinship networks and the leadership that survived. The Seminole Nation of Oklahoma today traces its history to these communities.

Not all Seminole were removed. At the end of the major hostilities in 1842, the army allowed a number of Seminole people to remain in southern Florida, in the Everglades and the Big Cypress, and estimates generally place their number in the hundreds. They lived in small, dispersed communities, avoided contact with the army and settlers, and maintained their languages, ceremonies, and subsistence practices in a challenging environment. Later pressure, including additional military operations in the 1850s that led to the removal of some more people, did not eliminate them. The descendants of these communities are the Seminole Tribe of Florida and the Miccosukee Tribe of Indians of Florida, both federally recognized in the twentieth century, and the continuing presence of the Seminole in Florida is among the clearest examples of Indigenous persistence in the face of removal. Chapter 67 examines the resistance that made this persistence possible.

Section 66.13

The Treaty of New Echota and Cherokee Opposition to Removal

The Treaty of New Echota, signed in December 1835 by a small group of Cherokee men with United States commissioners, is the legal foundation of the Cherokee removal, and its authority was challenged from the start. Chapter 65 examined the treaty's negotiation and the constitutional disputes it raised; here it is enough to recall three points. The signers, among them Major Ridge, John Ridge, Elias Boudinot, and Stand Watie, did not represent the Cherokee National Council or the elected leadership. The principal chief, John Ross, and the great majority of Cherokee citizens opposed the treaty. And the Senate approved it in May 1836 by a margin of a single vote, setting a deadline of May 1838 for Cherokee emigration.

The opposition was broad and organized. John Ross, who had led the nation since the 1820s and who had directed years of legal and political resistance, took the position that the treaty was fraudulent and that the Cherokee Nation could not be bound by an agreement signed by unauthorized individuals. He and the National Council sent delegations to Washington, issued memorials to Congress, and gathered signatures on petitions. A memorial submitted to Congress in April 1838 reported 15,665 Cherokee signatures opposing the Treaty of New Echota (House Document No. 316, 25th Congress, 2nd Session). This was an extraordinary expression of national opposition; it should not be conflated with separate petitions collected in earlier years or interpreted as an independently verified census count. The Cherokee also appealed to the American public through the press and through sympathetic allies, including missionaries and some members of Congress.

The strategy had limits. The courts had declined, in practice, to enforce the Cherokee position against Georgia, and President Jackson and then President Van Buren treated the treaty as valid. Congress did not repeal it. Ross's efforts, which included attempts to negotiate alternative arrangements and to persuade the president to reopen talks, did not prevent the approach of the deadline. A smaller number of Cherokee, including the Ridge party and its supporters, concluded that resistance was hopeless and emigrated early, in 1837 and early 1838; their departure divided families and communities and intensified the political bitterness within the nation.

Meanwhile, ordinary families prepared as best they could. Many continued to plant crops in the spring of 1838, in the hope that removal might be averted, while others sold property, often at a loss, and put their affairs in order. Georgia had already surveyed and distributed Cherokee land through a lottery, and white settlers had moved onto farms and into houses that had belonged to Cherokee families, pressing the Cherokee out of their homes even before the deadline arrived. Some families lived on small parts of their former property, tolerated or harassed by the new occupants. The sense of living at the edge of catastrophe is documented in the correspondence of missionaries and in Cherokee letters. The nation was torn between hope and dread, and the approach of the deadline made it clear that a federal military operation was coming.

The limits of Cherokee resistance should be understood not as a failure of will but as a reflection of the balance of power. The Cherokee had made extensive use of every available legal and political tool, and they had considerable public sympathy in parts of the country, but they faced a federal government and southern states determined to proceed, and a nation whose own political unity had been shattered by the treaty faction's actions. The next section turns to the military operation that followed.

Section 66.14

The Roundup of 1838: Soldiers, Stockades, and the Loss of Home

In the spring of 1838 the United States Army, under the command of General Winfield Scott, assembled in the Cherokee country to carry out the removal. Scott arrived in May with a force of regular troops and was supported by state militia and volunteers from Georgia, Tennessee, Alabama, and North Carolina, making a force of several thousand. He issued orders to his troops that called for restraint and humane treatment of the Cherokee, and he made an announcement to the Cherokee that removal would proceed. The orders, which are documented, did not prevent the violence and suffering that followed, but they show that the commander was aware that the operation could be abusive and that the army's conduct was an issue of concern.

The roundup began in late May 1838. Soldiers moved through the Cherokee country in small detachments, seizing families from their farms, their homes, and sometimes the fields where they were working. Accounts by Cherokee survivors, by missionaries, and by some soldiers describe families being taken with little warning and allowed to carry only what they had on hand. Livestock, household goods, tools, and crops were left behind, and neighbors, often white settlers waiting for the land, moved in, looting or occupying the abandoned property. Families were separated in the chaos, with children sometimes away from home or at school when soldiers arrived, and with relatives at work in separate places. The pace was swift: within a few weeks, thousands of people had been seized.

Those seized were marched to stockades and depots, temporary forts and camps built for the purpose, in Georgia, Tennessee, Alabama, and North Carolina. Among the principal posts were Fort Cass, near Charleston, Tennessee, and Ross's Landing on the Tennessee River, which served as the principal emigration depots. By mid-June, roughly thirteen thousand Cherokee were held in about thirty camps and forts. Conditions in confinement were harsh. The camps were crowded, with inadequate shelter, poor sanitation, and food that was unfamiliar to many and often insufficient. Heat in the summer and exposure to the elements, along with the strain of captivity, made illness widespread. Records and recollections mention dysentery, fevers, whooping cough, and other diseases that took a particularly heavy toll on children and the elderly. Deaths in the camps occurred daily.

Students should distinguish carefully among phases of loss. Some deaths occurred during the roundup itself, from violence, shock, or neglect. Others occurred in the camps and depots, from disease and privation, before the journey began. Still others occurred on the journeys, by water or overland, discussed in the next sections. And further deaths occurred after arrival in the West, in the first months and years of resettlement. Combining these into a single figure, as popular accounts often do, obscures the different conditions that produced them and complicates efforts to assess responsibility. Section 66.19 discusses the evidence.

The role of soldiers and state forces in the roundup was a source of subsequent controversy and memory. Federal regular troops, under Scott's orders, were instructed to behave humanely, but the militia and volunteers were less disciplined, and Cherokee accounts record acts of brutality and theft. Some individual soldiers behaved with compassion, and some later recalled the operation with shame, while others treated the Cherokee harshly or indifferently. The mixture of conduct is recorded, and the variation in individual behavior does not alter the fundamental fact that the operation was a forced removal of civilians from their homes by an army.

From the perspective of Cherokee families seized from their homes: The roundup meant sudden loss of home, property, and often the separation of relatives, followed by confinement in crowded camps. Militia from the surrounding states, many with a local interest in the land, were often less restrained than regulars.

From the perspective of missionaries and white neighbors: Missionaries who had worked among the Cherokee often protested and accompanied detachments, documenting events in journals and letters; some white settlers, however, saw the roundup as an opportunity, and moved to acquire property and land.
Section 66.15

Cherokee Removal by Water: Rivers, Boats, and Logistical Failure

The first Cherokee parties to depart in the summer of 1838 traveled primarily by water. From Ross's Landing on the Tennessee River, groups boarded steamboats and flatboats and set off downstream, following the Tennessee to the Ohio River, down the Ohio to the Mississippi, and then down the Mississippi and up the Arkansas River toward the Indian Territory. To federal officials this appeared the most efficient method of moving large numbers. Rivers promised speed and reduced the demands on wagons and provisions, and the army had used river transport in earlier removals.

The reality was different. The first three detachments left in June 1838, under army officers, in the middle of one of the hottest and driest summers in memory. The Tennessee and Arkansas rivers were low from drought, and boats ran aground or were delayed, and the Arkansas River at times could not be navigated to the destination. People were crowded onto decks and into holds in sweltering heat, with little shade, poor ventilation, and limited access to clean water. Disease spread rapidly, and the records and later accounts describe illness and death on the boats, with burials along the riverbanks. Overcrowding, delay, and the lack of medical care made these journeys deadly for many.

The early water parties raised an immediate crisis. Cherokee leaders, who were being held in the camps, and who received reports of the deaths and suffering of those who had gone, protested to the army and to Washington. The heat and drought continued, and the sick lists in the camps grew. In response, and in large part because of Cherokee appeals, General Scott agreed in the summer to suspend further removal until autumn, to hold the remaining people in camps during the worst of the heat, and to allow the Cherokee National Council, under John Ross, to take charge of organizing the rest of the emigration. This agreement, discussed in the next section, reflects the exercise of Cherokee agency under extreme pressure, though it should not obscure the harsh conditions in the camps during the delay.

Transport by boat did not disappear after the summer. Some detachments and many individuals continued to travel in part by river, especially those who were ill, who could not walk, or who traveled with Ross's party. But the early experience showed that water travel was neither automatically safe nor a minor part of the removal story. It shaped the early death toll, forced a change of plan, and left a record of logistical failure that the records of the contractors and the army attempted to explain in terms of weather and low water, while Cherokee accounts emphasized crowding and neglect.

Cause and Effect

Why Removal Killed: Treaty Obligations, Military Operations, Logistics, and Disease

Long-term causes

The political and economic drive of southern states and the federal government to acquire Indigenous land, expressed in state laws, the Indian Removal Act, and treaties negotiated under coercion; the dispossession and impoverishment that preceded departure.

Short-term causes

Treaty deadlines fixed by officials rather than by the capacities of travelers; the use of contractors paid by volume; schedules that sent people in winter or in summer drought; the military decision to confine large populations before transport.

Immediate triggers

Crowded camps and boats, poor or spoiled provisions, low rivers, severe weather, and the movement of thousands of people through regions where epidemic disease, including cholera and smallpox, was circulating.

Immediate effects

High mortality from exposure, hunger, and illness among children and the elderly; the breakup of families; the loss of property, livestock, and food; delays that deepened privation.

Long-term consequences

Demographic loss and the disruption of knowledge transmission; the creation of new political and economic circumstances in the West; the formation of communities that remained in the East; and a long memory of grievance.

Historical complexity

Disease, weather, and logistics mattered, but they operated within decisions about timing, confinement, and provisioning made by people. Deaths cannot be assigned to "disease" alone without noting that the conditions that spread it were produced by policy.

Why the pattern matters: Treating mortality as an accident of weather or sickness obscures the choices that created vulnerability, while treating it as deliberate extermination in every case would overlook the differences among nations and the role of improvisation, indifference, and cost-cutting. Historians generally emphasize a combination of coercive policy, administrative negligence, and environmental conditions.

Section 66.16

The Overland Cherokee Trail: Detachments, Winter, and Survival

After the suspension of removal in the summer of 1838, Scott and Ross reached an agreement under which the Cherokee National Council would organize the remaining people, roughly twelve thousand, into a series of detachments that would travel overland, conducted by Cherokee leaders, under contract with the government. This arrangement gave Cherokee leaders a significant role in the management of the journey, and it shaped its character. The detachments, commonly numbered at thirteen, each of several hundred to over a thousand people, were organized under conductors, who included prominent Cherokee leaders and ministers, with assistants, physicians, interpreters, and wagon-masters.

The detachments left the Tennessee depots between late August and early December 1838, and they traveled at different times and by different routes, so that the journey was not a single march but a series of separate movements. The routes crossed Tennessee and Kentucky, and then southern Illinois and Missouri, crossing the Ohio River and the Mississippi River at different points, and continued across Missouri and Arkansas toward the Indian Territory. Other routes crossed Tennessee and Arkansas by southern paths, through Memphis. Students should consult the map in Section 66.2 and the National Park Service's trail documentation, which identifies several distinct routes, to appreciate the variety. Some detachments were larger and some smaller; some departed in the early autumn when the weather was still mild, and others, leaving later, encountered the full force of winter.

The journey was long, and the difficulties were many. The travelers walked, or rode in wagons if they were ill, elderly, or very young, covering perhaps a dozen miles in a good day, and fewer in bad weather. River crossings, especially of the Ohio and Mississippi, caused lengthy delays; in the winter of 1838–1839, ice on the Mississippi at the Missouri shore stranded detachments for days or weeks, with people waiting in the open or in makeshift camps. Supplies were obtained along the way through contractors and purchases, and prices rose as people passed through the region. Heavy rain, snow, and cold added to the misery, and illness, including respiratory disease, dysentery, and fevers, spread through the camps at each stopping place. Deaths occurred every day, and the dead were buried along the road, often in graves that went unmarked.

Mutual aid and Cherokee organization mitigated some of the suffering. Conductors, ministers, and physicians, including missionary physicians, tried to care for the sick, and kin groups looked after one another. Detachments held religious services and kept up songs, prayers, and hymns, and some people carried with them seeds, tools, and sacred objects. The role of Cherokee leadership meant that decisions about pace, rest, and routes could be made by people with an interest in the welfare of the travelers, though they were limited by contracts, schedules, and the cost of delay. Ross himself, whose wife Quatie died in February 1839 near Little Rock, traveled with a party, by water in part, and his leadership was both administrative and symbolic.

The first overland detachments reached Indian Territory in January 1839, and the last arrived in March. The journey had taken between three and six months, depending on the detachment. The accounts of the journey come from many sources: the detachment journals and conductors' reports, the diaries of missionaries who accompanied the parties, letters from Cherokee travelers, and the testimony recorded in later decades. Each tells a part of the story.

Section 66.17

Cherokee Arrival, Political Reconstruction, and Those Who Remained

The Cherokee who arrived in Indian Territory in 1839 found a country already home to other Cherokee. Beginning in the early nineteenth century, groups of Cherokee people, known later as the "Old Settlers" or Western Cherokee, had moved to Arkansas and then, under treaties of the 1810s and 1820s, to lands in what is now northeastern Oklahoma. They had established their own communities and a government, and they did not necessarily welcome the arrival of thousands of newcomers, whom they referred to by the term "emigrants," or in the usage of the time the Ross party. The newcomers outnumbered the earlier settlers by a large margin, and their arrival raised immediate questions about authority, land, and the structure of the nation.

The reconstruction of the Cherokee Nation in the West began with these questions. Within weeks of the final detachments' arrival, Cherokee leaders convened councils in the spring and early summer of 1839. The tension between the Old Settlers, who wanted to retain their existing government, and the Ross party, who regarded the national council as the legitimate authority, and the further bitterness of the Treaty Party, the signers of New Echota and their followers, made negotiation difficult. In June 1839 a political crisis turned violent, as Major Ridge, John Ridge, and Elias Boudinot were killed in separate attacks by men who held them responsible for the treaty. Stand Watie, another treaty signer, escaped an attempt on his life. The killings, whose planning and authorization remain matters of historical discussion, opened a period of factional violence in the Cherokee Nation that continued, with interruptions, into the 1840s.

Despite the crisis, the Cherokee negotiated an agreement. In July 1839 an Act of Union, signed by representatives of the Old Settlers and the emigrants, joined the two groups into a single nation, and in September a new constitution was adopted at Tahlequah, which became the capital. The reconstructed government drew on the earlier Cherokee constitution and the experience of the eastern nation and preserved a legislative council, courts, and an elected principal chief, John Ross. The settlement did not eliminate division; the Treaty Party and its supporters continued to contest the legitimacy of the government, and violence persisted until a treaty in 1846 provided a measure of reconciliation. But the institutions endured, and the nation rebuilt schools, a printing press, and a newspaper, and reestablished Cherokee law.

Material reconstruction was also demanding. Families had to build houses, clear fields, and obtain tools, seed, and livestock. The government provided rations for a period, and annuities and payments under the treaty provided some resources, though the administration of these funds was a continuing source of complaint. The shock of the journey, the death of family members, and the loss of eastern homes left a deep imprint, and survivors worked, in the new country, to restore ceremony, religious life, and community ties. Churches, including Baptist and Methodist congregations, and older Cherokee ceremonial practices both continued, often side by side.

Not all Cherokee left the East. Article 12 of the December 1835 Treaty of New Echota originally proposed qualified pre-emption rights for some Cherokee householders who wished to remain in North Carolina, Tennessee, and Alabama. Those rights were explicitly declared void, however, by Article 1 of the March 1, 1836 supplementary agreement, which President Jackson required as part of the ratified treaty. Cherokee people who remained did so through other individual circumstances, local arrangements, or evasion of removal, not through a surviving general Article 12 allotment guarantee. The Oconaluftee Cherokee in North Carolina maintained a distinct community with particular connections to land and to William Holland Thomas. Others evaded the roundup by hiding in the mountains. The best-known episode involved a man called Tsali, who with his family resisted soldiers after an incident of violence during the roundup; later accounts hold that he and members of his family were captured and executed, and that his surrender helped persuade General Scott to allow some fugitives to remain. The historical accuracy of this story, which became a popular legend, is contested, and historians caution that it may combine documented events with later embellishment. What is clear is that perhaps a thousand or so Cherokee remained in the mountains of western North Carolina and adjoining areas.

These communities survived with the help of a white trader and lawyer, William Holland Thomas, who had been raised among the Cherokee and acted as their agent, purchasing land for them in his own name and representing their interests in state and federal matters. Over time, the remaining Cherokee gained a recognized status, and the land they held became the Qualla Boundary. They received a state charter as the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians in the late nineteenth century and today constitute a federally recognized tribal nation, with a government, land base, and cultural institutions of its own. The Eastern Band, the Cherokee Nation, and the United Keetoowah Band of Cherokee Indians in Oklahoma are separate governments, each with its own history, and all trace parts of their ancestry to the pre-removal Cherokee.

Section 66.18

Families under Removal: Women, Children, Elders, and Enslaved People

The statistics and routes of removal can obscure its most basic reality: it was experienced by households. Across the five nations, women, children, and elders faced removal in ways that shaped both the immediate suffering and the long-term course of community survival. This section compares those experiences, drawing on testimony where securely documented, and recognizing that evidence is uneven. Records written by officials and missionaries tend to speak of "emigrants" in the aggregate, and personal accounts of women and children are rarer, though not absent.

Women held primary responsibility in many households for food, childcare, and care of the sick, and removal placed enormous burdens on them. They gave birth on the road, sometimes in wagons, in camps, or at roadsides, and the records occasionally note births among the travelers. They cared for children and elders in cold and heat, prepared what food was available, and managed the transport of household goods. In the Southeast, where women had long controlled agricultural fields and the products of their labor, forced departure meant the abandonment of fields, seeds, and the accumulated knowledge of particular landscapes. Women were also central to the transmission of language, ceremony, and kinship, and they were agents of cultural continuity in the new settlements.

Children and elders were the most vulnerable. Illness, exposure, and hunger took the heaviest toll on the very young and the very old, a pattern reported in the records of every nation's removal. Parents watched children die and buried them on the road, often without the customary ceremonies, and the loss of elders deprived communities of those who held knowledge of language, history, medicine, and law. Orphans were taken in by relatives and neighbors, in keeping with kinship obligations. Children also carried memories: many of the later oral accounts of removal come from people who were children at the time, recalling the cold, the hunger, and the deaths of relatives, and these accounts must be read with an understanding of both their power and the passage of time.

Burial and mourning were disrupted. In all these nations, the treatment of the dead was a matter of deep obligation, tied to the location of ancestors' graves and to specific ceremonies. Removal forced families to leave the graves of relatives behind and to bury those who died on the road in haste, sometimes in shared graves or unmarked ground. The grief of leaving the ancestral dead, expressed in many contemporary statements, was a distinct dimension of loss, and the effort to reestablish ceremonial and burial practices in the West was a significant part of rebuilding.

Enslaved Black people experienced removal as well, and their experience demands separate attention. Many Choctaw, Muscogee, Chickasaw, and Cherokee households, especially wealthier ones, held enslaved people, and these people were compelled to move with their enslavers, on the same routes, under harsh conditions, and with the same exposure to disease. The 1835 Cherokee census, for example, counted roughly sixteen hundred enslaved people among the Cherokee population, and other nations held enslaved people in numbers that varied by nation and household. Enslaved people were forced to leave their own families and communities, sometimes separated from relatives held by other owners, and they bore a disproportionate share of the labor of the journey, driving wagons, tending animals, and carrying loads. In the West, they cleared and cultivated the new fields of their enslavers, and slavery became more entrenched in the economies of the southeastern nations there. Slavery in these nations was part of the broader history of slavery in the United States, examined in Chapter 53, and the removal of enslaved people with their enslavers underscores that the same event was experienced very differently by people in different social positions.

Everyday Life and Social History

Household, Hunger, and Obligation on the Road

The ordinary tasks of life continued on the trail, in altered form. Families had to find or purchase food, cook it, and ration it among the household; to find water; to shelter from rain, snow, and heat; and to care for the sick. Livestock, where families had been able to bring them, were a precious resource, and their loss on the road meant the loss of future food and wealth. Many families, in the rush of the roundup or the pressure of the deadline, sold livestock for little, and arrived in the West with little to start over.

Religion and ceremony also continued. Cherokee detachments held hymn-singing and prayer, and Baptist, Methodist, and Moravian ministers and lay leaders accompanied several parties. Muscogee, Choctaw, and Seminole communities carried sacred fire and ceremonial knowledge in forms appropriate to their traditions, and rebuilt ceremonial grounds in the West. The preservation of these practices, in the midst of suffering, is part of the social history of removal and of the origin of continuity in the new country.

The evidence for these experiences is fragmentary and filtered through the recorders: officers, physicians, missionaries, and later interviewers. Historians reading it must ask who recorded what, and why, and which voices are missing, especially those of women, children, and enslaved people.

Section 66.19

Counting the Dead: Mortality, Demographic Loss, and Historical Evidence

How many people died in the removals of the southeastern nations? The question is natural, and historians have tried to answer it. But the evidence does not support precise totals, and the figures that circulate in popular accounts are estimates, often conventional numbers repeated without regard to their basis. Understanding why requires examining the sources and their limits.

Historical Vocabulary
Removal Roll

Definition. A removal roll, or emigration roll, was a list of the people assigned to a detachment or party, prepared by federal officials, conductors, or tribal authorities, sometimes recording names, household heads, numbers, and details of property or enslaved people.

Historical context. Rolls were created at various points: when people were enrolled for emigration, when they were mustered at depots, and when they arrived at western posts. They were created for administrative purposes, such as payment and rations, and not as a demographic record of mortality.

Why it matters. Comparing departure and arrival lists is one way historians estimate loss, but rolls differ in coverage and quality, and counts at different stages are not always consistent.

Potential misunderstanding. A roll is not a census. The difference between the number who started and the number who arrived may reflect deaths, but also desertions, births, errors, and people who joined or left a party.

The sources for estimating mortality are several. Departure lists, compiled at depots, record the numbers of people who set out. Arrival records, compiled by agents at western posts, record those who reached the destination. Conductors' and officers' reports sometimes list deaths during the journey, but they were compiled irregularly, and officials had incentives to understate losses. Physicians' reports, missionary journals, and newspapers add further detail. Later estimates, made by missionaries, officials, and historians, attempt to synthesize these records. For none of the five nations does a complete, reliable register of deaths exist.

Several problems make the figures uncertain. First, the starting populations are themselves estimates, and many people were not counted, among them infants, those who traveled outside organized parties, and those who left before or after the main movements. Second, deaths occurred at different stages: during roundup and confinement, on the journey, and after arrival. Records of the first two stages are better, and deaths after arrival, from disease and privation in the first months and years of resettlement, are the hardest to count, as they merge into the ordinary mortality of a stressed population. Third, the unit of record often did not include enslaved people or small parties. Fourth, historians disagree about what to include: whether deaths of those held in camps, deaths during the first year after arrival, or deaths of those who had emigrated earlier should be counted as part of the removal.

For the Cherokee, a frequently cited estimate holds that about four thousand people died, roughly a quarter of those removed, a figure traced to a contemporary estimate by a missionary physician who accompanied a detachment. Later demographers have offered different numbers, with some higher estimates that count deaths over a longer period or from related violence, and some lower ones that count only deaths on the journey. For this reason students should treat the conventional figure as an approximate and contested estimate, not as an exact documented total. For the Choctaw, estimates commonly range in the low thousands among the twelve to thirteen thousand removed. For the Muscogee, estimates likewise run into the thousands, and the loss of life in confinement, transport, and the first years in the West was considerable. For the Chickasaw, most estimates place deaths in the hundreds, with smallpox the leading cause. For the Seminole, the records of capture and transport are fragmentary, and deaths in camps, ships, and the first years in the West are uncertain.

These uncertainties should not lead to the conclusion that the losses were small or unknowable in scale. Historians agree that mortality was severe in every case and that the combined toll across the five nations was in the many thousands. The point is to be honest about what the evidence permits: ranges, not exact numbers, and a recognition that the true cost includes the unrecorded deaths and the diminished futures of survivors.

Historical Debate

Whose "Trail of Tears"? Scope, Memory, and the Meaning of a Phrase

The question: Should the phrase "Trail of Tears" refer primarily to the Cherokee removal of 1838–1839, as it does in much popular usage and in the principal national historic trail, or should it be used for the removals of all the southeastern nations, and perhaps for other forced removals?

Interpretation A — The phrase should primarily identify the Cherokee experience: Advocates note that the phrase has long been associated with the Cherokee, that Cherokee memory and the extensive documentary record of the journey have given it that meaning, that the National Park Service trail and much commemoration are organized around the Cherokee routes, and that using the term broadly risks blurring the distinct histories of each nation.
Interpretation B — The phrase should be used more broadly: Others argue that the earliest recorded use of the phrase is associated with the Choctaw removal of 1831, that Choctaw, Muscogee, Chickasaw, Seminole, and other communities have used it for their own removals, that the narrow popular usage reflects the visibility of the Cherokee and the uneven attention of non-Indigenous historians, and that confining the phrase to one nation obscures the scale of the policy.

Evidence: The debate draws on contemporary newspaper reports, later recollections, the history of commemoration, tribal historical writing, and the legislative history of the national historic trail, which has been expanded over time to include additional routes. Scholars also note that the origin of the phrase is not as simple as popular accounts suggest.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement is mostly one of terminology and emphasis, not of fact. Few historians dispute that all five nations suffered forced removal; they differ on whether a single phrase should cover them and on how best to balance national particularity with a shared history.

Why it matters: Terminology shapes public memory. A narrow use risks making the Cherokee stand for all removals; a broad use risks flattening distinct experiences. This textbook uses the phrase with specification, naming the nation or nations to which it refers, and treats removal as a set of related but distinct histories.

Student task: Construct the strongest argument for each interpretation, and then identify what additional evidence, such as tribal historical writing or the history of the phrase's use, might clarify the issue. Consider whether a third approach, using the phrase with explicit qualification, is preferable.

Section 66.20

Beyond the Trail of Tears: Survival, Nationhood, and Historical Memory

The consequences of removal extended across sovereignty, kinship, land, religion, and demography. Politically, all five nations lost the homelands in which their governments had been rooted, and had to reconstitute authority in a new country, among new neighbors, under the supervision of federal agents. Yet each rebuilt. The Choctaw adopted written laws and a western structure of districts; the Muscogee reestablished their towns and council; the Chickasaw, after a period within Choctaw institutions, formed their own government in 1856; the Cherokee, through the Act of Union and a new constitution, rebuilt a national government; and the Seminole, though divided between Oklahoma and Florida, preserved their own communities. These governments were not copies of earlier ones, since the circumstances had changed, but they preserved continuity of institutions, leadership, and identity.

Land and economy changed too. The new territories were held in common under treaties that, in the nations' understanding, guaranteed permanent ownership, a guarantee later undermined by further federal policy, discussed in later chapters on allotment. In the West, families farmed, raised livestock, and traded, and in some areas built substantial agricultural economies, with slavery becoming a significant part of the economies of some of the nations. Kinship networks, disrupted by removal, were reconstituted through marriage, adoption, and the reestablishment of clan and town relationships. Churches and schools, many of them linked to missionary organizations, became institutions of community life, while older ceremonial traditions persisted alongside them.

The nations followed different trajectories, and the comparison is instructive. The Choctaw and Chickasaw, who moved largely by negotiated emigration, maintained relatively stable political institutions, though with the tensions of shared territory. The Muscogee, who endured fraud, military force, and high mortality, emerged with a strongly rooted town structure and sustained ceremonial life. The Cherokee, who faced the most intense political division, experienced a period of factional violence before consolidating. The Seminole, whose removal was incomplete and entangled with war, became three distinct communities, in Oklahoma, in Florida, and, for a time, among those who left for Mexico. In each case, the existence of communities that remained or returned in the East, the Mississippi Choctaw, the Eastern Cherokee, the Poarch Creek in Alabama, and Florida Seminole and Miccosukee communities, ensured that removal did not sever the connection to the homeland.

Historical knowledge was also preserved. Families transmitted accounts of removal across generations, through oral narrative, and the nations preserved records, such as Cherokee newspapers and Choctaw laws and letters. Tribal governments and historical societies today maintain archives, museums, and educational programs, and many conduct their own research and commemorations. This historical memory is itself a form of continuity and, as the Historical Debate in Section 66.19 suggests, a contested terrain.

Students should distinguish carefully between the history of removal and the later history of commemoration. The events of the 1830s and early 1840s are documented in the records and testimony discussed in this chapter. The memory of those events developed over the following decades and centuries through family stories, tribal commemoration, literature, public history, and, from the twentieth century, federal recognition of the routes, including the national historic trail. Memory can illuminate how communities understood their history, but it is not identical to the documentary record, and some widely circulated accounts of the removal have been shown to be unreliable. A careful historian uses both, and identifies which is which. Chapter 132 examines the broader politics of historical memory.

Finally, the chapter points forward. The Seminole who remained in Florida had not been defeated in the sense that the army had hoped, and the long resistance that made their continued presence possible is the subject of Chapter 67. The other nations, resettled in Indian Territory, would face new pressures in the decades to come, and Indigenous communities in the Southeast would continue to maintain themselves in the face of discrimination and dispossession. The history of removal is therefore both an ending and a beginning: the end of one chapter in the life of each nation, and the start of a long history of survival and reconstruction that continues to the present.

Continuity and Change Over Time

After Displacement: What Southeastern Nations Preserved, Adapted, and Rebuilt

What Changed

  • Homelands, burial grounds, and ceremonial sites in the Southeast were lost to the nations that moved west
  • Populations suffered severe losses, and the loss of elders disrupted transmission of knowledge
  • Governments were reconstituted in new territory, often with new constitutions, districts, and capitals
  • Economies shifted to new environments, and in several nations slavery became more entrenched in the West
  • Nations divided into western and eastern communities, with separate histories and, eventually, separate governments

What Stayed the Same

  • National identity, language, and citizenship in each nation persisted
  • Clan, town, and kinship structures were reconstituted and remained central to social life
  • Ceremonial traditions, sacred fires, and religious practices were carried west and, in the East, sustained by remaining communities
  • Claims to sovereignty and treaty rights were maintained and asserted
  • Communities that remained in the Southeast preserved ties to the homeland

Timing and character of change: Change was abrupt for those who were forced out, with the loss of homeland occurring within months, and gradual for the processes of political and economic reconstruction, which unfolded over decades. Those who were removed experienced change most directly, while communities that remained experienced greater continuity of place, though often with severe legal and economic marginalization.

Why the pattern matters: The combination of loss and endurance rules out two simple stories: that removal ended Indigenous history in the Southeast, and that Indigenous nations passed through it unchanged.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the removal of the Choctaw, Muscogee, Chickasaw, Seminole, and Cherokee nations was actually carried out, how each nation experienced and resisted it, and how displaced and remaining communities survived. Its central argument is that the "Trail of Tears" was not one uniform experience but a set of related removals that differed in chronology, treaty basis, degree of force, transportation, and long-term outcome, and that removal, however devastating, did not end Indigenous history in the Southeast or the West.

Building on Chapter 65, the chapter showed that removal depended on an administrative machinery assembled hurriedly from the army, Indian agents, and private contractors. Deadlines set by treaties became military orders and contract schedules, and payment systems rewarded economy over care. Historians distinguish voluntary migration under duress, coerced treaty emigration, military removal, and resistance, but these categories overlapped, and each nation experienced some combination of them.

The Choctaw were the first to move in large numbers. The Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek, negotiated under the pressure of Mississippi law, produced three waves of removal between 1831 and 1833, in conditions that included an unusually severe winter, food shortages, and cholera. Article XIV offered a route to remain in Mississippi, but poor administration and the hostility of settlers defeated most claims. Despite this, several thousand Choctaw stayed, and their descendants form the Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians, while the western Choctaw rebuilt institutions in a new territory.

The Muscogee faced a particularly severe sequence. The Treaty of Cusseta converted collective lands into allotments that could be sold, opening the way to widespread fraud, and state jurisdiction and settler intrusion made remaining untenable. The crisis of 1836 brought a military roundup in which many people, including those who had not fought, were confined, and men identified as hostile were marched in restraints. Transportation by road and river brought high mortality, including the loss of the steamboat Monmouth in 1837. In the West the Muscogee reconstituted their towns, council, and ceremonial life.

The Chickasaw sold their lands under the Treaty of Pontotoc Creek and sought, over several years, a western home, finally negotiating the Treaty of Doaksville with the Choctaw in 1837. Their emigration, largely organized with their own funds, differed from the military removals, but it brought smallpox, the loss of elders, and financial strain, and tensions within the Choctaw Nation led to the establishment of a separate Chickasaw government in the 1850s.

The Seminole rejected treaties they said their representatives could not make, and their removal became entangled with war and with the status of Black Seminole people, who faced the danger of re-enslavement. Most Seminole and Black Seminole people were deported as captives or surrendering families in the late 1830s and early 1840s, but several hundred remained in Florida, and the continuing resistance that made this possible is the subject of Chapter 67.

The Cherokee, who had resisted removal through law, petition, and politics, were removed by the army in 1838 under a treaty signed by a minority faction. The roundup confined roughly thirteen thousand people in stockades, where disease spread. The early water detachments suffered from heat, drought, and low rivers, leading to a suspension of removal and an agreement under which Cherokee conductors organized most of the overland detachments through the winter of 1838–1839. Deaths occurred during the roundup, in confinement, on the journeys, and after arrival, and these should be distinguished.

Removal was experienced differently by women, children, elders, and enslaved Black people. Women bore responsibility for care and cultural continuity, children and elders suffered the highest mortality, and enslaved people were forced to move with their enslavers and to labor on the journey and in the West. Estimates of mortality are uncertain for every nation, because starting populations, record-keeping, and definitions of what to count all vary, and no single figure, including the conventional Cherokee estimate, should be treated as an exact documented total.

After removal, the nations rebuilt governments, kinship networks, churches, schools, and ceremonial life, and preserved historical memory through oral tradition and tribal institutions. The Mississippi Choctaw, the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians, and the Seminole Tribe of Florida and Miccosukee Tribe demonstrate the persistence of communities in the Southeast. The history of removal must be distinguished from the history of its commemoration, and debates over the phrase "Trail of Tears" show how memory shapes public understanding. The chapter's governing sequence, from removal and resistance through displacement and suffering to survival and reconstruction, rejects narratives of disappearance.

Key Terms

Removal
The federal and state policy, and its implementation, of compelling Indigenous nations to give up eastern homelands and relocate west of the Mississippi River.
Indian Territory
The region west of the Mississippi, roughly present-day Oklahoma, to which southeastern nations were relocated, already home to other Indigenous peoples.
Detachment
An organized party of emigrants traveling under a conductor, ranging from hundreds to over a thousand people.
Emigration depot
A designated assembly point where people were gathered, counted, and provisioned before departure.
Conductor
The leader responsible for managing a detachment, whether a federal officer or, in many Cherokee detachments, a Cherokee leader under contract.
Contractor
A private supplier paid to provide provisions, wagons, and transport for emigrants, whose incentives often favored economy over care.
Commutation
The conversion of a treaty entitlement, such as rations or transport, into a cash payment, a practice open to abuse.
Remainer
A modern term for an Indigenous person or family who stayed in the homeland rather than emigrating.
Article XIV
The provision of the Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek allowing Choctaw heads of families to register for land allotments and remain in Mississippi.
Allotment
The assignment of individual tracts of land, as in the Treaty of Cusseta, that converted collective holdings into property that could be sold.
Talwa
The Muscogee town, the basic political and ceremonial community of the confederacy.
Treaty of Cusseta
The 1832 agreement ceding Muscogee land east of the Mississippi and creating allotments.
Treaty of Pontotoc Creek
The 1832 agreement by which the Chickasaw ceded their Mississippi lands to be sold for the nation's benefit.
Treaty of Doaksville
The 1837 agreement allowing the Chickasaw to settle in western Choctaw territory in return for payment.
Treaty of Payne's Landing
The 1832 agreement with some Seminole leaders providing for removal, whose validity Seminole leaders disputed.
Black Seminole
Black people living in close association with Seminole communities, including descendants of those who escaped slavery, for whom removal posed a danger of re-enslavement.
Treaty of New Echota
The 1835 agreement signed by a Cherokee faction that served as the legal basis for Cherokee removal despite widespread opposition.
Stockade
A temporary fort or camp in which Cherokee and Muscogee people were confined before transport.
Old Settlers
Cherokee who had moved west before the 1838 removal and established communities and a government there.
Act of Union (1839)
The agreement joining the Old Settlers and the emigrant Cherokee into one nation, followed by a new constitution.
Treaty Party
The Cherokee faction that signed the Treaty of New Echota, including Major Ridge, John Ridge, Elias Boudinot, and Stand Watie.
Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians
The federally recognized nation descended from Cherokee who remained in the mountains of western North Carolina, centered on the Qualla Boundary.
Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians
The federally recognized nation descended from Choctaw who remained in Mississippi.
Removal roll
A list of people assigned to a detachment or party, created for administrative purposes and useful but imperfect for estimating loss.
Monmouth
The steamboat that sank in October 1837 on the Mississippi with a large party of Muscogee emigrants, causing hundreds of deaths.
Trail of Tears
A phrase associated especially with the Cherokee removal but also applied to the removals of other southeastern nations; its scope is debated.
Mortality estimate
A reasoned approximation of deaths, derived from incomplete records, that should be reported as a range rather than an exact count.
Historical commemoration
The later public remembrance of events, distinct from the documentary record of what occurred.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows what a policy looks like when it meets human lives. Chapter 65 described removal as a set of laws, treaties, and arguments. Chapter 66 shows that these became deadlines, stockades, river crossings, burial places, and the daily work of families trying to keep one another alive. The comparison among five nations reveals that outcomes were shaped by treaty terms, by Indigenous political decisions, by the methods of enforcement, and by the physical conditions of the journeys, and that none of these can be reduced to a single cause.

The chapter also reveals larger processes. Removal was part of the expansion of settler states across the continent, and it shaped the geography of Indigenous North America for generations, concentrating many southeastern peoples in Indian Territory and creating new relationships among them, the United States, and the peoples already there. It deepened the entanglement of Indigenous nations with slavery in the West, and it set precedents for later policies of concentration and confinement examined in Volume VII.

At the same time, the chapter shows that removal did not end Indigenous history. The Choctaw, Muscogee, Chickasaw, Seminole, and Cherokee nations rebuilt governments and communities in the West, and the Mississippi Choctaw, Eastern Cherokee, Florida Seminole, and Miccosukee communities sustained life in the Southeast. These continuities link this chapter to the later histories of tribal governance, citizenship, and cultural resurgence in Volume X.

Finally, the chapter asks students to think carefully about how history is known and remembered. Official records, Indigenous testimony, mortality estimates, and public commemoration each offer partial views, and the work of history is to read them against one another. Understanding the Trail of Tears means holding together the scale of loss and the endurance of nations. The next chapter turns to the Seminole, whose resistance in Florida shaped both the course of removal and the survival of communities that were never removed.
