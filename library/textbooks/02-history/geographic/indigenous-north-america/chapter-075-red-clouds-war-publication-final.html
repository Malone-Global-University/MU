Red Cloud's War

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

In June 1866, while United States commissioners were still asking Lakota leaders at Fort Laramie to allow a road through the Powder River country, soldiers arrived to build forts along that road. The leaders who had come to negotiate saw the contradiction at once. Within two years the army would abandon those forts.

The negotiations at Fort Laramie in the summer of 1866 were meant to secure Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho consent to the Bozeman Trail, a wagon road that ran northwest from the Platte River valley, east of the Bighorn Mountains, toward the gold camps of Montana Territory. Federal Indian Office commissioners were still in council when Colonel Henry B. Carrington's battalion of the 18th Infantry marched into the post, on its way to build military posts along the very road under discussion. Red Cloud, an Oglala leader, and others who had come to hear the proposals concluded that the United States intended to take the country whether or not they agreed. Many of them left the council and returned to the Powder River country, where they would spend the next two years resisting the occupation.

This chapter examines that resistance, conventionally called Red Cloud's War. The label is a retrospective American one, and it can mislead in two ways. It suggests a single commander directing a single army, when the participants were independently governed Lakota, Northern Cheyenne, and Northern Arapaho communities that cooperated because they shared a threat. It also suggests a sequence of famous battles, when the war was a sustained campaign against a system: a road, three forts, and the wagon trains, woodcutting parties, hay crews, and herds that kept those forts alive. Understanding the war means understanding why a few hundred soldiers in isolated posts, far from their sources of supply, could be held in a state of siege by communities that never needed to capture the posts themselves.

The chapter's central question is how Red Cloud and allied Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho communities successfully resisted American military occupation of the Powder River country, and what their success reveals about Indigenous political power, military strategy, and the limits of United States expansion. The answer developed here rests on four conclusions. The conflict arose from competing territorial claims and an American effort to establish permanent military access through Indigenous hunting grounds. The resistance depended on cooperation among communities that retained separate political authority. The Fetterman Fight of December 1866 was a major Indigenous victory, while the Hayfield and Wagon Box fights of August 1867 showed changes in American weapons and tactics without reversing the strategic situation. And the American abandonment of the forts in 1868 was a significant Indigenous achievement whose consequences were bounded by the treaty system that followed.

The sequence of the chapter follows the logic of those conclusions. Division I establishes the Indigenous nations, the ecology of the Powder River country, the treaty order created at Fort Laramie in 1851, and the Montana gold rush that turned an Indigenous travel corridor into a wagon road. Division II traces the escalation from the Sand Creek aftermath and the 1865 campaigns through the failed council of 1866 and the construction of the forts. Division III examines Indigenous military strategy, the siege conditions around Fort Phil Kearny, and the Fetterman Fight. Division IV follows the war through 1867, including the Hayfield and Wagon Box fights. Division V explains why the United States reconsidered, how Red Cloud made abandonment a condition of peace, and what the withdrawal did and did not accomplish.

The evidence for this history is uneven, and the chapter says so as it goes. Army reports, correspondence, newspapers, and the memoirs of officers and their families provide detailed accounts of fort construction, garrison life, and the engagements, but they describe Indigenous intentions from outside and often with reference to their authors' reputations. Indigenous evidence includes winter counts, oral histories recorded decades after the events, and tribal historical accounts, which preserve Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho understandings of the war but were shaped by the circumstances of their recording and transmission. Archaeological work at the fort sites and battlefields adds material evidence. Casualty figures, especially for Indigenous participants, rest on estimates that vary widely, and this chapter does not treat any single number as established where the evidence does not allow it.

The chapter also sits at a boundary. It follows Chapter 74's account of Sand Creek, whose survivors carried the war north, and it precedes Chapter 76's examination of the 1868 Treaty of Fort Laramie and the Great Sioux Reservation. Chapter 75 owns the war and the withdrawal. It introduces the treaty only to explain how the war ended, and it leaves the treaty's territorial, legal, and long-term provisions to Chapter 76. It is also part of the larger story of continental conquest and resistance told in this volume, in which Indigenous nations repeatedly forced the United States to negotiate, and in which negotiated settlements repeatedly proved less secure than the victories that produced them.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how independently governed Lakota, Northern Cheyenne, and Northern Arapaho communities cooperated in resisting the Bozeman Trail forts without a centralized command.
  2. Describe the ecological and geographic foundations of Indigenous life in the Powder River country and why a permanent American road threatened them.
  3. Analyze the Fort Laramie Treaty of 1851 and the differences between boundaries recorded by the United States and the actual territorial relationships among Plains nations, including the Crow.
  4. Trace the transformation of an Indigenous travel corridor into the Bozeman Trail after the Montana gold discoveries of 1862–1863.
  5. Connect the Sand Creek aftermath and the campaigns of 1865 to the escalation of 1866.
  6. Evaluate why the Fort Laramie council of 1866 failed, with attention to the conflicting roles of the Indian Office and the army.
  7. Explain how Indigenous raiding, mobility, and attacks on wood, hay, and supply trains made the forts strategically difficult to sustain.
  8. Reconstruct the Fetterman Fight of December 21, 1866, distinguishing army reports from Indigenous accounts and identifying disputed points.
  9. Assess the Hayfield and Wagon Box fights and the role of breech-loading rifles without overstating technology as a determinant of outcomes.
  10. Distinguish tactical victory, strategic victory, and treaty settlement, and apply the distinctions to specific engagements.
  11. Interpret the American decision to abandon the forts as the product of interacting military, financial, and transportation factors.
  12. Evaluate the achievements and limits of Red Cloud's victory and explain why the full treaty settlement requires separate study in Chapter 76.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the United States treat the Powder River country as a transit zone when Lakota, Cheyenne, Arapaho, and Crow communities treated it as a place to live, hunt, and govern?
  2. How could communities that did not share a single government sustain a coordinated military campaign for two years?
  3. Why did a road of modest length require a chain of forts, escorts, and supply trains, and how did that requirement create vulnerabilities?
  4. What does the contradiction between the 1866 treaty council and the arrival of Carrington's troops reveal about how the United States government actually functioned?
  5. Why do accounts of the Fetterman Fight differ, and how should historians weigh army reports against Indigenous testimony?
  6. In what sense was the war won by Indigenous forces even though they suffered tactical reverses at Hayfield and Wagon Box?
  7. How did railroads, Montana's changing economy, and the cost of garrisons interact with battlefield pressure in shaping American policy?
  8. Why did Red Cloud insist on abandonment of the forts before signing, and what did that insistence achieve that a promise could not?
  9. What were the limits of the 1868 withdrawal, and why should it not be read as the permanent defeat of American expansion?
  10. How have later narratives about Fetterman, Red Cloud, and Crazy Horse shaped, and sometimes distorted, the historical record?

Historical Context

By the 1860s the northern Great Plains were a region of Indigenous political and economic strength. The horse, examined in Chapter 49, had reshaped Plains life over the preceding century, and the bison economy supported mobile communities with substantial military capacity. The Lakota had expanded westward across the Missouri and into the country west of the Black Hills during the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, and by midcentury the Oglala, Brulé, Miniconjou, and others ranged across the region between the Platte River and the Yellowstone. They did so alongside Northern Cheyenne and Northern Arapaho allies, and in competition and sometimes conflict with the Crow, Shoshone, and other peoples who had longer histories in parts of the same country.

The United States entered this region as a transit power. The Oregon, California, and Mormon trails along the Platte brought hundreds of thousands of emigrants across Indigenous hunting grounds from the 1840s onward, and the Fort Laramie Treaty of 1851 attempted to regulate that traffic while recognizing territorial divisions among the nations. Neither the trail traffic nor the treaty gave the United States a permanent presence in the Powder River country north of the Platte. The Civil War, the Dakota War examined in Chapter 73, and the conflicts on the central Plains, culminating in the Sand Creek massacre of Chapter 74, shifted the army's attention and also drove some Cheyenne and Arapaho refugees north, strengthening the alliances on which the later resistance depended.

The gold rushes of 1862 and 1863 in what became Montana Territory changed the calculation. Thousands of miners and the merchants and freighters who supplied them created demand for a shorter route from the Platte to the goldfields. The Bozeman Trail, opened in 1863, followed an Indigenous travel corridor along the eastern flank of the Bighorn Mountains, through some of the richest bison country remaining on the northern Plains. The road made the Powder River country a corridor of American interest for the first time, and when federal officials sought to protect travelers on it with permanent military posts, the question became whether the United States could establish a military presence on land its own treaties had not ceded.

The army that attempted this was exhausted and in transition. The volunteer regiments of the Civil War were being mustered out, the regular army was small and stretched across the South and the West, and the officers sent to the northern Plains were often brevet-rank veterans who had little experience of Plains warfare. The Indian Office and the army also pursued different aims. Indian Office officials sought treaties and the safety of emigrant travel at modest cost, while many army officers believed that only military occupation or decisive defeat would bring Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho resistance to an end. The conflict between these approaches runs through the entire chapter.

Division I

Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho Sovereignty in the Powder River Country

Section 75.1

The Lakota, Northern Cheyenne, and Northern Arapaho Peoples

The nations that resisted the Bozeman Trail forts had distinct political histories, and the war cannot be understood unless those histories are kept apart. The Lakota, the western division of the Oceti Sakowin or Seven Council Fires, spoke the Lakota dialect of the Siouan language family. They were organized into seven divisions: the Sičháŋǧu (Brulé), Oglála, Mnikȟówožu (Miniconjou), Itázipčho (Sans Arc), Sihásapa (Blackfeet), Húŋkpapȟa, and Oóhenuŋpa (Two Kettle). Of these, the Oglala, Miniconjou, and Brulé were the principal participants in the Powder River fighting, with individuals and groups from other divisions taking part. The Cheyenne, who call themselves Tsitsistas, and the Arapaho, who call themselves Hinono'eino, speak languages of the Algonquian family, which makes them linguistically unrelated to the Lakota. By the 1860s each had separated into northern and southern divisions that remained connected by kinship and ceremony but increasingly acted separately.

Lakota social organization rested on the tiyošpaye, the extended family band, which usually moved and camped as a unit and was led by a headman whose influence depended on generosity, wisdom, and the confidence of his relatives. A band joined others for the summer bison hunt and for the ceremonies of early summer, when large camps formed. In these camps, order was maintained by akíčita, societies of men who policed the hunt and enforced camp rules, and by councils of respected headmen. Oglala and other Lakota communities also recognized the Shirt Wearers, a small group of young men chosen for their bravery and character to serve as protectors of the people, an office whose duties combined military leadership with an obligation to settle disputes. Cheyenne political life centered on a council of forty-four peace chiefs, drawn from the bands, and on military societies, such as the Kit Fox, Elk, Crooked Lance, Dog, and Bowstring societies, that organized hunting, policing, and war. Arapaho communities had age-graded societies and a ceremonial life that bound the northern bands together.

Political decisions in all three nations were made by deliberation and persuasion rather than command. A leader who proposed war or peace could not compel a band to follow him, and individual warriors and bands sometimes acted against the preferences of respected headmen. This did not make Plains politics chaotic. It meant that leadership was situational: a war leader gained a following when he proposed a sound plan and had a record of success, and he lost it when his plans failed or his advice proved wrong. The distinction between military leadership and political consensus is central to this chapter, because the participants in the Powder River fighting did not constitute a single army with a chain of command, and the choices of one band could differ from those of another.

Several leaders emerged during the conflict, and their prominence in later accounts should be understood in this context. Red Cloud, an Oglala born about 1822, had earned a reputation as a warrior and had become a respected voice in Oglala councils. His influence came from war honors, kinship ties, and political skill rather than from hereditary chieftainship, and it was strongest among the Oglala bands that camped with his. Man Afraid of His Horses held a long-standing position of respect among the Oglala and, like his son, Young Man Afraid, took part in the councils and the fighting. Crazy Horse, a younger Oglala, was gaining a reputation as a war leader, and some accounts identify him among the Shirt Wearers. Spotted Tail, a Brulé, favored negotiation earlier than Red Cloud, a reminder that Lakota opinion was divided on how to respond to American demands. Among the Northern Cheyenne, leaders such as Dull Knife and Little Wolf were prominent in the period, and the Northern Arapaho had their own headmen, including Black Bear, whose village was attacked in 1865.

The three nations had long cooperated. The Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho had been allies since the early nineteenth century, hunting and fighting together against common rivals, and intermarriage created kinship networks across national lines. This history made military cooperation in 1866 possible without a single government. When the army began building forts in country all three nations used, each had its own reasons to resist and its own network of obligations to draw on. Shared territorial interests supplied the common purpose, kinship supplied the channels for communication, and leaders in each nation persuaded their own people. The result was an alliance that was powerful but not centralized, and one whose members pursued overlapping rather than identical aims, a point that becomes important in the diplomacy of 1867 and 1868.

Section 75.2

The Powder River Country: Buffalo, Rivers, Trade, and Indigenous Homelands

The Powder River country is the high plains and basin country that lies between the Black Hills to the east and the Bighorn Mountains to the west, bounded on the north by the Yellowstone River and extending south toward the North Platte. Its principal streams, the Powder, Tongue, and Bighorn rivers, flow north toward the Yellowstone, and their valleys, with the broken country between them, supported cottonwood groves, grass, and game. The Bighorn Mountains, rising to the west, provided timber, lodgepole pine for tipi poles, and summer pasture, and also served as a boundary and a refuge. The basin's winters were severe, but the river bottoms offered shelter, fuel, and winter grazing for horses, which made them favored places for winter camps.

For the Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho, the country's value lay above all in the bison that moved through it in the spring and summer, and in the ability to keep large horse herds and to move camps in response to the movement of the herds. Bison supplied meat, hides for tipi covers, robes, and containers, sinew, bone tools, and fuel. Women processed the hides and meat and managed the camp, so that the hunt was a collective enterprise on which the whole community depended. Seasonal movement followed the animals and the grass: communities gathered in large camps for summer hunts and ceremonies, dispersed into smaller bands for the fall hunt, and settled into sheltered winter camps. Mobility was therefore both an economic strategy and a political one. A mobile community could avoid conflict, relocate in response to threat, and combine with others when the need arose, and it could not easily be captured or compelled.

Trade also tied the region together. Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho communities traded robes and horses with trading posts on the Missouri and the North Platte, and they obtained cloth, metal goods, firearms, and ammunition in exchange, as well as through raiding and diplomacy. Annuity goods under the 1851 treaty added to these supplies. These relationships gave the communities access to rifles and ammunition, a point worth stressing because the army's occupation of the Powder River country took place among communities that were not isolated from the wider economy and were well armed with the firearms of the period, including some obtained through trade and through capture.

It would be a mistake to describe the region as the uncontested property of the Lakota and their allies. The Crow, who call themselves Apsáalooke, had long used the country along the Yellowstone, Bighorn, and Powder rivers, and they considered much of it their homeland. The Lakota and Cheyenne had pushed into the Powder River country during the first half of the nineteenth century, often in conflict with the Crow, and by the 1860s the region was a contested borderland where control shifted with the fortunes of war. Shoshone, Bannock, and other peoples also had claims and interests to the west. The American road and forts entered this landscape of overlapping claims, and some Crow leaders saw advantages in cooperating with the army against their Lakota and Cheyenne rivals. This does not make the Crow collaborators in any simple sense. They were pursuing their own interests, as were the Lakota and Cheyenne, in a region that had never been governed by a single authority.

The significance of these ecological and political facts for the war is that a permanent road, with its stations, forts, grazing herds, and wagon traffic, threatened the foundations of Indigenous life in the region. Travelers on the road cut timber, burned grass, and shot game, and their livestock competed for forage along the river bottoms where horses wintered. A permanent military post meant permanent consumption of wood, hay, and meat, and permanent armed force. The effects were not limited to the immediate vicinity of the road: bison herds were known to avoid heavily traveled routes, and the disruption of movement and of hunting could affect communities far from the trail. Indigenous leaders understood these consequences, and their opposition to the road reflected a clear understanding of what it meant for their economy and their independence.

Section 75.3

The Fort Laramie Treaty of 1851 and the Northern Plains Territorial Order

The Fort Laramie council of September 1851, held on Horse Creek downstream from the post, was among the largest diplomatic gatherings in the history of the Plains. Thousands of people, representing the Lakota, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Crow, Assiniboine, Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara, assembled to meet the commissioners of the United States, David D. Mitchell and Thomas Fitzpatrick. The United States wanted guarantees of safe passage for emigrants on the Platte road, the right to establish roads and military posts in Indigenous territory, and an end to intertribal warfare that disrupted travel. The nations present wanted compensation for the damage the emigrant traffic was doing to game and grass, recognition of their territories, and relief from the pressures of war with their neighbors.

The treaty recognized the territories of the participating nations, assigned them boundaries, and committed each to refrain from depredations against emigrants and against each other. In exchange, the United States promised annual payments of goods, which became known as annuities, and pledged to protect the nations from the depredations of its own citizens. The Senate later amended the annuity term, reducing the period of payments from fifty years to ten, with a possible extension of five more, a change the nations that had agreed to the original terms did not all clearly understand. The treaty also gave the United States the right to establish roads and military posts in the territories, a provision that would later be invoked to justify the Bozeman Trail forts.

The territories the treaty recorded were American representations of Indigenous geography, and they did not match the way the nations understood their relationships to the land. The United States recorded boundaries as lines; the nations that used the country understood their claims as overlapping zones of use and ties, defined by kinship, alliance, and the control that mobility and force allowed. The treaty assigned the country between the Platte and the Yellowstone, including the Powder River basin, to different nations in a manner that reflected a negotiated and incomplete understanding rather than a settled reality. The Crow were assigned land that included parts of the Powder River basin, the Lakota were recognized in country to the east, and the Cheyenne and Arapaho were assigned lands to the south. The Lakota and their allies continued to hunt in the Powder River country after 1851, and the Crow continued to contest their presence.

The treaty's provisions about roads and posts illustrate the gap between American and Indigenous understandings. American officials interpreted the right to establish roads and posts as a general authority, covering any road and any post the government considered necessary. Many Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho leaders understood the grant as limited to the Platte road that already existed and the posts that protected it. When the government proposed the Bozeman Trail, with its military posts, they regarded it as a new road through hunting grounds not covered by the agreement. The difference was not a matter of misunderstanding that careful translation might have resolved. It reflected a real conflict between a government that treated treaty rights as expansive and communities that treated them as limited, and it set the pattern for the disputes of the 1860s. The treaty also had a more immediate failure: the annuity goods were often late or inadequate, and the fighting that followed the Grattan affair of 1854 and the army's attack on a Brulé village at Ash Hollow in 1855 showed how fragile the diplomatic order was.

Historical Vocabulary
Unceded Territory and Indigenous Land Rights

Definition. Unceded territory is land that an Indigenous nation has not transferred to another government by treaty, sale, or other agreement. The term appears in the Fort Laramie treaty of 1868, which designated a large region as unceded Indian territory, and it has a specific legal meaning there. It is also used more broadly in historical and contemporary discussion to describe land that was never legally surrendered.

Historical context. In the 1860s, the Powder River country had not been ceded by the Lakota, Cheyenne, or Arapaho. The 1851 treaty recognized territories and rights of passage, but it did not transfer title to the United States. The American government nevertheless acted as though it could build roads and forts there, relying on the treaty's road provisions and on its own assertion of authority.

Why it matters. The concept shows that the war was a dispute over the rights of a government that had not acquired the land it was occupying. It also shows the difference between legal recognition and practical control. A treaty could recognize a nation's territory without the nation controlling it, and a nation could control territory without any treaty recognizing it. In the Powder River country, practical control was exercised by the Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho in the face of a competing Crow claim.

Potential misunderstanding. "Unceded" does not mean that a territory belonged exclusively to one nation. The Powder River country had overlapping Indigenous claims, including those of the Crow, and recognition of one nation's rights in a treaty did not extinguish the claims of others.

The distinction between a diplomatic boundary and lived territorial authority was especially consequential in the Powder River basin. A line recorded in an American treaty could recognize one nation's claim on paper while other communities continued to hunt, camp, and defend the same valleys. The 1851 council attempted to reduce intertribal conflict and accommodate emigrant travel, but the agreement itself did not settle those overlapping claims. Lakota and Cheyenne expansion, Crow defense of long-used homelands, and Arapaho hunting and travel all remained realities after the commissioners departed. In evaluating the later war, students must therefore avoid assuming that either a federal treaty map or a military map perfectly described who governed the ground. The army's road could threaten Indigenous subsistence even where the competing Indigenous territorial claims remained unsettled.

Section 75.4

Montana Gold, the Bozeman Trail, and the Transformation of Indigenous Territory

Gold discoveries in the Rocky Mountains of what is now southwestern Montana, at Grasshopper Creek in 1862 and Alder Gulch in 1863, brought thousands of miners to the region within a few years. Mining camps, among them Bannack and Virginia City, grew almost overnight, and the miners needed food, tools, and equipment that had to be hauled from the Missouri River, the Salt Lake valley, or the Platte. Freighting companies saw profit in supplying them, and routes proliferated. Most of the early traffic reached the goldfields from the south by way of Salt Lake City or from the east by steamboat to Fort Benton on the Missouri. A route from the North Platte directly northwest toward the goldfields promised to be shorter than either.

In 1863 John Bozeman and John Jacobs, two entrepreneurs with experience in the region, led a party along such a route, following a path that Indigenous travelers had long used along the eastern flank of the Bighorn Mountains. The route left the Platte road near the North Platte, crossed the Powder, Tongue, and Bighorn rivers, and turned west across the Yellowstone valley toward the goldfields. The result was a wagon road, which became known as the Bozeman Trail and was shorter than the alternatives. Jim Bridger soon identified another route west of the Bighorns that avoided much of the Powder River country, but the Bozeman route remained in use because it was the shortest. In 1864 traffic on the road increased, and the Lakota and their allies began attacking wagon trains and travelers.

The difference between transient movement across a region and the construction of a permanent corridor is important here. Indigenous peoples had always moved through the Powder River country, traded with each other along its trails, and allowed travelers to cross when relationships permitted. A wagon road was different in kind. It consumed wood, grass, and game in quantity and brought organized parties of armed men who intended to pass through and to return, and the mining economy created a continuing demand that would produce continuing traffic. Government officials saw the road as a convenience for travelers and a source of revenue for the territories it connected. Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho leaders saw it as the entry of a permanent American presence into a region that had been protected, for the time being, by the lack of one.

That judgment proved accurate. Travelers on the trail required protection, and the government's obvious answer was a chain of military posts along its length. Freighters, who had a financial interest in the road, lobbied for military escorts and forts, and the territorial governments of Montana and Idaho pressed the same case in Washington. The army, which had commanded most of the western garrison through the Civil War, now had to decide whether to commit the troops required. The decision to do so, made in 1866, turned an Indigenous travel corridor and a contested hunting ground into a militarized road, and the Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho responded as the occupation of their homeland required.

Division II

American Expansion and the Beginning of Red Cloud's War

Section 75.5

Sand Creek's Aftermath and the Powder River Conflict of 1865

The massacre at Sand Creek, examined in Chapter 74, was carried out by Colorado volunteers in November 1864 against a camp of Southern Cheyenne and Arapaho who believed themselves under military protection. Survivors scattered. Many Cheyenne and Arapaho moved north to join Lakota, Northern Cheyenne, and Northern Arapaho relatives on the Smoky Hill, Republican, and Platte rivers, and from there into the Powder River country. They carried with them an account of what had been done and an intense desire for retaliation. In January and February 1865 allied Cheyenne, Arapaho, and Lakota warriors raided along the South Platte, attacking the stage line and the town of Julesburg, and the war on the central Plains spread north. The movement of refugees and the alliances that formed strengthened the coalition that would later resist the Bozeman Trail forts.

The army responded in the summer of 1865 with a large campaign. The commander of the District of the Plains, Brigadier General Patrick E. Connor, organized the Powder River Expedition with the aim of punishing the Indigenous nations and opening the Bozeman route. It was a three-column operation: Connor led one column, Colonel Nelson Cole another, and Lieutenant Colonel Samuel Walker a third, and the three were to unite in the Powder River country. Before the expedition took the field, allied Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho forces struck at Platte Bridge Station, a post on the North Platte near present-day Casper, Wyoming. On July 26, 1865, a large force attacked the bridge garrison, and a detachment under Lieutenant Caspar Collins, sent out to meet an approaching wagon train, was driven back with Collins killed. A detachment of the wagon train under Sergeant Amos Custard was also destroyed at a site some miles away. The attack demonstrated that the allies could coordinate large forces over considerable distances.

Connor's expedition itself went poorly. His column built a post on the Powder River, first called Fort Connor and later renamed Fort Reno, and on August 29, 1865, attacked an Arapaho village on the Tongue River. The village was under Black Bear, and army accounts describe it as a hostile camp; Arapaho accounts and later historians emphasize that it included women, children, and elders, and that the attack fell on a community with its own reasons for being in the area. Army reports claimed substantial casualties among the Arapaho, but the reliability of those figures is uncertain, and the Arapaho who experienced the attack remembered it as a devastating assault on a village that had been camped with its families. The army captured horses and destroyed lodges.

The other columns did worse. Cole's and Walker's forces, moving through the Powder River basin in late summer, were harassed by Lakota and Cheyenne warriors, ran short of supplies, and lost large numbers of horses and mules in a September snowstorm. Soldiers were reduced to eating mule meat and wandering in search of the other columns, and the expedition ended without achieving its objectives. Connor himself was replaced late in the year. The campaign's failure had two effects. It gave the Lakota and their allies confidence that the army could be repelled, and it showed the army that campaigning in the Powder River country required a secure supply base, a lesson that would shape the decision to build forts in 1866.

The 1865 campaign should be understood as the immediate military background to Red Cloud's War, not the beginning of the concentrated 1866–1868 conflict. It also shows that the participating nations had different experiences and interests. The Northern Arapaho, whose village was attacked, had suffered a direct assault on a community. The Cheyenne who had fled Sand Creek had a particular desire for retaliation. The Lakota were primarily concerned with defending their hunting grounds. These differences did not prevent cooperation, but they remind students that the alliance was a coalition of communities with partly shared and partly distinct aims.

Section 75.6

Red Cloud, Indigenous Alliances, and the Defense of the Powder River Country

Red Cloud emerged as the most prominent Lakota spokesman against the Bozeman Trail forts, and his name became attached to the war because American officials and journalists identified him as its principal leader. He had, by the mid-1860s, a substantial following among the Oglala, and the Oglala, whose territory extended to the Powder River country, were the Lakota division most directly affected by the road. His standing rested on his record as a war leader, his ability to speak effectively in council, and his reputation for opposing American incursions. Those qualities made him an effective advocate for resistance, but they did not give him authority over bands that did not follow him, and they did not make him a commander in the sense American officers understood the term.

The resistance was organized through the ordinary institutions of Plains politics. Councils of headmen met to discuss the American advance, and camps of different communities exchanged information through scouts and messengers who moved between them. Kinship networks, built through marriage and alliance, enabled the Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho to communicate and to arrange cooperation. When a leader proposed a particular operation, he sought volunteers, and warriors from different bands joined according to their own judgment. Military societies, including those of the Cheyenne and the akíčita of the Lakota, helped to maintain order in large camps and to organize parties, and they provided a framework of discipline for operations that involved many warriors.

Within this system, military leadership was situational. Some leaders were recognized for their skill in organizing raids and ambushes, and their advice carried weight when a major operation was proposed. Crazy Horse was among those whose reputation grew in this period, and later narratives assign him a decisive role in the Fetterman Fight and other engagements. The participant evidence on his specific actions is disputed. Accounts from Lakota and Cheyenne witnesses, recorded decades later, differ on who led the decoy party on December 21 and on the details of its role. This chapter does not assign particular tactical decisions to particular leaders where the evidence does not support it. It treats the war's operations as the product of collective planning and individual initiative within an alliance whose members sometimes disagreed.

The alliance's cohesion should be distinguished from centralization. The Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho coordinated because they faced a common threat, shared a set of relationships, and trusted each other's leaders. They did not have a common government, a common treasury, or a common command structure. Cooperation could break down, as it did when bands dispersed for winter or when a major operation failed and leaders blamed each other. It could also be sustained for long periods when a clear objective, such as the destruction of a wood train or the siege of a fort, held the participants together. The ability of such an alliance to maintain a campaign for two years, with a changing cast of participants and without central direction, reflects the strength of the kinship ties and political traditions on which it was built.

Cooperation also depended on work beyond the battlefield. Large gatherings required food, ammunition, horses, scouting, and information that could move between winter camps, trading places, and seasonal meetings. Leaders could encourage participation through persuasion and kinship, but families had to decide whether they could spare warriors while also maintaining hunts and protecting children. The coalition's military strength fluctuated with the calendar, the distribution of bison, and the condition of horses. A force large enough to confront an army detachment could assemble for a particular operation and disperse afterward without ceasing to share a political objective. This pattern makes contemporary American reports of a single, continuously organized hostile army particularly misleading. A succession of attacks could sustain pressure even though the participants changed from one engagement to the next.

Section 75.7

The Fort Laramie Council of 1866 and the Breakdown of Negotiations

In the spring of 1866 the federal government sought agreement for the Bozeman Trail through a treaty council at Fort Laramie. The effort was led by the Indian Office, whose representative, Commissioner E. B. Taylor of the Northern Superintendency, invited Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho leaders to meet. Some, including Spotted Tail's Brulé and Red Cloud and Man Afraid of His Horses of the Oglala, came to hear what the government proposed. The Indian Office's aim was to obtain the consent of the nations to the opening of the road, in exchange for presents and annuities. Taylor and his colleagues presented the proposal as a modest request for the right to pass through the country, without mentioning, at first, that the army intended to build forts.

While the council was in progress, Colonel Henry B. Carrington arrived at Fort Laramie with the 2nd Battalion of the 18th Infantry, which had been ordered to garrison and build posts along the Bozeman Trail. Carrington, an Ohio lawyer and Civil War staff officer with administrative skill and little experience of field command, had received orders from the Department of the Platte to occupy the road. His arrival, with soldiers, wagons, and the equipment of a military expedition, made clear what the United States intended. According to accounts that survive, Red Cloud and other leaders asked what the troops were for, and their suspicions were confirmed. The details of the exchange differ among sources, and accounts by commissioners and officers disagree on what was said and who said it, but the essential facts are not in dispute: Red Cloud and those who followed him left the council, and the negotiators proceeded to obtain the signatures of leaders who remained.

The central question is how the negotiations could have been credible when one department of the government was seeking agreement and another was already implementing occupation. The Indian Office and the army answered to different authorities and pursued different aims, and neither had full control over the other. Taylor appears to have wanted an agreement and to have minimized the military purpose of Carrington's expedition, and Carrington carried out his orders, which assumed the road would be occupied. The Lakota who observed the proceedings drew the obvious conclusion that the government's promises were not reliable, since the government could not control its own agents. The contradiction between negotiation and occupation, which the state historical interpretation in Wyoming identifies as a decisive cause of Red Cloud's refusal to sign, was not a mere accident. It reflected the structure of the American government and the divergent aims of its departments.

The agreement the government obtained was with the leaders who stayed, including some Brulé, and Taylor reported that a treaty had been made and that the road was open. The leaders who had left, among them the principal Oglala headmen whose territory the road crossed, had not agreed. The United States thus claimed an agreement that did not include the communities that would experience the occupation most directly, and it proceeded on that claim. Red Cloud returned to the Powder River country, and the war that followed was in part a consequence of the government's decision to treat a limited and disputed agreement as a general consent.

For the leaders who departed, refusing to sign was a substantive political act rather than simply a breakdown in communication. Their objection was to the presence of soldiers who were already implementing a policy for which consent was still being requested. Their withdrawal also exposed a limit of treaty diplomacy with decentralized nations: a commissioner could collect signatures from those remaining at the council, but that document did not necessarily bind the communities whose camps and hunting grounds were closest to the proposed forts. The army could construct the posts, yet construction itself did not create acceptance or secure the traffic it was meant to protect. That unresolved problem carried directly into the fighting of the summer and autumn of 1866.

Section 75.8

Fort Reno, Fort Phil Kearny, and Fort C. F. Smith: The Military Occupation of 1866

Carrington's mission was to establish and garrison military posts along the Bozeman Trail and to protect its travelers. He moved his command north in June 1866 and began with the existing post on the Powder River, which he renamed Fort Reno, after a Civil War general, and which he strengthened. He then selected a site for a new post farther north, near the Bighorn Mountains, on a plateau between Big Piney and Little Piney creeks, and in July 1866 began construction of Fort Phil Kearny, named for a Union general killed in 1862. In August a detachment under Major Nathaniel C. Kinney built a third post, Fort C. F. Smith, on the Bighorn River, close to Crow country. The three forts were spaced along the road in an arrangement intended to allow mutual support and to provide shelter for travelers, though the distances between them were long.

These forts differed from Fort Laramie, which was an older post on the North Platte with an established relationship to Indigenous nations and a role as a trading and diplomatic center, and from the posts that protected the Union Pacific Railroad as it advanced across the plains. The Bozeman Trail forts were new, were located within Indigenous hunting grounds, and had no function other than to hold the road. Fort Phil Kearny, the largest, was built with a stockade of logs and enclosed barracks, quarters, storehouses, and corrals, and it served as the headquarters of the district. Its garrison in 1866 numbered several hundred men, including infantry and a small cavalry detachment, along with civilian employees, teamsters, and a few officers' wives and children.

The forts were designed to secure travel and American authority, but their construction created isolated positions that depended on a continuing flow of supplies. Timber for construction and fuel had to be cut in the mountains, often five or more miles from the post at Fort Phil Kearny, where a pinery on the slopes of the Bighorns supplied logs. Hay for the garrison's animals had to be cut in the river bottoms. Food, clothing, ammunition, and equipment arrived by wagon train over a road of hundreds of miles from Fort Laramie, and every delivery was a vulnerable operation. Horses and mules, essential for hauling and for the cavalry, had to be grazed and guarded. A force that could not obtain wood, hay, and supplies could not hold its position, and the soldiers who guarded these activities spent much of their time in vulnerable positions outside the stockade.

This dependence explains why Indigenous resistance could make the posts difficult to sustain without capturing or destroying their fortifications. An attack on a wagon train, a woodcutting party, or a herd did not have to overcome the defenses of the fort. It had only to make the activities that sustained the fort too dangerous to continue. The forts were also isolated from each other and from the settlements that supplied them, and a relief force from one post to another had to cross country controlled by their opponents. The strategic problem the army faced was inherent in the design of the occupation, and it did not depend on the skill or failings of any particular officer. Carrington, who was criticized then and later for the defensive posture he adopted, was attempting to hold a system whose structure made it vulnerable.

Division III

The 1866 Campaign and the Fetterman Fight

Section 75.9

Indigenous Military Strategy: Raids, Mobility, and the War against Supply Lines

From the summer of 1866, Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho forces carried on a sustained campaign against the Bozeman Trail posts. The campaign consisted mostly of small operations rather than large battles: attacks on wagon trains, raids on herds, ambushes of woodcutting parties and hay crews, and harassment of travelers on the road. These operations were carried out by war parties of varying size, assembled by individual leaders and drawn from different bands and nations. Their aim was to make the road and the forts costly to maintain, and in this they proved effective, because the army's dependence on regular movement of men and material outside the stockades exposed it to constant danger.

Mounted mobility was the foundation of this strategy. Plains warriors were expert horsemen with large herds of horses, and they could cover long distances quickly, concentrate for an attack, and disperse afterward. They knew the country intimately: the creeks, ravines, ridges, and timber where parties could hide, the places where wagon trains had to slow or halt, and the routes by which an attacker could withdraw. Scouts watched the forts and the road, and information passed among camps by messenger and signal. The army's mounted force, by contrast, was small, its horses were often worn and underfed, and its soldiers had little knowledge of the terrain. Attackers could choose the time and place of engagement and could avoid pursuit by retreating into broken country where cavalry could not safely follow.

Surprise and deception were common elements of these tactics. A frequent method was the decoy: a small party of warriors would appear near a fort or a train, attract pursuit, and lead the pursuers toward a larger concealed force. American accounts describe such tactics repeatedly, and they were evidently well understood by the participants and by some officers, who warned against being drawn out. Attackers also took advantage of the army's routine. Wood trains left the fort at regular times along the same road to the same pinery, and the parties that met them knew the schedule and the ground. Attacks on herds served a double purpose: capture of animals reduced the army's mobility and supplied the attackers with horses, and the pursuit that followed offered opportunities for ambush.

Control of timber and hay was strategically central. A fort built of logs, heated by wood fires, and dependent on animals that required hay could not function without regular supplies of wood and forage. The Lakota and their allies understood this, and attacks on the wood trains and hay camps repeatedly interfered with the garrison's ability to sustain itself. Soldiers detailed to cut wood and hay had to be heavily guarded, which reduced the number available for other duties. The repeated attacks on small detachments could therefore have strategic effects even when the forts themselves remained intact: they forced the army to commit large numbers of men to routine tasks, exhausted horses and men, and lowered morale among a garrison that was largely confined to the stockade.

The operation of this strategy was shaped by the organizational limits of the alliance. Bringing together warriors from several communities required time, negotiation, and consensus, and large concentrations could not be maintained for long because they depended on the availability of food and grass for horses. Many bands left the Powder River country in autumn to establish winter camps, and large operations were therefore most likely in summer and in the lull periods when camps were close together. Not all attacks were centrally ordered, and American assumptions that they were directed by Red Cloud reflect the army's own habit of attributing unified command to its opponents. The war was conducted by many hands, with different aims and different degrees of coordination, and its effectiveness derived from persistence and from the structural weakness of the occupation it targeted.

Historical Vocabulary
Coalition Warfare and Military Logistics

Definition. Coalition warfare is military cooperation among separate political communities that retain their own governments and decision-making. Logistics is the supply and movement of the food, forage, fuel, ammunition, and animals an armed force needs in order to operate.

Historical context. The Lakota, Northern Cheyenne, and Northern Arapaho fought as a coalition, joined by shared interests, kinship, and consultation among leaders rather than by a single government. The United States Army had a formal command structure but depended on a fragile supply line stretched across hundreds of miles of country that its opponents knew better than it did.

Why it matters. The concepts explain how the war was won without a central Indigenous army and without a conventional battle. The coalition could concentrate and disperse as needed, and its attacks on logistics, rather than on garrisons, exploited the weakness of the American system.

Potential misunderstanding. A coalition is not a state, and its leaders were not generals in the army sense. Describing Red Cloud as "commander" of the Indigenous forces imposes a hierarchy that did not exist.

American reports repeatedly emphasized attacks on wood trains, hay details, travelers, and animals because these were precisely the activities that took the garrisons outside defensible stockades. The same wagon roads that were supposed to bind the posts together also revealed where escorts and supply parties would appear. Indigenous scouts could observe departures, select difficult terrain, and withdraw before substantial reinforcements arrived. Yet the method required its own discipline: pursuing soldiers too near a fort could expose warriors to artillery, and repeated operations demanded ammunition, mounts, and food that had to be acquired or produced by the wider community. This was a war of mobility and sustainment on both sides, not an uninterrupted blockade in the conventional European sense. The forts remained occupied, but their intended purpose—reliable passage through the surrounding country—was persistently frustrated.

Section 75.10

Fort Phil Kearny under Pressure, Summer and Autumn 1866

Fort Phil Kearny, built under pressure in July and August 1866, was from the beginning a besieged place. Soldiers working on the stockade, the quarters, and the storehouses were under constant threat, and parties sent out for timber or hay had to be escorted. The pinery, several miles west on the slopes of the Bighorns, was the most dangerous destination. Wood trains, which carried logs back to the fort, followed a road through broken country and were repeatedly attacked. In the months that followed, soldiers and civilians were killed or wounded in a succession of small actions, and herds of horses and mules were run off. Casualties accumulated gradually: no single event in the summer and autumn of 1866 matched the scale of the December disaster, but the toll of dead and wounded mounted, and the garrison's sense of danger grew.

Carrington's response was shaped by the limits of his force and by his own temperament. He had a small garrison, divided among three posts, with a limited number of cavalry and of effective weapons; the infantry's principal arm was the muzzle-loading rifle-musket, a weapon with a slow rate of fire, and ammunition was in short supply. He sought to protect the wood trains by assigning escorts, to limit the garrison's exposure by restricting movement beyond the stockade, and to avoid being drawn into pursuit of decoys. He also tried to complete the fort's defenses, with a stockade, blockhouses, and a system of signals. These measures reduced losses but could not eliminate the danger, and they left the initiative with the attackers. His caution won him criticism from subordinates who wanted more aggressive action, and it contributed to the tensions that later shaped the Fetterman tragedy.

The garrison was not united. Many of the officers who had come to the northern Plains were veterans of the Civil War who had seen decisive battles and had little patience for what they regarded as passive defense. A number of them, including Captain William J. Fetterman, viewed the Lakota with contempt and believed that a determined attack would break their resistance, a view that reflected Civil War assumptions that did not fit the conditions of Plains warfare. Others, more experienced on the frontier, such as the scout Jim Bridger, who served as a guide at Fort Phil Kearny, were more cautious. The difference between those who favored defense and those who advocated pursuit produced friction in the command, and it affected how orders were interpreted and carried out.

The soldiers and civilians who lived at the fort experienced the autumn of 1866 as a time of constant strain. They lived in unfinished quarters, ate rations that were often monotonous and sometimes short, and knew that any trip outside the stockade could be fatal. Their circumstances should be understood alongside the aims of the people who surrounded them. The Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho were not attacking the fort's inhabitants out of general hostility. They were responding to an occupation of their hunting grounds, and the attacks on wood trains and herds were attempts to compel the withdrawal of the force that had entered their country. The suffering of the garrison and the territorial objectives of its opponents were parts of the same conflict, and both were real.

Everyday Life and Social History

Camps, Hunts, Families, and Fort Life

The war was fought by people whose daily lives were embedded in family and community. A Lakota, Cheyenne, or Arapaho camp was a household economy on the move: women raised and dismantled the tipis, processed hides and meat, tended children, and managed the camp's stores, while men hunted, guarded the horse herds, and joined war parties. Children learned to ride and to care for horses at an early age, and elders transmitted knowledge of the country, of ceremony, and of history. When a war party left, families remained in camp and depended on the food that the hunt supplied. A prolonged campaign therefore required that communities continue to hunt, to feed their people, and to move safely, and the war's burden fell on families as well as on warriors.

At the forts, the population was different but also varied. Infantrymen, many of them recent recruits and some of them immigrants, performed construction, guard, and escort duty. Teamsters, laborers, and civilian employees hauled wagons and cut timber. A few officers' families lived at Fort Phil Kearny, including Margaret Carrington and, later, Frances Grummond, whose writings preserve a view of fort life unavailable from official reports. Their accounts describe isolation, anxiety, and the effort to maintain domestic routine in unfinished quarters, but they also reflect the preoccupations and prejudices of their authors. The lives of enlisted men, civilian workers, and the Indigenous scouts and interpreters who sometimes served the army are far less fully documented, and historians must read official records and archaeological evidence from the fort sites for indirect traces of their experience.

Both Indigenous and American communities experienced the war as a disruption of their routines. For the fort's garrison, the work of cutting wood and hay became a hazard. For Indigenous families, the closure of the best bison and winter-camp country to safe use required them to hunt farther afield or to risk encounters with soldiers. Both sides depended on animals, horses and mules for the army and horses for Indigenous communities, and both suffered from the loss of herds.

Section 75.11

December 1866: Escalation, Military Decisions, and the Approach to Fetterman

By December 1866 the pressure on Fort Phil Kearny had mounted. Winter reduced the grass available to horses and made the wood trains more essential, since the garrison needed fuel against the cold. On December 6, a wood train was attacked, and Carrington himself led a relief force. A cavalry detachment pursuing a decoy party was drawn into a trap, and Lieutenant Horatio S. Bingham and Sergeant Gideon Bowers were killed. The incident, in which a small party of warriors drew soldiers into pursuit toward a concealed force, was in several respects a rehearsal for December 21. It sharpened disagreements within the command: Carrington had been cautious, and his critics regarded the pursuit as evidence that a stronger response was needed, while others drew the opposite conclusion about the danger of being drawn out.

Captain William J. Fetterman had arrived at Fort Phil Kearny in November. A Civil War veteran with a distinguished record, he held a brevet rank higher than his regular rank and was an officer of considerable self-confidence. His relationship with Carrington was strained. Fetterman and some of the younger officers, including Captain Frederick H. Brown and Lieutenant George W. Grummond, believed that the Lakota could be defeated by decisive action and criticized what they regarded as Carrington's passivity. Later accounts attribute to Fetterman a boast that a small force could defeat the Lakota, but the authenticity and wording of such statements are uncertain, and they were reported by others after the event. What the evidence supports is that Fetterman and his associates were impatient with defense, and that their attitudes shaped the context in which orders were given on December 21.

The army's concerns at this point were practical as well as personal. The garrison depended on the wood train and could not abandon it. Attacks on the train were frequent, and each posed the question of how far a relief force should pursue. Officers who favored aggressive action argued that attackers who struck and withdrew could be punished only if soldiers followed them. Carrington's view, conditioned by the limits of his force and the recent experience of December 6, was that pursuit beyond a certain distance was too dangerous. These competing expectations, together with the officers' differing assessments of the opponent, set the stage for the deployment that became the Fetterman Fight.

The geography around the fort was crucial. The fort stood on a plateau between Big Piney and Little Piney creeks, and the road to the pinery ran west through the valley. North of the fort lay Lodge Trail Ridge, a long ridge running east to west, beyond which the road toward Fort C. F. Smith descended into the valley of Peno Creek. Soldiers at the fort could see the ridge's southern slope but not what lay on the far side, and a detachment that crossed the ridge would be out of sight and beyond the fort's ability to support it by gunfire or rapid reinforcement. This terrain made the ridge a natural place for an ambush, and the Lakota and their allies, who knew the country, could place a force on the far side while drawing soldiers over the top.

On December 19 another attack on a wood train drew a relief party commanded by Captain James W. Powell. Unlike the soldiers who had pursued decoys on December 6, Powell did not permit the smaller hostile party to draw his detachment into a prolonged chase. Contemporary military accounts show the immediate tactical problem clearly: rescuing workers was necessary, but following apparently retreating warriors toward terrain hidden from the fort could turn a relief operation into an ambush. The December 19 episode therefore belongs between the better-known fights of December 6 and December 21. It reveals that the danger was understood within the garrison before Fetterman's command went out, without proving that all officers interpreted their instructions in the same way.

Section 75.12

The Fetterman Fight, December 21, 1866: A Major Indigenous Victory

On the morning of December 21, 1866, the wood train from Fort Phil Kearny was attacked on its way to the pinery. The alarm was sounded at the fort, and Carrington assembled a relief detachment. Command was given to Captain Fetterman, who had asked for it, and the force that left the fort consisted of a mixed group of infantry and cavalry, along with two civilians, James Wheatley and Isaac Fisher, who carried privately owned repeating rifles. According to the accounts that survive, the detachment numbered eighty-one in all. Carrington's report stated that he had ordered Fetterman to relieve the wood train, to avoid pursuing beyond Lodge Trail Ridge, and to return promptly. Other participants, including survivors' families, later disputed what orders had been given or how clearly they had been understood.

The attack on the wood train appears to have been part of a plan to draw soldiers out. A small party of Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho warriors, acting as decoys, engaged the wood train and then retreated, drawing the relief force in pursuit. Fetterman's detachment, instead of relieving the wood train and returning, followed the retreating warriors north over Lodge Trail Ridge, out of sight of the fort. On the far side, along the ridge and the descent toward Peno Creek, a much larger force lay concealed. Estimates of its size vary widely, from about a thousand to about two thousand, and no precise number can be established. As the soldiers advanced, the concealed warriors rose and attacked from several directions. The detachment was surrounded, and within a short time, probably less than an hour, all of its members were killed.

Who directed the ambush is disputed. Later Lakota and Cheyenne accounts describe a large force gathered for an operation against the fort and a decoy party chosen from among their best horsemen, and they identify various individuals, including Crazy Horse, as participants or leaders of the decoy group. Different narrators emphasize different individuals, and their accounts, recorded years or decades afterward, differ in details. The evidence is insufficient to establish with confidence which leaders planned the ambush or who led the decoys. What is clear is that the operation involved cooperation among Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho warriors and required both planning and discipline, qualities that contradict the American assumption that Indigenous fighters acted by impulse.

The soldiers at the fort heard firing and saw smoke but could not see the fight. Carrington sent out a relief detachment, which reached the ridge after the fighting was over and found the bodies of Fetterman's men. They had been stripped and mutilated, in a manner that army accounts described with horror and that Indigenous accounts understand within their own conventions of warfare. The National Park Service, in its account, records that all 81 people in Fetterman's detachment, comprising 79 soldiers and two civilians, were killed. The figure of 81 should not be described as 81 soldiers. The dead included three officers, an estimated forty-nine infantrymen and twenty-seven cavalrymen, and the two civilians.

American accounts called the engagement the Fetterman Massacre, a name that reflected the shock of defeat and the later habit of describing Indigenous victories over soldiers as massacres. The Cheyenne remember it as the fight of the Hundred in the Hands, a name that has been rendered in several ways in English and that refers to the number of the enemy taken or killed, and Lakota winter counts record the winter by reference to the destruction of a body of soldiers. These names reflect different understandings: the American name stressed victimhood, while the Indigenous names recorded a battle. Historians now generally describe the event as a battle or fight, reserving the term massacre for the killing of noncombatants, as at Sand Creek.

Indigenous casualties are not known. American accounts give figures that vary from a handful to several dozen killed, and Indigenous participants gave different numbers; none can be verified. The battle's significance did not depend on the exact count. It was a major Indigenous victory, achieved through coordination, knowledge of terrain, and a well-executed ambush, and it was the heaviest loss suffered by the army in a single engagement on the Plains up to that time, although such comparisons depend on how earlier events are counted. It did not, however, end the war. The Lakota and their allies had destroyed a detachment but had not captured the fort, and the garrison, though shaken, remained. The fight demonstrated the vulnerability of the American position but also the limits of what an ambush could achieve, since the question of whether the army would persist in the Powder River country was still open.

Division IV

The War of 1867: Continuing Resistance and Changing Military Tactics

Section 75.13

The Fetterman Aftermath: American Investigations, Blame, and Military Reorganization

News of the disaster reached Fort Laramie and the telegraph in the days after the fight, carried, according to well-known accounts, by a courier who rode through winter weather to bring the message. Later retellings of that ride have grown in the telling, and the details vary. The news produced shock and anger in the army and in the press. Newspapers in the East, which had paid little attention to the Bozeman Trail, now reported a massacre, and demands for retaliation came from officers, politicians, and the public. General William T. Sherman, commanding the Military Division of the Missouri, took a hard line, and army headquarters ordered reinforcements. The commander of the Department of the Platte was replaced, and Carrington was removed from command of the district.

Responsibility for the disaster became a bitter dispute. Carrington, in his reports, blamed Fetterman for disobeying orders. Fetterman and his supporters, who could no longer speak for themselves, were defended by other officers, and by the families of the dead, who argued that Carrington's own decisions had left the garrison exposed. The question of what orders had been given and how they had been understood could not be settled by testimony because the principal participant was dead, and the disagreement continued for decades. Frances Grummond Carrington's memoir, published in 1910, defended her first husband, Lieutenant Grummond, and criticized Carrington's conduct. Margaret Carrington's earlier account, published in 1868, supported her husband. The competing narratives illustrate how personal and professional interests shaped the historical record.

Army and civilian authorities held inquiries into the disaster, and officers testified about the conduct of the command. The inquiries did not produce a clear verdict that satisfied everyone, and later historians have reached different conclusions. Some emphasize Fetterman's recklessness and his failure to follow orders. Others emphasize Carrington's defects as a commander and the structural difficulty of the position he had been given. More recent work, including studies that draw on archaeology and on Indigenous testimony, has tended to place the defeat in the context of Indigenous planning and of a system of occupation that left small garrisons exposed. Students should read the contemporary reports separately from the later efforts to defend or discredit particular officers, because the latter were often written with an interest in vindicating a reputation.

The reaction in the army also revealed a reluctance to acknowledge Indigenous military capability. Many accounts attributed the defeat to treachery, to the savagery of the opponents, or to the mistakes of individual officers, rather than to the strategy and skill of the Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho. This framing, reinforced by the label of massacre, shaped public memory and made it easier to see the war as a series of outrages to be avenged than as a contest over territory that the army was losing. It also fed demands for escalation that the army was in no position to meet, since the forces available on the northern Plains in 1867 were limited and the cost of a large campaign was high.

Section 75.14

Continued Indigenous Resistance and the Changing Military Balance in 1867

Command of Fort Phil Kearny passed to Colonel Henry W. Wessells, who had been at Fort Reno, and the garrison was reinforced. The army remained on the defensive. Attacks on wood trains, hay crews, and herds continued through the winter and spring of 1867, and travel on the road was restricted. Civilian traffic on the Bozeman Trail, which had never been large, fell further, and the forts stood in country that the army could not control. The ability of Indigenous forces to restrict movement and resupply continued, and the army's attempts to secure the road with escorts and detachments produced casualties without bringing the campaign to a conclusion.

The coalition also faced difficulties. Sustaining a campaign required food, horses, and the support of families, and it depended on the ability of communities to hunt. In winter, camps dispersed and communities were occupied with survival. The summer hunting season brought communities together again, but the demands of hunting competed with the demands of war. Large concentrations of people and horses strained the grass and game of any one area, so camps had to move frequently. Leaders had to persuade bands to take part in operations, and not every band was willing to fight at a given time. Differences in objectives also surfaced: some leaders were willing to talk with American officials when circumstances seemed favorable, while others held to the demand that the forts be abandoned before any negotiation. These tensions were a normal part of the politics of a coalition, and they did not prevent cooperation, but they limited the scale and duration of operations.

The costs of war fell heavily on families. The disruption of hunting grounds, the need to move camps to avoid soldiers, and the loss of warriors and horses affected the whole community. Women, who managed the camps and processed the hunt's products, bore the burden of moving households and maintaining supplies in unsettled conditions. The war also exposed communities to the risk of army reprisals against camps, as the 1865 attack on the Arapaho village had shown. The practical costs of prolonged warfare were considerable, and the decisions of leaders about when to fight, when to negotiate, and when to withdraw to winter camps reflected an assessment of those costs.

A significant change on the American side was the introduction of breech-loading rifles. In 1866 the army began converting muzzle-loading Springfield rifle-muskets to breech-loaders, producing the Springfield-Allin conversion, and by the summer of 1867 these weapons reached the garrisons on the Bozeman Trail. A soldier with a breech-loader could fire much faster than one with a muzzle-loader and could reload under cover, including while lying down. The change increased the firepower of small defensive parties, especially those behind improvised barricades. It would be a mistake, however, to conclude that the new weapons determined the course of the war. They improved the defense of fixed positions and small groups, but they did not solve the army's basic problems of supply, mobility, and the vulnerability of its isolated posts.

Multiple Perspectives

Living with the Bozeman Trail War

Lakota communities (Oglala, Miniconjou, and Brulé): For Oglala and allied Lakota bands, the road and forts threatened the bison ranges and winter camps on which their economy depended. Their diplomatic aims were related to, but not identical with, those of the Lakota.
Northern Arapaho: The Northern Arapaho had experienced a direct army attack on a village in 1865. Their participation in the resistance was bound up with their alliance with the Lakota and Cheyenne, and their later treaty in May 1868 was a separate instrument that addressed their own situation.

Section 75.15

The Hayfield Fight, August 1, 1867: Fort C. F. Smith and Allied Resistance

Fort C. F. Smith, on the Bighorn River in Montana Territory, was the northernmost of the Bozeman Trail posts, and its location made it the most isolated. Hay was essential for the garrison's animals, and each summer a detail of soldiers and civilians was sent to cut it in a meadow some miles from the post. In 1867, the hay-cutters worked under the protection of a small detachment commanded by Lieutenant Sigismund Sternberg, and the party set up a camp in the hayfield with a defensive enclosure built of wagon boxes and logs, a corral that served as the position of last resort. The National Park Service identifies 19 soldiers and six civilians as the defenders of the position.

On August 1, 1867, a large force of Indigenous warriors attacked the hayfield camp. Accounts identify Lakota and Cheyenne participation in differing proportions, and Cheyenne accounts give the fight an important place in their memory of the war. The sources do not permit a precise determination of which communities were present, in what numbers, or under whose leadership, and the attack cannot simply be assigned to Red Cloud. Sternberg was killed early in the fighting, and the remaining defenders, under the direction of surviving leaders, retreated into the corral. The attackers made repeated charges and attempted to overcome the position, and the fighting lasted much of the day.

The defenders held out in large part because of their weapons. The soldiers in the corral were armed with breech-loading Springfield rifles, and some civilians carried repeating rifles. The ability to fire rapidly from behind a barricade, and to reload without exposing themselves, allowed a very small party to maintain a heavy volume of fire against attackers advancing in the open. The attackers, who had expected to find a vulnerable party, suffered casualties and withdrew when a relief force arrived from the fort. American losses were small, a few killed and wounded, and Indigenous losses are uncertain. American estimates were high, and Indigenous accounts were lower, and no figure can be verified.

The fight shows the effect of changing military technology on small-scale actions, but it must be interpreted within the strategic situation. The attackers sought to eliminate a hay-cutting party and, by extension, to make the garrison's supply of forage uncertain, and the effort failed in that instance. Yet the hay camp's survival did not make the fort more secure. The garrison still depended on parties that had to leave the stockade, still lay far from supply, and still served a road with little traffic. The Hayfield Fight was a tactical defeat for the attackers and a tactical success for the defenders, but it did not resolve the underlying strategic problem, and it showed again how much the garrison's survival depended on protecting activities beyond the walls.

The physical construction of the hayfield defense is also significant. The NPS describes a corral assembled from upright posts, crosspieces, and interwoven branches, with wagon boxes and other equipment inside. That arrangement gave defenders more effective cover than the open field offered to attackers, although it did not make the position invulnerable. Sternberg's death early in the engagement deprived the party of its lieutenant, and civilian Don A. Colvin helped direct the surviving defenders. The distinction matters to a chapter about military organization: survival was not the result of weapons alone but of quickly adapting command, keeping people behind cover, and sustaining fire until help arrived. The comparatively small defending party still faced losses in soldiers and animals, and the engagement left the strategic problem of exposed hay-cutting work unchanged.

Section 75.16

The Wagon Box Fight, August 2, 1867: Technology, Defensive Firepower, and Historical Interpretation

The following day, August 2, 1867, a separate engagement occurred near Fort Phil Kearny, many miles to the south of the Hayfield. This was the Wagon Box Fight. The two fights were distinct: they took place at different forts, involved different defenders, and probably involved different Indigenous groups, although the same broad campaign of pressure on supply lines produced both. Contractors had been cutting wood for the fort at a camp near the pinery, and a detachment of soldiers under Captain James W. Powell guarded them. The wagon beds had been removed from their running gear and set in an oval on the ground, creating an improvised enclosure that served as a defensive position.

On the morning of August 2, a large force of Indigenous warriors, probably including Lakota and Cheyenne, attacked the woodcutters' camp. Accounts differ about the number of attackers and the sequence of events. According to American accounts, the warriors made several charges, including a mounted attack that came close to the wagon boxes, and were repulsed by volleys from the defenders, who numbered about thirty soldiers and civilians. The soldiers were armed with the new breech-loading rifles, and ample ammunition had been placed in the enclosure. The fight lasted for some hours, until a relief force with a howitzer arrived from the fort and the attackers withdrew. American losses were small: Lieutenant John C. Jenness and two enlisted men were killed.

Estimates of Indigenous casualties vary enormously. Captain Powell reported that he had killed or wounded a very large number of warriors, and some participants and later writers gave figures running into the hundreds. These claims were made by American participants who had reason to emphasize the scale of their success and who could not have counted accurately casualties that Indigenous fighters often carried off. Indigenous accounts suggest much lower losses, and later historians have generally treated the nineteenth-century figures as inflated. The large numbers reported by American participants must not be treated as verified counts. The true figure is unknown and may never be established with confidence.

Compared with the Fetterman Fight, the Wagon Box Fight shows the difference between an ambush against troops caught in the open and an assault on a prepared position held by well-armed defenders. In December 1866, the soldiers were out of reach of the fort, armed with slow muzzle-loaders, and surrounded in terrain of the attackers' choosing. In August 1867, the soldiers were behind cover, equipped with rapid-fire weapons, and attacked in open ground near the fort. The change in weapons contributed to the difference in outcome, but so did the circumstances: the Wagon Box defenders had made a stand in a prepared position and had not been lured away from support. The lesson was not that the army had found the means to defeat its opponents, but that a small party in a good position with good weapons could hold out for hours.

The central historical issue is why an Indigenous coalition could win strategically despite suffering tactical reverses in particular engagements. The answer is that the war was not decided by the outcome of individual fights. The coalition's objective was the withdrawal of the occupying force, and that objective depended on making the cost of occupation exceed its value. The garrisons could defend themselves, but they could not make the road safe, bring more traffic, or end the cost of supplying posts that were under constant threat. A tactical success by the army could not repair that situation, and a tactical setback for the attackers did not remove the pressure. The distinction between winning a fight and winning a war is the subject of the next feature.

Historical Vocabulary
Tactical Victory, Strategic Victory, and Treaty Settlement

Definition. A tactical victory is success in a particular engagement, such as holding a position or destroying a detachment. A strategic victory is the achievement of the larger objectives for which a war is fought. A treaty settlement is the political agreement, negotiated afterward, that formalizes or modifies the military outcome.

Historical context. At Fetterman, the Lakota and their allies won a tactical victory that supported their strategic aim. At Hayfield and Wagon Box, the defenders won tactical successes but did not change the strategic situation. The ultimate outcome, the abandonment of the forts, was a strategic victory for the coalition, which was then formalized, with significant limits, in the 1868 treaty relationships examined in Chapter 76.

Why it matters. The three concepts help students avoid reading the war as a list of battles won and lost. They show that a force that loses engagements can win a war, and that a victory on the ground does not automatically produce the legal terms its winners want.

Potential misunderstanding. Treaty terms do not simply record military results. They reflect negotiation, the aims of officials, and the interpretation of language, and they can fall short of, or exceed, what the battlefield outcome suggests.

Division V

Diplomacy, American Withdrawal, and the Significance of Red Cloud's Victory

Section 75.17

Military Costs, Strategic Failure, and the American Search for Peace

By the autumn of 1867 the United States government was reconsidering its commitment to the Bozeman Trail. The army had committed several hundred soldiers to three isolated forts, and the cost of sustaining them, in wagons, animals, escorts, and pay, was high. Casualties, though not numerous by the standards of the Civil War, were politically costly, since the loss of Fetterman's detachment had been widely publicized. The forts did not make the road safe, and the road was used by few travelers. Civilian traffic had declined from its earlier level, in part because of the danger and in part because alternatives had developed, and the value of what the posts were protecting was therefore small compared with the cost of protection.

The transportation economy was changing. The Union Pacific Railroad was building westward across Nebraska and into present-day Wyoming, and its rails reached Cheyenne in the autumn of 1867 and pushed on toward Utah in the following year. Freight and passengers bound for the Montana goldfields could increasingly travel by rail to a point in the Rocky Mountain region and then by wagon road from the south, or by steamboat up the Missouri River to Fort Benton. These routes avoided the Powder River country altogether. The Montana mining boom was also past its peak, and the commercial pressure that had driven the opening of the Bozeman Trail was weaker than it had been in 1863. The army, which built Fort Fetterman on the North Platte in 1867 to guard the Platte route, was beginning to redirect its resources to the railroad corridor, where its protection mattered more to the government.

In the political sphere, the Fetterman disaster and the continuing war increased the pressure for a different approach. Many in Congress and in the Indian Office, including reformers who criticized the conduct of the war and the treatment of Indigenous nations since Sand Creek, argued that peace could be had by negotiation at lower cost than by military campaigns. In July 1867 Congress created the Indian Peace Commission, composed of civilian officials and army officers, including Commissioner of Indian Affairs Nathaniel G. Taylor and Generals William T. Sherman, William S. Harney, Alfred H. Terry, and C. C. Augur, and charged it with ending the wars on the Plains and removing their causes. The commission's work began on the central and southern Plains in the autumn of 1867, and in the north it sought to open talks with the Lakota and their allies.

The reasons for American reconsideration should not be reduced to a single cause. The forts' costs and the casualties they had suffered, the decline of traffic on the road, the development of rail and river alternatives, and competition for federal resources all interacted. The battlefield pressure applied by the Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho gave the other factors force, because a quiet road might have been tolerated at modest expense, but a road that required constant defense could not be justified. Likewise, changing transportation priorities would probably not have ended the occupation so quickly had the war not made it so expensive. The withdrawal was the product of both, and neither the military nor the economic explanation alone is sufficient.

Cause and Effect

Why the United States Abandoned the Bozeman Trail Forts

Long-term causes

American westward expansion and the claim, drawn from the 1851 treaty, to build roads and posts in Indigenous territory; the Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho economy and political independence centered on the bison ranges of the Powder River country; the weak state of army logistics on the northern Plains.

Short-term causes

Montana gold discoveries of 1862–1863 and the opening of the Bozeman Trail; Sand Creek and the failure of Connor's 1865 campaign; the failure of the 1866 council; the construction of the forts in Indigenous hunting grounds.

Immediate trigger or context

The Fetterman Fight of December 1866, the continued pressure on the forts through 1867, the Hayfield and Wagon Box fights, and the creation of the Indian Peace Commission.

Event or transformation

The decision, in 1868, to abandon the Bozeman Trail posts as part of a settlement of the war, followed by the evacuation of the forts.

Immediate effects

Withdrawal of garrisons; burning of the abandoned posts; closure of the road; and the treaty of April 1868 and the separate Cheyenne-Arapaho treaty of May 1868.

Long-term consequences

A period of Lakota control over the unceded country; the treaty system examined in Chapter 76; and renewed conflict in the 1870s, including the Great Sioux War (Chapter 77).

Historical complexity: Neither battlefield pressure nor changing transportation routes alone explains the withdrawal. Indigenous resistance made the forts expensive, and the railroad and river alternatives made them unnecessary, and the combination of the two led the government to a decision that either alone might have delayed.

The Peace Commission also illustrates the difference between a national policy change and a single commander's decision. Congress's 1867 act provided for civilian members Nathaniel G. Taylor, Senator John B. Henderson, Samuel F. Tappan, and John B. Sanborn, together with senior army officers William T. Sherman, William S. Harney, Alfred H. Terry, and C. C. Augur. They did not all attend every council, and their opinions on Indigenous policy were not identical. Their instructions sought to end hostilities while also protecting transportation and railroad development. Negotiation was therefore not a renunciation of expansion. It was a reconsideration of how to secure American objectives when the direct military occupation of the Powder River route had failed to provide the promised safety at an acceptable cost.

Section 75.18

Red Cloud's Diplomatic Position and the Demand for Withdrawal

The government's search for peace brought it back to the diplomatic table, and the position of Red Cloud and his allies was clear. They would not sign a treaty while the forts remained. In late 1867, when the Peace Commission sought to open talks, Red Cloud sent word that he would not meet until the soldiers had left the Powder River country. This was a political position as well as a military one. Having made the forts the object of their campaign, and having forced the army onto the defensive, the Lakota and their allies saw no reason to accept a settlement that left the forts in place, since the abandonment of the forts was the central objective of the war.

Other Lakota leaders were willing to negotiate earlier. Spotted Tail and other Brulé headmen, whose territory lay farther from the Bozeman Trail and who had already had dealings with the government, were prepared to sign agreements that Red Cloud and the Oglala resisted. The commissioners, meeting at Fort Laramie in November 1867 and again in the spring of 1868, were able to obtain the signatures of leaders who attended, but they could not obtain Red Cloud's. This division within the Lakota reflected the political structure described in Section 75.1: each leader spoke for his own following, and one leader's agreement did not bind others. The government's repeated attempts to negotiate with whoever was willing created a risk of treaties that did not include those most directly affected, as in 1866, and the commissioners gradually recognized that peace required the participation of the Oglala and their allies.

Cheyenne and Arapaho diplomatic interests were related to, but not identical with, those of the Lakota. The Northern Cheyenne and Northern Arapaho were also concerned with the forts, but they had distinct concerns, including the safety of their camps, access to hunting grounds, and the desire for a place in which to live with their relatives. They did not negotiate through the Lakota. The government treated them separately, and the agreement with them, signed on May 10, 1868, was a different instrument from the Lakota treaty of April 29. The distinction is important because it reflects the independent political standing of each nation and because the Northern Cheyenne and Northern Arapaho later found themselves in different circumstances from the Lakota under the terms of their treaty.

The significance of making withdrawal a condition of peace is that it replaced a promise with an action. The United States had made many promises to Indigenous nations, and many had been broken or left unfulfilled. Red Cloud's insistence that the forts be abandoned before he signed meant that his agreement would follow a visible change in American behavior rather than precede it. This was a sophisticated diplomatic judgment, resting on an assessment of the government's record and of the leverage that the war had created. By waiting, he allowed the army's withdrawal to demonstrate that the government had accepted the terms, and he obtained a result that a treaty provision alone could not have guaranteed. The National Park Service records that Red Cloud refused to sign until the forts were abandoned.

Section 75.19

The Abandonment of the Bozeman Trail Forts in 1868

The decision to abandon the forts was made in the spring of 1868, as the Peace Commission concluded the principal Fort Laramie treaty with its initial signatories on April 29. The army, which had already been moving toward withdrawal, prepared to evacuate the three posts, and the treaty included an article providing that the posts would be abandoned and the road closed. The army's orders and the government's commitments were not identical, and the withdrawal followed its own schedule, driven by the practicalities of moving garrisons and equipment across the country. During the summer of 1868, the garrisons withdrew.

Fort C. F. Smith, the northernmost post, was evacuated on July 29, 1868, when its garrison marched out, bound for the south. Fort Phil Kearny was vacated by early August in several historical accounts, while Fort Reno was evacuated on August 18, 1868, according to its final post return. The army had little use for the buildings, and the departing soldiers did not take all they had built. As the soldiers left, Indigenous people, including Northern Cheyenne, who had been waiting to see the forts abandoned, moved in, and the forts were burned. Accounts of the burning of Fort Phil Kearny, which are reported in army and Indigenous sources, describe fire consuming the stockade and buildings shortly after the garrison's departure. The National Park Service records the destruction of Fort C. F. Smith and Fort Phil Kearny following their evacuation.

The destruction of the abandoned forts should not be characterized as the destruction of occupied positions captured in battle. The forts had been given up, and the burning of empty buildings was a symbolic act, not a military one. Its symbolism was nevertheless considerable. For the Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho who had resisted the occupation for two years, the burning of the posts was a public demonstration that the soldiers had been forced to leave, and that the road and the forts that had threatened their country were gone. For the army, the evacuation was an admission that the Bozeman Trail experiment had failed, and the silence of many official records about it reflects the embarrassment it caused.

The distinction between a military victory on the ground and the precise legal obligations of the treaty relationship is important here. The withdrawal was a fact, achieved through war and negotiation, and it needed no treaty to make it real. The treaty, however, recorded obligations of the United States and the Lakota, including the promise to close the road, and these obligations were subject to interpretation and enforcement in ways that the withdrawal itself was not. The soldiers had left, and they could not easily be sent back without breaching an agreement, but the treaty also contained provisions about reservation lands and the transformation of Lakota society that the war had not addressed. The fuller analysis of those provisions belongs to Chapter 76. Here it is enough to note that the withdrawal was the first and most visible fulfillment of Red Cloud's conditions, and that his signature, given on November 6, 1868, followed it.

Section 75.20

Red Cloud's Victory: Indigenous Sovereignty, Military Success, and the Limits of the Settlement

Red Cloud's War ended with the United States abandoning an attempted military occupation of the Powder River country. The result was not an accident of American policy. It followed from two years of sustained resistance by Lakota, Northern Cheyenne, and Northern Arapaho communities who defended their homelands against a road-and-fort system by making it too costly to maintain. Their success depended on several factors: the coalition's capacity to cooperate without a central government, the knowledge of the terrain that allowed them to choose the time and place of attack, the logistical pressure they exerted on isolated forts, the discipline and skill shown at Fetterman, and the diplomatic judgment of leaders like Red Cloud, who understood that withdrawal had to precede settlement.

The war also reveals aspects of Indigenous political power that are often overlooked. The coalition's decentralization was a strength, because it allowed communities to join the campaign according to their own decisions and to withdraw when needed without the collapse of the whole. The war showed that Indigenous communities were capable of strategic thinking, of identifying their opponents' vulnerabilities, and of sustaining a campaign over time. It demonstrated that the United States, with all its resources, could be forced to abandon a policy by a determined opponent in a region where its logistics were weak, and it confirmed the principle, central to the history of Indigenous diplomacy, that treaties and withdrawals followed from the balance of power as much as from the justice of any claim.

The limits of the victory must be stated with equal clarity. The withdrawal did not end American expansion across the Plains. The Union Pacific, to the south, continued its advance, bringing settlers, soldiers, and commercial hunters to the central Plains, and the destruction of the bison herds examined in Chapter 81 would soon undermine the economy on which the Powder River communities depended. The withdrawal did not resolve intertribal territorial questions, as the Crow continued to assert their claims against the Lakota and Cheyenne, and conflicts among Indigenous nations persisted. And it did not eliminate the pressures toward reservation confinement: the same treaty that provided for abandonment of the forts also established a reservation and envisioned a gradual transformation of Lakota life, a subject treated in Chapter 76 and in the broader account of the reservation order in Chapter 84.

The next stage of the story is the treaty. The Treaty of Fort Laramie, signed on April 29, 1868, by its initial signatories, defined a Great Sioux Reservation and designated a larger region as unceded Indian territory. Red Cloud signed it on November 6, 1868, after the forts had been abandoned. The Northern Cheyenne and Northern Arapaho concluded a separate treaty on May 10, 1868. This chapter has introduced these agreements only to explain how the war ended. The reservation boundaries, the rights and obligations they created, and their long-term consequences, including the conflict over the Black Hills that led to the Great Sioux War, belong to Chapters 76 and 77.

Historical Debate

Was Red Cloud's War an Indigenous Military Victory, an American Strategic Withdrawal, or Both?

The question: Historians agree that the United States abandoned the Bozeman Trail forts in 1868. They differ about how to describe that outcome and how much weight to give Indigenous military success compared with American decisions made for other reasons.

Interpretation A — An Indigenous military victory: Many historians emphasize that the Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho forced the army to abandon an occupation by sustained attacks on its supply lines and by victories such as Fetterman. On this view, the war was won on the Plains, and the American decision reflected acknowledgement that the forts could not be held.
Interpretation B — An American strategic withdrawal: Others stress that the army's tactical record in 1867 was good, that the forts held, and that the government's decision reflected the declining value of the road and the arrival of the railroad. On this view, the withdrawal was a reallocation of resources, and Indigenous resistance was one factor among several.
Interpretation C — Both, in interaction: A third approach holds that the two explanations are not exclusive. Indigenous pressure raised the cost of occupation, and changing American priorities lowered its value, and the withdrawal resulted from their combination. This chapter largely adopts this view while emphasizing that the Indigenous demand for withdrawal was made explicit and was met.

Evidence: Army reports on costs and casualties, Indian Peace Commission records, Red Cloud's insistence on withdrawal before signing, the sequence of withdrawal and signature, the development of the railroad, and the timing of decisions in Washington.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns causation and emphasis more than the facts. It also concerns terminology, since "victory" and "withdrawal" carry different implications for how the war is remembered.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand Indigenous power. If the withdrawal was merely a convenience for the United States, Indigenous agency recedes. If it was a response to resistance, it demonstrates that Indigenous strategy shaped national policy.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Indigenous Territorial Authority Before, During, and After the War

What changed?

  • The Powder River country moved from a contested but largely unoccupied region to a militarized corridor in 1866, and back to a region without American garrisons in 1868.
  • The Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho moved from loosely coordinated resistance to sustained coalition warfare and then to treaty relationships with the United States.
  • The army acquired breech-loading rifles and learned lessons about supply that influenced later campaigns.

What remained continuous?

  • Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho political independence and their reliance on bison, horses, and mobility.
  • Overlapping Indigenous claims, including those of the Crow.
  • American pressure for access, transportation, and settlement.

When and how did change occur? Change was uneven and contested. The occupation came suddenly in 1866 and was reversed after two years. The most consequential longer-term changes, including the destruction of the bison and the pressures toward reservation life, were gradual and had barely begun. Who experienced change most directly? Communities living along the road and near the forts experienced the occupation most directly, while Lakota bands farther from the road had a different experience. Why does the pattern matter? It shows that territorial authority can be defended and regained, and also that a regained position can be undermined by forces the victory did not touch.

Calling the outcome a victory also requires stating whose objective was achieved. For Red Cloud and the resisting communities, the immediate object was to eliminate an unwanted military corridor in territory still essential to hunting and seasonal movement. The abandonment achieved that object. For the federal government, withdrawal freed soldiers and spending for routes and priorities it increasingly valued more, especially those connected with the advancing railroad. These interpretations are compatible rather than mutually exclusive. The United States could recalculate its transportation strategy precisely because continued occupation had become so costly under Indigenous pressure. A responsible assessment of the victory must recognize Indigenous military and diplomatic agency while acknowledging the institutional capacity of the United States to redirect its expansion rather than end it.

Chapter Summary

Red Cloud's War was a sustained political and military campaign by Lakota, Northern Cheyenne, and Northern Arapaho communities to defend the Powder River country against an American road-and-fort system. It was not simply a sequence of battles, and it was not directed by a single commander. Its American name, which credits one Oglala leader, obscures the cooperation of independently governed nations whose leaders persuaded rather than commanded their followers.

The conflict arose from competing territorial claims. The Powder River country was a region of bison ranges, winter camps, and horse herds on which Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho economic and political independence depended, and which was also claimed or used by the Crow and others. The Fort Laramie Treaty of 1851 recorded territorial understandings and recognized emigrant routes, but it did not cede the Powder River country, and the United States and the Indigenous nations understood its road-and-post provisions differently.

The Montana gold rushes of 1862 and 1863 created demand for a shorter route from the Platte to the goldfields, and in 1863 John Bozeman and John Jacobs adapted an older Indigenous travel corridor into a wagon road. The difference between transient travel and a permanent military transportation corridor explains why Indigenous leaders regarded the road as a threat to their homelands. The aftermath of Sand Creek, the Platte Bridge fighting, and Connor's failed expedition of 1865 intensified the conflict and demonstrated both Indigenous coordination and the army's logistical weakness.

The failure of the Fort Laramie council of 1866 followed from a contradiction within the American government: the Indian Office sought consent for the road while the army moved to occupy it. Red Cloud and other leaders left the council, and Carrington built Fort Reno, Fort Phil Kearny, and Fort C. F. Smith in the summer of 1866. These posts were isolated and dependent on wood, hay, herds, and supply trains that had to move outside the stockades, and that dependence made them vulnerable to an opponent who did not need to capture them.

Indigenous strategy exploited this weakness through mobility, knowledge of terrain, decoys, and attacks on the activities that sustained the forts. Pressure on Fort Phil Kearny in 1866, intensified by the winter, produced the Fetterman Fight of December 21, 1866, in which a decoy drew a relief detachment of 81 people, including 79 soldiers and two civilians, over Lodge Trail Ridge, where all were killed. The fight was a major Indigenous victory, but it did not end the war, and the evidence does not permit an exact count of Indigenous casualties or a certain attribution of the ambush to particular leaders.

The aftermath produced disputes over responsibility among Carrington, Fetterman, and other officers, and these disputes shaped later narratives, including the memoirs of Margaret Carrington and Frances Grummond Carrington. Historians must read contemporary reports separately from later defenses. In 1867, as breech-loading rifles reached the garrisons, the Hayfield Fight (August 1) and the Wagon Box Fight (August 2) showed that small, well-armed parties behind barricades could withstand large attacks. These separate engagements, at different forts, were tactical successes for the defenders, but they did not reverse the strategic situation, and nineteenth-century casualty claims for them cannot be accepted as verified.

The United States reconsidered its commitment through the interaction of military costs, casualties, declining traffic, the advance of the railroad and alternative routes, and competition for federal resources. The Indian Peace Commission of 1867 marked a shift toward negotiation. Red Cloud insisted that the forts be abandoned before he would sign, preferring an action to a promise, while Cheyenne and Arapaho diplomacy proceeded on related but separate lines, culminating in a distinct treaty on May 10, 1868.

In the summer of 1868 the army abandoned the forts, with Fort C. F. Smith evacuated on July 29, and Indigenous people burned the empty posts. The burning was symbolic and should not be confused with the capture of occupied positions. Red Cloud signed the Fort Laramie treaty on November 6, 1868, after the withdrawal, and his signature must be distinguished from the April 29 negotiations.

The withdrawal was a real Indigenous strategic achievement that demonstrated the power of coalition warfare, territorial knowledge, and diplomatic judgment. It was not the permanent defeat of American expansion. The railroad, the destruction of the bison, intertribal rivalries, and the pressures toward reservation confinement remained, and the treaty that followed created new obligations and restrictions. Those provisions are the subject of Chapter 76, and the later conflict over the Black Hills belongs to Chapter 77.

  1. Gather sources. Using library and archival resources, locate at least three kinds of evidence: official military reports, officers' or family memoirs, and Indigenous accounts, such as oral histories or tribal historical materials, along with archaeological reports on the site where available.
  2. Document each source. For each, record the creator, date, audience, circumstances of creation, and provenance, and note how it was transmitted or recorded.
  3. Compare accounts. Identify where the sources agree, where they conflict, and which conflicts concern facts, interpretation, or responsibility.
  4. Trace the historiography. Using scholarly works, outline how interpretations of the fight have changed from the nineteenth century to the present.
  5. Write your findings. In a 4–6 page essay or equivalent presentation, state what can be established, what remains uncertain, and what additional evidence would help. Use careful language such as "the evidence indicates" or "this remains disputed."

Library resources to use: the National Archives treaty collection, National Park Service resources on the Bozeman Trail and the Fort Phil Kearny sites, the Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian's materials on Plains treaties, tribal historic preservation offices and tribal college libraries, and the MGU History Library's collections on Plains history.

Further Reading and Documentary Resources

Publication details below have been checked against library catalogs or publisher records where available. These works support further research and do not replace consultation of primary documents.

  • McDermott, John D. Red Cloud's War: The Bozeman Trail, 1866–1868. Two volumes. Arthur H. Clark Company, 2010. The most detailed military and documentary study of the campaign.
  • Monnett, John H. Where a Hundred Soldiers Were Killed: The Struggle for the Powder River Country in 1866 and the Making of the Fetterman Myth. University of New Mexico Press, 2008. Critical analysis of the Fetterman Fight and of the narratives built around it.
  • Larson, Robert W. Red Cloud: Warrior-Statesman of the Lakota Sioux. University of Oklahoma Press, 1997. Biography emphasizing Red Cloud's leadership and diplomacy.
  • Ostler, Jeffrey. The Lakotas and the Black Hills: The Struggle for Sacred Ground. Viking, 2010. Lakota sovereignty, treaty interpretation, and long-term consequences of the 1868 settlement.
  • Brown, Dee. Fort Phil Kearny: An American Saga. Putnam, 1962. An older narrative history, useful for the army's perspective and for historiographical comparison.
  • Carrington, Margaret Irvin. Ab-sa-ra-ka, Home of the Crows. Philadelphia: J. B. Lippincott & Co., 1868. A participant account by Colonel Carrington's wife.
  • Carrington, Frances C. Grummond. My Army Life. 1910. A participant memoir that defends her first husband and criticizes Carrington's conduct.
  • Grinnell, George Bird. The Fighting Cheyennes. 1915. Includes Cheyenne accounts of the war, recorded decades later.

Chapter Reflection

Red Cloud's War matters because it shows Indigenous nations setting the terms of their relationship with the United States through force and diplomacy. The Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho did not simply react to American expansion. They identified the vulnerabilities of a particular strategy, acted on them for two years, and obtained a concession that few Indigenous nations achieved: the evacuation of a military road built through their country. The chapter's central lesson is that Indigenous political and military power could be decisive when it was exercised in familiar terrain, through established networks, against a weakly supplied opponent.

The war also reveals how the American government actually worked in this period. A state that sought a treaty and occupied the country at the same time lost credibility with the people it addressed, and a military presence designed to open a road produced costs that made the road not worth its price. These patterns recurred in later conflicts, and they help explain why Indigenous leaders approached treaty negotiations with caution and why they placed such importance on actions that could be observed, such as the burning of the forts.

The victory shaped what followed, and it was bounded by what it left untouched. The treaty system of 1868, examined in Chapter 76, created a reservation and an unceded territory whose boundaries, rights, and obligations would later be contested, and the pressures of the railroad, the bison hunters, and the search for gold would test them within a decade, as Chapters 77 and 81 show. The reservation order examined in Chapter 84 would eventually confine communities that had won this war.

The chapter also leaves a lesson about historical writing. The war has long been told through the memoirs of officers and their families and through the myth of Fetterman, while Indigenous accounts, which survive in winter counts, oral histories, and tribal tradition, have often been treated as supplementary. A history of this conflict that takes Indigenous strategy, kinship, and diplomacy as its organizing subject, as this textbook does, reads it differently: not as a frontier tragedy but as a contest over territory, and one in which Indigenous nations, for a time, prevailed.
