Chapter Opening

Indigenous nations in the United States did not acquire courts when federal law permitted them. They had law before the United States existed, and the modern history of tribal courts is the history of how that law was disrupted, rebuilt, limited, and reasserted inside a federal system that kept redefining where tribal authority ended.

In August 1881, on the Rosebud Reservation in Dakota Territory, a Brulé Lakota man named Kan-gi-shun-ca, known in English as Crow Dog, shot and killed Sinte Gleska, known as Spotted Tail, a prominent Brulé leader. The Brulé community responded through its own methods of reconciliation, which scholars generally describe as involving restitution and the restoration of relations between the families. Territorial authorities nevertheless prosecuted Crow Dog in a federal-territorial court, and in 1883 the Supreme Court held in Ex parte Crow Dog that the court lacked jurisdiction over a killing of one Indian by another within Indian country. Congress answered in 1885 with the Major Crimes Act. The episode contains in miniature the pattern this chapter follows: an Indigenous legal order operating, a non-Indian government contesting it, a judicial decision about its limits, and a legislative reply that enlarged outside authority.

Nearly a century later, in August 1973, tribal police on the Port Madison Reservation in Washington arrested a non-Indian man during the Suquamish Tribe's annual Chief Seattle Days celebration. The prosecution that followed became Oliphant v. Suquamish Indian Tribe (1978), in which the Supreme Court concluded that tribes lack inherent criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians unless Congress authorizes it. In July 2020 the Court decided McGirt v. Oklahoma, holding that the Muscogee (Creek) Reservation in eastern Oklahoma remained a reservation for purposes of the Major Crimes Act, and in 2022 it decided Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta, which recognized concurrent state authority over certain crimes by non-Indians against Indians in Indian country. These decisions were not isolated verdicts. Each was embedded in treaties, statutes, local communities, and tribal institutions with their own histories.

This chapter asks how the courts of Indigenous nations in the United States came to occupy their present position. Its central argument is that tribal courts demonstrate the continuing governmental authority of Indigenous nations, but that their modern jurisdiction developed through repeated conflicts among inherent tribal sovereignty, federal legislation, Supreme Court decisions, and state jurisdiction. The resulting system is neither complete tribal independence nor ordinary local government. It is a historically constructed and frequently contested distribution of authority, and it differs from nation to nation depending on geography, treaties, statutes, and the legal status of the people and land involved.

The chapter proceeds in five divisions. The first examines Indigenous legal traditions and the nineteenth-century foundations of federal Indian law. The second follows the formation of modern tribal judiciaries and the doctrines governing civil jurisdiction. The third turns to criminal jurisdiction, including Oliphant, the Duro controversy, and Public Law 280. The fourth treats reforms of the twenty-first century: the Tribal Law and Order Act, the Violence Against Women Act's special tribal jurisdiction, sovereign immunity, and revitalized community justice. The fifth examines policing, McGirt, Castro-Huerta, and the continuing debates that surround them. The chapter gives most attention to the period from 1934 through 2026, while treating earlier developments as foundations.

Students should keep several distinctions in view from the outset. Tribal sovereignty is inherent rather than a grant from the United States. Civil and criminal jurisdiction follow different rules. Jurisdiction to hear a case, authority to regulate conduct, and immunity from suit are separate legal questions. And no simple formula, such as tribes prosecute Indians, states prosecute non-Indians, and the federal government handles major crimes, describes the actual system, which varies with the location, the identities of the accused and the victim, the offense, and the statutes applicable to a particular nation.

The evidence for this history is unusually varied. It includes treaties and statutes, Supreme Court opinions and lower-court records, tribal constitutions and codes, tribal court decisions, congressional hearings, reports of federal agencies, and the testimony of judges, police officers, victims, and community members. Each carries a perspective. Federal opinions describe tribal powers from outside, congressional records reflect the concerns of legislators, and tribal sources record how Indigenous governments understood their own authority. A responsible history reads them against one another.

This chapter concentrates on the legal development of tribal judicial authority in the United States. Canadian constitutional recognition was the subject of Chapter 117, and the broader history of policing and incarceration is reserved for Chapter 130. Where Canadian comparisons appear, they are brief. Related questions of the Indian Child Welfare Act, water, land title, gaming, and the crisis of violence against Indigenous women are treated in Chapters 131, 120, 119, 122, and 129 respectively, and the chapter refers to them only as needed to explain jurisdiction.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the diversity of Indigenous legal traditions and explain why they should not be characterized as universally informal or lacking enforceable law.
  2. Explain why tribal sovereignty is understood as inherent, and distinguish the existence of tribal authority from federal limits on its exercise.
  3. Trace the sequence from Ex parte Crow Dog through the Major Crimes Act, the Courts of Indian Offenses, the Indian Reorganization Act, and the rise of tribally controlled courts.
  4. Define Indian country and explain how landownership, reservation boundaries, and the identities of parties affect jurisdiction.
  5. Analyze the Indian Civil Rights Act and Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez, including the tension between collective self-government and individual rights.
  6. Distinguish tribal civil jurisdiction over nonmembers (Montana) from tribal criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians (Oliphant).
  7. Interpret the roles of the General Crimes Act, Major Crimes Act, Public Law 280, and congressional reforms in dividing criminal jurisdiction.
  8. Differentiate sovereign immunity, adjudicative jurisdiction, and regulatory authority as separate legal questions.
  9. Assess the significance of the Tribal Law and Order Act of 2010 and the Violence Against Women Act reauthorizations of 2013 and 2022.
  10. Evaluate McGirt, Cooley, and Castro-Huerta in their historical and statutory contexts, without treating them as simple victories or defeats.
  11. Evaluate competing arguments about whether federal Indian law adequately protects tribal sovereignty.

Historical Context

When Europeans arrived in North America, Indigenous nations already governed themselves through councils, clans, societies, hereditary and achieved offices, and bodies of law transmitted orally and through objects such as wampum. Early treaties between Indigenous nations and European and later American governments presupposed that Indigenous peoples were political entities capable of making agreements. The first decades of United States history developed this assumption into a body of doctrine in which Indigenous nations were distinct political communities, but ones subject to the federal government's claimed powers over Indian affairs. Chapter 65 examined how that doctrine was tested during the removal era, and Chapter 64 examined the Cherokee Nation's own constitutional and judicial institutions.

By the late nineteenth century, the military conquest of most Indigenous nations had given way to a reservation system, and federal policy aimed at assimilation. Agents administered reservations, allotment divided communal land into individual parcels (Chapter 86), and boarding schools removed children from their communities (Chapter 87). Federal administrators regarded Indigenous legal institutions as obstacles to assimilation and, in the 1880s, created their own courts in place of them. At the same time, Congress asserted the power to extend federal criminal law into Indian country, and the Supreme Court, in a line of decisions beginning in the 1880s, described that power as extending very broadly.

The twentieth century brought a series of policy reversals. The Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 (Chapter 97) encouraged tribal constitutional governments, termination-era policy beginning in the 1950s (Chapter 104) shifted jurisdiction to states and sought to end federal recognition of some nations, and the era of self-determination (Chapter 114) allowed tribes to contract for federal programs and operate their own justice systems. Tribal courts grew within this last framework. But their expansion coincided with a second line of Supreme Court decisions, beginning in the 1970s, that restricted tribal jurisdiction over non-Indians and, in some respects, nonmember Indians. Understanding the history of tribal courts therefore requires attention to both trends: institutional growth within Indigenous nations and doctrinal limitation by outside courts.

Division I — Indigenous Law and the Foundations of Tribal Sovereignty
Section 118.1

Indigenous Legal Traditions and Systems of Justice

Indigenous nations possessed legal orders long before European colonization. The assumption that law requires written codes, professional judges, and prisons reflects one historical tradition, not a universal definition. Across North America, Indigenous societies regulated conduct, resolved disputes, protected property and resources, controlled access to territory, and maintained political order through institutions that were often embedded in kinship, ceremony, and community obligation. Their law was enforceable because communities treated it as binding and had means of securing compliance, including public opinion, the intervention of respected elders, the loss of standing, restitution, banishment, and, in some societies, designated officers.

These traditions differed substantially. Among the Haudenosaunee, the Great Law of Peace, transmitted orally and recalled through wampum, established the confederacy's councils and a framework for resolving conflict. Clan mothers and clan leaders had roles in selecting and removing leaders and in dispute resolution, and the Condolence ceremony provided a way to address death and grief, including through compensation to the bereaved. For the Diné, law was bound up with k'é, the web of kinship and reciprocal obligation, and with hózhó, a concept of harmony and right order. Respected leaders known as naat'aanii mediated disputes, and restitution (nályééh) aimed to repair relationships rather than simply assign blame. Pueblo communities developed governmental and religious offices through which village authorities addressed internal disputes, and these systems persisted under Spanish and later American rule even as colonial governors and other officers were layered upon them.

Plains nations organized law partly through societies and councils. Lakota akicita societies, for example, enforced regulations during communal buffalo hunts, protecting a collective food supply from individuals who might scatter the herd. Cheyenne governance combined a council of chiefs with military societies that had policing duties during hunts and ceremonies. In the Southeast, the Cherokee and the Muscogee (Creek) Nations developed councils, town structures, and, by the early nineteenth century, written laws and officers such as the Cherokee Supreme Court established in the 1820s, which Chapter 64 treats as part of Cherokee constitutionalism. These examples show that Indigenous legal systems ranged from consensus-based councils to codified republican forms.

Historians should avoid two errors. The first treats Indigenous justice as uniformly informal, as though it lacked rules, authority, or consequences. In fact, many systems had clear norms, recognized decision-makers, and means of enforcement. The second treats Indigenous law as a single pan-Indian tradition. Restitution among one people, banishment among another, and clan-based responsibility among a third reflect distinct histories and values. The sources for reconstructing these systems include oral tradition, wampum and other mnemonic records, treaties, observations by missionaries and agents (whose interpretations reflect outside assumptions), twentieth-century ethnographies such as the legal anthropology of the Cheyenne by Karl Llewellyn and E. Adamson Hoebel, and, increasingly, decisions of tribal courts that identify and apply customary law.

This background matters for the rest of the chapter. When federal authorities in the 1880s created courts to replace Indigenous systems, or when the Supreme Court treated an Indian-on-Indian killing as outside federal jurisdiction, they were interacting with functioning legal orders, not with a legal void. Modern tribal courts are in part the continuation, adaptation, and reconstruction of those orders.

Section 118.2

The Federal Recognition of Tribal Sovereignty and Its Limitations

The modern doctrine of tribal sovereignty rests on early nineteenth-century decisions of the Supreme Court under Chief Justice John Marshall. In Cherokee Nation v. Georgia (1831), the Cherokee Nation asked the Court to stop Georgia from extending its laws over Cherokee territory. The Court declined to hear the case under its original jurisdiction, concluding that the Cherokee Nation was not a "foreign state" in the constitutional sense, and described Indian nations as "domestic dependent nations." The phrase has been used both to affirm that tribes are distinct political communities and to justify federal supervision over them. A year later, in Worcester v. Georgia (1832), involving the conviction of a missionary under Georgia law, the Court held that Georgia's laws could have no force within Cherokee territory and that relations with Indian nations were a matter for the federal government. Chapter 65 recounts why the decision did not stop Cherokee removal.

Three ideas from these decisions shaped later doctrine. First, treaties recognized Indigenous nations as political entities. Second, the federal government, rather than the states, held primary authority in Indian affairs. Third, tribal nations retained powers of self-government that had not been surrendered or taken away. Over time, the Court added a fourth idea that complicated the others: that Congress possessed a "plenary" authority over Indian affairs, a power later invoked to uphold statutes extending federal criminal law into Indian country and, in Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock (1903), to permit Congress to modify treaty provisions without tribal consent. The result was a doctrine in which tribal sovereignty was acknowledged, but the federal government claimed broad power to limit it.

The most important analytical distinction for this chapter is between the existence of tribal authority and the exercise of that authority. Tribal nations governed themselves before the Constitution. The Court in United States v. Wheeler (1978) reasoned that tribal power to punish members comes from the tribe's own inherent sovereignty, not from a federal delegation. But the Court has also held that tribes may lose certain powers by treaty, by statute, or by reason of their dependent status. In other words, federal law does not create tribal nationhood, but it determines in important respects what powers courts will recognize as presently exercisable. This is the foundation of the modern sovereignty doctrine.

Historical Vocabulary
Inherent Sovereignty and Delegated Authority

Definition. Inherent sovereignty is the authority a nation possesses by virtue of its existence as a self-governing political community, rather than by grant from another government. Delegated authority is power that one government confers on another.

Historical context. The distinction became legally significant when the Court had to decide whether tribal prosecutions of members were an exercise of tribal power or federal power. In Wheeler the Court said they were tribal, which meant a later federal prosecution did not place a defendant twice in jeopardy for the same offense by the same sovereign. In United States v. Lara (2004), the same question arose when Congress restored tribal power over nonmember Indians.

Why it matters. The characterization determines which constitutional limits apply to tribal courts, whether federal and tribal prosecutions can both proceed, and whether Congress's role is to grant, recognize, or relax limits on power.

Potential misunderstanding. To say that tribal sovereignty is inherent does not mean it is unlimited. The Court has recognized that Congress may restrict it, and that some limits arise from judicial interpretation of tribes' dependent status.

Section 118.3

Ex parte Crow Dog, the Major Crimes Act, and Federal Intervention

The Brulé Lakota had agreed to treaty relationships with the United States in the 1860s and 1870s, and by 1881 lived on the Rosebud Reservation under agency administration. Sinte Gleska (Spotted Tail) was a leading figure in dealings with the government, and Kan-gi-shun-ca (Crow Dog) had been associated with the agency's Indian police and with opposition to Spotted Tail's influence. In August 1881 Crow Dog shot and killed Spotted Tail. The communities involved sought to resolve the matter through customary means. The historian Sidney Harring, who studied the case extensively, describes a reconciliation involving payments to the victim's family and the restoration of peace between the Brulé factions, and the amounts commonly repeated in the literature derive from that work and related accounts. Whatever the specific terms, the Brulé settlement represented an exercise of Lakota legal authority.

Federal and territorial authorities rejected that settlement as inadequate. Crow Dog was arrested, tried in the territorial court in Dakota Territory, convicted of murder, and sentenced to death. His lawyers petitioned the Supreme Court for a writ of habeas corpus. In December 1883, in Ex parte Crow Dog, the Court held that the territorial court lacked jurisdiction. The reasoning turned on statutes and treaties: federal criminal law had been extended to Indian country, but with an exception for crimes of one Indian against another, and the Sioux treaty of 1868, together with a later agreement, did not authorize the prosecution. The opinion also observed that applying American criminal law to the Lakota would impose on them a legal system foreign to their understanding. The decision recognized that tribal law governed such internal matters, though its reasoning rested more on statutory interpretation than on a declaration of tribal sovereignty as such.

The reaction was swift. Federal officials and reformers argued that the decision left serious crimes among Indians unpunished by American law, a claim that ignored the existence of tribal means of redress. In March 1885, Congress added a provision to an appropriations act, now known as the Major Crimes Act, extending outside criminal jurisdiction over specified serious offenses committed by Indians against Indians or non-Indians. The original act distinguished crimes in United States territories, whether on or off a reservation, from crimes committed within Indian reservations situated inside states; the two branches assigned prosecution through different jurisdictional rules. The original list included murder, manslaughter, rape, assault with intent to kill, arson, burglary, and larceny, and Congress later expanded the list. In United States v. Kagama (1886), the Court upheld the statute, grounding Congress's authority in the guardianship relationship between the United States and Indian nations and in the federal government's power over their territories, rather than in an enumerated constitutional power over crime.

The consequences were lasting. The Act did not abolish tribal criminal jurisdiction, and the Supreme Court later recognized that tribes could prosecute members for the same conduct, but it established federal prosecution as a routine feature of criminal justice in Indian country and tied it to Congress's authority over Indian affairs. It also contributed to a pattern, which this chapter will follow, in which a legal ruling that favored Indigenous jurisdiction produced legislative expansion of outside jurisdiction. A distinction should be maintained between the federal prosecution of enumerated offenses and the continued operation of tribal law; the former adds to the latter rather than replacing it.

Primary Source Window

Ex parte Crow Dog (1883) and the Major Crimes Act (1885)

Creators: The Supreme Court of the United States (opinion by Justice Stanley Matthews); the United States Congress · Dates: 1883; 1885 · Type: Judicial opinion and federal statute

Context: The opinion resolved a habeas corpus petition following Crow Dog's conviction in a Dakota Territory court. The statute was enacted as a rider to an Indian appropriations act roughly fifteen months after the decision.

Paraphrase of the opinion (not a quotation): The Court concluded that the federal statutes extending United States criminal law to Indian country excepted offenses committed by one Indian against another, that the treaties with the Sioux did not authorize the prosecution, and that treating the killing as subject to territorial law would subject Indians to a system they had not agreed to and did not understand. Resolution of the matter, in the Court's view, belonged to the Indians' own laws and customs unless Congress provided otherwise.

Paraphrase of the statute (not a quotation): Section 9 of the March 3, 1885, act enumerated seven offenses: murder, manslaughter, rape, assault with intent to kill, arson, burglary, and larceny. For such crimes committed by Indians within a United States territory, whether inside or outside a reservation, territorial criminal law and courts applied. For the same enumerated crimes committed by Indians within an Indian reservation located in a state, the statute provided for trial under the laws and in the courts applicable to offenses within the exclusive jurisdiction of the United States. Both branches were present in the original 1885 law; subsequent legislation modified and expanded the statute rather than first extending it to state reservations.

Students should consult the full text of both documents in an authenticated source, such as the United States Reports and the Statutes at Large, before quoting from either. The paraphrases above are summaries, not the authors' words.

Analysis: Read together, the documents show how a decision about statutory limits invited a legislative remedy. The Court's holding rested on the absence of congressional authorization, and Congress supplied authorization within two years.

Limitations: The opinion does not describe Lakota law in detail. It reflects the Court's understanding rather than Brulé testimony, and the statute reflects the views of members of Congress and reformers rather than those of the Lakota. The tribal side of the dispute is better reconstructed from other evidence, including agency records and later scholarship.

Perspective: Both documents are products of the American government. Brulé perspectives appear only indirectly.

Section 118.4

Reservations, Indian Country, and the Geography of Jurisdiction

Federal law uses the term Indian country to determine where federal and tribal jurisdiction, rather than ordinary state jurisdiction, generally applies. The statutory definition, codified at 18 U.S.C. §1151, includes three categories: all land within the limits of an Indian reservation under federal jurisdiction, notwithstanding the issuance of any patent and including rights-of-way running through it; dependent Indian communities; and Indian allotments whose Indian titles have not been extinguished. The definition appears in the criminal code, but the Court has treated it as generally applicable to questions of civil jurisdiction as well.

The importance of the definition arises from the history of land loss. The General Allotment Act of 1887 and later surplus-land legislation, discussed in Chapter 86, divided many reservations into individual allotments and opened the remainder to non-Indian settlement. Over time, many parcels passed out of Indian ownership into non-Indian fee ownership, producing checkerboard patterns in which Indian trust land, tribal fee land, and non-Indian land lie side by side within reservation boundaries. The question then arises whether Congress, in opening the land, also diminished or disestablished the reservation. The Court has treated this as a question of congressional intent, examined through the text of the statute, the circumstances of its passage, and later treatment of the area. In Solem v. Bartlett (1984), involving the Cheyenne River Sioux Reservation, it stressed that diminishment must be clearly shown; in Nebraska v. Parker (2016), it held that the Omaha Reservation boundaries had not been diminished by an 1882 statute.

Land status and governmental authority are related but not identical. A reservation may continue to exist legally even though much of its land is owned by non-Indians, and the Court has held that fee ownership by non-Indians does not by itself remove land from reservation jurisdiction under §1151. At the same time, in civil matters, Montana and its progeny treat nonmember ownership of fee land as highly relevant to the scope of tribal regulatory authority. Jurisdiction therefore depends on several variables: whether the place is Indian country; the land's ownership; whether the accused and victim, or the parties to a civil dispute, are Indians or members of the relevant nation; the nature of the conduct; and the statutes applicable to the particular reservation.

Alaska illustrates the complexity of the category. In Alaska v. Native Village of Venetie Tribal Government (1998), the Court held that lands conveyed to Native corporations under the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act (Chapter 115) were not a "dependent Indian community," and consequently that most Alaska Native village lands are not Indian country. Alaska has only limited reservations, such as the Annette Island Reserve of the Metlakatla Indian Community. Alaska Native tribal courts therefore exercise authority in a setting where territorial jurisdiction is less clear and where membership-based jurisdiction, for example over child custody, has been especially important. The Alaska Supreme Court recognized tribal authority of that kind in John v. Baker (1999).

Historical Vocabulary
Indian Country

Definition. A legal category defined by federal statute, comprising reservations, dependent Indian communities, and qualifying allotments.

Historical context. The statutory definition consolidated earlier judicial and legislative usage that had developed since the early nineteenth century, when Indian country described the region west of settled areas in which federal Indian trade and intercourse laws applied.

Why it matters. The category determines whether state jurisdiction is presumptively displaced and whether federal and tribal law apply.

Potential misunderstanding. Indian country is not the same as Indian ownership, nor does it include all land that tribal members occupy or consider homeland.

Division II — The Development of Tribal Courts and Civil Jurisdiction
Section 118.5

Courts of Indian Offenses and the Formation of Modern Tribal Judiciaries

In 1883, the Secretary of the Interior, Henry Teller, authorized the creation of Courts of Indian Offenses on reservations, and the Commissioner of Indian Affairs issued regulations for them. The courts were staffed by Indian judges, usually appointed by the agent, and applied rules promulgated by federal officials. Their stated purpose was to civilize Indians and to suppress practices that agents considered obstacles to assimilation, including certain ceremonies, plural marriage, and the work of medicine people, alongside conventional offenses. A federal district court in United States v. Clapox (1888) described the courts as instruments of federal administration rather than as courts of the United States or of tribal nations. Their significance is therefore double: they provided a forum for resolving disputes, but they were designed to serve federal policy and displace Indigenous authority.

The Courts of Indian Offenses must be distinguished from judicial institutions governed by tribal nations. The former were federal creations, funded and supervised by the Interior Department. Some Indigenous nations, such as the Cherokee, the Muscogee (Creek), and the other Five Tribes, had maintained their own courts in the nineteenth century, but the Curtis Act of 1898 abolished tribal courts in Indian Territory as part of the preparation for Oklahoma statehood. Elsewhere, traditional dispute-resolution persisted alongside the federal courts, often informally and out of sight of agency officials.

The Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 (Chapter 97) changed the framework. It encouraged tribes to adopt constitutions and governmental structures, and the Bureau of Indian Affairs circulated model constitutions and law-and-order codes. Many nations adopted IRA constitutions, some of which created tribal councils with judicial functions or provided for tribal courts. Others declined: the Navajo Nation, for example, rejected the IRA in a 1935 referendum, and several nations organized governments through other means. The IRA era therefore produced a variety of structures, and the assumption that all nations followed the same model is mistaken. In practice, the period saw continued federal influence over tribal court operations, and many tribal courts remained dependent on federal funding and approval of codes.

The Navajo Nation's establishment of its own court system on April 1, 1959, is a significant case study. The Navajo Nation had a federally administered Court of Indian Offenses, but the Navajo Tribal Council moved to establish a tribally governed judiciary, which began operating in 1959 under a code that authorized its courts to apply Navajo customs and usages where consistent with federal law. The change came in the same year the Supreme Court decided Williams v. Lee, in which a non-Indian trader on the reservation sued Navajo customers in Arizona state court. The Court held that state jurisdiction would infringe the Navajos' right to make their own laws and be ruled by them, and that the matter belonged in tribal court. Over the following decades, the Navajo Nation developed a judicial branch with trial courts, a Supreme Court, rules of procedure, and a body of written opinions. Chapter 114 discusses the broader self-determination policies that supported this growth.

Section 118.6

Tribal Constitutions, Judicial Institutions, and the Administration of Justice

Modern tribal courts differ widely in size, structure, and resources. Some nations operate multiple trial courts, a court of appeals or supreme court, specialized dockets, and a staff of clerks, probation officers, and prosecutors. Others, particularly small nations, may have a single judge who sits part-time, or may contract with an intertribal court system that provides judges and appellate review to several communities. Examples include the Northwest Intertribal Court System, which has served nations in western Washington, and the Intertribal Court of Southern California. These consortia reflect an adaptive response to limited resources and the demand for trained judges.

Tribal constitutions and codes establish the judiciary's relationship to other branches of government. Provisions vary: some nations choose judges by election, others by appointment by the council or executive, often with confirmation, and many include terms, qualifications, and removal procedures designed to protect independence. The Navajo Nation, the Cherokee Nation, and other larger nations have adopted explicit separation-of-powers structures, while in smaller nations the council may retain some judicial functions. Judicial independence is thus an ongoing institutional issue rather than a settled fact, and tribal judges have at times faced removal or pressure after unpopular decisions. The same is true of many state court systems and of federal courts at various times.

The law applied by tribal courts has several sources: tribal constitutions, enacted codes, resolutions, treaties, applicable federal law, precedent from the nation's own courts, and customary or traditional law. Many nations have adopted statutes that direct judges to consider custom. The Navajo Nation Council, for example, recognized Diné fundamental law in legislation of 2002, which articulates principles drawn from Diné teachings, and Navajo courts have used those principles in written opinions. Other nations rely more heavily on statutory codes modeled on state law, and some borrow from state or federal precedent. Each approach reflects choices about how to balance continuity, predictability, and accessibility.

Tribal courts are governmental institutions. They exercise the power of a government to adjudicate disputes, enforce laws, and, in appropriate cases, impose penalties. They are not cultural programs or subordinate branches of state courts, and the federal courts have generally recognized that they are courts of competent jurisdiction in matters within tribal authority. The Supreme Court has described tribal courts as playing a vital role in tribal self-government and has required exhaustion of tribal remedies before related federal litigation in many cases, as in National Farmers Union Insurance Cos. v. Crow Tribe (1985). Federal policy in the later twentieth century supported them through the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975, which allowed tribes to contract for programs previously run by the Bureau of Indian Affairs, and the Indian Tribal Justice Act of 1993, which authorized support for tribal justice systems. The adequacy of actual appropriations has been a persistent concern. The National American Indian Court Judges Association, founded in 1969, has been among the organizations through which tribal judges share practices and advocate for support.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Indigenous Law to Modern Tribal Judiciaries

What Changed

  • Decision-makers moved from councils, elders, and societies to formal judges, clerks, and written codes in many nations
  • Between the 1880s and 1930s, federal administrators controlled the courts that operated on many reservations
  • Tribal courts became subject to federal statutes, such as the Indian Civil Rights Act, and to federal court interpretation of their jurisdictional limits
  • Many tribal courts now handle civil, family, contract, probate, regulatory, and criminal cases in a form recognizable to outside lawyers

What Stayed the Same

  • The claim that tribal communities have authority to decide disputes among themselves persisted through every period of federal intervention
  • Customary principles of reciprocity, restitution, and communal responsibility continued in family and community settings and are now written into some tribal law
  • Diversity among nations remained: no single tribal-court model replaced the many Indigenous traditions

Pattern: Change was uneven and often externally imposed, but continuity was sustained by Indigenous institutions and community practice. The earlier chapters' warning against treating either change or continuity as the whole story applies here.

Section 118.7

Tribal Membership, Internal Affairs, and the Indian Civil Rights Act

One of the core attributes of a self-governing nation is the power to determine its own membership. The Supreme Court has recognized that power as central to tribal sovereignty, and tribal nations set their own standards, which may rest on lineal descent, blood quantum, residence, community recognition, or other criteria. Chapter 144 treats the politics of enrollment. The power extends to a range of internal affairs, including elections, succession of leaders, family relationships, domestic relations among members, and the resolution of disputes over governmental authority.

Until the late 1960s, the federal Constitution's guarantees did not apply to tribal governments. In Talton v. Mayes (1896), the Court held that the Fifth Amendment's grand-jury requirement did not bind the Cherokee Nation, reasoning that tribal powers of local self-government pre-existed the Constitution and were not created by it. The decision reflected the principle of inherent sovereignty, but it also meant that individuals subject to tribal authority did not have the protections of the Bill of Rights against tribal government action. During the 1960s, a Senate subcommittee chaired by Senator Sam Ervin of North Carolina investigated complaints about the treatment of individuals by tribal governments, and Congress responded with the Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968 (ICRA).

ICRA applied to tribal governments many guarantees resembling those in the Bill of Rights, including free speech, protection against unreasonable searches and seizures, due process, equal protection, and protection against double jeopardy. It deliberately omitted some provisions, such as the establishment clause and a right to appointed counsel at government expense, and it limited tribal criminal punishment (initially to six months' imprisonment and a $500 fine, later increased). Importantly, the statute did not incorporate the Bill of Rights as such. It created a distinct statutory regime tailored, in the view of its supporters, to tribal governments and their different institutions and cultures. Its principal federal remedy was the writ of habeas corpus for persons detained by tribal authorities.

The Supreme Court's decision in Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez (1978) tested the limits of enforcement. Julia Martinez, a member of Santa Clara Pueblo in New Mexico who had married a Navajo man, challenged a 1939 tribal ordinance that denied membership to children of women who married outside the Pueblo but conferred it on children of men who did so. Her daughter Audrey, raised in the Pueblo, was thus denied membership. The Court held that ICRA did not authorize a federal civil action against a tribe or its officers to enforce the statute, other than the habeas remedy, and that tribal sovereign immunity barred the suit. The opinion stressed Congress's interest in promoting tribal self-government and noted that tribal forums remained available.

Martinez is both a landmark of sovereignty doctrine and a source of continuing debate. Supporters argue that it protects tribal nations' power to define their own communities, free from federal judicial supervision of internal decisions, and that decisions about membership are inseparable from cultural survival. Critics, including some Indigenous women, have argued that it left individuals without a federal forum when tribal governments applied discriminatory rules. The case thus embodies the tension between collective sovereign authority and individual rights, especially in matters of gender and membership. Tribal courts have since become important forums for such disputes, and many tribal constitutions and codes now contain their own bills of rights. Nations have also revised membership rules through their own political processes.

Section 118.8

Civil Jurisdiction over Nonmembers: Montana v. United States

Civil jurisdiction concerns a tribe's authority to regulate conduct and adjudicate disputes involving nonmembers. Early decisions favored tribal authority: in Williams v. Lee (1959), the Court barred state court jurisdiction over a debt claim against Navajos arising on the reservation, and in Merrion v. Jicarilla Apache Tribe (1982), it upheld a tribal severance tax on oil and gas extracted by non-Indian companies from tribal land. These decisions treated tribal civil authority as a component of inherent sovereignty, subject to defeat by treaty or federal law.

Montana v. United States (1981) introduced a more restrictive framework for a particular setting. The Crow Tribe sought to regulate hunting and fishing by nonmembers on the Big Horn River within the Crow Reservation, including on land owned in fee by non-Indians. The Court held that the tribe could not regulate nonmember hunting and fishing on non-Indian fee land. It announced a general rule that tribes lack inherent authority over the activities of nonmembers on such land, subject to two exceptions: one for nonmembers who enter into consensual relationships with the tribe or its members, and one for conduct that threatens or directly affects the political integrity, economic security, or health or welfare of the tribe. The Court also concluded that the riverbed itself belonged to the state, which was relevant to the analysis.

Later decisions applied and extended Montana. In Strate v. A-1 Contractors (1997), the Court held that a tribal court lacked jurisdiction over a civil suit between nonmembers arising from a collision on a state highway right-of-way crossing the Fort Berthold Reservation, treating the right-of-way as equivalent to non-Indian fee land for this purpose. In Nevada v. Hicks (2001), it held that a tribal court lacked jurisdiction over claims against state officers who had executed a search warrant on tribal member's land within the reservation. Atkinson Trading Co. v. Shirley (2001) invalidated a Navajo Nation hotel occupancy tax as applied to a nonmember-owned hotel on fee land, and Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co. (2008) held that a tribal court lacked jurisdiction over a discrimination claim arising from a nonmember bank's sale of fee land. In Dollar General Corp. v. Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians (2016), an equally divided Court left in place a decision affirming tribal court jurisdiction over a tort claim against a nonmember company that had entered a consensual relationship with the tribe, and so set no precedent.

The doctrine generates three distinctions that students should keep separate. First, Montana principally concerns civil regulatory and adjudicative authority, not criminal jurisdiction. It does not eliminate tribal authority over nonmembers, because its two exceptions and the continuing tribal power to exclude nonmembers from tribal land remain. Second, a tribal court's adjudicative jurisdiction over nonmembers generally cannot exceed its legislative authority, as Strate reasoned. Third, the courts' restrictive interpretation of nonmember jurisdiction contrasts with broader claims by tribal governments, which have argued that sovereignty over a territory should logically encompass those who enter it and that the doctrine undervalues tribal governments' responsibility for public welfare on reservations. These disagreements remain active.

Division III — Criminal Jurisdiction and the Division of Sovereign Authority
Section 118.9

Federal Criminal Jurisdiction: The General Crimes Act and Major Crimes Act

Two federal statutes provide the basic framework for federal criminal jurisdiction in Indian country. The General Crimes Act, also called the Indian Country Crimes Act (18 U.S.C. §1152), traces to early nineteenth-century legislation and extends federal criminal law applicable in areas of exclusive federal jurisdiction to Indian country. It contains exceptions: it does not apply to offenses committed by one Indian against another, to Indians who have already been punished by the local law of the tribe, or where treaties give tribes exclusive jurisdiction. The Major Crimes Act (18 U.S.C. §1153), discussed in Section 118.3, gives the federal government jurisdiction over enumerated felonies committed by Indians in Indian country, whether the victim is an Indian or a non-Indian. The list has grown since 1885 and now includes offenses such as murder, manslaughter, kidnapping, certain sexual offenses, felony assault, felony child abuse or neglect, arson, burglary, robbery, and certain felony thefts.

Neither statute displaced tribal jurisdiction. The Supreme Court held in United States v. Wheeler (1978) that tribal and federal prosecutions of the same Indian for related conduct do not violate the double jeopardy clause, because the two governments are separate sovereigns. A tribal nation may therefore prosecute a member for an offense that the federal government may also prosecute. In practice, however, many offenses that could be prosecuted by either are handled by only one, and federal declinations to prosecute have been a persistent concern. A further layer arises from the Assimilative Crimes Act, which allows federal prosecutors to apply state criminal law to fill gaps in federal law in federal enclaves, including Indian country.

Section 118.10

Oliphant v. Suquamish Indian Tribe and Criminal Jurisdiction over Non-Indians

The Port Madison Reservation lies across Puget Sound from Seattle. By the 1970s, most land within it was owned by non-Indians, and the reservation population included many non-Indians. In August 1973, during the Suquamish Tribe's annual Chief Seattle Days, tribal police arrested Mark David Oliphant, a non-Indian resident of the reservation, and charged him with assaulting a tribal officer and resisting arrest. A second petitioner, Daniel Belgarde, was charged with recklessly endangering another and injuring tribal property after a high-speed chase. Both sought release through federal habeas corpus, arguing that the tribe could not try non-Indians.

In Oliphant v. Suquamish Indian Tribe (1978), the Supreme Court, in an opinion by Justice William Rehnquist, agreed. The majority reasoned that the historical assumptions of Congress and the executive branch, reflected in treaties and in statutes such as the 1834 trade and intercourse acts, supported the view that tribes did not possess criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians, and that exercising it would be inconsistent with the tribes' status as dependent nations. This reasoning has been called the implicit divestiture doctrine: tribes may lose certain inherent powers not by explicit treaty or statute but because the powers are inconsistent with their dependent status. The Court added that Congress could grant such authority, and, as Section 118.15 shows, it later did so in limited form. The dissent argued that inherent sovereignty continued unless Congress had expressly removed it and that the majority had relied on weak historical evidence.

The consequences were practical as well as doctrinal. After Oliphant, tribal police on many reservations could detain a non-Indian suspect only for transfer to state or federal authorities, who then decided whether to prosecute. Federal prosecutors have limited resources and, in many areas, a large territory, and prior federal reports found that prosecutions of some offenses were declined at high rates. Where the state lacked jurisdiction, as in a case involving a non-Indian accused of crimes against an Indian in many places before Castro-Huerta, only the federal government could proceed. Advocates documented the consequences for victims, particularly in domestic and sexual violence, and Chapter 129 examines that crisis. The Indian Law and Order Commission, created by Congress in 2010, later recommended legislative restoration of tribal jurisdiction over non-Indians in certain settings.

Contemporary evaluation separates the Court's reasoning from its consequences. Defenders of the decision argue that it protected non-Indians from being tried by governments in which they have no vote and in courts that were, at the time, not always bound by the full range of constitutional protections, and that the Court reasonably applied historical assumptions. Critics argue that the decision conflicted with precedents recognizing inherent tribal powers, relied on a selective reading of nineteenth-century sources, and left reservations with a public-safety gap. Because the doctrine rests on judicial interpretation of Congress's assumed intent, Congress retains authority to alter its practical effect, a point confirmed in Lara.

Section 118.11

Duro v. Reina, Congressional Restoration, and United States v. Lara

The next question was whether tribes could prosecute Indians who were not members of the prosecuting nation. In Duro v. Reina (1990), Albert Duro, an enrolled member of the Torres-Martinez Band of Cahuilla Mission Indians, was charged by the Salt River Pima-Maricopa Indian Community with unlawfully firing a weapon in connection with the shooting of a young person on the reservation. He had lived on the reservation and worked for a tribal-affiliated company, but was not a member of the community. The Supreme Court held that tribes lack inherent criminal jurisdiction over nonmember Indians, extending the reasoning of Oliphant. A consequence was that a nonmember Indian could potentially commit a minor offense on a reservation without any tribal prosecution, leaving, in some places, a gap where neither the state nor the federal government might act.

Congress responded promptly. In 1990 it enacted a temporary amendment, and in 1991 made permanent a change to the Indian Civil Rights Act that defined the "powers of self-government" of Indian tribes to include the inherent power to exercise criminal jurisdiction over all Indians. This legislation is commonly called the Duro fix. Its wording, which describes the power as inherent rather than delegated, was chosen deliberately, because the choice determined whether federal and tribal prosecutions could both proceed without double jeopardy problems.

That question reached the Supreme Court in United States v. Lara (2004). Billy Jo Lara, a member of the Turtle Mountain Band of Chippewa Indians, lived on the Spirit Lake Reservation in North Dakota, where he had a relationship with a tribal member and had been excluded from the reservation after trouble with authorities. When he returned and struck a federal officer, he was prosecuted in the Spirit Lake tribal court and later in federal court. Lara argued that the federal prosecution violated double jeopardy because the tribal prosecution had been a delegation of federal power. The Court, in an opinion by Justice Stephen Breyer, held that Congress has constitutional power to relax restrictions that the courts had placed on tribal authority and that the tribe had acted under its own inherent power. The decision did not resolve whether Congress could expand tribal jurisdiction in every setting, and several justices wrote separately. Justice Clarence Thomas, concurring, questioned the entire framework of plenary congressional authority over tribes.

The sequence is instructive. Duro and Lara show that the Court's limitations on tribal power in Oliphant and Duro were treated as judicially defined defaults that Congress could revise, and that the distinction between inherent and delegated power has real legal consequences. They also reveal that the plenary power doctrine could operate to restore tribal authority as well as to curtail it, a feature that tribal advocates have used strategically.

Section 118.12

Public Law 280 and the Expansion of State Jurisdiction

Public Law 280, enacted in 1953, was part of the termination-era policy discussed in Chapter 104. Congress, seeking to reduce federal responsibility and to promote assimilation, transferred broad criminal jurisdiction in Indian country to five initially designated states and authorized their courts to hear specified civil disputes involving Indians. A sixth mandatory state was added later. The original states were California, Minnesota (except the Red Lake Reservation), Nebraska, Oregon (except the Warm Springs Reservation), and Wisconsin, with Alaska added in 1958 (except for the Annette Island Reserve). The statute also permitted other states to assume jurisdiction, and several did so in whole or in part, including Washington, Montana, Idaho, Nevada, Florida, and others. The legislation was enacted without tribal consent, and tribal leaders objected to its effect on self-government and to the lack of accompanying resources.

It is important to describe the statute precisely. Public Law 280 granted the identified states criminal jurisdiction over offenses by or against Indians in Indian country and conferred jurisdiction on state courts over civil causes of action between Indians or to which Indians were parties. But the Supreme Court held in Bryan v. Itasca County (1976) that the civil provision did not give states general civil regulatory or taxing power over tribes and tribal members, only a forum to adjudicate private civil disputes. The law also did not extinguish tribal jurisdiction. According to a recent Government Accountability Office report on Alaska, with few exceptions, tribes and states in P.L. 280 states share concurrent criminal jurisdiction.

The 1968 Indian Civil Rights Act, which also included amendments to Public Law 280, required tribal consent for any future assumption of jurisdiction by states and authorized the federal government to accept retrocession, that is, the return of previously assumed jurisdiction to the federal government. Retrocession has occurred in a number of places, among them the Omaha Reservation in Nebraska (federal acceptance in October 1970) and the Yakama Nation in Washington (partial retrocession federally accepted in October 2015), but it requires action by the state and the federal government, not by the tribe alone. The Tribal Law and Order Act of 2010 added a mechanism allowing a tribe in a mandatory state to request that the federal government exercise concurrent jurisdiction, subject to the discretion of the Attorney General.

Experience under Public Law 280 has not been uniform. In some places, state and county law enforcement provide adequate service. In others, tribes, particularly in remote areas and in Alaska, have reported slow response times, limited coverage by state police, and difficulty in obtaining federal funding for tribal police and courts, because federal programs have in some cases treated the states as responsible for law enforcement. Some tribal nations in P.L. 280 states, including in California, have developed courts, often with limited resources, and have worked to define the relationship between their courts and state systems. Others have sought retrocession or opted for cooperative agreements. These differences underscore why tribal experiences with state jurisdiction cannot be treated as uniform, and why the debates discussed in Section 118.20 concern different nations in different legal circumstances.

How Did Criminal Jurisdiction Become Divided?

Long-term causes

Treaties and early statutes that recognized tribal self-government while asserting federal authority; assimilation policy that treated Indigenous legal systems as obstacles; changing concepts of congressional power over Indian affairs.

Short-term causes

The 1881 killing of Spotted Tail and the federal reaction to Crow Dog; termination-era pressure to shift costs and responsibilities to states; the growth of non-Indian populations on reservations after allotment.

Triggers and decisions

The Major Crimes Act (1885); Courts of Indian Offenses (1883); Public Law 280 (1953); Oliphant (1978) and Duro (1990), which restricted tribal jurisdiction through implicit divestiture.

Long-term consequences

A fragmented system dependent on the identities of parties and location; congressional responses such as the Duro fix, the Tribal Law and Order Act, and VAWA tribal provisions.

No single cause produced the division. Federal policy, judicial reasoning, state interests, tribal action, and the circumstances of particular crimes interacted, and outcomes differed from nation to nation.

Division IV — Reform, Accountability, and the Reassertion of Tribal Judicial Authority
Section 118.13

Tribal Sovereign Immunity, Economic Regulation, and Civil Enforcement

Sovereign immunity is a doctrine under which governments cannot be sued without their consent. The Supreme Court has recognized that Indian tribes possess sovereign immunity from suit, which may be waived by the tribe or abrogated by Congress. The doctrine was applied in Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez and extended to commercial contexts in Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Citizen Band Potawatomi Indian Tribe (1991). In Kiowa Tribe of Oklahoma v. Manufacturing Technologies, Inc. (1998), the Court held that tribal immunity applied to a suit on a promissory note executed off the reservation in connection with a commercial transaction, while observing that Congress could limit it. In Michigan v. Bay Mills Indian Community (2014), the Court held that a state could not sue a tribe in federal court to enjoin a casino operating off its reservation, though it noted that the state had other means to address the conduct, including actions against individual officials or the use of its own regulatory powers.

Subsequent cases clarified the doctrine's boundaries. Lewis v. Clarke (2017) held that tribal immunity does not bar a suit against a tribal employee in his individual capacity for conduct within the scope of employment, where the employee, not the tribe, is the real party in interest. Lac du Flambeau Band of Lake Superior Chippewa Indians v. Coughlin (2023) held that the Bankruptcy Code abrogates the sovereign immunity of tribes, as it does for other governments. Courts have also recognized that tribes may waive immunity through contracts, including arbitration clauses, and that waivers must be clear. Debates about commercial dealings with tribes, including consumer lending, have frequently turned on whether the entity involved is an arm of the tribe.

Immunity is a part of governmental accountability, not its absence. Tribal governments establish their own mechanisms for redress, including tribal tort claims acts, employment and contract procedures, administrative appeals, and limited waivers. Commercial partners commonly negotiate limited waivers of immunity, designated forums for disputes, and arbitration or tribal-court choice-of-law provisions, thereby managing the legal risk that immunity otherwise creates. The practice makes tribal courts important in commercial law, and Chapter 122 explains how this operates in the gaming sector without duplicating the regulatory history of compacts.

Historical Vocabulary
Three Separate Questions: Jurisdiction, Regulation, and Immunity

Definition. Adjudicative jurisdiction is a court's authority to hear a dispute. Regulatory authority is a government's power to make rules governing conduct. Sovereign immunity is protection from being sued unless consent has been given or Congress has abrogated the protection.

Historical context. The Court addressed each question at different times. Montana and Strate concern regulatory and adjudicative authority over nonmembers; Kiowa and Bay Mills concern immunity.

Why it matters. A tribal court may have jurisdiction over a dispute, the tribe may have authority to regulate the conduct, and yet the tribe itself may be immune from suit. The three inquiries can have different answers.

Potential misunderstanding. Immunity from suit does not mean that tribal governments are above the law, nor does it mean they can regulate any person. It means a particular kind of lawsuit may not proceed absent consent.

Section 118.14

The Tribal Law and Order Act of 2010 and Criminal Justice Reform

By the early twenty-first century, federal reports, congressional hearings, and tribal testimony described a justice system in Indian country marked by fragmented authority, limited resources, and uneven prosecution. Tribal leaders reported that tribal courts and police were underfunded, that federal declinations were frequent and unexplained, and that sentencing limits left tribal courts unable to respond adequately to serious repeat offenses. These findings produced the Tribal Law and Order Act of 2010 (TLOA), signed in July 2010.

TLOA addressed several problems. It required federal agencies to track and share information about declinations of prosecution and to coordinate with tribal officials; it encouraged the appointment of tribal-liaison and specially designated prosecutors; it provided for training and for broader tribal access to criminal databases; and it created the Indian Law and Order Commission, which reported in 2013 with recommendations for further changes. Most significant for tribal courts, it expanded sentencing authority. Under ICRA as amended in the 1980s, tribal courts could impose up to one year's imprisonment and a fine of $5,000 per offense. TLOA allowed tribal courts to impose up to three years' imprisonment per offense, up to a total of nine years for multiple offenses, and fines up to $15,000, provided specified safeguards were met.

The safeguards reflected the concern that expanded sentences required greater procedural protection. Eligibility for punishment exceeding one year or a $5,000 fine under 25 U.S.C. §1302(b) also requires that the defendant have a prior conviction for the same or a comparable offense in a United States jurisdiction, or be prosecuted for an offense comparable to one punishable by more than a year in federal or state court. The following procedural conditions are additional, not substitutes for that threshold. A tribe seeking to exercise enhanced authority must provide an indigent defendant with effective assistance of counsel at the tribe's expense, ensure that the presiding judge has sufficient legal training and is licensed to practice law, make the tribe's criminal laws and rules publicly available, and maintain a record of the proceeding. Many nations, particularly those with resources and established judicial systems, implemented enhanced sentencing. Others found that requirements such as licensed counsel and judges, public defenders, and recording were costly or difficult to satisfy, and some elected not to adopt the enhanced authority. The Act therefore expanded the options available without altering the underlying limitation on jurisdiction over non-Indians.

The reforms illustrate a recurring relationship between expanded tribal authority and the federal government's responsibility to support effective institutions. Expanded jurisdiction without funding for judges, defenders, detention facilities, and technology can be of limited practical value. Tribes also faced difficulty in providing detention space, and federal contracting for incarceration was limited. The adequacy of federal support remains a theme in the reports discussed in Section 118.20.

Section 118.15

The Violence Against Women Act and the Restoration of Particular Tribal Criminal Powers

The Violence Against Women Act reauthorization signed in March 2013 was the first modern federal statute to recognize tribal criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians in a specified category of offenses. The legislation followed years of advocacy and documentation of violence against Native women, including reports such as Amnesty International's 2007 Maze of Injustice, and Chapter 129 treats that broader history. The statute recognized the authority of participating tribes to exercise what it called "special domestic violence criminal jurisdiction" over non-Indians who committed domestic violence, dating violence, or violations of certain protection orders in Indian country, provided that the defendant had specified ties to the tribe, such as residence or employment in the tribe's Indian country or a relationship with a tribal member or Indian resident.

Congress conditioned the authority on procedural protections. Participating tribes had to provide rights comparable to those required for enhanced sentencing under TLOA, including effective assistance of counsel, a licensed and law-trained judge, publicly available laws, and a jury drawn from sources reflecting a fair cross-section of the community, without systematic exclusion of non-Indians. Defendants could petition for federal habeas review and seek a stay of detention. The authority took effect generally in March 2015, but a pilot project allowed three nations, the Pascua Yaqui Tribe of Arizona, the Tulalip Tribes of Washington, and the Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation of Oregon, to begin in February 2014. Participating tribes made arrests and obtained convictions, and the Department of Justice and tribal officials described the experience as workable, though implementation costs were significant.

The 2013 statute had limits. It covered only certain crimes, required the defendant to have the prescribed ties to the tribe, and did not generally extend to Alaska Native villages outside Indian country, apart from the Indian-country setting of Metlakatla. It did not reach, for example, a stranger who committed sexual assault or violence against a child, or an assault on a tribal police officer. The 2022 reauthorization, signed in March 2022, expanded what the statute calls special Tribal criminal jurisdiction to include additional covered crimes: sexual violence, stalking, sex trafficking, child violence, obstruction of justice, and assaults on tribal justice personnel, along with the original categories. The 2022 legislation also removed the 2013 requirement that a non-Indian defendant have specified residential, employment, or intimate/dating-relationship ties to the participating tribe. Special Tribal criminal jurisdiction nevertheless remains limited to statutorily covered crimes and normally to offenses in the participating tribe's Indian country. For offenses in which both the defendant and alleged victim are non-Indian, the statute generally bars special Tribal criminal jurisdiction, except for obstruction of justice and assault against tribal justice personnel. Covered cases involving Indian victims remain within the statutory framework. A separate Alaska pilot pathway, codified at 25 U.S.C. §1305, addresses qualifying offenses in designated Alaska Native villages without simply treating all village lands as Indian country. The expanded provisions took effect on October 1, 2022.

This history shows how the Court's holding in Oliphant was modified through targeted legislation rather than reversed. The statute is a specific statutory recognition of jurisdiction, not an unrestricted restoration of criminal power over all non-Indians. Many offenses, such as ordinary property crimes or drug offenses by non-Indians, remain outside the statute. Participation requires a tribe to have a functioning court that satisfies procedural requirements, and nations with limited resources have had difficulty meeting them. For victims, however, the reforms offered a means of obtaining a tribal response where previously there might have been none.

Section 118.16

Tribal Peacemaking, Restorative Justice, and Community-Based Legal Institutions

While tribal courts adopted structures resembling other American courts, many nations also revitalized traditional approaches to dispute resolution. The best-known example is the Navajo Nation's peacemaking program. Navajo peacemaking draws on k'é and on the role of respected community members, called naat'aanii, who guide the parties in discussing the dispute, its causes, and the obligations of those involved. The Navajo Judicial Conference established a Peacemaking Court in 1982, and the Navajo judicial branch has used peacemaking in family, community, and some criminal matters. Robert Yazzie, who served as chief justice of the Navajo Nation Supreme Court, wrote and spoke extensively about the program and its relationship to Diné legal thought. In this model, the goal is less to determine who should be punished than to restore balance, identify responsibilities, and agree on repair, and the outcome may be incorporated into a court order.

Other Indigenous nations have developed restorative and community-based programs reflecting their own traditions. Circle sentencing, elders' panels, family group conferences, healing-to-wellness courts for substance-related offenses, and tribal drug courts have been adapted by various nations. Reconciliation, restitution, communal responsibility, and the interests of victims interact in these programs differently. In some settings, victims and communities have found them more responsive than conventional adjudication, because they address relationships and underlying causes. Because traditions differ, these practices are not interchangeable: a Diné peacemaking session, a Haudenosaunee condolence process, and a Plains council differ in values and method. The adoption of a "traditional" model by a nation is therefore a choice and a work of interpretation, not simply a revival.

Restorative approaches are also not suitable for every dispute. Critics and some practitioners have cautioned that where there is serious violence, a power imbalance between the parties, or a risk to a victim, a process that emphasizes reconciliation can pressure the vulnerable party, and that victims' safety and wishes must be central. Tribal courts therefore generally use peacemaking alongside conventional procedures, and many ensure that participation is voluntary or that a judge supervises outcomes. The relationship between formal courts and community processes is a continuing subject of experimentation and debate within Indigenous nations.

Ultimately, tribal justice systems face the challenges of financing, training legal professionals, maintaining public confidence, and making courts accessible. Many tribes rely on federal grants, including funds authorized under the Indian Tribal Justice Act, that have historically fallen short of estimated needs, and tribal revenues are often committed to other pressing services. Recruiting law-trained judges, prosecutors, and defenders to rural reservations is difficult. Intertribal courts, tribal bar associations, law schools with Indian-law programs, and the exchange of opinions among tribal courts have helped. The credibility of courts depends on independence, consistent procedures, and visible results, and communities have sometimes expressed concern about delay, limited legal assistance, or the influence of politics. These practical realities are a significant part of the history, alongside the doctrines that define jurisdiction.

Everyday Life and Social History

What a Tribal Court Does on an Ordinary Day

Most tribal court business does not concern the Supreme Court cases discussed in this chapter. It concerns domestic relations, protection orders, child custody and guardianship, probate, landlord and tenant matters, traffic and misdemeanor offenses, hunting and fishing regulations, contract and employment disputes, and licensing. For many community members, the tribal court is the government institution they are most likely to encounter, and its fairness, language, location, and cost determine whether they regard tribal government as accessible.

Jurisdictional rules affect daily life directly. A resident who calls tribal police after an assault by a non-Indian may find that the response depends on which government has authority and what resources it has. A family involved in a custody dispute may need to know whether the matter belongs in tribal, state, or federal court, and Chapter 131 returns to this subject. Defendants, including members, may have limited access to counsel in remote areas. Court clerks, probation officers, and lay advocates, often tribal members, translate between legal procedure and community expectations. These institutional realities show why the history of jurisdiction is also a history of how communities experienced justice.

Section 118.17

Tribal Police, Cross-Jurisdictional Enforcement, and United States v. Cooley

Policing exposes the gap between holding jurisdiction and having the means to exercise it. On many reservations, tribal police are the first responders, but their authority has been uncertain when they encounter non-Indians, nonmember Indians, or suspects whose offenses fall within federal or state jurisdiction. Some tribal police departments are staffed and funded through tribal government; others operate under contracts or compacts with the Bureau of Indian Affairs, and in some areas the Bureau's own officers provide service. A November 2026 GAO report on Bureau of Indian Affairs law-enforcement staffing indicates that staffing remains a subject of federal concern, and Chapter 130 develops the broader history of policing.

To bridge jurisdictional gaps, tribes, states, counties, and federal agencies have developed cooperative arrangements. Cross-deputization agreements authorize officers of one government to enforce the laws of another, so that a tribal officer might be deputized as a county deputy or federal officer, or the reverse. Mutual aid agreements, shared dispatch, and prosecution agreements allow communities to coordinate. These arrangements depend on trust, funding, training, and sometimes political negotiation, and they have been uneven. Disputes have arisen over arrest and detention authority, over the handling of evidence, and over whether state officers may enter reservations to pursue suspects.

In United States v. Cooley (2021), a Crow Tribe police officer on a public highway within the Crow Reservation in Montana encountered Joshua Cooley, a non-Indian, parked on the roadside in a truck. The officer observed signs of possible impairment and saw weapons, detained Cooley, and a search revealed methamphetamine. Cooley was prosecuted in federal court for drug and firearm offenses, and the lower federal courts suppressed the evidence, holding that the tribal officer lacked authority to detain and search a non-Indian. A unanimous Supreme Court reversed. Applying the second Montana exception, it held that a tribal police officer may temporarily detain and search a non-Indian traveling on a public right-of-way within a reservation when it is apparent that the non-Indian has violated or is violating state or federal law. The Court reasoned that without such authority a tribal officer might be unable to protect the community.

Cooley was a limited decision. It concerned the power to detain and search for the purpose of turning a suspect over to the appropriate authority, not tribal power to prosecute non-Indians, which remains governed by Oliphant and the VAWA provisions. It illustrates three separate concepts that students should distinguish: legal jurisdiction (which government's law applies and which court may hear the case), investigative authority (which officers may stop, search, and arrest), and prosecutorial capacity (which government has the resources and willingness to bring charges). A tribe may have one without the others, and public safety depends on all three. The case also shows that Montana, often read as restrictive, retains exceptions that support tribal authority in situations threatening the community's welfare.

Section 118.18

McGirt v. Oklahoma and the Persistence of Reservation Boundaries

The Muscogee (Creek) Nation was removed from the Southeast to Indian Territory in the 1830s, and treaties in 1832, 1833, 1856, and 1866 defined and guaranteed its new lands. In the late nineteenth century, allotment and the Curtis Act ended the Nation's courts and prepared the way for Oklahoma statehood in 1907. For decades, Oklahoma exercised criminal jurisdiction over large parts of eastern Oklahoma on the assumption that the reservations had been disestablished. In McGirt v. Oklahoma (2020), Jimcy McGirt, a citizen of the Seminole Nation, challenged a state conviction for sexual offenses against a child on the ground that the crimes occurred on the Creek Reservation and, as an Indian defendant, he was subject to federal rather than state prosecution under the Major Crimes Act.

By a 5–4 vote, in an opinion by Justice Neil Gorsuch, the Court agreed. It reasoned that Congress had established a reservation for the Creek Nation by treaty, that only Congress can disestablish a reservation, and that Oklahoma had not shown that Congress had done so, despite the many statutes that had reduced tribal government and opened land to non-Indian ownership. The land therefore remained Indian country for purposes of the Major Crimes Act. Chief Justice John Roberts, dissenting, argued that the historical record and the long practice of state jurisdiction supported the view that the reservation had been disestablished and warned of significant disruption. The Court had also considered a related case, Sharp v. Murphy, and the Tenth Circuit's earlier decision.

The decision's significance is considerable but should be stated precisely. It affirmed the persistence of treaty-defined reservations and the principle that Congress must clearly express an intent to disestablish. It meant that, for crimes by Indians within the Creek Reservation covered by the Major Crimes Act, the federal government, and, for offenses under their own law, the Muscogee (Creek) Nation, held jurisdiction rather than Oklahoma. Oklahoma courts later applied similar reasoning to the Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw, and Seminole reservations, and Oklahoma's highest criminal court held in 2021 that the decision would not apply retroactively to final convictions. The decision did not transfer private lands to the Muscogee Nation. Most parcels within the reservation remain privately owned, and the status of a reservation is distinct from ownership of individual parcels. It also did not resolve all civil or regulatory questions, which depend on other doctrines.

The institutional effects were substantial. The Muscogee (Creek) Nation and other Oklahoma nations expanded their courts, prosecutors, police, and cooperative agreements with Oklahoma and local governments to handle increased responsibility. Federal prosecutors in Oklahoma faced greater caseloads for Indian defendants, and state officials expressed concern about the capacity of the federal system and about the status of earlier convictions. Tribal officials emphasized the treaties and the nation's capacity to govern. Because the subject is recent, historians must treat evaluations of its consequences as provisional. The decision's main historical lesson is that treaty promises and reservation boundaries retained legal force a century after statehood, a point that connects to the chapters on land and sacred landscapes (Chapter 119).

Section 118.19

Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta and the Continuing Contest over State Authority

In Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta (2022), Victor Manuel Castro-Huerta, a non-Indian, was convicted in an Oklahoma state court of child neglect involving his stepdaughter, who was a Cherokee Nation citizen, in a location within the Cherokee Reservation. After McGirt, he argued that Oklahoma lacked authority to prosecute him. The Supreme Court, by a 5–4 vote in an opinion by Justice Brett Kavanaugh, held that the federal government and a state have concurrent jurisdiction to prosecute crimes committed by non-Indians against Indians in Indian country. The majority reasoned that Indian country is part of a state's territory, that states possess jurisdiction there unless it is preempted by federal law or would unlawfully infringe on tribal self-government, and that neither the General Crimes Act nor Public Law 280 had preempted state authority in this setting.

Justice Gorsuch, joined by Justices Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan, dissented. The dissent argued that the decision departed from the principle established in Worcester that states lack authority in Indian country unless Congress provides otherwise, that the majority relied on a misreading of precedent, and that the decision undermined treaty promises of tribal self-government. The disagreement was thus not only about statutory interpretation but about how to read the history of federal Indian law: whether state jurisdiction is the default subject to preemption, or tribal and federal authority is the baseline unless Congress has conferred state power.

It is important to avoid describing the decision as simply reversing McGirt. The two cases addressed different questions. McGirt concerned whether the Creek Reservation continued to exist for purposes of the Major Crimes Act, affecting prosecutions of Indian defendants. Castro-Huerta addressed whether a state could prosecute a non-Indian defendant for a crime against an Indian in Indian country, a question that does not depend on whether a reservation was disestablished. Reservation boundaries remained intact, and the decision did not alter tribal jurisdiction. It did, however, reduce the practical significance of McGirt for cases involving non-Indian defendants, since Oklahoma could prosecute them concurrently with federal authorities.

Implications for law-enforcement cooperation have been debated. Supporters of the result argued that it prevented gaps in prosecution, since federal resources were limited and the state could ensure that offenders were prosecuted. Tribal governments and critics argued that it intruded on self-government, bypassed tribal consent, and would complicate cooperative arrangements by removing the leverage tribes had in negotiating them. The decision also did not address tribal jurisdiction over non-Indians, leaving the VAWA provisions as the principal route for tribal prosecution. Together with McGirt and Cooley, it shows that recent Supreme Court decisions do not point in a single direction: one reaffirmed a reservation, one supported tribal police authority, and one expanded state authority.

Section 118.20

The Historical Legacy of Tribal Courts and the Modern Sovereignty Doctrine

The history traced in this chapter runs from Indigenous legal orders through nineteenth-century federal intervention, the creation and transformation of courts, the doctrinal restrictions of the 1970s and 1980s, congressional reforms from 1990 to 2022, and recent decisions about reservation boundaries and state authority. Several accomplishments stand out. Indigenous nations rebuilt judicial institutions after periods in which federal administrators suppressed or replaced them. They developed written laws, appellate systems, and bodies of precedent, and in some cases integrated customary law into modern jurisprudence. They sustained tribal authority over membership, family, land use, and conduct, and they used the legislative process to secure recognition of additional powers. Courts such as those of the Navajo Nation, the Cherokee Nation, the Muscogee (Creek) Nation, and many smaller nations function as governments, not as ceremonial survivals.

The constraints are also clear. Jurisdiction over nonmembers is limited in civil matters by Montana and its progeny and in criminal matters by Oliphant, with narrow statutory exceptions. State authority has expanded in some contexts through Public Law 280 and Castro-Huerta. Law-enforcement resources, detention capacity, and funding for courts remain insufficient in many places. Overlapping legal systems create confusion for residents and officials, and federal power to define the scope of tribal authority remains substantial, as both the Court's decisions and Congress's responses demonstrate.

Contemporary debates concern different nations in different circumstances. Public Law 280 is one example. In November 2026, the Government Accountability Office issued a report titled Tribal Justice: Congressional Action Needed to Support Tribal Sovereignty (GAO-27-108087). It reported that, as of October, roughly 65 percent of the 575 federally recognized tribes were located in the six mandatory Public Law 280 states. This percentage measures the location of tribal nations, not the share actually subject to state criminal jurisdiction under the statute: some reservations are excluded from its coverage, and some jurisdictions have been retroceded. GAO also described generally concurrent tribal–state criminal jurisdiction where the law applies and limitations on access to certain public-safety and justice funding. The report recommended that Congress consider changes allowing tribes to initiate retrocession and requiring their consent to state-proposed retrocession. This November investigation primarily assessed the lower 48 states; GAO had separately examined Alaska in February 2026 (GAO-26-107533), where land status and jurisdiction present distinct questions. These reports are recent institutional assessments, and their findings and recommendations should be read in light of their methods, which relied on research, federal data, and views from tribal, federal, and state officials. They should not be taken to describe every tribal nation, since experiences in California, Alaska, Minnesota, Wisconsin, Washington, and other places differ. This chapter's research cutoff is 2026, and later developments are beyond its scope.

Historical Debate

Does Federal Indian Law Adequately Protect Tribal Sovereignty?

The question: Does the constitutional, statutory, and judicial framework of federal Indian law adequately protect the sovereignty that tribal nations claim, or does it systematically limit it? Participants do not always use the word sovereignty in the same sense.

Interpretation A: the framework adequately balances competing authority. Defenders emphasize that the Constitution gives Congress authority over Indian affairs, that federal law has recognized tribal self-government in treaties, statutes, and cases such as Worcester, Wheeler, Williams v. Lee, and Cooley, and that Congress has expanded tribal powers when it chose to. They argue that limits on nonmember jurisdiction protect persons who lack a vote in tribal government and that the Court's decisions reflect legitimate concerns about federalism and due process.
Interpretation B: the framework repeatedly erodes inherent sovereignty. Critics, including many tribal governments and scholars, argue that doctrines such as implicit divestiture, plenary power, and the Montana rule lack firm basis in the Constitution or treaties, that they rest on assumptions about dependency and assimilation, and that they leave communities without effective authority over people who commit crimes in their territory. They point to Oliphant, Hicks, Plains Commerce, and Castro-Huerta as examples, and to the repeated need for congressional correction.
Interpretation C: protections for individuals and accountability must be weighed alongside sovereignty. Others emphasize civil liberties, the interests of nonmembers who live or work on reservations, and the accessibility of remedies, noting the tension seen in Martinez. Some argue that enhanced tribal jurisdiction should be accompanied by procedural protections, as Congress required in TLOA and VAWA, and some Indigenous voices, including critics of particular tribal governments, press for stronger individual remedies.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the same sources: treaty texts, nineteenth-century opinions, the legislative history of ICRA, TLOA, and VAWA, Supreme Court opinions and dissents, tribal court records, and federal reports on public safety.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation of history (what the framers of treaties and statutes assumed), the legal weight of precedent, the empirical consequences of jurisdictional limits, and what kinds of authority a government must have to be called sovereign.

Why it matters: How historians assess the framework affects whether recent reforms are seen as correcting a flawed structure or fine-tuning a workable one, and how the roles of Congress and the courts are understood.

Multiple Perspectives

Who Has Authority to Administer Justice in Indian Country?

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives that synthesize responsibilities and concerns documented in public sources. They are not quotations from any individual, and no group holds a single uniform view.

A tribal judge: Responsible for applying the nation's law, deciding disputes fairly, and maintaining legitimacy with the community, while working within limits on whom the court may sanction and with limited staff and funding.
A community member or crime victim: Concerned primarily with safety, timely response, and whether any government will act, and may care less about which sovereign prosecutes than about whether someone does.
A defendant, Indian or non-Indian: Concerned with fair procedure, access to counsel, the right to challenge jurisdiction, and, for non-Indians, being tried by a government in which they may not have a vote.
A federal prosecutor: Responsible for enumerated serious offenses over a large territory and limited investigative resources, who must decide which cases to bring and which to decline.
A state official: Responsible for public safety within the state's boundaries, concerned about gaps in prosecution and about costs and administrative burdens, and sometimes in conflict with tribal governments over authority.
A tribal government leader: Responsible for protecting self-government and treaty rights, seeking resources and recognition for tribal courts and police, and weighing the benefits and risks of cooperative agreements.

Historical note: These actors do not have equal power. Federal and state governments have greater coercive resources, while tribal governments have the closest relationship to the affected communities.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the history of tribal courts in the United States and the modern doctrine that defines their authority. Its central argument is that tribal courts demonstrate the continuing governmental authority of Indigenous nations, but that their jurisdiction developed through repeated conflicts among inherent tribal sovereignty, federal legislation, Supreme Court decisions, and state jurisdiction. The result is a historically constructed and contested distribution of authority.

The chapter began with Indigenous legal traditions, which were diverse, enforceable, and rooted in kinship, council, ceremony, and community responsibility. The Haudenosaunee, Diné, Pueblo peoples, Plains nations, Cherokee, and others had distinct ways of regulating conduct and resolving disputes. These systems were not legal voids awaiting replacement.

Early federal Indian law, especially Cherokee Nation v. Georgia and Worcester v. Georgia, described tribes as distinct political communities and "domestic dependent nations" and excluded state authority from their territories, while later doctrine added congressional plenary power. The distinction between the existence of tribal authority and the exercise that federal law permits became the foundation of the modern doctrine.

Ex parte Crow Dog (1883) held that federal territorial courts lacked jurisdiction over one Indian's killing of another. Congress answered with the Major Crimes Act of 1885, upheld in Kagama. The same decade saw federally administered Courts of Indian Offenses, which served assimilation policy and must be distinguished from courts governed by tribal nations.

The twentieth century brought institutional change. The Indian Reorganization Act encouraged tribal governments, termination-era Public Law 280 transferred jurisdiction to certain states, and the Navajo Nation's assumption of its own courts in 1959 became a major example of tribal control. The Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968 imposed statutory protections, and Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez preserved tribal authority over membership while leaving a continuing tension with individual rights.

In the late 1970s and after, the Supreme Court restricted tribal authority over non-Indians and, for a time, nonmember Indians. Oliphant concerned criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians, while Montana and later cases such as Strate, Hicks, and Plains Commerce concerned civil authority over nonmembers. The two doctrines are distinct and neither eliminated all tribal authority over nonmembers.

Congress responded with targeted reforms. The Duro fix, upheld in Lara, restored jurisdiction over nonmember Indians; the Tribal Law and Order Act of 2010 expanded sentencing authority; and VAWA in 2013 and 2022 recognized participating tribes' jurisdiction over specified crimes by non-Indians. These measures reflect a pattern in which restriction by courts was followed by selective restoration by Congress.

Sovereign immunity, adjudicative jurisdiction, and regulatory authority were shown to be separate questions. Cases such as Kiowa, Bay Mills, Lewis v. Clarke, and Coughlin define the limits of immunity without equating it with the absence of accountability. Peacemaking and restorative programs, notably among the Navajo, show that tribal courts also draw on Indigenous legal thought.

The recent decisions of Cooley, McGirt, and Castro-Huerta do not point in a single direction. Cooley supported tribal police authority to detain non-Indians; McGirt confirmed the continued existence of the Muscogee (Creek) Reservation for purposes of the Major Crimes Act; and Castro-Huerta recognized concurrent state jurisdiction over certain crimes by non-Indians against Indians. Castro-Huerta did not reverse McGirt.

The chapter's evidence base includes treaties, statutes, judicial opinions, tribal codes, and federal reports, each with its own perspective. Recent GAO reports on Alaska and Public Law 280 provide institutional context but do not describe all nations alike.

In conclusion, tribal sovereignty is historically persistent, but the scope of its judicial exercise has been repeatedly redefined through federal law, tribal action, and judicial interpretation. The history of tribal courts is therefore both a history of constraint and a history of Indigenous institutional resilience.

Phrase from Cherokee Nation v. Georgia describing Indian nations as distinct political communities subject to federal relations; used both to affirm and to limit tribal status.
Plenary power
The broad authority the Court has attributed to Congress over Indian affairs, a doctrine that has been used both to limit and to restore tribal powers.
Ex parte Crow Dog
1883 decision holding that federal territorial law did not reach one Indian's killing of another in Indian country.
Major Crimes Act
1885 statute giving the federal government jurisdiction over enumerated serious offenses by Indians in Indian country.
General Crimes Act
Statute extending federal criminal law to Indian country, subject to exceptions for Indian-on-Indian offenses and others.
Indian country
Statutory category (18 U.S.C. §1151) comprising reservations, dependent Indian communities, and qualifying allotments.
Allotment
Division of tribal land into individual parcels, which led to non-Indian fee ownership within many reservations.
Diminishment and disestablishment
Reduction or termination of a reservation's boundaries by Congress, which requires clear congressional intent.
Court of Indian Offenses
Federally administered court begun in the 1880s, distinct from courts established by tribal nations.
Indian Civil Rights Act
1968 statute imposing civil-rights protections on tribal governments, enforced in federal court principally through habeas corpus.
Customary law
Norms and practices of a community recognized as binding, which some tribal courts apply alongside written law.
Montana rule
General principle from Montana v. United States that tribal civil authority over nonmembers on non-Indian fee land is limited, subject to two exceptions.
Implicit divestiture
Doctrine that tribes lose certain inherent powers, such as criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians, because they are inconsistent with tribal dependent status.
Oliphant
1978 decision holding that tribes lack inherent criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians unless Congress authorizes it.
Duro fix
Congressional amendment of 1990–1991 recognizing inherent tribal criminal jurisdiction over all Indians, upheld in Lara.
Concurrent jurisdiction
Authority shared by two or more governments over the same conduct.
Public Law 280
1953 statute transferring criminal and some civil jurisdiction in Indian country to certain states.
Retrocession
Return to the federal government of jurisdiction previously assumed by a state.
Sovereign immunity
Doctrine protecting a government from suit absent waiver or congressional abrogation.
Adjudicative jurisdiction
A court's authority to hear and decide a dispute.
Tribal Law and Order Act
2010 statute expanding tribal sentencing authority, subject to procedural safeguards, and addressing federal responsibilities.
Special Tribal Criminal Jurisdiction
Authority of participating tribes, recognized by VAWA and codified at 25 U.S.C. §1304, over specified crimes by non-Indians.
Cross-deputization
Arrangement allowing officers of one government to enforce another government's laws.
Peacemaking
Community-based dispute resolution drawing on Indigenous traditions, notably in the Navajo Nation.
McGirt v. Oklahoma
2020 decision holding that the Muscogee (Creek) Reservation had not been disestablished for purposes of the Major Crimes Act.
Castro-Huerta
2022 decision recognizing concurrent federal and state jurisdiction over crimes by non-Indians against Indians in Indian country, absent preemption.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because courts are one of the clearest places where sovereignty becomes concrete. A claim to nationhood is tested when a government must decide a dispute, protect a community, and have its decision respected. The history of tribal courts shows Indigenous nations performing these functions under legal conditions that outside governments repeatedly reshaped.

It also reveals a larger process of continuing negotiation. Federal courts and Congress defined, restricted, and sometimes restored tribal authority, while Indigenous nations rebuilt institutions, used legislation strategically, and drew on their own legal traditions. Neither dependence nor independence adequately describes the outcome.

Later chapters build on this framework. Chapter 129 and Chapter 130 examine violence, policing, and criminal justice outcomes; Chapter 131 considers families; Chapter 119 and Chapter 120 consider land and water; Chapter 122 considers tribal economies; and Chapter 139 surveys contemporary governments. In each, questions of who may exercise authority, over whom, and where, recur.

The continuities are as important as the changes. Indigenous legal principles of restitution, relationship, and community responsibility persisted through periods of suppression and reappear in modern peacemaking and customary-law jurisprudence. The chapter therefore supports the textbook's larger argument that Indigenous nations are continuing political communities, whose histories and institutions are indispensable to understanding the continent's legal order.
