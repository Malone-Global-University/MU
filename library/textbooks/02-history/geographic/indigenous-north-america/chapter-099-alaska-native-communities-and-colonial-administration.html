Chapter Opening

On November 5, 1912, twelve Alaska Native men met in Juneau to establish an organization that would hold conventions, petition lawmakers, and bring civil-rights claims before American officials and courts. These founders came from Southeast communities whose peoples had practiced political leadership and collective decision-making long before the United States acquired Russian claims in 1867.

The founders of the Alaska Native Brotherhood were men from several Southeast Native communities, many educated in mission schools, and they chose a model of organization that mixed old and new. They organized local "camps," a central "Grand Camp," elected officers, and conventions governed by rules of procedure familiar from American fraternal societies. At the same time, they drew on a political world in which clans, houses, and kinship networks already organized authority, property, ceremony, and obligation. Within a few years, Native women formed a parallel body, the Alaska Native Sisterhood. Together these organizations would press campaigns over schooling, voting, fisheries, land, and public discrimination, culminating in 1945 in a territorial law against discrimination in public accommodations.

The contrast that frames this chapter is therefore between two systems of authority. One was a set of long-established Indigenous institutions, differing greatly among Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshian, Athabascan, Yup'ik, Iñupiat, Sugpiaq/Alutiiq, and Unangax̂ communities. The other was a set of American colonial institutions, introduced after 1867, that were often thin, fragmentary, and underfunded, yet capable of consequential intervention in land, schooling, commerce, and legal status. The history of Alaska Native communities under American rule is the history of how these two systems met, and of how Native people used, resisted, adapted, and sometimes reshaped the institutions imposed upon them.

The chapter's central argument is that American rule transformed the legal and economic environment of Alaska Native communities without eliminating Indigenous governance, cultural continuity, or political agency. Transformation was real and often harsh. Gold rushes, canneries, railroads, and non-Native towns altered access to land and water; federal and missionary schools separated children from families and discouraged Native languages; epidemics, most severely in 1918–1919, devastated communities; and legal categories such as "uncivilized" and "civilized" were used to deny rights. But the chapter also shows that Native people were never merely recipients of policy. They held and defended territories under their own law, worked as fishers, trappers, packers, herders, teachers, and lawyers, built churches and cooperatives, and created new political organizations that pursued rights through American law while remaining rooted in Indigenous communities.

The chapter proceeds in five divisions. Division I establishes Indigenous Alaska and explains how the transfer of Russian claims in 1867 produced American administration, culminating in the Territory of Alaska in 1912. Division II examines land, resources, and economic transformation. Division III turns to missions, schools, public health, and the survival of families and languages. Division IV traces the growth of organized rights advocacy, women's leadership, struggles over citizenship, and the first legal campaigns over land. Division V examines the reorganization era of the 1930s and the anti-discrimination campaign that ended this chapter's principal period in 1945.

The evidence is uneven, and students should read it with that in mind. Treaties, statutes, congressional reports, Bureau of Education and Indian Service records, missionary letters, census counts, and newspapers form a large documentary record, but nearly all of it was created by outsiders with their own purposes. Tlingit and other Native oral histories, clan-owned stories and songs, photographs, Native-authored petitions and newspapers, convention proceedings of the Brotherhood and Sisterhood, and later interviews preserve other perspectives. Archaeology and linguistics supplement the record, especially for periods and regions where documents are thin. The historian's task is to read these sources against one another.

Chapter boundaries matter in a book of this scale. The earlier Indigenous history of Alaska's regions is treated in Chapters 33 through 35, and Native Christianity more generally in Chapter 54. Boarding and residential schooling across the United States and Canada is the subject of Chapters 87 and 88, the Indian New Deal in the continental United States of Chapter 97, and First Nations politics in Canada of Chapter 98. Chapter 100 addresses Inuit North America across Alaska, Canada, and Greenland, Chapter 101 the Second World War, and Chapter 115 the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of 1971. This chapter concerns the period from 1867 to 1945 and the foundations that later chapters build upon.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the diversity of Alaska Native societies and homelands on the eve of American rule, distinguishing Southeast, interior, southcentral, southwestern, western, and northern Alaska.
  2. Explain what the Treaty of Cession of 1867 did and did not transfer, and why Indigenous consent and Indigenous land rights were left unresolved.
  3. Trace the development of American administration from military and customs rule through the Organic Act of 1884 to the Territory of Alaska in 1912.
  4. Analyze how the Organic Act, the Alaska Native Allotment Act of 1906, and related laws addressed Native land use without settling aboriginal title.
  5. Assess the effects of mining, transportation, salmon canneries, and the fur and reindeer economies on different Native regions.
  6. Evaluate the roles of missions and of Sheldon Jackson in establishing separate educational systems, and the consequences of the 1905 Nelson Act.
  7. Examine boarding schools, language suppression, and families' responses, including the Irene Jones school-integration case of 1929.
  8. Interpret the demographic and community effects of epidemic disease, especially in 1918–1919, without reducing Native history to decline.
  9. Explain the founding, structure, and campaigns of the Alaska Native Brotherhood and the Alaska Native Sisterhood, and the leadership of Native women.
  10. Distinguish formal citizenship from effective political equality, using the 1924 Indian Citizenship Act and Alaska's 1925 literacy requirement.
  11. Compare interpretations of the Alaska Reorganization Act of 1936 and of American land law as protection or dispossession.
  12. Contextualize the Anti-Discrimination Act of 1945 and the leadership of Elizabeth and Roy Peratrovich within longer Indigenous political traditions.

Historical Context

Alaska's Indigenous peoples had lived in the region for many thousands of years before any outsider recorded their presence, and their histories are treated in earlier chapters of this textbook (Chapters 33 through 35). By the eighteenth century they included peoples speaking languages from at least five distinct families, among them Tlingit and Eyak, Haida, Tsimshian, many Athabascan languages, Yup'ik and Inuit-Yupik languages, and Unangam Tunuu (Aleut), with the Alutiiq language closely related to Yup'ik. Tlingit, Haida, and Tsimshian societies of the Southeast belonged to the Northwest Coast cultural region; Athabascan peoples spread across the interior; Yup'ik and Iñupiat peoples occupied the western and northern coasts and rivers; and Unangax̂ and Sugpiaq/Alutiiq peoples lived on the Aleutian Islands, the Alaska Peninsula, and the Gulf of Alaska coasts.

Russian colonization began in the middle of the eighteenth century with fur-trading expeditions seeking sea otter pelts, and became institutionalized in 1799 with the chartering of the Russian-American Company. Its effects varied enormously. Among the Unangax̂ and Sugpiaq/Alutiiq peoples, Russian traders and later the Company compelled labor in the sea otter hunt, taking men far from their families and contributing to sharp population decline through violence, disease, and disruption. Russian Orthodox missionaries, arriving in the Kodiak region in 1794, established a church presence that took lasting root in many communities. In Southeast Alaska, by contrast, the Tlingit were never conquered. They contested Russian expansion, most dramatically at Sitka in 1802 and 1804, and the Russian settlement at Sitka (New Archangel) remained dependent on Tlingit trade, and its authority was limited to the ground it occupied and the ships it controlled. Large parts of the interior and the Arctic coast saw little direct Russian administration at all.

Russian colonial society also produced a distinctive social category, the "creoles," people of mixed Russian and Native descent who could hold positions in the Company and had defined legal standing. Smallpox epidemics, notably in 1835–1840, struck Tlingit and other communities hard, and demographic loss shaped the world the Americans entered. Thus the United States acquired claims not over an empty territory but over a patchwork of Indigenous nations whose relations with Russia ranged from forced labor to commercial exchange to practical independence.

Two features of American law in 1867 shaped everything that followed. First, the United States had a long tradition of treating Indigenous peoples through treaties, trade and intercourse laws, and the doctrine that Indigenous nations held a right of occupancy that only the federal government could extinguish. How far that tradition applied to Alaska was left uncertain. Second, the post-Civil War United States was beginning a broader federal reservation and assimilation policy in the continental West (see Chapters 84 and 86), and the officials, missionaries, and reformers who turned their attention to Alaska brought those ideas with them. Alaska was thus acquired at a moment when federal Indian policy was moving toward assimilation, but without the treaties, agents, reservations, and administrative machinery that existed elsewhere.

Section 99.1

Indigenous Alaska Before and During the American Transfer

In 1867 Alaska was not a single cultural region. Its Native peoples organized themselves in ways suited to very different environments, and the American administrators who arrived after the transfer faced societies whose politics they only partly understood. In Southeast Alaska, Tlingit society was organized around two moieties, commonly called Raven and Eagle or Wolf, divided into matrilineal clans and then into houses (hít), which owned territory, fishing sites, crests, names, stories, and songs. Each clan house had leaders who managed these properties, and property and rank were validated through the potlatch, a ceremonial occasion at which hosts distributed wealth, honored the dead, and witnesses acknowledged claims. Tlingit communities were also grouped into regional kwaans. Haida and Tsimshian societies shared many features of ranked matrilineal organization, though they spoke unrelated languages. Slavery also existed in Northwest Coast societies, a subject treated in Chapter 53.

In the interior, Athabascan peoples lived in smaller, more mobile bands whose seasonal movement followed salmon runs, caribou herds, and other resources. Many were organized into matrilineal clans linked across communities, with authority exercised by respected leaders, elders, and in some regions by prominent hunters or trading chiefs. Along the Yukon, Tanana, Kuskokwim, and Copper Rivers, fish camps and trapping lines organized the year. In southcentral Alaska the Dena'ina, Ahtna, Eyak, and Sugpiaq/Alutiiq peoples lived at the junction of coast and interior, and these communities had long histories of exchange with neighbors.

In southwest and western Alaska, Yup'ik and Cup'ik villages commonly centered on the qasgiq, a communal men's house that served as a place for ceremony, instruction, and decision-making. Villages comprised extended families linked through kinship and shared resource use, with leadership drawn from successful hunters and elders. In the north, Iñupiat communities were organized around kinship, with whaling captains (umialit) who recruited crews, organized hunts, and redistributed the harvest. The Unangax̂ of the Aleutians and Pribilofs and the Sugpiaq of Kodiak and Prince William Sound lived in maritime economies; both had experienced decades of Russian colonial labor demands and Orthodox missionization, producing communities in which Orthodox practice and Native identity were closely interwoven.

American officials in 1867 knew little of these arrangements. The Russian presence had been strongest where Native labor mattered most, among the Unangax̂ and Sugpiaq peoples, and weakest where Native power remained intact. The transfer therefore meant different things in different places. In Southeast Alaska, Tlingit leaders had not been conquered, and many viewed the Russians as occupants of a trading post rather than owners of territory. In the Aleutians and on Kodiak, the departure of the Russian-American Company ended an exploitative labor regime but also removed institutions on which many communities had come to depend. In the vast interior and Arctic, the transfer was barely noticed for years, but its eventual consequences were no less far-reaching.

The diversity of governance should not be mistaken for an absence of political boundaries. In Southeast Alaska a claim to a salmon stream could belong to a particular clan, while neighboring groups maintained diplomatic and ceremonial relationships that made travel and exchange possible. In the interior, the seasonal movements of extended families followed social as well as ecological knowledge: camps were remembered, productive places could be subject to negotiated access, and hospitality imposed obligations. In western Alaska, customary expectations concerning the distribution of successful harvests linked household survival to cooperation. American officials sometimes characterized these arrangements as a lack of property or government because they did not resemble a courthouse registry or elected municipal council. Such descriptions tell historians at least as much about the assumptions of the observers as about the communities described.

Alaska was also a region of journeys and interconnections. River corridors joined the interior to Bering Sea trade; coastal routes joined communities around the Gulf of Alaska and southeastward toward the Pacific Northwest; crossings of Bering Strait connected families and trading partners on opposite shores. Marriage, language knowledge, ceremonial visits, and exchange moved goods and obligations across what later appeared on an American map as remote administrative districts. Neither imperial transfer nor the drawing of a boundary immediately ended these relationships. The geopolitical change mattered increasingly when officials regulated shipping, licenses, land entry, and schools, but Indigenous geography was never reducible to the new government's administrative map.

Different Russian colonial encounters produced different expectations of the Americans. Some communities had long negotiated with foreign vessels and traders and expected an additional commercial partner rather than a comprehensive governing authority. Other communities had endured coerced labor, epidemics, and the authority of the Russian-American Company, making a change in colonial regime consequential even without local consultation. Orthodox churches and Native-speaking clergy, trading obligations, and mixed-descent families did not disappear with the flag change. This uneven inheritance helps explain why later American policies met distinctive local forms of accommodation, resistance, and institutional continuity rather than a single, uniform Alaska Native response.

Using a physical map of Alaska without modern borders or boroughs, students should consider the following:

Section 99.2

The Alaska Purchase of 1867 and Indigenous Political Status

The Treaty of Cession was negotiated by Secretary of State William H. Seward and the Russian minister in Washington, Eduard de Stoeckl, and signed on March 30, 1867. The Senate approved it on April 9, and the United States agreed to pay $7.2 million. The formal transfer ceremony took place at Sitka on October 18, 1867. Russia's motives included financial strain, vulnerability of a distant colony to British power, and the declining profitability of the fur trade. American advocates argued for commercial and strategic benefits, including fisheries, trade with Asia, and a counterweight to British Columbia.

What the treaty transferred was the Russian state's claims to sovereignty over the territory, together with public property such as buildings, and, as the treaty described it, the territory free of private claims by the Russian-American Company, though not of the private property of individuals. The treaty asserted a transfer of Russian sovereignty claims, but whether and to what extent those claims affected Indigenous land interests was a continuing legal and political dispute: Russia had not purchased, conquered, or effectively administered much of the land used and governed by Native communities. Many Tlingit later insisted that Russia had held only the ground it occupied and had no title to sell. The legal doctrine that European or American powers acquired exclusive rights against other imperial powers while Indigenous rights of occupancy remained is the context in which the later land question must be understood. How Congress and courts treated those rights became the central dispute of Alaska Native legal history.

The treaty also sorted the people of Alaska into legal categories. Under Article III, inhabitants other than Native peoples, including Russians and creoles, could return to Russia within three years or remain and be admitted to the rights of United States citizens. The "uncivilized native tribes," in the treaty's phrase, were instead made subject to whatever laws and regulations the United States might adopt for "aboriginal tribes of that country." Native people were thus excluded from the treaty's guarantees on the basis of a classification that neither defined "civilized" nor consulted them. The distinction later supplied an argument that Natives who abandoned "tribal" ways could qualify for citizenship and public services, and it shaped legislation and school policy for decades.

A diplomatic instrument negotiated in Washington necessarily registered the perspectives of states rather than those of the Indigenous peoples whose homelands were at issue. American maps increasingly colored the entire region as United States territory, but the map did not demonstrate that clans, villages, or regional nations had transferred their own rights. Indigenous rights and imperial claims belonged to different legal and political languages. In American jurisprudence, the distinction between the sovereign power to deal with a territory and Indigenous occupancy rights created continuing disputes over whether, how, and with what compensation those rights might be extinguished. The precise legal consequences were contested; it would be misleading to state either that the purchase unquestionably erased Native title or that subsequent courts uniformly recognized an enforceable Native fee title.

The practical questions were immediate even before lawyers addressed them. Who could authorize a commercial fish trap at the mouth of a clan-owned stream? Could a miner occupy ground used for generations as a camp? Would an Indigenous leader's account of inherited territory receive the evidentiary status of a survey, patent, or deed? The treaty itself supplied no administrative mechanism for resolving such questions. That silence made later statutes, court decisions, and petitions especially important, because institutional arrangements adopted long after 1867 governed relationships that had existed long before it.

Article III's differentiation of inhabitants created a second enduring problem. The treaty described a route to citizenship for certain residents while excluding those placed in a colonial category of 'uncivilized native tribes.' It did not define membership in that category by a neutral test. Later officials attempted to distinguish people who allegedly lived a 'civilized' life from those who maintained Indigenous customs, turning cultural judgments into access rules for political and educational institutions. Native residents could seek the protections promised to American citizens without accepting the premise that their identity was evidence of incapacity. Much of the political history of the next eight decades grew from confronting this contradiction.

Section 99.3

Military Rule, Federal Neglect, and Early Colonial Administration

After 1867, Alaska had no civil government. Congress assigned the territory to the Army, which stationed garrisons at Sitka and a few other points, and to Treasury customs officials, who enforced customs, navigation, and trade laws. In 1877 the Army withdrew, and for several years customs collectors and the revenue service were the principal federal presence. From 1879 to 1884, the Navy took on a policing role from Sitka, with ships moving among Southeast communities. Congress did not extend a general system of civil or criminal courts, land offices, or local government. Residents, Native and non-Native, lived in a legal environment that was partly American, partly improvised, and partly shaped by the Indigenous law that continued to govern most Native communities.

This thin administration did not mean gentle administration. Where military and naval officers intervened, they often did so forcefully and in response to episodes of violence involving non-Natives. In January 1869 the U.S. Navy bombarded Kake villages in retaliation for the killing of two non-Native men, destroying houses, canoes, and winter provisions. In October 1882, naval vessels bombarded Angoon after a dispute arising from the death of a Tlingit shaman during a commercial whaling operation; the village was heavily damaged, and Tlingit accounts record the deaths of children. These events illustrate a recurring pattern: the American government did not provide ordinary courts or protection of Native property, but it used force to demand compensation or retaliate when non-Native interests were affected. Tlingit people treated such events within their own legal framework of compensation and balance between clans, and remembered them as acts of destruction by a power that claimed jurisdiction without offering justice.

Commercial interests moved into this administrative vacuum. The Alaska Commercial Company, formed in 1868, took over much of the Russian-American Company's trading network and secured the lucrative lease for harvesting fur seals on the Pribilof Islands, where Unangax̂ labor was central. The Hudson's Bay Company's post at Fort Yukon was found to be on American territory and closed in 1869, and American traders and steamboat operators moved up the Yukon. Missionaries arrived in the late 1870s, led by Presbyterians in Southeast Alaska. In the 1870s and 1880s, new canneries, mines, and trading posts began to appear. Native people interacted with all of them, as suppliers, laborers, guides, customers, and negotiators, and often on terms they themselves set.

Native legal status remained uncertain. Were Alaska's Natives "Indians" for purposes of federal law? Did the federal trade and intercourse laws apply? Were they citizens or subjects, wards or free persons? Congress, courts, and administrators gave inconsistent answers. This uncertainty allowed government and private interests to treat Native people as lacking the rights of citizens but also as lacking the protections of treaty-based tribes. Students should see how this contradiction, rather than a single policy, defined the era's character.

The lack of a regular territorial legislature also obscured responsibility. A Navy officer, a customs collector, and a missionary could each assert authority over particular interactions without supplying a stable system of remedies. Disputes involving ships, trading houses, fishing places, or intercommunity obligations did not necessarily fit American criminal-law categories. Indigenous dispute resolution could involve compensation and relationships among kin groups, whereas an American officer might identify a single individual to punish. When officers interpreted a demand for compensation as a threat, or treated collective responsibility as justification for destroying a village's provisions, conflict escalated across incompatible legal frameworks. The violent interventions at Kake and Angoon should therefore be analyzed in relation to the institutions that authorized them, not as isolated instances of personal misconduct.

Those episodes also expose the difficulties of documenting colonial violence. Official reports frequently describe an operation as a punitive expedition and emphasize the safety of naval personnel. Indigenous testimony and community memory more often preserve the destruction of homes, winter food, and objects carrying historical or ceremonial significance. These bodies of evidence need not agree on every numerical detail to establish the fact of coercion. Students should ask who counted losses, how survivors passed on accounts, and whether the institutions responsible for violence had incentives to minimize its consequences. An administrative record is an indispensable source but not a disinterested account of the exercise of force.

Commercial networks were not simply substitutes for an absent state. In some places companies assumed quasi-public functions by maintaining transport, extending credit, distributing imported goods, or shaping access to employment. But dependence was never absolute: Native traders could shift partners, use Indigenous routes, sustain subsistence production, and mobilize kin relationships across settlements. The resulting political economy created opportunities for individual families and new vulnerabilities when prices fell or a monopolistic operator controlled access to a market. These long-term arrangements explain why later petitions for legal protection frequently concerned both land and the regulation of commercial power.

Section 99.4

The Organic Act, Territorial Government, and Unequal Authority

In 1884 Congress passed the first Organic Act for Alaska, which created a civil government for what it called the District of Alaska. A governor, a judge, a district attorney, a marshal, and other officials, all appointed in Washington, were to administer the district from Sitka, applying the general laws of Oregon where federal law was silent. There was no elected legislature. The statute also included provisions on Native land use (examined in Section 99.5) and appropriated funds for the education of children "without reference to race," a phrase that promised more than later practice delivered.

Over the next three decades, gold rushes brought rapid growth in the non-Native population, and demands for fuller self-government increased. In 1900 Congress passed a second Organic Act and a criminal code and made Alaska's governmental structure more elaborate. The capital moved from Sitka to Juneau in 1906, and in the same year Alaska received a non-voting delegate in Congress. In 1912, the Second Organic Act created the Territory of Alaska, with a legislature consisting of an elected senate and house, an appointed governor, and a federal judiciary. Congress, however, reserved authority over key matters, including the disposition of public lands, fisheries, and Native affairs.

For Native residents, these changes meant little political voice. Voting and office-holding depended on citizenship, and Native people were commonly treated as not qualified unless they could show they had abandoned "tribal" ways. The territorial legislature was elected overwhelmingly by non-Native voters, and its priorities, such as roads, schools for non-Native children, and a favorable climate for business, often conflicted with Native interests. Native leaders thus faced a government whose institutions were growing in size but that treated Native communities as an administrative problem to be managed by the federal government rather than as political communities with rights of their own.

This point is important for interpreting the entire chapter. The growth of American institutions in Alaska is sometimes narrated as a story of increasing legal order and progress toward self-government. For Native communities, the pattern was different: territorial government expanded while the legal recognition of Native political authority remained underdeveloped, and the ideology of assimilation was used to withhold equal rights until Natives demonstrated "civilization." Native political organizing after 1912 was a response to precisely this combination.

The Organic Act illustrates the difference between a formal promise and an enforceable system of protection. A provision telling officials not to disturb occupants of land could appear protective on its face. Yet the statute postponed the rules for acquiring title, while federal public-land administrators, private claimants, and later forestry officials operated through written instruments of ownership unfamiliar to many residents. If a community's land was described through seasonal fishing, inherited access, or communal stewardship, officials could fail to recognize the continuity of its claim. The ambiguity was not simply a drafting problem; it distributed power toward institutions that controlled surveys, hearings, and official documentation.

Territorial development likewise needs to be explained as a sequence rather than one sudden transition in 1912. Federal law gradually created courts and local administrative powers, and the territorial regime later offered a legislative forum, though Congress retained ultimate authority. Alaska's elected delegate in Washington could speak and introduce measures without a vote on the floor of the House. Within Alaska, property, racial exclusion, and education-based qualifications affected Native participation. The existence of a legislature did not itself guarantee that a Native fishing family or interior village had meaningful representation in the laws governing its resources. Native petitions to legislators and Congress should be understood as political action undertaken amid unequal access, not evidence of political passivity.

The new governmental geography also diverged sharply from the geography of daily life. A district officer based in a coastal town might know little of an interior community several days' travel away; a missionary school could be a family's main point of contact with federal policy; a trader might provide more information about regulations than the authorities who enacted them. The capacity of government therefore varied by place and purpose. The federal state could be thin in health services yet intrusive in schooling or land designation, a combination that made the experience of colonial administration uneven and frequently contradictory.

Section 99.5

Indigenous Landholding and the Unresolved Question of Aboriginal Title

Alaska Native peoples had long-established systems of land and resource tenure. Among Tlingit, Haida, and Tsimshian peoples, clans and houses held salmon streams, berry grounds, hunting territories, intertidal areas, and village sites, and these claims were validated through ceremony, oral history, and crests such as those displayed on poles and in songs. In the interior, extended families and communities used defined fish camps, trapping areas, and hunting grounds, often with well-recognized priority rights. Yup'ik and Iñupiat communities recognized customary rights to hunting, fishing, and camp locations as well. These were not undefined "wilderness" uses; they were property regimes with their own rules and enforcement.

American law offered an uncertain translation of these arrangements. Section 8 of the Organic Act of 1884 declared that Indians and others in Alaska "shall not be disturbed" in possession of lands actually in use, occupation, or claimed by them, and it reserved the terms under which title could be acquired for future legislation. The provision recognized Native use and occupancy but left title unresolved. It did not create a procedure for confirming Native title, and Congress did not provide one for decades. Moreover, other public land laws allowed non-Native settlers and miners to locate claims, and administrators and courts often did not treat Native use as an obstacle.

Historical Vocabulary
Aboriginal Title

Definition. In United States law, a right of occupancy and use of land based on long possession by Indigenous people, held collectively, and, according to the doctrine articulated by the Supreme Court in the early nineteenth century, capable of being extinguished only by the federal government.

Historical context. In Alaska the doctrine was applied unevenly. Congress's 1884 promise not to disturb Native possession acknowledged occupancy but deferred the question of title. Later court decisions, including the 1955 decision in Tee-Hit-Ton Indians v. United States, held that aboriginal title in Alaska, though recognized as a right of occupancy, was not compensable under the Fifth Amendment when taken by Congress.

Why it matters. The legal status of Native land rights determined whether Native communities could seek compensation or protection when land was taken.

Potential misunderstanding. Aboriginal title is not the same as fee-simple ownership under American property law, and it is not the same as the Indigenous legal systems under which land was actually held. Federal recognition came late and incompletely; the rights already existed under Native law.

Historical Vocabulary
Allotment

Definition. The division of land, historically held in common, into individually owned parcels assigned to Native persons, usually under restrictions on sale or taxation.

Historical context. The Alaska Native Allotment Act of 1906 authorized the Secretary of the Interior to allot up to 160 acres of non-mineral land to eligible Native applicants who were heads of families or at least twenty-one years old, subject to the statutory qualifications and the administration of use-and-occupancy requirements. The policy descended from the continental allotment program discussed in Chapter 86 (the Dawes Act), but applied in a very different landscape.

Why it matters. Allotment recognized individual claims but did not address the collective rights of clans, villages, and regions. Applications were burdened by bureaucratic requirements, and relatively few allotments were completed in the early decades.

Potential misunderstanding. The act did not give Native people land they did not already use; it offered a way of receiving title to specific parcels, and a Native person could also fail to pursue an allotment because it conflicted with community-based use of land.

The Alaska Native Allotment Act of 1906 was the principal congressional response. It authorized the Secretary of the Interior to grant restricted title to parcels of up to 160 acres to eligible Alaska Native applicants who were heads of families or at least twenty-one years old, subject to the statute’s requirements and administrative procedures. The act created a path to individual title, but it translated collective, clan-based and village-based tenure into private parcels, imposed administrative requirements, and left much land unsecured. The Alaska Native Townsite Act of 1926 later allowed Natives to obtain restricted title to homesites in townsites. Meanwhile, the federal government reserved large areas as national forests (the Tongass and Chugach in 1907), and Native people whose territories fell in these areas continued to use them while the legal basis of their rights remained unsettled. The one significant exception was the Annette Island Reserve, set aside by Congress in 1891 for the Tsimshian community that had migrated from British Columbia in 1887 under the missionary William Duncan and established Metlakatla.

A central difficulty was that the vocabulary of American property administration did not match many Indigenous forms of tenure. A surveying officer might look for the uninterrupted cultivation or residential occupation of a bounded parcel. A coastal clan might identify its rights through recognized ancestors, crests, oral testimony, a network of harvesting sites, and the obligations owed to neighboring groups. An interior family might maintain regular use of fishing places and hunting territories while living seasonally in several locations. Neither arrangement was 'unused' merely because no permanent American-style house stood on every site. A legal standard requiring an applicant to prove a particular pattern of residence could therefore obscure the very system through which rights had been maintained.

The 1906 allotment statute offered one partial route to individual security, not a settlement of collective claims. It authorized the Secretary of the Interior, at discretion and under administrative rules, to allot up to 160 acres of nonmineral land to an eligible Alaska Native who was the head of a family or at least twenty-one years old. The distinction matters: a qualified adult did not necessarily have to be a household head. The terms also required attention to land availability, documentation, and the administrative process. Obtaining a patent could take time and money, and a grant for one household could not readily protect an entire salmon drainage, migration corridor, or community territory.

The larger unresolved question was whether Native occupancy would be protected when Congress assigned another purpose to the same ground. Forest administration, mineral claims, townsites, and federal reservations created overlapping jurisdictions whose written records appeared authoritative even where Indigenous tenure remained older and continuous. The limited reservation at Annette Island should not be treated as a model automatically available throughout Alaska: it arose from a specific congressional act involving a particular community. The history of Alaska land claims therefore turns on distinctions among possession, recognition, allotment, reservation, and compensation. Collapsing those terms would disguise the actual choices faced by Native communities.

Section 99.6

Mining, Settlement, Transportation, and Territorial Dispossession

The gold rushes brought the largest and fastest changes to Alaska's human geography. The Juneau discovery of 1880, in which Tlingit leader Kowee of the Auk people guided prospectors Joe Juneau and Richard Harris, led to the founding of a town on Auk and Taku Tlingit land and to the large Treadwell mines on nearby Douglas Island. The Klondike rush of 1896–1898 sent tens of thousands of prospectors through Dyea and Skagway on Tlingit territory. The Chilkoot and Chilkat Tlingit had long controlled important trade routes into the interior and, during the rush, many worked as packers and guides. But Skagway and the White Pass and Yukon Route railway, completed in 1900, bypassed the Chilkoot packing trade and brought new non-Native townsites into Native territory.

On the Seward Peninsula, the discovery of gold in 1898 led to the founding of Nome and to an influx of miners to Iñupiat country. The Fairbanks gold discovery of 1902 transformed the Tanana Valley, affecting Athabascan communities such as the people of Chena. Copper mining at Kennecott in the Wrangell Mountains, beginning in the early twentieth century, and the railway that supported it affected Ahtna lands. The federal government's own projects had similar consequences. The Alaska Railroad, authorized in 1914 and built in the following decade, ran through Dena'ina and Athabascan territory, and the railroad construction camp that became Anchorage in 1915 was located on Dena'ina land.

The consequences varied. Where Native communities lay close to commercial centers, they confronted loss of land and fishing sites, new diseases, alcohol, and discrimination, but also opportunities for wage work and trade. Where communities were remote, the effects arrived more indirectly, through traders, steamboats, and shifts in game and fish availability. Existing political relationships mattered: communities with strong clan or village leadership, such as many in Southeast Alaska, were able to negotiate, petition, and sometimes secure compensation or concessions, while smaller or more scattered communities had fewer means. The Native share of Alaska's population also changed. Native people were a majority at the time of the transfer, but by the early twentieth century, as the non-Native population rose, they made up well under half of the territory's total, which affected both their electoral weight and the political attention they received.

Timber and forest policy added another layer. The creation of the Alexander Archipelago Forest Reserve in 1902 and the Tongass National Forest in 1907 placed millions of acres of Tlingit and Haida territory under federal forest administration. Forest regulation, designed for national purposes, did not recognize Native tenure as such, though Native residents continued to use the land. Towns often developed a pattern of segregation, with Native villages located on the periphery or in separate sections, and with municipal services and public spaces unevenly available to Native residents.

Gold-rush geography shows how transportation changed the relationship between a seemingly distant government and Indigenous homes. Trails to the interior became regulated passageways for prospectors and freight; steamships and riverboats concentrated trade at particular stops; later railway construction created new demands for timber, gravel, labor, and water access. The benefits and costs were unequally distributed. A person with local geographical knowledge could guide newcomers, provide food, or sell transportation services, while the establishment of a mine or port might permanently redirect access to productive land. Relationships that began as negotiation could shift once outside capital obtained a concession supported by territorial law.

The historical role of Indigenous intermediaries needs careful attention. Kowee's association with the Juneau gold discovery shows that prospecting did not occur in an empty landscape without local knowledge. Nevertheless, describing a Native guide only as an assistant in a settler discovery story obscures the land relationships and political calculations that accompanied encounters with outsiders. Elsewhere, Native people acted as river pilots, packers, traders, fishers, construction workers, and organizers of supply. Some earned cash useful for obtaining equipment and necessities; others confronted coercive or highly unequal bargaining arrangements. These were economic decisions made within the constraints of an expanding colonial system.

Nor did every frontier experience follow the same timetable. Southeast Alaska experienced sustained competition over shorelines and canneries relatively early. Interior communities confronted varying impacts as river transport and mining expanded at different moments. Along the Arctic coast, distant federal regulations might matter less initially than commercial whaling or a trading station. To reconstruct displacement accurately, students should connect a specific enterprise to an identifiable route, resource, and community rather than assume a single Alaska-wide event. The cumulative pattern was the conversion of Indigenous space into a landscape of concessions, towns, routes, and federal administrative categories.

Section 99.7

Salmon Fisheries, Canneries, and the Transformation of Coastal Economies

Salmon were the basis of subsistence and trade for many Alaska Native peoples long before American rule, and the commercial salmon industry that developed after 1878 competed for the same fish. The first canneries were established in 1878, one at Klawock, on Prince of Wales Island, and one at Sitka. In the following decades, canneries multiplied across Southeast Alaska and, from 1884, in Bristol Bay. By the early 1890s corporate consolidation, including the formation of the Alaska Packers Association in 1893, concentrated much of the industry under companies based in San Francisco and Seattle, whose capital and ships supplied the canneries, and which brought in seasonal labor from outside Alaska.

Native people took part in the industry in many ways. Native fishers sold fish to canneries; Native women and men worked in cannery lines; families moved seasonally to cannery sites and combined wage work with their customary fishing, processing, and subsistence. In Southeast Alaska, Native fishers eventually owned their own boats and became important participants in the commercial fleet. But industrialization also brought conflict over access. Companies built fish traps and weirs that intercepted salmon before they reached the streams, and Native communities, which understood those streams as clan-owned and ancestrally managed, saw their fisheries depleted and their traditional rights ignored. Federal fisheries legislation, including the White Act of 1924, was intended to conserve salmon but did not recognize Native rights as such and was heavily influenced by industry. The ANB campaigned against fish traps and for Native fishers' interests.

Bristol Bay illustrates a different pattern. Yup'ik, Dena'ina, and Alutiiq people there lived next to some of the world's largest salmon runs, but regulations confined the commercial fishery to sail-powered boats until 1951, and the canneries relied heavily on outside labor, so that Native participation was more as seasonal workers and as fishers using sail craft than as owners of the industrial fleet. In several regions, Native families developed seasonal rounds that wove together subsistence fishing, wage work, and trapping.

The Tsimshian community at Metlakatla provides an important case of rights-holding. The Annette Island Reserve created by Congress in 1891 was extended by presidential proclamation in 1916 to include nearby waters, protecting the community's fishing. When a cannery company contested the exclusion of its fish trap, the Supreme Court in Alaska Pacific Fisheries v. United States (1918) held that the original 1891 congressional reservation included adjacent waters necessary to the community’s fishery; its judgment did not depend on upholding the separate 1916 presidential proclamation. The case shows that Native communities, with the right legal basis, could defend their resources, and also how rare such protections were elsewhere.

The distinction between a salmon run and a commercial catch is central to the dispute. A salmon-bearing river supported food preservation, winter provisioning, relationships among households, and the exercise of customary rights; a cannery converted large volumes of fish into a commodity whose value was realized through vessels and markets far from the river. Fish traps could intercept migrating salmon near shore, affecting the timing and availability of fish at customary sites. Native objections therefore included economic competition and claims about the authority to manage a resource that was embedded in community obligations. Conservation rhetoric sometimes masked disagreements about whose harvest would be limited and whose claim would receive official recognition.

Women and men experienced the industry differently, although tasks varied among places and companies. Some Native women prepared fish for sale, worked in processing, or managed households during seasonal employment; families retained knowledge of drying, smoking, and storing fish even when members entered cannery work. Cash income could finance boats or goods that strengthened subsistence operations rather than replacing them. Company stores, seasonal contracts, housing arrangements, and the availability of independent marketing all shaped whether commercial work increased community control or increased dependence. The record should not classify a person as either a 'traditional subsistence fisher' or a 'modern wage worker' when a single household could organize its year around both.

The Annette Island dispute illustrates how legal language altered commercial outcomes. Congress created the reserve in 1891; a 1916 presidential proclamation separately addressed nearby fishing waters. When the Supreme Court decided Alaska Pacific Fisheries v. United States in 1918, it construed the original congressional reservation as extending to the adjacent waters needed for the Metlakatla community's fishery. It did not base its decision on a judicial validation of the later proclamation. That distinction matters both for legal accuracy and for historical interpretation: the community's ability to exclude a commercial trap rested on the court's understanding of the purposes of the original reserve, a protection unavailable to many other Alaska Native fishing communities.

Section 99.8

Fur Trading, Reindeer Herding, Subsistence, and Regional Economic Adaptation

Fur was the oldest commercial link between Alaska Natives and the wider world, and it continued to shape economic life under American rule. After 1867 traders, many of them agents of the Alaska Commercial Company and its competitors, extended credit to trappers against furs to be delivered later, a system that supplied goods but could produce dependence and indebtedness. Athabascan and Yup'ik trappers hunted marten, mink, land otter, beaver, and fox, and the interior steamboat economy created demand for wood cutting, freighting, and fish for dog teams. The fur economy was volatile. Prices rose with fashion and fell with depression, and by the 1930s declining prices and overtrapping left many families economically vulnerable.

On the western and northern coasts, commercial whaling by American vessels in the second half of the nineteenth century reduced bowhead whales and introduced alcohol and disease, while also creating wage opportunities and trade. Iñupiat whaling captains adapted by working with shore stations and traders. When baleen prices collapsed early in the twentieth century, the commercial industry declined, and the communities turned to other resources, trapping, and later reindeer herding, while continuing subsistence whaling.

Reindeer herding was introduced as a deliberate federal project. Sheldon Jackson, concerned about food shortages among Native communities after commercial whaling depleted game, brought reindeer from Siberia beginning in 1892. In 1898 Sámi herders from Norway were hired to teach herding techniques. The program trained Native apprentices, and herds were loaned to mission stations and then to Native herders; by the 1920s and early 1930s, the herd had grown very large, with many Native owners. But non-Native entrepreneurs also acquired significant herds, and conflict developed between Native and non-Native ownership; the Reindeer Act of 1937 limited ownership of reindeer to Alaska Natives. Reindeer herding had mixed results: it provided income and meat for some communities, but disease, range limitations, and competition from caribou and market conditions limited its long-term success.

Across all regions, the key pattern was mixed economy. Families combined wage labor, trapping, trade, and cooperative stores with subsistence hunting, fishing, and gathering, adjusting the balance as opportunities changed. In Southeast Alaska, commercial fishing and cannery work were central. In the interior, the cycle of fish camp, trapline, and seasonal wage work defined the year. In western and northern Alaska, subsistence hunting remained foundational, supplemented by trade, reindeer, and wage work. Native leaders and entrepreneurs experimented with cooperative stores and community enterprises, and some adopted new systems while others resisted debt and restrictions. Such choices show that Native communities were economic decision-makers.

Trapping and trading relationships depended on prices determined elsewhere. An interior trapper might exchange valuable pelts for flour, firearms, tools, cloth, or transportation; a fall in the international fur market could sharply reduce the purchasing power of that work without changing the labor required to obtain a skin. Credit made some purchases possible before the winter catch was available, but it also made a family vulnerable when a poor season followed an advance. Merchants often recorded these relationships in account books. Historians must read such ledgers against oral histories and community knowledge, since they register debts and sales more readily than household sharing, unpaid labor, or subsistence harvests.

Federal reindeer policy should be analyzed as both environmental intervention and an educational project. Officials advocated importing and expanding herds partly because of food insecurity and disturbances to marine-mammal economies, while often assuming that an introduced pastoral system could remake Indigenous livelihoods. Alaska Native apprentices and herders acquired sophisticated practical experience, and some communities developed substantial ownership or management interests. Yet ownership arrangements, the use of missionary stations, and competing private herds generated conflict about who controlled animals and grazing areas. The policy did not replace the ecological knowledge embedded in caribou hunting or coastal subsistence, and its results varied sharply by locality and period.

Commercial whaling also reveals the limits of a simple story of technological change. Industrial demand for baleen and oil, the movement of crews between ships and shore stations, and the availability of imported goods affected local economies. But Indigenous whaling continued to be embedded in cooperative labor, captain-led crews, household preparation, and the distribution of food. Commercial and subsistence institutions could exist side by side while producing different obligations and ecological pressures. The broader analytical issue is not whether Alaska Native communities 'entered' a cash economy—they had exchanged valuable goods across regions for generations—but how the terms of participation and control over resources shifted under American rule.

Section 99.9

Missionary Expansion and Religious Transformation

Missionary institutions shaped Alaska Native life in ways that often overlapped with, but were distinct from, federal administration. The Russian Orthodox Church had been present since 1794, and its influence persisted after 1867. The church lost the support of the Russian state, and its episcopal center eventually shifted to San Francisco, but it continued to serve Unangax̂, Sugpiaq/Alutiiq, Yup'ik, Dena'ina, and Tlingit communities, with Native lay readers, church wardens, and clergy. Many communities came to see Orthodoxy as their own tradition, and it remains an important element of Native religious life in southwestern and southcentral Alaska. The use of Native languages in liturgy and instruction in many Orthodox communities contrasted with the English-language emphasis of federal schools.

Protestant and Catholic missions expanded rapidly after the Presbyterian mission at Fort Wrangell was established in 1877. Presbyterians were prominent in Southeast Alaska; Methodists, Baptists, Episcopalians, Moravians, Congregationalists, Friends (Quakers), Covenant, and Catholic Jesuits established stations elsewhere. Sheldon Jackson encouraged an informal arrangement of denominational "comity," dividing regions so that missions would not compete in the same area. Missionaries ran schools, clinics, and orphanages, and they often functioned as the only effective outside authority in a community, which gave them influence over everything from marriage to land use.

Native responses varied. Some individuals and families embraced Christianity and the education that accompanied it, seeing in it new opportunities, tools for dealing with outsiders, or a moral language compatible with Native values. Many Native Christians became leaders in their communities and later in organizations such as the ANB. Others resisted missionary demands, particularly where missionaries condemned potlatching, shamanic practice, and ceremonial life. In practice, many communities blended elements, maintaining clan obligations and memorial ceremonies, adapting them to a Christian context, or practicing them more discreetly. Yup'ik winter ceremonies, Tlingit memorial potlatches, and Athabascan funeral feasts continued in altered forms. Missionary records, the principal documentary source for much of this history, often exaggerate conversion and understate persistence, so historians must read them cautiously and complement them with Native testimony and ethnographic evidence.

The relationship between missions and government was close. Missionary societies supplied teachers, and the federal government, through Sheldon Jackson's system of contract support, funded mission schools. This cooperation expanded schooling but raised problems, since public money supported denominational agendas and missionaries shaped policy. It also left Native people dealing with institutions that combined religious, educational, and administrative power.

Religion was neither an external force that communities simply accepted nor a cultural boundary dividing an unchanged Indigenous past from an imported present. The Orthodox Church's long presence in some coastal settlements was mediated by Native families, interpreters, and religious practitioners who preserved local languages and reshaped ceremonial life. Protestant missions arrived with their own denominations, priorities, and rivalries, often combining preaching with school instruction, medical assistance, and programs of work. Individuals could belong to an Indigenous kinship network, participate in Christian worship, and continue to defend customary rights. Historians should trace these overlapping commitments rather than sort communities into mutually exclusive categories of conversion and resistance.

The denominational allocation of fields of mission activity is particularly revealing. Under the informal arrangements associated with Sheldon Jackson's system, missionary organizations divided responsibility for different regions to avoid competing for students and funds. Such arrangements could appear efficient from a bureaucratic perspective while leaving local families few alternatives when they disagreed with the teacher or cleric whose institution was dominant nearby. Missions sometimes established literacy in a Native language and sometimes insisted that English alone constituted educational progress. The consequences depended on the denomination, the individual teacher, available funding, and the leverage local people could exercise.

Missionary accounts require special source criticism. Annual reports tended to emphasize conversions, school enrollment, and conduct that donors would recognize as improvement. They might understate the independence of Native decision-making, describe customary ceremonies in derogatory terms, or overlook the practical reasons families sought instruction. Conversely, such records can preserve names and details absent from territorial administrative sources. Reading them alongside church records, Native-authored texts, and oral histories helps recover the diversity of religious choices. The central question is how communities made use of, negotiated with, and sometimes rejected institutions whose religious and governmental roles were intertwined.

Section 99.10

Federal Education, Sheldon Jackson, and the Segregated School System

Sheldon Jackson, a Presbyterian minister who had traveled widely in the West, helped found a mission school at Sitka in 1878 that later became the Sheldon Jackson School. In 1885, he was appointed general agent of education in Alaska under the federal Bureau of Education, a post he held until 1908. Jackson used federal funds, church resources, and his own network of supporters to build a school system. He encouraged missions to open schools, supplemented them with public funds, and promoted an educational program emphasizing English, Christian instruction, industrial and domestic training, and the replacement of what he regarded as "heathen" customs. His other major initiative, the introduction of reindeer, reflected the same blend of paternalism, philanthropy, and economic planning.

The 1884 Organic Act had provided for education of children "without reference to race," but in practice the system divided. The Nelson Act of 1905 provided for schools outside incorporated towns, funded by license fees, for white children and for "children of mixed blood who lead a civilized life," while Native schools remained under the Bureau of Education. The statute and its implementation thus established two systems: one for non-Native children and Native children judged "civilized" by officials, and another for most Native children, typically less well funded, with fewer facilities and a curriculum emphasizing vocational training. The criterion of "civilized life" invited arbitrary decisions by local officials, and it tied school access to cultural conformity.

The result was a system in which Native families had to decide whether to accept the segregated schools or seek admission to schools for non-Native children, and in the latter case to prove that they had abandoned "tribal" life. Native parents objected both to the inferior quality of many Native schools and to the stigma of the classification. Funding disputes, controversies about who should operate schools, and the 1931 transfer of administration of Native education from the Bureau of Education to the Office of Indian Affairs further complicated matters. The ANB and ANS later made integration of schools a central political goal.

The evolution of Alaska schooling cannot be reconstructed simply by counting classrooms. Authorities debated who should pay for schools, who should appoint teachers, which languages should be used, and which children could attend institutions associated with settler towns. The distinction between a federally funded Native school and a territorial or locally supported school also reflected a claim about the social position of the pupil. A family could reasonably want English instruction or a wider curriculum while refusing the implication that a child had to abandon Native relatives or ceremonies to qualify for it. Such disputes made school admission an important arena of citizenship politics before the great civil rights campaigns of the 1940s.

The phrase 'mixed blood who lead a civilized life' in the Nelson Act placed extraordinary discretion in the hands of officials. It did not supply a consistent educational measure; rather, it linked racial classification to outsiders' judgments about culture and conduct. Decisions could vary among districts, and families confronted a system in which the same child might be considered eligible by one official and excluded by another. The measure also encouraged the mistaken assumption that cultural identity determined intellectual ability or readiness to learn. Modern analysis should quote the discriminatory statutory language only where it is necessary to show how public authority worked, not reproduce it as a neutral description of the people affected.

Administration changed again when Native education passed from the Bureau of Education to the Office of Indian Affairs in 1931. That transfer altered institutional supervision but did not automatically end separation, underfunding, or the dominance of vocational expectations. The schools' practical capacities depended on transport, buildings, teacher continuity, health conditions, and seasonal family movement. A remote village might have a school only intermittently, whereas a territorial town offered facilities that Native children could not freely enter. These differences explain why education became at once a means of acquiring useful skills and one of the most visible demonstrations of unequal colonial government.

Section 99.11

Boarding Schools, Language Suppression, and Educational Resistance

Boarding schools and orphanages formed part of the system in Alaska, as they did elsewhere in the United States and Canada (Chapters 87 and 88). The Sitka Industrial Training School and mission boarding schools such as the Jesuit mission at Holy Cross on the Yukon, the Moravian mission at Bethel, and Methodist and other homes took Native children away from their families for months or years. Some Alaska Native children were sent to boarding schools outside the territory, in places such as Oregon. In 1932, the federal government opened the Wrangell Institute, a boarding school for Native students, and the number of federal boarding facilities expanded after the transfer of Native education to the Indian Service. The schools emphasized English, Christian instruction, and vocational skills such as carpentry for boys and domestic work for girls, and many forbade or discouraged the use of Native languages.

The consequences were mixed and sometimes sharply divergent. Many children suffered homesickness, illness, harsh discipline, and cultural dislocation, and family separation weakened language transmission and customary education. At the same time, schools provided literacy, skills, and connections that some Native students used to become teachers, lawyers, ministers, and political leaders. Not all churches applied the same approach: Orthodox and some Moravian missions used Native languages for religious and literacy purposes, and in some regions Yup'ik literacy developed alongside English. Historians therefore resist treating the school system as uniformly one thing; the same institutions could damage community life and also produce the educated leadership that later challenged discrimination. Native parents and elders sometimes sought schooling for their children as a means of dealing with outsiders, while also trying to retain language and values at home.

In Ketchikan, Alaska Native student Irene Jones challenged her exclusion from the public school. On November 29, 1929, Judge Justin W. Harding ruled that the school board had discriminated against her and directed that she be admitted, with attorney’s fees assessed against the board. The judgment offered a remedy to the student and was an important example of Native legal resistance, but it did not end Alaska’s wider dual-school structure. The contemporary records are accessible through the National Archives education collection.

The effects of institutional schooling must be separated by generation, region, and individual experience. Some pupils encountered separation from relatives, restrictions on customary language, unfamiliar diets, dangerous illness, or degrading discipline. Others formed lasting friendships, gained access to English reading and written advocacy, or found teachers who respected Native languages. These experiences could coexist within one institution. It is possible to recognize educational opportunity without treating the damage of compulsory or coercive assimilation as an acceptable price. Detailed accounts should distinguish policies officially required by a school from what a student or family later remembered, and should not infer that every pupil experienced identical treatment.

Parents' decisions were often made under severe constraint. A family might seek schooling because government rules increasingly required English literacy for public participation or because employment prospects changed. At the same time, that family could have little authority over school governance, its child's living conditions, or the language permitted in class. Villages and extended families helped transmit knowledge during breaks and after students returned. Oral histories and family recollections can reveal these efforts, while institutional registers chiefly record enrollments, attendance, and official evaluations. Neither genre of evidence alone captures the whole history.

Irene Jones's case exposes how formal equality could be blocked at the schoolhouse door. In Jones v. Ellis, the federal district court ruled on November 29, 1929 that the Ketchikan school board had discriminated against her; Judge Justin W. Harding ordered that Irene be admitted to the public school and that the board pay attorney's fees. The decision matters because a Native family and its supporters used litigation to challenge a concrete exclusion. Its scope must also be stated precisely: a judicial remedy in an individual case did not abolish the separate Native educational system across Alaska. The broader conflict over who controlled education continued beyond the judgment.

Section 99.12

Public Health, Epidemics, Families, and Cultural Continuity

Federal medical provision for Alaska Natives was limited. Doctors and nurses were few, distances were great, and health services were often tied to schools and missions. Epidemic diseases such as measles, whooping cough, influenza, and tuberculosis repeatedly struck communities. Census counts suggest that the Native population reached a low point in the early twentieth century, though early counts are imperfect and should be used cautiously. Colonization, new diseases, malnutrition, crowded housing, and disrupted subsistence together produced high mortality, and tuberculosis remained a major cause of death for decades.

The influenza pandemic of 1918–1919 was among the most catastrophic events of this period. The disease reached Alaska in the autumn of 1918, and though some areas of the territory imposed quarantines and escaped lightly, the epidemic devastated many Native communities, especially in western and southwestern Alaska. Estimates of Native deaths are uncertain, but many sources place them at roughly two thousand, with some villages losing a majority of their populations. At the Seward Peninsula community of Brevig Mission, for example, most of the residents died within days. Children left without parents were taken in by relatives or placed in orphanages; the Jesse Lee Home, a Methodist institution, later relocated to Seward in the 1920s, and other missions also cared for orphans.

Yet the history of these communities cannot be told as simple decline. Survivors reconstituted households, combined villages, adopted orphans, and rebuilt leadership. Women played a central role as caregivers, midwives, and transmitters of language and knowledge. Elders, aunts, and grandmothers maintained stories, songs, and naming practices. Local healers continued to be consulted alongside missionary doctors. Communities also used new institutions, including churches, cooperatives, and later the ANB and ANS, to organize assistance and press for better health care. Demographic trauma was real, and it shaped everything that followed, but Native communities survived and reorganized themselves.

Public-health catastrophe was not simply an environmental accident. The severity of epidemics depended in part on transport, available caregivers, access to food and medical supplies, housing, and the speed with which an outbreak moved among connected settlements. Influenza in 1918–1919 struck communities already affected by preceding epidemics and by constraints on the delivery of medical care. When adults who prepared food, maintained boats, or cared for children became ill or died, the damage continued after infection subsided. Loss of skilled hunters, teachers, midwives, and elders could interrupt both material support and the intergenerational transmission of knowledge. Such consequences differed among villages; a single territorial mortality figure cannot substitute for local reconstruction.

Government reports frequently describe death totals and the distribution of relief, whereas community histories may emphasize burial responsibilities, orphaned children, disrupted households, and the long recovery of social networks. Historical comparison must identify what population and period each figure represents. An estimate for western coastal communities cannot be presented as a territory-wide death rate; numbers of confirmed deaths cannot automatically be equated with excess mortality. Where record keeping was sparse or inconsistent, authors should state the uncertainty rather than supply a precise number to suggest certainty. Students benefit from seeing both the evidence and its limits.

Survival depended on community institutions that remained important despite colonial intrusion. Relatives absorbed children into households, families exchanged food and assistance, and local leaders appealed for medical support or organized care. Religious networks, traders, and government agencies sometimes provided assistance, but their reach and reliability varied. The experiences of a Southeast coastal town, an interior river village, and a western Alaska settlement should not be treated as identical. Rebuilding meant preserving relationships and practical knowledge while responding to losses that could affect a community for decades. This history belongs to the chapter's account of continuing Indigenous agency as surely as the better-documented petitions and legislative campaigns.

Everyday Life and Social History

Four Regional Lives, c. 1900–1930

Southeast Alaska (Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshian). Households combined seasonal fishing, wage work in canneries and sawmills, gardening, and subsistence harvests. Clan obligations remained important in marriage, mourning, and property, even as families attended church and sent children to school. Native villages were often separate neighborhoods in or near towns, and public spaces such as theaters and restaurants were frequently segregated.

The interior (Athabascan). The annual cycle moved among fish camps in summer, hunting in fall, trapping lines in winter, and spring camps. Women processed fish and furs and sewed clothing; men hunted and trapped; children learned both subsistence skills and, increasingly, school lessons. Steamboat wood cutting and roadhouse work supplemented family income.

Southwest and western Alaska (Yup'ik and Cup'ik). Villages relied on salmon, seals, and other resources, and household work was organized by gender and age. The communal qasgiq and the household were both places of teaching, while mission churches and schools introduced new routines. The 1918–1919 epidemic reshaped many villages.

Northern Alaska (Iñupiat). Whaling, sealing, caribou hunting, and trade structured the year. Whaling captains and their wives (who played ritual and organizational roles) led crews, and the community shared the harvest. Reindeer herding and cooperative stores were additional parts of the economy.

Why it matters. These sketches, drawn from missionary reports, ethnographic notes, and later oral histories, show that daily life varied sharply across regions, that women and children were essential economic participants, and that families adapted new institutions into existing patterns of work and obligation.

Section 99.13

The Alaska Native Brotherhood and the Emergence of Organized Rights Advocacy

The Alaska Native Brotherhood began at a meeting in Juneau on November 5, 1912, attended by twelve Native men from Southeast communities, including Peter Simpson, a Tsimshian organizer associated with Metlakatla. William Paul Sr. became a prominent Brotherhood lawyer and political leader later, but was not an original charter member. Many of the founders were Presbyterian mission-school graduates, and some had experienced discrimination directly. Their organization drew on the fraternal society model, with local "camps," a Grand Camp, annual conventions, elected officers, and written constitutions. These forms were familiar to American society and gave the organization legitimacy in dealing with officials. But the Brotherhood also reflected Indigenous political traditions of consultation, clan and village leadership, and collective responsibility, and it brought together Tlingit, Haida, and Tsimshian people in a regional organization that crossed older kinship and village boundaries.

The Brotherhood's early program emphasized citizenship, education, English, civil rights, and the end of discrimination. In its early years some leaders discouraged practices that missionaries condemned, including aspects of potlatching, and emphasized assimilation as a path to equal rights. Historians debate how far this reflected the founders' convictions, the influence of mission teaching, or a strategic judgment that equal rights would be easier to win by demonstrating "civilization." In later decades, many ANB leaders defended Native art and ceremony, and the organization became a vehicle of cultural pride as well as civil rights advocacy.

The Brotherhood used a range of methods. It petitioned legislatures and federal officials, hired and trained lawyers, organized voter registration, brought test cases, and published newspapers, including a paper for Native fishers in the 1920s. It supported Native fishers against fish traps and cannery interests, and it also functioned as an economic and mutual-aid organization. The use of lawyers, among them William Paul, who was admitted to the bar in 1920 and elected to the territorial House in 1924 (see Section 99.15), represented a new stage in Native political practice: Native people used American law against the discriminatory application of American law. The Brotherhood also helped organize campaigns for school integration, public accommodations, and later land claims. Over time it expanded beyond its Southeast Alaska origins, though its strongest base remained Tlingit, Haida, and Tsimshian communities.

The location and membership of the Brotherhood's founding matter because they reveal how a regional Indigenous network became an organized political institution. On November 5, 1912, twelve Native men met in Juneau to establish the Alaska Native Brotherhood; Peter Simpson was among the founders. Organizing subsequently spread through camps in Southeast communities, including Sitka. William Paul Sr. entered the movement as an influential leader in later years, but he should not be listed as an original charter member. This distinction prevents the prominence of a later attorney from obscuring the collective action and earlier generation of organizers that created the institution in the first place.

The organization used the conventions of a fraternal society for purposes that were far from merely social. A camp provided a local forum for dues, mutual assistance, and discussion; conventions helped communities coordinate across long stretches of shoreline where travel could be costly and seasonal. Petitions and resolutions translated local grievances into claims that legislatures and federal officials could receive. The choice of English-language legal advocacy reflected the political rules imposed by colonial institutions, but the ability to cooperate across clans and villages also depended on existing Native practices of alliance and responsibility. The Brotherhood was therefore both an adaptation to American political forms and an expression of Indigenous collective organization.

Early Brotherhood rhetoric sometimes adopted assimilationist language that later generations found troubling. A source-conscious history should neither conceal that language nor assume it represents every founder's deepest beliefs. Some members had been taught in mission schools that political equality required visible conformity to American norms; others saw such language as a strategic response to laws that classified people by 'civilization.' As the organization increasingly challenged voting restrictions, school segregation, fish traps, and land dispossession, its political agenda could exceed the assumptions encoded in its earliest charter. This development illustrates why an Indigenous organization must be studied through changing campaigns and membership, not judged solely through a single founding document.

Section 99.14

The Alaska Native Sisterhood and Women's Political Leadership

The Alaska Native Sisterhood grew out of women's organizing in Southeast Alaska in the mid-1910s. Sources differ on whether the organization dates from 1914 or 1915, and on where and how the earliest meetings took place. The difference partly reflects the distinction between initial organizing and subsequent formal organization, and this textbook does not assert a single founding date. What is clear is that Native women organized soon after the Brotherhood and quickly formed their own camps, adopted their own constitution, and held their own conventions. The Sisterhood was not an auxiliary of the Brotherhood; the two organizations met jointly in a Grand Camp convention but each had its own officers, priorities, and methods.

The women who built the Sisterhood came from societies in which women, especially in Tlingit, Haida, and Tsimshian matrilineal systems, held important roles in lineage, property, and ceremony. Clan membership passed through the mother, and senior women were respected authorities. The Sisterhood extended this tradition into new forms. Its work included raising funds for scholarships and community needs, supporting education and health, assisting families in distress, and pressing for civil rights. It organized community events, supported schools, and lobbied officials. Its members visited legislators and testified, and they helped coordinate the campaigns that culminated in the 1945 act (Section 99.19).

Native women's political leadership in this period deserves to be treated on its own terms. Women decided strategy, organized across communities, and built institutions that endured. The Sisterhood and Brotherhood developed complementary approaches. The Brotherhood often took the lead in legal and electoral campaigns, while the Sisterhood's community networks and social work provided organizational strength and sustained campaigns over time. Disagreements and differences of emphasis occurred as well. A history that treats women merely as supporters of male leaders misses both the Sisterhood's independent agenda and the role of women such as Elizabeth Peratrovich, who emerged from its ranks.

The Sisterhood's development should be placed in the context of Native women's established authority, but not explained as an automatic survival of an unchanging tradition. Women organized in the face of specifically twentieth-century problems: unequal schools, limits on public accommodations, expensive travel, and the demand for sustained political communication among towns. Through local camps, meetings, fundraising, and conventions, they created durable infrastructure. A convention decision needed people willing to carry messages home, collect contributions, help families, and coordinate the next campaign. These tasks were substantive political work even when territorial newspapers assigned public prominence to male speakers or lawyers.

The organization also challenged the convenient separation of 'social service' from 'politics.' Assistance to students could improve access to qualifications required by an unequal employment market. Raising funds could support travel to a hearing. Organizing families and local supporters helped an electoral or legislative campaign reach beyond one town. Women used association-building to translate experiences of exclusion in restaurants, schools, and commercial spaces into claims about the duties of territorial government. Their strategies can be traced through surviving minutes, correspondence, newspapers, photographs, and later interviews, with attention to whose names and decisions appear in each source.

The Sisterhood's history intersects with, but is not reducible to, Elizabeth Peratrovich's later leadership. Her 1945 intervention arose from decades of organizing by members who confronted discrimination before she held a prominent territorial role. There is also a methodological issue in the early-1910s founding date: accounts refer variously to initial meetings and formal conventions, which do not necessarily identify the same event. Rather than manufacture precision, distinguish the early formation of Sisterhood camps from later regional organization. In the narrative that follows, the Brotherhood and Sisterhood should appear as collaborating institutions whose membership and priorities changed over time, not as a single undifferentiated movement.

Section 99.15

American Citizenship, Voting Rights, and Territorial Exclusion

Whether Alaska Natives were citizens was an uncertain question in the early twentieth century. The 1867 treaty denied the status to "uncivilized native tribes," and subsequent practice made citizenship dependent on departing from tribal life. A 1904 federal district court decision in Alaska (In re Minook) held that a Native man with a non-Native father could be treated as a citizen, but the ruling did not settle the broader question. In 1915 the territorial legislature enacted a procedure under which Natives could be certified as citizens if they could show that they had abandoned "tribal customs" and adopted "civilized" life, often through testimony from non-Native residents. The process was demeaning and little used, and it encoded the assumption that Native identity was incompatible with citizenship.

Historical Vocabulary
Territorial Citizenship

Definition. The legal status and practical rights, including voting and office-holding, available to residents of a territory under combined federal and territorial law.

Historical context. In Alaska, federal citizenship (conferred on Native-born Indians in 1924) coexisted with territorial laws, including a 1925 requirement that voters be able to read English, that determined who could actually vote and participate.

Why it matters. The Alaska case shows that formal citizenship did not automatically produce political equality.

Potential misunderstanding. Territorial residents did not have the same federal political rights as residents of states; Alaska had only a non-voting delegate in Congress and was governed under laws in which Congress retained final authority.

The Indian Citizenship Act of 1924 declared all non-citizen Indians born in the United States to be citizens, a development treated for the continental United States in Chapter 93. In Alaska the act had real significance, though it did not end disputes about the practical exercise of citizenship. In 1925 the territorial legislature adopted a literacy requirement, and a related federal provision specified English reading and writing qualifications for territorial elections from March 3, 1927, subject to statutory exemptions. The requirements affected many Native adults whom government schools had not equipped with comparable English-language instruction. The Brotherhood and its allies worked to register voters, challenge discriminatory practice, and elect Native candidates. William Paul, who had been admitted to the bar in 1920, was elected to the territorial House in 1924, the first Alaska Native to serve in the legislature. Native voters and candidates continued to confront discrimination, and efforts to enforce equal access to the polls and jury service continued.

The history of citizenship in Alaska thus illustrates a contrast between legal form and practical exclusion. It also shows Native people using the language of citizenship in a politically strategic way: claiming the rights of citizens did not mean abandoning Native identity or Native claims. The Brotherhood's leaders held both ideas together, insisting on equal treatment in American law while defending Native land rights and cultural continuity. This dual claim became a durable feature of Alaska Native politics.

Alaska Native citizenship campaigns unfolded within overlapping legal orders. A person belonged to a Native family, clan, or village according to relationships not created by a federal statute; the United States meanwhile classified people through treaty provisions, naturalization doctrine, and the Indian Citizenship Act. The 1915 territorial law that offered a citizenship pathway conditioned on purported abandonment of tribal customs should be explained as a discriminatory law that Native leaders contested, not an objective test of integration. Citizenship could matter for public schooling, officeholding, and participation in elections, but it did not automatically resolve ownership of Native land or the right of a community to govern itself.

William Paul Sr.'s election to the Alaska Territorial House in 1924 made visible a new mode of Indigenous political advocacy. Legal training and public office enabled a Native leader to contest exclusions within the very institutions that had perpetuated them. Yet one elected representative did not erase the barriers faced by people in settlements far from polling sites or unable to meet a biased voting qualification. Literacy requirements appeared formally neutral when described as applying to voters generally, but their consequences were highly unequal in a territory where government education had segregated Native children and often failed to provide comparable instruction.

Precision about dates is necessary. The territorial legislature adopted a literacy requirement in 1925, while related federal legislation specified an operative rule from March 3, 1927 for territorial elections, with an exemption for certain earlier voters. Treating these as one undifferentiated measure would obscure the relationship between territorial action and congressional authority over federal territorial elections. The general point remains: formal federal citizenship in 1924 did not prevent subsequent rules from restricting electoral access. Native political organizers therefore addressed not only legal status but the administrative conditions through which citizenship could be exercised: registration, polling places, public information, and the willingness of officials to recognize a legitimate voter.

Section 99.16

Land Rights Advocacy and the Tlingit-Haida Jurisdictional Act of 1935

By the 1920s, Southeast Alaska Native leaders were pursuing the land question through legal and legislative channels. The creation of the Tongass National Forest, cannery and townsite expansion, and the continuing refusal to confirm Native title led the Brotherhood and its allies to seek a forum in which Native claims could be heard. Their strategy followed a path used elsewhere in the United States: because Native nations generally could not sue the United States without its consent, they asked Congress to pass a "jurisdictional act" authorizing a court to hear their claims. William Paul and other leaders worked for this goal, with support from Alaska's delegate in Congress.

Congress enacted the Tlingit-Haida Jurisdictional Act in 1935. It authorized the Tlingit and Haida Indians of Alaska to bring claims against the United States before the Court of Claims for the loss of lands and rights. The act gave a legal forum; it did not itself award compensation or return land. The litigation moved slowly. A Tlingit and Haida Central Council, representing the communities, was organized to pursue the claim. The case continued for decades, illustrating the difference between permission to bring suit and the obtaining of a remedy. Later litigation and twentieth-century land-claims settlement policy are treated in Chapter 115; for this chapter, the decisive development is the creation of a Native-led legal pathway in 1935, not a resolution of the title question.

The Tlingit-Haida land campaign developed in response to overlapping forms of dispossession rather than one single seizure. The Tongass National Forest contained territories already associated with particular clans and communities, while commercial operators and expanding municipalities claimed places significant for subsistence and collective identity. Native leaders argued that a transfer between Russia and the United States had not conveyed the land interests of those who occupied and governed these places. To obtain a judgment, however, they needed a court with jurisdiction to hear the case. This was an institutional obstacle as well as a legal argument about the merits of their claims.

Congress's June 19, 1935 jurisdictional legislation created that pathway. It permitted the Tlingit and Haida peoples to present claims involving land and other community property rights alleged to have been taken without compensation, including claims connected to the original purchase. The act was not itself a judicial finding that a particular acreage had been taken, nor did it transfer title to any parcel. Its importance lay in creating a forum where evidence about use, occupancy, governmental action, and valuation could be examined. The distinction between authority to sue, success on the merits, and delivery of a remedy is fundamental to understanding this chapter.

Litigation required the conversion of extensive Indigenous territorial knowledge into forms legible to American law. Claimants and advocates assembled accounts of settlements, customary harvesting territories, trails, fishing places, family histories, and historical dealings with imperial officials. Maps and archival documents could be used alongside oral testimony, but the latter had often been discounted by colonial administrators. The legal process therefore raised difficult questions about how an inherited place-based claim should be described in the language of compensation and property rights. Attorneys and political organizers had to balance the interests of multiple communities while confronting an evidentiary system designed by the state they were challenging.

The later progress of the lawsuit belongs principally to the subsequent land-claims chapter. Here the critical development is the appearance of a sustained Native-led regional claims strategy before the Second World War. It connected the long-standing defense of ancestral territory with associations, trained advocates, federal legislation, and organized efforts to produce documentary evidence. The campaign also shows why the 1884 promise to protect existing possession had not solved the issue: a community could continue to assert rights yet lack a practical legal method to obtain recognition or compensation when government acts conflicted with them.

Historical Debate

Did American Law Protect Alaska Native Land Rights or Institutionalize Dispossession?

The question: Between 1884 and 1945, did the statutes and legal procedures governing Alaska Native land primarily protect Native rights, or did they primarily create a framework in which dispossession could occur?

Interpretation A — Protective but incomplete: Some historians emphasize that Congress included explicit protections for Native possession in 1884, created the Annette Island Reserve, authorized allotments in 1906, and opened a litigation forum in 1935. They argue that these measures show recognition of Native rights and some capacity to defend them, and that the slow pace reflected administrative and political limits more than design.
Interpretation B — Institutionalized dispossession: Others stress that the promise of protection was never implemented through a title procedure, that allotment transformed collective tenure into small individual parcels, that forests, mines, canneries, and townsites expanded without compensation, and that the litigation route yielded remedies far smaller than the land and resources lost. On this view, law facilitated the transfer of resources while leaving Native claims formally unresolved.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on statutes, land-office records, court decisions, and Native petitions. They weigh differently the text of the laws, their administration, and their outcomes.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation of purpose versus effect, the significance of limited enforcement, and how to weigh the exceptional cases (such as Annette Island) against the general pattern.

Why it matters: How historians answer shapes how they understand the origins of the later land-claims movement and the continuing significance of unresolved title.

Student task: Distinguish the stated objectives of each law from its practical effects. Which evidence best supports each interpretation, and can both be partly right?

Section 99.17

The Indian New Deal and the Alaska Reorganization Act of 1936

The Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 (Chapter 97) had not applied fully to Alaska. In 1936 Congress extended selected provisions to the territory through the Alaska Reorganization Act. The statute allowed groups of Alaska Natives with a common bond of occupation, association, or residence in a defined community or district to organize, adopt constitutions and bylaws, and incorporate; it also made available federal loans through a revolving fund and authorized the Secretary of the Interior to designate reserves. The act reflected the reform currents of the New Deal, the lobbying of Native organizations, and the support of officials in the Interior Department. Ernest Gruening, who became territorial governor in 1939, also supported many Native rights initiatives.

Native communities used the act in different ways. Many villages adopted constitutions and established councils recognized by the federal government, creating bodies that could manage community affairs, administer funds, and interact with agencies. Some groups organized cooperative stores and enterprises with federal loans. A small number of reserves were established under the act, including Venetie, home of Gwich'in people, and Karluk on Kodiak Island. Hydaburg, a Haida community formed in 1911–1912 when Haida families from older villages relocated, was among the Southeast communities that organized under the Act, and its example is often cited for cooperative enterprises.

The relationship between the new councils and existing Native leadership was complex. In many communities, clan leaders, elders, and church officials already exercised authority; federally recognized councils had to fit into this landscape. Some councils drew on existing leaders; others created tension or ran alongside older institutions. Federal supervision, including the need for Interior Department approval of constitutions and the standardized model constitutions, shaped the forms the councils took. Organizations such as the Brotherhood and Sisterhood remained important political actors alongside the new councils.

The Alaska statute of 1936 did not simply replicate the Indian Reorganization Act on another map. Congress extended selected features, while permitting Alaska Native groups that had not previously been recognized as bands or tribes to organize where members shared occupation, association, or residence within a defined community or rural district. That formulation could give a village a practical route to a federally recognized council despite the absence of treaty-era institutional structures familiar in other parts of the United States. At the same time, the requirement of a legally described community or organization did not always correspond neatly to seasonal territories, extended kin networks, or the larger jurisdiction of an Indigenous people.

New constitutions could supply a means of meeting federal officials, managing programs, and representing local positions in documents recognized by government. Incorporation and access to loans offered potential tools for economic development. But formal adoption of a constitution did not establish that the resulting council exercised exclusive authority over fishing rights, family obligations, ceremonial matters, or all the members of a geographically dispersed people. Historians need to ask who participated in the drafting and approval process, how elders and earlier leaders were represented, and what decisions continued to be made outside council meetings. Political reorganization could strengthen practical capacity while simultaneously narrowing the range of institutions that officials were prepared to acknowledge.

The development of reserves in places such as Venetie and Karluk illustrates both the possibility and the limits of federal territorial protection. Reserving land for a community could improve security for local use, but it did not constitute a territory-wide settlement of Native title. Communities outside such areas continued to face uncertain legal standing. This distinction matters when comparing Alaska with reservation systems elsewhere: federal administrators adopted some familiar Indian New Deal tools, but the majority of Alaska's land questions remained unresolved. The era is best understood as one of selectively expanded organizational options rather than a comprehensive transfer of authority back to Indigenous governments.

Historical Debate

Did Reorganization Strengthen Indigenous Self-Government or Constrain Its Institutional Forms?

The question: Did the Alaska Reorganization Act expand Native self-government, or did it channel Native political life into federally prescribed constitutional models?

Interpretation A — Opportunity and recognition: The act gave Native villages a means of obtaining federal recognition of governing bodies, access to loans, the ability to organize enterprises, and, in some cases, land reserves. Supporters argue that it enabled communities to organize on their own terms and to enter relations with the federal government as recognized political entities.
Interpretation B — Constraint and imposition: Critics argue that the act encouraged standardized constitutions that did not reflect clan, kinship, or regional authority, that federal approval powers limited autonomy, and that the councils had limited powers compared with older institutions.

Evidence: Village constitutions, Interior Department correspondence, council minutes, oral histories, and the records of Native organizations.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns how to weigh formal powers against practical effects, and how much variation among villages should shape generalization.

Why it matters: The question bears on how historians understand the relationship between federal administrative forms and Indigenous political continuity.

Section 99.18

The Great Depression, Village Government, and Uneven Regional Change

The Depression of the 1930s reached Alaska through falling prices for salmon, fur, and copper. Native households dependent on wage work or fur sales lost income, and many relied more heavily on subsistence. New Deal programs brought some relief and new opportunities. The Civilian Conservation Corps and the Forest Service sponsored a project to restore and copy Tlingit and Haida totem poles in Southeast Alaska in the late 1930s and early 1940s, which employed Native carvers and, whatever its mixed motives, contributed to the survival of carving skills. At the same time, the federal government's most publicized agricultural venture, the Matanuska Colony of 1935, brought non-Native farm families from the Midwest to Alaska, while Native communities had far less comparable support.

Reorganization produced uneven results across regions. In Southeast Alaska, where Native organizations, fishing economies, and strong clan traditions already existed, communities used the new tools in combination with the Brotherhood and Sisterhood. Hydaburg's cooperative enterprises, including a sawmill and cannery, are often cited. In interior Alaska, small and dispersed villages like those of the Gwich'in sought reserves to protect hunting and trapping lands, with the Venetie Reserve of 1943 as an example. In western Alaska, where villages were distant from administrative centers and often relied on mission and school structures, adoption of constitutions and cooperatives varied with leadership, local needs, and the presence of federal personnel. On St. Lawrence Island, cooperative enterprises gained importance.

Why did outcomes vary so widely? Geography mattered: remote communities had less contact with federal agents. Subsistence patterns shaped economic priorities: where wage opportunities were few, organizing for land protection might matter more than enterprise loans. Pre-existing leadership structures mattered: communities with established clan or village councils could integrate new forms, and those with divisions might find reorganization contentious. Political priorities also differed, from land and hunting rights to fishing access and civil rights. This variety is the central historical lesson: reorganization did not produce a single Alaska Native political model.

The Depression did not produce a uniform Alaska Native economy in crisis. Families with substantial cash incomes from fish sales or mining-related work were exposed to collapsing markets, while those with more diversified seasonal production could reduce some purchases but still needed cash for fuel, tools, ammunition, or transportation. Scarcity of paid employment could increase pressure on local fish and game at precisely the moment when government regulations or commercial competition constrained access. A household's ability to adapt depended on kin ties, knowledge of several harvest areas, and whether it could travel safely between them. The distinction between wages and subsistence should therefore be treated as a shifting allocation of labor rather than as a rigid division between modern and traditional economies.

Federal work projects had similarly mixed consequences. Conservation and restoration work in Southeast Alaska produced paid jobs and generated renewed attention to some Tlingit and Haida carvings, but it also placed cultural objects within a federal preservation and tourism framework. A carved pole might embody clan history and property claims, not merely represent an attractive regional craft. Students should ask who selected objects, which artists or knowledge-holders participated, and how the restored work was interpreted to visitors. Preservation could create resources and opportunities while failing to convey the community's own understanding of ownership and meaning.

Regional case studies clarify the institutional variety of the 1930s. A Southeast community with organized Native associations and access to a fishery might seek cooperative ownership or better marketing terms; an interior settlement could regard protection of hunting territory and access to trading routes as more urgent; a western village might weigh a reindeer or local store proposal against ecological and transport limitations. None of these outcomes alone measures the success of 'reorganization.' The central historical question is whether residents gained effective influence over the decisions shaping their lives. Local charters, cooperative accounts, agency correspondence, and oral histories provide different kinds of evidence for that assessment.

Political life also included institutions that the New Deal did not invent. The Brotherhood and Sisterhood continued to convene, advocate, and coordinate community responses. Clan and village authorities remained central to many decisions, and church organizations provided additional channels of communication. A new federal council might collaborate with those groups or compete with them for legitimacy. This overlap was not necessarily a failure of governance; it reflected the multiple relationships through which Native people exercised authority. The Depression years thus prepared the ground for both the practical work of village administration and broader campaigns against racial discrimination.

Section 99.19

Elizabeth and Roy Peratrovich and the Anti-Discrimination Act of 1945

By the early 1940s, Native people in Alaska's towns faced signs and practices that excluded them from hotels, restaurants, theaters, and other public places. Notices announcing that Natives were not welcome were common in parts of the territory, and Native residents were often seated in separate sections or refused service. The Brotherhood and Sisterhood had campaigned against such practices for years. Elizabeth Peratrovich (Tlingit, born in Petersburg in 1911) and her husband Roy Peratrovich (Tlingit, from Klawock) were leaders in these organizations in the 1940s, each serving in senior positions, and they became prominent after moving to Juneau, where they wrote to Governor Ernest Gruening about discrimination. Gruening, who supported anti-discrimination legislation, urged the legislature to act.

An earlier bill had failed in the territorial House in 1943. In 1945 the campaign succeeded. The bill passed the House and went to the Senate, where it was debated at a hearing on February 5, 1945. Elizabeth Peratrovich was among those who testified, and contemporary press reports and later accounts record that she answered opponents' arguments directly and emphasized the daily injury of discrimination. The Senate passed the bill, and Governor Gruening signed the Alaska Anti-Discrimination Act on February 16, 1945. The act prohibited discrimination in public accommodations and forbade discriminatory signs, with penalties of fines and brief jail terms. It preceded the federal Civil Rights Act of 1964 by almost two decades.

The significance of the act lies both in what it achieved and what it left undone. It addressed segregation in public accommodations, which had been a daily humiliation for Native people. It was a legislative victory won by Native political organization and by allies, and it demonstrated that the Brotherhood and Sisterhood could shape territorial law. But the act did not address employment, housing, or schooling directly, relied on criminal enforcement, and did not by itself end prejudice; Native people continued to experience discrimination after 1945. Alaska later honored the achievement with Elizabeth Peratrovich Day on February 16. Students should treat the act as a major milestone within, rather than the end of, a longer struggle.

The civil rights campaign cannot be understood only through a dramatic speech at the end of a legislative hearing. Brotherhood and Sisterhood members had documented exclusion, communicated with public officials, supported one another in confrontations with businesses, and sought statutory remedies long before 1945. The failure of a proposed law in 1943 did not end the project. Organizers assessed opposition, built alliances, contacted territorial legislators, and returned with an argument that the government had a responsibility to prevent exclusion in places serving the public. The campaign joined Indigenous community institutions to a wider political language of equality while insisting on the reality of discrimination in Alaska itself.

Elizabeth and Roy Peratrovich's public role developed through this collective infrastructure. Roy held leadership responsibilities in the Brotherhood; Elizabeth led the Sisterhood and wrote and spoke forcefully about discriminatory treatment. Their interventions matter because they joined lived experience to practical legislative strategy. They did not merely ask non-Native legislators to be sympathetic: they challenged the claim that existing social customs justified exclusion and demonstrated that Native citizens were organized participants in territorial political life. Students should attend to the work of local camp members, women who mobilized support, and other Native advocates whose activities made a successful legislative vote possible.

The evidence surrounding Elizabeth's 1945 remarks deserves an especially careful description. A contemporary Daily Alaska Empire report describes the Senate hearing and its reception. The Smithsonian preserves an archival typescript identified as a transcript of testimony on the equal-rights bill. These sources support analysis of the arguments advanced, but a printed or typed text should not automatically be described as a verified stenographic transcript without establishing its creation and transmission. Accounts place the Senate debate and hearing in early February, although different records and retellings label the relevant session February 5, 6, or 8. The best practice is to connect each date to its particular legislative, journalistic, or archival source instead of inventing a single seamless chronology.

The law Governor Gruening signed on February 16, 1945 prohibited discrimination in places of public accommodation and addressed posted notices of exclusion. Its enforcement mechanisms and coverage were limited; it did not settle land rights, guarantee equal schools, or eliminate informal prejudice. Nevertheless, its enactment was a demonstrable political achievement that changed territorial law. The long campaign leading to it is evidence of Native organizational capacity, not proof that equality was bestowed by government benevolence. Its significance resides in the combination of Indigenous leadership, sustained organizing, legislative success, and unresolved struggles beyond the statute.

Section 99.20

Alaska Native Communities at the Mid-Twentieth-Century Turning Point

By 1945, Alaska Native communities had experienced nearly eighty years of American rule. The legal and economic environment had changed greatly. The territory had a civil government, a modern cash economy, extensive schooling, and new transportation links; the non-Native population was far larger than in 1867; and federal and territorial agencies exerted much greater administrative power. The war years, treated in Chapter 101, would accelerate these trends, but their roots lay in earlier decades.

Yet the period also showed persistent Native institutions and political agency. Clans, houses, village councils, subsistence systems, and languages continued, adapted, and in some places grew stronger. The Brotherhood and Sisterhood built a regional political infrastructure. Native lawyers, teachers, ministers, fishers, and elected officials took roles in the new order. Villages organized under federal law while retaining older forms of leadership. The anti-discrimination law was one achievement among many, along with the Tlingit-Haida Act, the survival of Annette Island's protected fishing rights, and countless community-level adaptations.

At the same time, fundamental questions remained unresolved. Aboriginal title had not been settled, and Native communities lacked secure legal title to much of the land they used. Federal and territorial classifications of "Native" and "civilized" persisted, and unequal schooling and health care continued. These conditions framed the later land-claims campaigns, the sovereignty debates, and the settlement of 1971 examined in Chapter 115, and the broader Inuit history examined in Chapter 100. This chapter has concentrated on the foundations rather than on those later developments.

The comparative history of Alaska between 1867 and 1945 demonstrates several distinct meanings of colonial rule. To a coastal fishing community, it might mean companies intercepting salmon and officials failing to recognize inherited resource rights. To a town family, it might mean a child excluded from the public classroom or a parent refused service at a restaurant. To an interior village, it could mean a new trading route, a federal school, or an administrative category applied to longstanding hunting territories. These experiences were related through American law and markets, but they were not interchangeable. A continental history must hold both the common political structure and the local differences in view.

Across these cases the strongest continuity was not institutional immobility. Indigenous people adopted forms that could be effective under new conditions: elected committees, modern legal briefs, territorial electoral campaigns, mission literacy, associations, cooperative businesses, and federally approved village councils. Such changes were not evidence that older political or cultural systems had vanished. Kinship, customary territorial knowledge, ceremonial obligations, subsistence expertise, and Native languages continued to inform decisions and claims. Sometimes a new institution reinforced those relationships; sometimes its bureaucratic boundaries or economic incentives brought them into conflict. Historical change therefore took the form of negotiated and uneven transformation rather than a clean replacement of one system by another.

The unfinished issues were substantial. The purchase treaty did not obtain Indigenous consent. Government land designations and resource concessions continued to intersect with territories claimed and used by Native communities. Some educational and electoral restrictions survived legislation intended to increase equality, and political recognition through a federal charter did not solve every question of jurisdiction. The 1945 anti-discrimination victory demonstrated what organized advocacy could accomplish, but public accommodations were only one arena of unequal treatment. Students should distinguish a particular reform's measurable achievement from an assertion that an entire colonial order had been overcome.

The chapter therefore ends at a historically meaningful threshold rather than with a completed resolution. Alaska Native associations, litigation strategies, territorial political experience, and village councils existed by the mid-twentieth century; they would become part of the institutional setting for subsequent developments. Chapter 100 broadens the focus to Inuit histories across northern North America, Chapter 101 treats wartime experiences, and Chapter 115 reconstructs later Alaska Native land-claims negotiations and settlement. Those developments should be understood as extensions of a history in which Indigenous communities had already been making political decisions, resisting exclusion, and defending land for generations.

Continuity and Change Over Time

When and for whom? Change was uneven: abrupt in gold-rush towns and cannery regions, more gradual in remote villages; epidemics produced sudden disruption in 1918–1919. Those near commercial centers experienced change most directly, while communities far from them often experienced greater continuity. Why the pattern matters: It shows that Native political modernization did not begin with federal reform; new organizations were adaptations of existing agency.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how Alaska Native peoples experienced American rule between 1867 and 1945. Its central argument is that American colonial administration transformed the legal and economic environment of Native communities without eliminating Indigenous governance, cultural continuity, or political agency. Alaska's Native societies were highly diverse, with distinct institutions, languages, and relationships with the land, and the American transfer had different meanings in different regions.

The Treaty of Cession of 1867 transferred Russian claims without Native consent and classified inhabitants in a way that excluded "uncivilized native tribes" from citizenship. It could not transfer Native rights that Russia had not extinguished. Administration thereafter was fragmentary, moving from military and customs rule to the Organic Act of 1884 and the Territory of Alaska in 1912, but the growth of governmental institutions did not enlarge Native political rights.

Land policy remained unresolved. The Organic Act protected Native possession while deferring the question of title; the Allotment Act of 1906 provided a limited individual route; forests, mines, canneries, railroads, and towns expanded onto Native territory. Exceptions such as the Annette Island Reserve show that where legal protection existed, Native communities could defend resources. Whether American law protected Native land rights or institutionalized dispossession remains a subject of debate.

The economy changed profoundly. Gold rushes, salmon canneries, the fur trade, whaling, and reindeer herding created wage and market opportunities, along with restrictions, dependency, and competition. Native people adapted through mixed economies that combined wage work and subsistence, and they participated as workers, fishers, traders, and herders, though they usually lacked control of capital and legal protection.

Missions and schools were powerful institutions. Russian Orthodox continuity, Protestant and Catholic expansion, Sheldon Jackson's federal-missionary system, the Nelson Act of 1905, and boarding schools created segregated education and suppressed Native languages, while also providing literacy and skills that Native people used in their own interests. Families and communities resisted discrimination, including through the Irene Jones case of 1929.

Disease and demographic trauma, culminating in the influenza pandemic of 1918–1919, devastated many communities, but survivors rebuilt households and institutions, and women's work in caregiving and cultural transmission was central. This history cannot be reduced to decline or disappearance.

The Alaska Native Brotherhood, founded in 1912, and the Alaska Native Sisterhood, organized in the mid-1910s, combined Indigenous political traditions with American organizational methods. They campaigned for citizenship, schooling, fishing rights, voting, and land. Native women acted as independent political leaders. William Paul Sr. and others used law and elections as tools, while Alaska's 1925 literacy requirement showed that formal citizenship after 1924 did not guarantee practical political participation.

The Tlingit-Haida Jurisdictional Act of 1935 gave Native claimants a legal forum, but not land restoration, and the underlying land question remained. The Alaska Reorganization Act of 1936 extended Indian New Deal institutions, offering opportunities and constraints; outcomes varied by region according to geography, economy, leadership, and political priorities.

The Anti-Discrimination Act of 1945, won by Native organizing and the leadership of Elizabeth and Roy Peratrovich, was a major achievement that addressed discrimination in public accommodations while leaving other inequalities unaddressed. It exemplifies the chapter's argument: Native political action adapted existing agency to new institutions.

By 1945, Native communities had both endured and transformed. The conditions established in these decades, unresolved title, unequal institutions, and experienced political organizations, shaped the later struggles over land, sovereignty, and self-determination examined in Chapters 100, 101, and 115.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 99 matters because it shows how an Indigenous political tradition met a colonial administration that was at once thin and consequential. The history of Alaska Native communities under American rule is sometimes told as a sequence of federal acts, but the evidence points to a more complex pattern: Native people acted within and against those acts, using American law, schools, churches, and elections while maintaining and adapting their own institutions.

The chapter also reveals larger processes in the history of Indigenous North America. The tension between recognition of use and recognition of title, the use of classifications such as "civilized," the segregated provision of education, and the contrast between formal citizenship and effective rights appear in many regions covered in this textbook. Alaska is distinctive, however, in the lateness and brevity of its treaty-free administration, in the persistence of subsistence economies, and in the unresolved land question that remained when many other regions had already been settled by reservation and allotment policies.

The developments of these decades shaped the chapters that follow. The Brotherhood and Sisterhood's organizational experience, the Tlingit-Haida claim, and the establishment of village councils formed part of the background to the land-claims campaigns culminating in the 1971 settlement examined in Chapter 115. The legal categories created in 1867 and the unresolved status of Native title continued to matter. Inuit and Iñupiat histories examined in Chapter 100 and the wartime developments of Chapter 101 build on the foundations described here.

Finally, the chapter supports one of this textbook's governing principles: Native political modernization did not begin with federal reform. Alaska Native communities already possessed institutions, governing traditions, territorial relationships, and systems of collective responsibility. The organizations of the early twentieth century were extensions of that agency under new conditions. Understanding this continuity is essential to reading the remaining chapters of Volume IX, and the sovereignty struggles of Volume X, as parts of a continuous Indigenous history rather than as responses to a federal initiative alone.
