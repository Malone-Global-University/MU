Indigenous Peoples and the American Revolution

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

On August 6, 1777, in a wooded ravine in the Mohawk Valley, Oneida warriors fighting beside American militia met Seneca and Mohawk warriors fighting beside a British-led force. Members of the same Confederacy, whose founding tradition forbade killing one another, were now shooting at each other across a few yards of ground. Oriskany made visible what the Revolution did to many Indigenous communities: it did not simply ask them to choose a side, it cut through their councils, clans, and families.

The conventional story of the American Revolution is a story about thirteen colonies, a king, a Congress, and a war that produced a republic. Indigenous peoples appear in it chiefly as frontier menaces, as allies of Britain, or as the cause of grievances in the Declaration of Independence. This chapter reverses the angle of view. It treats the Revolution as one of several overlapping conflicts that Native nations had to read, interpret, and survive: a colonial rebellion against Britain, an imperial war within the British Empire, a contest among Britain, Spain, and the emerging United States for influence in the continental interior, and, above all, a struggle over whose land, whose laws, and whose governments would endure.

Indigenous nations did not enter this crisis as blank slates. By 1775 they had spent more than a century managing European empires through the diplomatic systems examined in Chapters 55 through 58: the Covenant Chain, gift exchange, council protocol, and the playing of rivals against one another. They had fought in the Seven Years' War, had seen Britain's victory in 1763 remove the French counterweight, and had resisted British occupation in Pontiac's War. They also carried specific grievances against colonial land speculators, squatters, and governments. The Revolution therefore arrived for them as the latest, and in some respects the most dangerous, phase of a long struggle over the Appalachian and Great Lakes frontiers.

The governing argument of this chapter is that the American Revolution was not only a struggle for independence from Britain. For Indigenous nations it was also a conflict over land, sovereignty, diplomacy, and political survival. Their choices helped shape the war's course, from the failed British siege of Fort Stanwix to the long attrition in Kentucky and the Ohio Country. Yet the victory of the United States created a new and increasingly expansionist power with which they would have to negotiate, and whose leaders in 1783 and 1784 claimed rights over Indigenous lands that no Indigenous government had granted.

The chapter resists two simplifications. The first is that Indigenous peoples were uniformly British allies, a view encouraged by Patriot propaganda and by later nationalist memory. In reality some nations and many communities supported the Americans, many tried to stay out of the war, and many changed course as events shifted. The second is that those who sided with the Americans were exceptions without motives of their own. Oneida, Stockbridge-Munsee, Lenape, Catawba, and Passamaquoddy participants acted for reasons rooted in their own politics, alliances, and calculations of survival. Neutrality, too, was not passivity but an active strategy that could involve councils, treaty negotiations, intelligence gathering, and attempts to restrain warriors.

Historians reconstruct these choices from sources that are uneven in revealing ways. British Indian Department correspondence, Continental Congress records, treaty journals, military reports, missionary diaries, and settler narratives survive in abundance, but almost all were written by non-Indigenous officials and observers. Indigenous perspectives reach us through speeches transcribed by interpreters, through oral traditions maintained by Haudenosaunee, Cherokee, Lenape, and other communities, through wampum and other mnemonic records, through archaeology of destroyed towns, and through later Indigenous-authored histories. Each type of source illuminates something and hides something else, and the chapter signals throughout whose voice a given source carries.

The chapter follows a geographic and thematic progression across twenty sections. Division I examines the revolutionary crisis from the vantage of Indigenous political communities. Division II follows the Haudenosaunee Confederacy through its division, the northern war, and the devastation of 1779. Division III widens the lens to the Atlantic Northeast, Cherokee country, and the Gulf South. Division IV turns to the Ohio Country and the violence of 1782. Division V considers displacement, the Treaty of Paris, the competing settlements of 1784, and the political conditions Indigenous nations faced after independence. It ends at 1784, leaving the Northwest Indian War, the Treaty of Greenville, the Treaty of Canandaigua, and the longer evolution of American Indian policy to Chapter 60.

Finally, this chapter should be read as part of the continental argument of the whole textbook. The Revolution did not end Indigenous history; it redirected it. Indigenous nations emerged from the war with their governments intact, their homelands contested, their populations disrupted, and their diplomatic options narrowed. The settlements of 1783 and 1784 did not extinguish Indigenous sovereignty, but they opened a new phase in which that sovereignty had to be defended against a republic that insisted on its own definitions of land, law, and conquest.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the American Revolution was simultaneously a colonial rebellion, an imperial war, a regional rivalry among Britain, Spain, and the United States, and an Indigenous struggle over land and sovereignty.
  2. Trace the land disputes of 1768 to 1774, including the Treaty of Fort Stanwix of 1768 and Lord Dunmore's War, and explain how they shaped Indigenous responses to the Revolution.
  3. Analyze the offers, assurances, and limits of British and American diplomacy toward Indigenous nations in 1775 and 1776.
  4. Evaluate neutrality as an active Indigenous political strategy and identify the pressures that made it difficult to sustain.
  5. Distinguish a nation's collective position from the decisions of individual towns, leaders, clans, and war parties, using the Haudenosaunee, Cherokee, Lenape, and Creek cases.
  6. Reconstruct the campaigns of 1777 to 1782, including Oriskany, the Sullivan–Clinton expedition, the Cherokee campaigns, and the Ohio Country war, emphasizing their effects on civilians and food systems.
  7. Assess the roles of women, councils, kinship networks, and diplomatic intermediaries in Indigenous decision-making.
  8. Compare the Treaty of Fort Pitt (1778), the Treaty of Paris (1783), and the Treaty of Fort Stanwix (1784) as evidence of competing claims to authority over Indigenous land.
  9. Interpret the massacre at Gnadenhutten and its consequences for Indigenous trust in American promises, while avoiding the portrayal of all Lenape communities as combatants.
  10. Contrast the separate American and British postwar arrangements for Haudenosaunee peoples, including the Haldimand Proclamation and the Grand River settlement.
  11. Evaluate the claim that American independence was not equivalent to Indigenous independence, and explain how consequences differed among Native nations.

Historical Context

The Revolution's Indigenous history begins with the settlement of 1763. After the Seven Years' War, Britain acquired New France east of the Mississippi and Spain acquired Louisiana west of it. Britain then faced the cost of governing and defending a vast interior it barely controlled. The Royal Proclamation of 1763 attempted to regulate the movement of colonists by reserving lands west of the Appalachian watershed for Indigenous peoples and by placing land purchases under imperial supervision. Chapter 58 examined how Pontiac's War, and the negotiated settlements that followed, forced Britain to recognize Indigenous power in the interior. The Proclamation was imperfectly enforced and widely resented by colonial land speculators and settlers, but it established a principle that Native nations later invoked: that land could be acquired only through negotiated cession with recognized Indigenous authorities.

The Indigenous nations that would face the Revolution were politically varied. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy of the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca, joined since the early eighteenth century by the Tuscarora, remained a major power in the Northeast and held the Covenant Chain relationship with British colonial governments (Chapters 26 and 56). To the west and south, the Lenape, Shawnee, Wyandot, Mingo, Miami, and other nations of the Ohio and Great Lakes country maintained their own networks and often resented Haudenosaunee claims to speak for them. The Cherokee, Muscogee (Creek), Choctaw, and Chickasaw of the Southeast were agricultural nations living in autonomous towns, linked to British and Spanish traders and agents through a deerskin trade whose terms shaped diplomacy (Chapter 28). In the Atlantic Northeast, the Wabanaki peoples and their Mi'kmaq and Wolastoqiyik (Maliseet) neighbors managed a borderland between New England and Nova Scotia.

Across these regions, decision-making was dispersed. Councils, clan relationships, women's authority over lineage and land use, and the autonomy of individual towns meant that no leader, however prominent, could commit an entire nation. European agents often misread this, treating a chief's signature as the collective will. Many of the worst misunderstandings of the war, and many of the legal controversies after it, came from this mismatch between Indigenous consensus politics and European expectations of centralized authority.

Land hunger was the structural pressure beneath everything. Colonial population growth, speculation by companies such as the Ohio Company and the Transylvania Company, and the movement of settlers through Cumberland Gap and down the Ohio River pushed against the Proclamation line. Indigenous nations differed among themselves about who had rights to the contested lands, especially the hunting grounds of Kentucky, which Haudenosaunee, Cherokee, and Shawnee communities each claimed in different ways. British officials sometimes exploited such disputes, negotiating with one claimant to open lands that another considered its own.

The Revolution thus began not as the start of Indigenous trouble but as a new phase of an old one. Understanding it requires attention to what had already happened: the cessions of 1768, the pressures that produced Lord Dunmore's War in 1774, and the long habit of Native diplomats of testing every imperial promise against the question of land. Those experiences explain why many Indigenous leaders met both British and American appeals with skepticism.

Section 59.1

The American Revolution as an Indigenous and Imperial Conflict

The war that began at Lexington and Concord in April 1775 meant different things to different participants. For the colonists who supported it, it was a defense of rights and, increasingly, a project of independence. For Britain, it was a rebellion within an empire whose survival in North America depended on holding colonies, posts, and alliances. For Spain and France, which entered the war in 1779 and 1778 respectively, it was an opportunity to weaken a rival. For Indigenous nations, it was a crisis in which several of these conflicts converged on their lands, and in which the question was not which government deserved loyalty but which course would best protect communities, territory, and political authority.

That is why the war cannot be reduced to a contest between Patriots and Loyalists. Those labels describe the political divisions among colonists of European and African descent. Indigenous peoples were not colonists of the British Crown in the sense those terms imply. They were members of nations with their own governments and treaties, often with the Crown, but not as subjects. Some Indigenous leaders spoke of the Crown as a father or brother in the language of diplomacy, but that language described an alliance, not an obedience. When Mohawk, Seneca, Shawnee, or Cherokee fighters joined British campaigns, they did so as allies pursuing objectives of their own, and when Oneida, Stockbridge, or Lenape fighters joined American forces, the same was true.

Different Native nations assessed the consequences of the war differently because their circumstances differed. For nations whose lands were being encroached upon by colonial settlers, such as the Shawnee in Kentucky or the Cherokee in the Watauga country, American victory looked like a direct threat, since the rebellious colonial governments were the entities most eager to open western lands. For nations with long trading and diplomatic ties to the British Indian Department, the Crown appeared the better protector of the Proclamation principle that land could be taken only by negotiated cession. For others, such as the Oneida, whose neighbors were New York settlers and whose spiritual life had been shaped by missionary contact, American proximity and persuasion mattered more. For many communities the best course seemed to be to stay out of a white man's quarrel, a position that proved increasingly hard to maintain.

The conflict was also a regional rivalry. In the Southeast, British, Spanish, and American agents competed for the allegiance of Creek, Choctaw, and Chickasaw towns whose trade depended on them. In the Great Lakes and Ohio Country, Detroit was the hub of British influence, while Virginia and Pennsylvania competed with each other as well as with Indigenous nations for control of the upper Ohio. In the Atlantic Northeast, Massachusetts, Nova Scotia, and Wabanaki communities negotiated across a contested border. Understanding the war from Indigenous perspectives requires holding these regional systems in view at the same time.

Historical Vocabulary
Revolution, Sovereignty, Neutrality, and Alliance

Definition. Revolution here refers to the colonial rebellion that became the American war for independence, 1775 to 1783. Sovereignty refers to a people's inherent authority to govern itself and its territory. Neutrality was the deliberate decision to avoid taking a side in the war, usually enforced through council decisions and diplomacy. Alliance was a negotiated relationship of mutual obligation between two political communities, which could be limited, conditional, and revocable.

Historical context. Indigenous diplomatic vocabulary used kinship terms (father, brother, uncle, nephew) and metaphors such as the covenant chain and the council fire to define relationships. Europeans sometimes heard these as statements of subordination, but within Indigenous diplomacy they described reciprocal obligations and relative status that could be renegotiated.

Why it matters. Treating Indigenous participation as simple loyalty to one side erases the political reasoning behind each decision. Treating neutrality as non-participation erases the diplomatic effort it required.

Potential misunderstanding. Students often assume that Indigenous warriors fought as auxiliaries within British or American armies. In practice, many fought as autonomous allies who joined and left campaigns according to their own councils and objectives, and who frequently disagreed with commanders over strategy and the treatment of prisoners.

The important question is what political independence meant in different Indigenous settings. Sovereignty could be exercised by a confederated council, by an autonomous Cherokee or Muscogee town, or by a network of related Ohio Valley settlements in which leaders held authority through deliberation rather than compulsory commands. European diplomats often preferred the signature of one recognized chief, yet the person who signed might lack the consent necessary to bind distant towns or even other families within the same community. The gap between the powers claimed by an imperial representative and those recognized by Indigenous participants repeatedly produced broken promises. It also complicates the historian's use of terms such as ally, enemy, neutral, or conquered: those categories describe relationships at a particular moment, not permanent identities. A community's decision in 1776 need not explain its conduct in 1779, and the conclusion of peace between Britain and the United States in 1783 settled neither the internal claims nor the external boundaries of Native nations. Understanding such changes is essential to this chapter's geographic organization; an event in the Mohawk Valley cannot simply stand for the experience of people in the Cherokee, Wabash, or Gulf South regions.

Section 59.2

Land, Sovereignty, and the Road to Revolution, 1768–1774

The Revolution's Indigenous history begins with a treaty that many of the peoples most affected by it did not sign. At Fort Stanwix in the autumn of 1768, Sir William Johnson, the Crown's Superintendent for Indian Affairs in the north, negotiated with Haudenosaunee and other representatives to establish a boundary that would move the Proclamation line of 1763 westward. The resulting cession, running from the Mohawk Valley southwest to the Ohio River and down that river, opened for colonial purchase and settlement lands that included much of western Pennsylvania and, by the Haudenosaunee claim, the hunting grounds south of the Ohio that would become Kentucky. Related negotiations with the Cherokee, at Hard Labour in 1768 and Lochaber in 1770, adjusted the line on the southern side.

The legal and political basis of this cession was contested from the start. The Haudenosaunee claimed rights over lands south of the Ohio on the strength of conquests and tributary relationships that other nations did not accept, and the Cherokee had their own claims in the same region. The Shawnee, who lived north of the Ohio and hunted extensively south of it, were not parties to the agreement and regarded the cession as illegitimate. British officials understood that the lands had multiple claimants, but they found it convenient to treat Haudenosaunee representatives as having authority to sell. The 1768 treaty therefore created a documentary basis for land acquisition while leaving unresolved the question of who actually held rights in the lands concerned.

  • The routes through Cumberland Gap and down the Ohio that settlers and surveyors used to reach Kentucky.
  • The Lenape, Wyandot, and Mingo towns of the upper Ohio and Muskingum country, close to Fort Pitt.

Settlers did not wait for the line to be fully surveyed. Through the early 1770s, families, land companies, and surveyors moved down the Ohio and into Kentucky, and violence on the upper Ohio escalated. In April 1774, a group of Virginia frontiersmen killed a number of Mingo and Shawnee people at Yellow Creek, among them relatives of the Mingo leader known as Logan. Logan's retaliation helped set off a series of raids and counter-raids. Virginia's governor, John Murray, Lord Dunmore, then organized a campaign into the Ohio Country, in part to secure Virginia's claims against rival Pennsylvania claims and in part to assert control over the Kentucky lands.

On October 10, 1774, at Point Pleasant, where the Kanawha River meets the Ohio, a force of Shawnee, Mingo, and allied warriors under the leadership of the Shawnee Cornstalk attacked the wing of Dunmore's army led by Andrew Lewis. The fighting was fierce and costly, and the Virginians held the field. Dunmore, advancing with a separate force toward the Scioto towns, negotiated with Cornstalk and other leaders at Camp Charlotte. The Shawnee agreed to cease hostilities, return captives, and accept the Ohio River as a boundary for hunting and settlement, and provided hostages as a guarantee. The text of Logan's famous lament, widely reprinted by Virginians, is of uncertain provenance and was shaped by those who transmitted it; students should treat its precise wording with caution while recognizing the real killings and the real grievance.

Lord Dunmore's War ended with a negotiated peace that temporarily reduced fighting but resolved none of the underlying disputes. The Shawnee had accepted terms under military pressure; many in their towns believed that they had conceded too much, and that leaders such as Cornstalk had been forced into a settlement the nation might not honor. At the same time, a significant political change was beginning among the Cherokee. In March 1775, at Sycamore Shoals on the Watauga River, Richard Henderson and the Transylvania Company purchased from a group of Cherokee elders, including Attakullakulla and Oconostota, a vast tract between the Kentucky and Cumberland Rivers. Dragging Canoe, a younger leader, spoke in opposition to the sale and left the council, warning that the cession would not satisfy settler appetite. The warning proved prescient, and the split it represented would shape Cherokee politics throughout the war.

By the spring of 1775, therefore, land pressure and Indigenous resistance already defined the frontier. The Revolution would add a new set of actors, the revolutionary governments and the Crown's embattled agents, to a landscape of unresolved claims. Indigenous leaders approached the new conflict with accumulated evidence that both colonial governments and British officials had been willing to treat land as negotiable at the expense of the peoples who lived on it.

Section 59.3

British and American Diplomacy: The Struggle for Indigenous Alliances, 1775–1776

When fighting began in Massachusetts, both sides understood that Indigenous nations could affect the war's outcome and that neither could afford to leave them to the other. The Second Continental Congress moved quickly. In the summer of 1775 it created three Indian departments, northern, middle, and southern, each overseen by commissioners authorized to treat with Native nations, to regulate trade, and to seek neutrality or, if necessary, alliance. It also issued formal addresses, called talks, to the Six Nations and other peoples. These talks described the conflict as a quarrel within the English family in which Indigenous peoples had no stake, urged them to stay at home, and warned that the British ministry intended to use them against colonists who had done them no harm.

The British position was more ambiguous but not less active. Britain's imperial structure already included a Department of Indian Affairs with two superintendents, Guy Johnson in the north (who succeeded his uncle and father-in-law, Sir William Johnson, after Sir William's death in 1774) and John Stuart in the south, together with deputies such as Alexander Cameron, Daniel Claus, and John Butler, and a corps of interpreters and traders. Governor Guy Carleton in Quebec and Lieutenant Governor Henry Hamilton at Detroit managed relations in the Great Lakes. In 1775, London authorized the use of Indigenous allies to suppress the rebellion, though officials in the colonies debated how far to rely on warriors whose conduct they could not fully control. Johnson held a large council at Oswego that summer and worked to keep Haudenosaunee support; his departure from the Mohawk Valley for Montreal marked the beginning of the northern Indian Department's operations from Canadian bases. In the south, Stuart, driven from Charleston by Patriot pressure, worked from Florida and used Cameron, based among the Cherokee, to maintain influence.

The offers made by each government reveal what they believed Indigenous leaders wanted. British agents promised protection of lands and a continuation of the Proclamation principle, a steady supply of trade goods, presents, and ammunition, and, as the war continued, military pay and provisions. American commissioners offered friendship, trade, and the argument that British rule had encouraged land seizures, together with assurances that Congress would respect Indigenous boundaries. Neither government, however, could guarantee its promises. Britain had presided over the 1768 cession that opened western lands, and its soldiers and officials were stretched thin. The Americans spoke for a revolutionary government whose constituent colonies were the very entities pursuing western land, and whose weak central authority could not restrain them.

Indigenous leaders listened carefully and doubted both. They noted that colonial settlers and speculators acted independently of any authority that could restrain them, and they understood that a British victory might restore the Proclamation system but did not guarantee it. In councils at Albany in the late summer of 1775, Six Nations representatives heard Congress's request for neutrality and responded that they intended to remain at peace. At Pittsburgh in the autumn, Virginia and Congress commissioners met Lenape, Shawnee, Seneca, and other delegates, and Cornstalk, speaking for the Shawnee, reaffirmed the peace of the previous year while stressing the Shawnee's concern about settler incursion. These councils produced assurances of peace rather than alliances, and they reflect a strategy shared by many Indigenous leaders in 1775: neither side's promises were worth risking war.

Cause and Effect

Why Indigenous Nations Approached the Revolutionary Conflict Differently

Long-term causes

Centuries of alliance and trade with particular European powers, Covenant Chain diplomacy, differing experiences of land loss, and distinct regional economies (fur, deerskin, agriculture) that tied communities to different suppliers.

Short-term causes

The cessions of 1768 and 1775, Lord Dunmore's War, settler pressure in Kentucky and Watauga, the location of British and American posts, and the presence or absence of persuasive agents and missionaries.

Immediate context

Competing American and British appeals in 1775 and 1776, shortages of trade goods caused by the war, and the proximity of armies and settlements to each community.

Effects

A patchwork of neutral, British-allied, American-allied, and divided communities, with divisions within nations and sometimes within families, and different consequences for each after 1783.

Historical complexity: No single factor explains any community's choice. Trade dependence, land threats, kinship, missionary influence, local leadership, and the changing course of the war interacted, and many communities revised their positions as circumstances changed.

Trade proved a decisive lever. Indigenous communities depended on European manufactured goods, especially firearms, powder, lead, cloth, and metal tools. British access to these supplies, through Canada, Florida, and Detroit, and the disruption of colonial trade by the war, gave the Crown an advantage in many regions, though Spain and, from 1778, French and American supplies complicated the picture. Understanding the conflict from within Indigenous communities requires seeing diplomacy and supply as intertwined: commitments made in councils were tested by the practical question of who could provide what the community needed to hunt, defend, and survive.

The practical machinery of diplomacy explains why official assurances could be more fragile than they sounded. Translators and messengers carried talks over long distances, and speeches delivered in councils could be translated, condensed, and then recorded in English by people who did not share the speaker's understanding of political relationships. Wampum and customary gifts authenticated the relationship for many Indigenous participants, while written instructions and commissions carried greater weight for European officials. A commissioner might promise that Congress would prevent trespass, yet the families already occupying the disputed land were seldom present to accept that restriction. Each town therefore assessed not just the words of an alliance but the evidence of compliance: Were traders admitted? Were land surveyors restrained? Would a captive be returned? Could powder and cloth still be acquired in winter? Such questions made diplomatic policy inseparable from food security and everyday survival.

Section 59.4

Indigenous Councils, Kinship, Women, and the Politics of Choosing Sides

To understand how Indigenous nations decided anything in 1775, one must understand how they governed. Most operated through councils in which respected men and women debated until a consensus emerged, and in which leaders held influence by persuasion rather than command. In Haudenosaunee communities, hereditary chiefs (often called sachems or, in Haudenosaunee terms, rotiyanehson) were nominated by clan matrons, who also held authority over land and could remove leaders who failed their obligations. War chiefs and warriors acted in concert with, and sometimes independently of, civil leaders. Cherokee towns were governed by councils that included peace chiefs, war leaders, and women's representatives, and each town retained considerable autonomy. Similar patterns of dispersed authority shaped Lenape, Shawnee, Creek, and other nations.

This meant that a nation's position on the war could not be set by a single decision. A council could resolve on neutrality, yet young men eager for glory, plunder, or revenge could form war parties and join one side or the other. A council could advise alliance, yet individual towns, clans, or leaders could refuse. Messengers, interpreters, and cultural brokers carried news between towns and between Indigenous and colonial worlds, and their reports shaped decisions. Kinship obligations could pull in different directions: a warrior might owe loyalty to a clan whose members lived in a community taking a different side from his own. The politics of choosing sides was therefore a politics of obligation, persuasion, and sometimes irreconcilable local interests.

Women were central to these processes. Among the Haudenosaunee, clan matrons held the formal power to nominate and depose chiefs, controlled agricultural land, and managed food supplies on which warriors depended. Women's councils could influence decisions about war and peace, and women's refusal to supply provisions could limit military action. Cherokee women held comparable influence, and the title of Beloved Woman, held by Nancy Ward of Chota, recognized a woman's voice in council and in decisions about war and captives. Women also managed the households and fields on which communities relied, and their labor sustained both subsistence and war.

Historians must be careful in reconstructing individual women's actions. Molly Brant (Konwatsi'tsiaienni), a Mohawk woman of high standing and the partner of Sir William Johnson, was a documented political actor whose influence among the Mohawk and with the British was recognized by contemporaries. A tradition holds that she sent a warning to the British before the Oriskany ambush of 1777, but the evidence for this specific act is traditional and has been embellished in retelling, so it should be treated as plausible rather than established. Nancy Ward's warning to settlers in 1776 and her role in the peace councils of 1781 and later are well attested in contemporary accounts, though some of the stories told about her in later centuries go beyond the documentary record. The chapter treats documented actions and retrospective tradition as distinct kinds of evidence.

Multiple Perspectives

Choosing Sides: Councils, Kinship, and Women's Political Influence

Note: these are analytical reconstructions of positions documented in the historical record, not quotations from named individuals.

From the perspective of a council elder: The obligation of a council is to preserve the community, and a decision made by consensus after long debate binds more strongly than any promise from an outside power. Neutrality protects the people while allowing time to see which way events turn.
From the perspective of a young warrior: Settlers have already taken hunting grounds, relatives have been killed, and the council's caution seems to ask for patience that the invasion of land does not allow. A British or American campaign offers the chance to act against a concrete threat and to gain supplies and standing.
From the perspective of a clan matron or Beloved Woman: Fields, food stores, children, and the continuity of the lineage are at stake. Advice on war or peace reflects what the community can sustain, and a woman's voice carries weight because she controls the resources and relationships on which warriors depend.
From the perspective of a British or American agent: A chief's presence at a council appears to signify the nation's commitment, and agents often reported such commitments with more confidence than the Indigenous political system warranted. Disappointments were then blamed on Indigenous unreliability rather than on a misreading of how decisions were made.

The result of these dynamics was that by 1776, and increasingly thereafter, Indigenous participation in the war was fragmented. This was not a failure of Indigenous politics but a product of its structure. The same consensus-based, decentralized systems that had allowed Native nations to resist concentration of power by outsiders made it difficult to impose uniform wartime policy, particularly when the pressure to choose came from armies on their doorsteps. Division II examines the most consequential example, the Haudenosaunee Confederacy.

The position of women was not identical in every Indigenous nation, and historical generalization can obscure important differences. Within Haudenosaunee matrilineal institutions, senior women possessed recognized political responsibilities associated with clan continuity and the selection of titled leaders; that should not be translated automatically into the modern assumption that every woman had equal influence in every council. Cherokee women's status and the exceptional authority of a Beloved Woman likewise require attention to particular communities and circumstances. Decisions about prisoners could involve the preferences of kin who had lost family members, while decisions to evacuate or remain in a threatened town affected families who would have to carry food, tools, and children through unfamiliar territory. These interests were political, not merely domestic. Because many surviving wartime papers were written by male officers, historians must recover women's roles through community traditions, household evidence, and the consequences of policy, while stating clearly where an individual woman's direct intervention is not documented.

The Haudenosaunee Confederacy and the Northern War

Section 59.5

Haudenosaunee Neutrality and the Division of the Six Nations

The Haudenosaunee began the war committed to neutrality. At councils held at Albany in 1775 and at German Flatts and elsewhere in 1775 and 1776, Six Nations leaders told both Congress's commissioners and British officials that they intended to remain at peace. Neutrality fit the Confederacy's long diplomatic tradition of balancing among European powers, and it protected communities that lay between New York settlements and British-held Canada. It also reflected a political judgment that a war between English-speaking peoples offered the Six Nations more danger than benefit. For roughly two years it largely held, though it was always under strain.

The pressures that eroded neutrality came from several directions. British agents pressed hard, supplying presents and arguing that an American victory would threaten Haudenosaunee lands. Missionaries and neighbors pressed from the other side: Samuel Kirkland, a missionary among the Oneida, cultivated Oneida leaders sympathetic to the Americans. Settlers and militia in the Mohawk Valley, hostile and fearful, harassed Mohawk communities, and the departure of Guy Johnson and his circle, including the Mohawk leader Joseph Brant, to Canada and Britain showed where influence among the Mohawk lay. Sir William Johnson's long relationship with the Mohawk, and Molly Brant's standing, drew many Mohawk toward the Crown.

By 1777 the Confederacy's members had largely taken different paths. The Oneida and most Tuscarora leaned toward the Americans, motivated by proximity, missionary influence, and a judgment about where their interest lay. The Mohawk, much of the Seneca and Cayuga, and portions of the Onondaga leaned toward Britain. The Onondaga, whose council fire was the Confederacy's ceremonial center, contained both factions. Haudenosaunee tradition holds that in 1777 the Grand Council, unable to reach consensus, covered its council fire, a symbolic acknowledgment that the Confederacy could no longer act as one, and allowed each nation to decide for itself. The precise circumstances and dating of this council are better documented in Haudenosaunee tradition than in colonial records, but the substance of the decision is consistent with the divided positions evident in the military record.

The covering of the council fire did not dissolve the Confederacy, and the Haudenosaunee continued to understand themselves as a union of nations. It did, however, remove the formal constraint that had held wartime action within bounds and permitted nations to fight on opposite sides. The political cost was severe. Neighbors, clan relatives, and members of the same nation now found themselves in opposing camps, and the principle that Haudenosaunee did not shed one another's blood, central to the Great Law of Peace, was broken at Oriskany.

Historical Debate

Could the Haudenosaunee Confederacy Have Maintained Neutrality?

The question: Historians disagree about whether the Confederacy's division was inevitable once war reached the Mohawk Valley, or whether neutrality might have held under different circumstances.

Interpretation A — Neutrality was structurally unsustainable: The Confederacy's position between New York and Canada, the competing pressures from missionaries and Indian Department agents, and the dependence of communities on trade goods made it likely that nations would eventually align with the party that could supply them or protect them. On this view, the decentralized structure of Haudenosaunee politics, which required consensus, made unified action unlikely once consensus failed.
Interpretation B — Neutrality was a viable strategy undermined by contingent decisions: The Confederacy had practiced neutrality successfully in earlier conflicts, and its early wartime councils expressed genuine commitment to it. On this view, British decisions to launch the 1777 expedition through the Mohawk Valley and to recruit Haudenosaunee warriors, together with American responses, destroyed a policy that might otherwise have continued.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on treaty and council records from 1775 and 1776, British and American correspondence, military records of 1777, and Haudenosaunee oral tradition regarding the Grand Council.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns causation and contingency rather than the basic facts: how much weight to give structural pressures versus particular decisions by outside commanders.

Why it matters: If neutrality was unsustainable, the Confederacy's division appears as an unavoidable consequence of its position. If it was viable, responsibility shifts toward the choices of the British and American governments that drew Haudenosaunee communities into the fighting.

Student task: Identify evidence from this section that supports each interpretation, and explain what additional evidence might help decide between them.

A closer look at the structure of the Confederacy shows why neutrality was difficult to sustain without implying that confederal government had simply ceased to function. The council fire at Onondaga represented a relationship among distinct nations, not a power that could send conscripted armies wherever a central executive ordered. Each nation faced particular geography: Mohawk communities were linked to the Johnson family's estates and the Mohawk Valley; Oneida communities dealt closely with neighboring colonial settlements and missionary Samuel Kirkland; Seneca communities farther west had ties to Niagara and the broader Great Lakes world. From their perspective, choosing a side also meant deciding which nearby routes might remain open for trade and whether an allied commander could protect threatened homes. Those judgments shifted as revolutionary forces occupied forts and British officials recruited in Canada. Some families and settlements maintained contact with people across the lines even after warriors began fighting. Consequently the common formulation that 'the Six Nations joined the British' is not merely incomplete; it erases deliberate political disagreement among people who continued to value the Confederacy. The image of the council fire being covered captures the gravity of war among kin, but surviving English accounts cannot establish a single date or a single unanimous meeting that caused all subsequent divisions. The military evidence demonstrates a gradual fragmentation during 1776–1777, not a neat constitutional dissolution.

Section 59.6

Fort Stanwix and the Battle of Oriskany, 1777

The Mohawk Valley campaign of 1777 was part of a larger British plan. General John Burgoyne would advance south from Canada toward Albany, while a smaller force under Lieutenant Colonel Barry St. Leger would move from Lake Ontario by way of Oswego and the Mohawk Valley to meet him. St. Leger's column included British regulars, Loyalist units such as Sir John Johnson's Royal Greens, Hessian and Canadian troops, and a substantial body of Indigenous allies, many of them Seneca and Mohawk, among them Joseph Brant and Seneca war leaders. The expedition's route led past Fort Stanwix (renamed Fort Schuyler by Americans), a post that commanded the portage between the Mohawk River and Wood Creek.

St. Leger's force reached Fort Stanwix in early August and began a siege. The American garrison, under Colonel Peter Gansevoort, held out. Learning of the threat, Brigadier General Nicholas Herkimer assembled several hundred militia from Tryon County to relieve the fort, and was joined by a party of Oneida warriors, a significant fact because it meant that Haudenosaunee fighters were on both sides. On August 6, the relief column marched into a ravine near the Oneida village of Oriska, six miles or so from the fort. British-allied Indigenous warriors and Loyalists, positioned to ambush the column, struck from the surrounding woods.

The battle that followed was one of the bloodiest of the northern war, fought at close range and in confusion. The ambush shattered the militia column, but American forces rallied, and a violent storm interrupted the fighting. Herkimer, wounded in the leg, continued directing his men from beneath a tree and died of his wound some days later. Meanwhile a sortie from Fort Stanwix raided the British-allied camps, seizing supplies and scattering the camp followers there. Casualties among the militia were high; the Seneca and Mohawk also suffered heavy losses, including several respected leaders, and Oneida fighters were killed and wounded on the American side. The Seneca in particular felt the blow, because many of those lost were prominent men.

The relief column did not reach the fort, but the siege was nevertheless broken. When a force under Benedict Arnold approached the fort later in August, rumors, partly manipulated by American intelligence, magnified the size of the relief, and St. Leger's Indigenous allies, angry over their losses and discouraged by the failure to take the fort, withdrew. St. Leger retreated to Oswego, and the northern prong of Burgoyne's plan collapsed. The Haudenosaunee fighters had not been the cause of the failure but had been central to the events surrounding it, and their departure demonstrated that they acted according to their own judgment of costs and prospects.

The human and political consequences of Oriskany reached beyond the battlefield. Oneida and Tuscarora communities, who had supported the Americans, would later suffer from raids by British-allied Haudenosaunee, and many Oneida would eventually take refuge near American lines at Schenectady. The Seneca, having lost leaders, were driven to seek revenge, and the border war of 1778 and the American retaliation of 1779 grew in part from this cycle. Oriskany did not by itself determine the Confederacy's later history, but it fixed the memory of a division that had been prepared by earlier choices and that no council could now repair.

The battle's human implications extended beyond an encounter between two armed columns. Many Oneida warriors helped the revolutionary defenders of the fort through intelligence and service, while Mohawk and Seneca fighters participating in the British expedition entered the Mohawk Valley with their own expectations of defending routes, alliances, and lands. An American victory in the larger Saratoga campaign did not resolve their political predicament. People who had lived within traveling distance of one another were compelled to evaluate whether a returning relative was now an enemy, whether a messenger would be trusted, and where children could safely remain during the next season of fighting. Oriskany is therefore a poor example for any narrative that assigns collective guilt or merit to an entire Indigenous nation. Its significance resides in the costs of fighting among related communities and in the realization that European commanders could not preserve the internal peace of the Confederacy merely by requesting Indigenous assistance.

Section 59.7

Joseph Brant, Molly Brant, Loyalist Networks, and the Northern Borderlands

After 1777 the northern war shifted from set-piece campaigns to raids across a borderland extending from Niagara and Oswego to the Susquehanna and Mohawk Valleys. Its organizers were British officers of the Indian Department and Loyalist units, notably the rangers raised by John Butler and his son Walter, together with Haudenosaunee war leaders including Joseph Brant, Sayenqueraghta, and Cornplanter. Fort Niagara became the principal base, supplying warriors and sheltering the families of those who fought.

Joseph Brant (Thayendanegea) emerged as the most famous of these leaders. Born into a Mohawk family and educated partly through Sir William Johnson's connections, he had served the Indian Department as an interpreter and traveled to England in 1775 and 1776. He returned committed to the British alliance and led Mohawk and Loyalist forces in raids on settlements along the Susquehanna and Mohawk Valleys, including a campaign in 1778 against Cobleskill and other frontier communities. Brant was a politician as much as a soldier: he lobbied British officials for promises about land and protection, and he sought to hold Haudenosaunee fighters together in the absence of the Confederacy. His political and diplomatic activity would shape the Grand River settlement after the war.

Molly Brant, Joseph's sister, exercised influence of a different kind. After the Mohawk Valley became too dangerous, she withdrew to British-held territory, eventually settling near Fort Niagara and Carleton Island. As a respected Mohawk matron connected by marriage to the Johnson family and by status to Haudenosaunee networks, she was a channel for information, a mediator between the Indian Department and Mohawk communities, and an advocate for her people's claims on the Crown. British officials relied on her counsel, and after the war she lived on a British pension. Her significance lay in sustaining the kinship and diplomatic networks that held British-allied Haudenosaunee communities together.

The borderland war of 1778 produced events that became fixed in American memory. In July, a force of Loyalist rangers and Seneca and other warriors under John Butler defeated a Connecticut militia force in the Wyoming Valley in Pennsylvania, and killings occurred among those who fled or surrendered. Later accounts, shaped by Patriot propaganda, described a massacre of civilians, and they exaggerated scale and conflated different episodes; the best evidence indicates that the principal killing was of militiamen in and after the battle, though civilians suffered displacement and destruction. In November, Walter Butler's rangers and Seneca and Mohawk warriors attacked Cherry Valley, killing roughly thirty noncombatants and soldiers, an episode in which Indigenous participants and Loyalist officers disagreed over responsibility, and for which the term "massacre" is used by many sources. These events gave American commanders a rationale for the 1779 campaign even as they masked the Indigenous grievances that preceded them.

Indigenous participation on the American side was also real and costly. The Stockbridge-Munsee, a community of Mahican and Munsee people from western Massachusetts and the Hudson Valley, had enlisted in American service. On August 31, 1778, at Kingsbridge in what is now the Bronx, a Stockbridge company under Daniel Nimham was ambushed by British dragoons and Loyalist rangers and suffered severe losses, including Nimham himself. The Stockbridge community, already reduced by land loss, was further diminished. The Oneida, too, contributed: according to tradition, they carried corn to the Continental Army at Valley Forge in the winter of 1777–1778, a story associated with Polly Cooper, and Oneida scouts served American forces. Neither participation spared these communities from later hardship.

Everyday Life and Social History

Indigenous Soldiers, Scouts, Households, and Military Service

Indigenous military service was embedded in household and community life. Warriors who joined campaigns left behind families who had to plant, harvest, and defend the community in their absence. War parties typically formed around kin and clan, and their size, duration, and objectives were negotiated within communities rather than imposed by outside commanders. Scouts and interpreters, valued by both armies, held knowledge of terrain and language that could not be replaced, and their service gave them leverage in negotiations about supplies and strategy.

Service was not always voluntary or free of tension. British and American officers complained about Indigenous allies who withdrew after a victory, refused to besiege fortified positions, or insisted on taking captives according to their own customs. These practices reflected Indigenous warfare's goals of limited casualties, captive-taking, and the preservation of community strength, rather than the total-victory strategies of European armies. Indigenous allies also expected provisions, presents, and pay, and frequently threatened to withdraw when these failed to appear. Women supplied food, mended clothing and moccasins, and cared for the wounded, and in refugee camps at Niagara they sustained thousands of displaced people.

Historians read these dimensions largely through officers' complaints and administrators' accounts, which reveal Indigenous practices through the lens of European expectations. The point is to see service as an activity of communities pursuing their own purposes rather than as the behavior of auxiliaries.

The northern borderland also exposes the dangers of reducing Indigenous political labor to conspicuous battlefield commanders. Joseph Brant could negotiate, travel, recruit, and lead armed parties, but his influence depended on relationships with other leaders and communities; a raid credited to him in a later narrative might have involved multiple officers or a different local command. Molly Brant's work as an intermediary was often conducted through correspondence, intelligence, family relationships, and visits rather than a position in the British military hierarchy. Conversely the Stockbridge-Munsee contingent in American service illustrates that Indigenous support for independence was not rewarded consistently. Daniel Nimham and his son Abraham were among the men killed when the Stockbridge company was ambushed near Van Cortlandt's Woods on August 31, 1778. The losses were compounded by the danger that wartime absence, indebtedness, and neighboring settler pressure posed to Stockbridge landholdings. The same cause that made service attractive as a possible means of securing a community could thereby expose that community to additional insecurity.

Section 59.8

The Sullivan–Clinton Campaign of 1779 and the Destruction of Haudenosaunee Homelands

The raids of 1778 convinced American commanders that frontier security required striking at the sources of British-allied raiding in Haudenosaunee country. In the spring of 1779, George Washington directed Major General John Sullivan to lead a major expedition into the heartland of the Six Nations. His instructions, dated May 31, set as the immediate objective the total destruction and devastation of Haudenosaunee settlements, and directed that the country be not merely overrun but ruined, so that the Haudenosaunee would be unable to feed raiding parties or their families. Taking prisoners was a secondary aim. The campaign's central purpose was the destruction of homes, fields, and food stores, not the defeat of an army in battle.

The operation drew on several columns. Sullivan's main force, several thousand strong, assembled at Easton, Pennsylvania, marched to the Wyoming Valley and on to Tioga in the Susquehanna country, where it joined with a column under Brigadier General James Clinton that had come by way of the Mohawk Valley and Otsego Lake. A separate force under Colonel Daniel Brodhead moved up the Allegheny River from Fort Pitt and destroyed Seneca and Munsee towns in that region. Earlier in the spring, a detachment under Colonel Goose Van Schaick had struck Onondaga towns, burning houses and taking prisoners, an attack that hit a nation that had been trying to avoid the fighting.

The main army met organized opposition only once. On August 29, at Newtown, near present Elmira, New York, British-allied Indigenous and Loyalist forces under Joseph Brant, John Butler, and Seneca leaders took a fortified position and fought to delay the invasion. American artillery and numbers overwhelmed the position, and the defenders withdrew. The battle was a defeat but not a decisive one, since the defenders' purpose was to buy time for civilians to move. After Newtown, the army advanced largely unopposed, and the rest of the campaign was one of systematic destruction.

From late August into September, Sullivan's army moved through the Finger Lakes country, burning dozens of towns. Contemporary American journals, kept by officers and soldiers, record the destruction in detail: substantial log and frame houses, some of them of a size and quality that surprised the soldiers; extensive cornfields; orchards of apple, peach, and other fruit trees; gardens of beans, squash, and vegetables; and large stores of harvested and stored food. Soldiers cut down orchards and burned fields of ripening corn. Estimates of the quantity of corn destroyed run to many tens of thousands of bushels, and some commonly cited figures reach roughly 160,000, though such numbers derive from officers' reports and are approximate. At Genesee Castle, the largest Seneca town in the Genesee Valley, American troops destroyed the town and its fields in mid-September. Several American soldiers, including Lieutenant Thomas Boyd, were captured and killed during a scouting action in the same period, and Boyd's death was cited by Americans to justify further destruction.

The destruction of food systems was central to the campaign's design and effect. Haudenosaunee communities depended on agriculture, centered on the women's cultivation of corn, beans, and squash, supplemented by hunting and fishing. The loss of a harvest, together with the stored surplus of previous years, meant that communities entering autumn faced the winter without reserves. Washington's instructions had anticipated this: the campaign aimed to remove the capacity for raiding by eliminating the base that supported it. It also had consequences for civilians, including women, children, and the elderly, who lacked other sources of food, and for allies of the Americans as well. The campaign did not distinguish carefully between the towns of British-allied nations and those of neutral or American-aligned communities. Oneida and Tuscarora towns suffered from the war's disruptions, and Onondaga communities, which had sought to remain neutral, were attacked directly.

Refugees streamed west and north to Fort Niagara, where British authorities struggled to feed them. The winter of 1779 and 1780 was exceptionally severe, and the refugee camps were crowded, short of food, and vulnerable to disease. Contemporary British accounts record mortality among the displaced, though precise numbers are uncertain, and Haudenosaunee tradition remembers the period as one of intense suffering. Haudenosaunee memory has long associated Washington with the name rendered in English as Town Destroyer, a name that the 1779 campaign reinforced. The campaign did not end Haudenosaunee military activity, since British-allied warriors resumed raiding in 1780 and 1781 and Oneida settlements were struck. It did, however, break the Confederacy's economic base in its homelands, force a large population into dependence on British supplies, and set the stage for the displacement that followed the war. The destruction also shaped postwar negotiations: American commissioners in 1784 would treat the Haudenosaunee as a defeated people, an interpretation Haudenosaunee leaders rejected but could not easily overcome.

The campaign's legacy is contested in memory. For many Americans of the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries it was a military success that opened western New York to settlement, and it was commemorated with monuments. For Haudenosaunee peoples it was an invasion that destroyed homes, food, and lives, and its remembrance has become a subject of public history and renaming debates, issues treated in Chapter 132. Historians generally agree on the campaign's scale and its intent to destroy the material foundations of Haudenosaunee communities, while differing over how to characterize it, with some scholars using terms such as scorched-earth warfare and others emphasizing its genocidal implications or its character as a war of terror against civilians. The chapter's evidence supports describing the destruction of crops, towns, and food stores as an intended objective rather than incidental damage.

Section 59.9

Wabanaki, Mi'kmaq, and Wolastoqiyik Diplomacy in the Atlantic Northeast

The Revolution in the Atlantic Northeast unfolded along a seam between two competing governments. To the west lay Massachusetts, whose district of Maine extended east toward the St. Croix River; to the east lay Nova Scotia, a British province whose settler population included many New Englanders sympathetic to the rebellion. Between and around them lived the Wabanaki peoples, including the Penobscot and Passamaquoddy; the Wolastoqiyik (Maliseet) of the St. John River; and the Mi'kmaq of Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, and the Gulf of St. Lawrence. These peoples had long histories of diplomacy with both French and British colonial governments, and many were Catholic, with ties to missionaries and to the French past.

Their position was precarious. Trade goods, especially powder and provisions, came from British Halifax or from Massachusetts, depending on the season and the politics. Settlers, fishermen, and loggers pressed upon their lands and rivers. Each community had to calculate which government could offer more security, and the answers differed. Penobscot and Passamaquoddy communities, closer to Massachusetts, tended toward the American side, while communities in Nova Scotia and along the St. John were more exposed to British power and more divided.

American authorities moved early to secure Indigenous support. In July 1776, Massachusetts negotiated a treaty at Watertown, then the seat of its revolutionary government, with delegates from Mi'kmaq and St. John River (Wolastoqiyik) communities. The agreement established friendship and set out commitments about mutual military assistance, with Article VI proposing a contingent of six hundred men, or as many as the represented communities could furnish. Articles VII–IX set out provisions for pay, equipment, transport, and supplies. This was a written commitment, not evidence that six hundred warriors actually assembled or marched. The treaty did not bind every Wabanaki or Mi'kmaq community; it was an agreement between Massachusetts and the particular delegates and communities they represented, and other communities acted differently.

The treaty's practical effects were limited and uneven. In the autumn of 1776, a party of American sympathizers led by Jonathan Eddy attacked British-held Fort Cumberland in Nova Scotia, supported by a number of Wolastoqiyik and Mi'kmaq participants, but the attack failed, and British reinforcements restored control. Massachusetts appointed an agent to the Indigenous communities, John Allan, who worked from Machias to maintain relations and distribute supplies. Britain, for its part, worked to win these communities back, and its officials in Nova Scotia, including the Indian superintendent Michael Francklin, negotiated with Mi'kmaq and Wolastoqiyik leaders in the following years to secure their neutrality or support.

The difficulty of sustaining the American alliance was in large part a matter of supply. The revolutionary government's capacity to deliver the goods it promised was limited, and communities that depended on those goods were vulnerable to British blandishments and to scarcity. Epidemic disease, fishing and timber competition, and settler pressure continued throughout the war. By its end, the communities of the Atlantic Northeast had been drawn into a conflict that offered them little prospect of protection from either side, and the creation of the United States and the continued existence of British North America would divide their homeland by a new international boundary through the St. Croix region, a development whose consequences extended well beyond the Revolution.

The text of the Watertown agreement is unusually informative because its numbered provisions distinguish formal alliance from later implementation. Articles I and II established friendship and reciprocal obligations against enemies; Articles III through V provided procedures for restitution and peaceful settlement of injuries. Article VI anticipated six hundred armed men, or as many as could be furnished, traveling toward the Continental Army under Washington. Subsequent articles concerned pay, guns, passage to Boston, the gathering of additional volunteers from neighboring nations, and trade supplies at Machias. The agreement thus treated the delegates as diplomatic partners asked to provide costly service, not simply as passive recipients of Massachusetts patronage. Its wording does not establish that all those men actually reached Washington, or that every Mi'kmaq, Wolastoqiyik, Penobscot, or Passamaquoddy community accepted the treaty. Nor does the ten-article text itself make the specific land and religious guarantees sometimes attributed generally to the negotiations. Such claims require separate, identified council records rather than being read into this document. The history is clearest when three questions are separated: what the written treaty committed its signatories to do, how far the parties carried out those obligations, and what the wider Indigenous communities understood their political relationship to be.

Section 59.10

Cherokee Sovereignty and the Revolutionary War of 1776–1777

The Cherokee entered the Revolution divided and under pressure. Their towns in the southern Appalachians were commonly grouped in four major regions: the Lower Towns in the upper Savannah drainage, the Middle Towns along parts of the upper Little Tennessee and Tuckasegee, the Valley Towns along the Valley and Hiwassee rivers, and the Overhill Towns on the lower Little Tennessee and Tellico rivers. Some contemporary accounts additionally distinguished Out Towns; these were geographic and political patterns, not four centrally administered provinces. They had suffered in the Anglo-Cherokee War of 1759 to 1761, had ceded land in the 1770 Treaty of Lochaber and in 1775 at Sycamore Shoals, and now faced settlers in the Watauga and Holston valleys who were living on lands the Cherokee did not consider ceded. Older leaders such as Attakullakulla favored accommodation; younger leaders such as Dragging Canoe, who had opposed the Sycamore Shoals sale, favored resistance.

British agents worked among them. Alexander Cameron, the deputy superintendent who lived in Cherokee country, and Henry Stuart, brother of Superintendent John Stuart, maintained relations and urged restraint at first, since British officials hoped to coordinate Cherokee action with operations on the coast and did not want premature attacks. In the summer of 1776, however, a delegation of Northern Indigenous peoples, including Shawnee and Mohawk representatives, arrived at the Overhill Towns, urging united resistance to colonial encroachment. Dragging Canoe and his supporters, convinced that the settlers' advance would not stop, prevailed over more cautious voices, and in July Cherokee war parties attacked settlements on the Watauga, Holston, and in Carolina, Virginia, and Georgia.

The attacks were coordinated poorly and met with prepared resistance, in part because of warnings. Nancy Ward, the Beloved Woman of Chota, is recorded as having warned settlers at Watauga, and she took the part of peacemaking in the aftermath, protecting a settler woman, Lydia Bean, who had been taken captive. Her role underscores that Cherokee opinion was not uniform and that women held political influence in these decisions. At Island Flats on July 20, 1776, Cherokee forces suffered a defeat, and throughout the summer frontier militias mobilized.

The American response was destructive. In the late summer and autumn of 1776, four coordinated expeditions entered Cherokee country: from South Carolina under Andrew Williamson, from North Carolina under Griffith Rutherford, from Virginia under William Christian, and from Georgia. These forces burned dozens of Cherokee towns, destroyed cornfields and stored food, killed or captured inhabitants, and in some cases sold prisoners as slaves. The Lower, Middle, and Valley towns were especially hard hit, and the destruction of food supplies left many communities hungry as winter approached. As in the 1779 invasion of Haudenosaunee country, the campaigns aimed at civilian infrastructure as well as at warriors, and their effect was to break the capacity of Cherokee communities to sustain further war.

In 1777 the Cherokee were compelled to negotiate. At DeWitt's Corner in South Carolina in May, representatives of the Lower Towns ceded much of their remaining land in that colony. At Long Island of the Holston, in July, delegates from the Overhill and other towns, including Attakullakulla and Oconostota, agreed to cede lands in what is now North Carolina and Virginia and to acknowledge a boundary that reduced Cherokee territory. These agreements were made under duress, after the devastation of 1776, and they were challenged by Cherokee who had not consented. The Cherokee Nation had survived intact as a political entity, but it had lost land, lives, and food, and had been reminded of the cost of war.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Cherokee Relations with Colonies Before and During the Revolution

What Changed

  • Territorial losses accelerated: the cessions of 1768 to 1770, 1775, and 1777 reduced the Cherokee homeland substantially
  • The balance of power shifted from a Cherokee nation that could threaten colonial frontiers to one repeatedly invaded and compelled to negotiate
  • Political division deepened, with the emergence of a resistant faction that left established towns
  • Direct American armies, rather than British colonial governments alone, became the principal adversary

What Stayed the Same

  • Town autonomy and council decision-making persisted despite the pressure of invasion
  • Dependence on the deerskin trade and on British, and later other, suppliers continued
  • Women's political influence, represented by figures such as Nancy Ward, remained a feature of Cherokee diplomacy
  • The Cherokee insistence that land could be ceded only through legitimate negotiation, even where colonial governments disregarded it

Why the pattern matters: The Revolution accelerated trends already underway rather than creating them. Recognizing both the continuity of Cherokee political practice and the scale of change helps explain the later Cherokee political reforms examined in Chapter 64 and the removal crisis examined in Chapters 65 and 66.

Geography shaped the war's devastating effects in Cherokee country. The Lower, Middle, Valley, and Overhill districts followed different river systems, so an attacking force coming through one mountain pass did not automatically threaten every town in the same way. The Valley Towns, situated in the Valley and Hiwassee watersheds, were a major regional grouping and should not be treated as a minor appendage of the Middle Towns. Cherokee political decisions also varied among towns and families within these regions: some leaders prioritized negotiations to preserve crops and avoid another invasion, while other people judged that treaties signed after military destruction could never secure their descendants' lands. The demands of the colonial expeditions were material as well as territorial. Burning cornfields and provisions before winter transferred the burden of war onto women, children, and elders, including people who had not joined attacks on colonial settlements. When the 1777 agreements were signed, colonial leaders often read them as cessions binding the Cherokee generally, whereas resisting communities questioned who had authority to surrender particular land. The resulting tension helps explain the continued struggle from the Chickamauga and Tennessee River towns without assuming that an entire Cherokee nation became a single war party.

Section 59.11

Dragging Canoe and the Continuing Southern Appalachian Struggle

Dragging Canoe rejected the treaties of 1777. Together with a body of followers drawn from several towns, he withdrew from the Overhill country and established new towns along Chickamauga Creek and the Tennessee River, in the area of present-day Chattanooga. From there, and from additional communities in the Lower Tennessee country, the group maintained relations with British agents in Florida and the Gulf, drew supplies from them, and continued to resist the advance of settlers into Cherokee lands. The relocation was a deliberate political act: a statement that the treaties made under duress did not bind those who had not consented, and a strategic move to a position farther from American armies and closer to potential allies.

The term Chickamauga Cherokee, used by Americans and later writers, requires care. It originated in the name of the creek where Dragging Canoe's followers settled, and American contemporaries used it as though it described a separate group. In fact, the Chickamauga towns were Cherokee towns, and their residents did not regard themselves as having left the Cherokee Nation. Their politics diverged from those of towns that sought peace, but kinship, language, and shared interests continued to link the communities, and individuals moved between them. Historians now generally treat the term as a label for a resistant faction and its towns rather than as the name of a distinct people. The unified Cherokee Nation continued to exist, and some Cherokee leaders worked at once for peace and for the protection of the whole.

The resisting towns continued to fight. They drew some support from northern Indigenous allies, especially Shawnee and Mingo, and from British officers and traders, though Britain's capacity to supply them was limited. They conducted raids on settlements, particularly in the Watauga, Holston, and Cumberland areas, and the settlers' own expansion, including the establishment of Nashville on the Cumberland in 1779 and 1780, continued to fuel conflict. American forces responded. In April 1779, a force under Evan Shelby destroyed Chickamauga towns, burning houses and food stores. In subsequent years, other expeditions struck Cherokee towns, and the fighting produced a recurring pattern of attack, retaliation, and displacement.

Disease compounded the damage. A smallpox epidemic that spread across much of the continent in the late 1770s and early 1780s reached the Cherokee in 1780, adding to the toll of war, and the destruction of fields and towns left communities more vulnerable. In December 1780, at Boyd's Creek, American forces under John Sevier defeated a Cherokee force, and in early 1781 additional expeditions struck the Overhill and Middle Towns, burning more settlements. Cherokee peace advocates, including Nancy Ward, spoke at councils such as the one at Long Island of the Holston in 1781 for an end to the fighting. The Cherokee as a whole were now exhausted, even as Dragging Canoe's followers continued to resist.

The Revolution's end in 1783 did not end the southern Appalachian struggle. Settlement continued, the new state governments of North Carolina, Virginia, and Georgia asserted rights to Cherokee land, and Dragging Canoe's towns remained a center of resistance until his death in 1792, after which the political community he had led merged more fully back into the broader Cherokee Nation. The history of these years illustrates two features of Indigenous politics. First, resistance and accommodation coexisted within a single nation, and each reflected a considered response to the same pressures. Second, the consequences of the Revolution for the Cherokee, a peace that was not peace, anticipated the experience of many other nations.

The relocation of resisting households imposed costs as well as tactical advantages. A town established in a more defensible location still required arable fields, seed corn, labor to clear land, and access to paths along which traders or allies could carry ammunition. Elders and children had to move alongside warriors, and families displaced by destruction frequently entered communities whose houses and food supplies were already occupied. Leaders therefore had to sustain consent among settlers in the new towns, not simply plan raids. Diplomatic connections with neighboring Muscogee and Shawnee people offered possibilities of support but did not erase disputes over territory or resources. Even as armed parties continued to resist, other Cherokee people sought to restore fields and trading relations through negotiations. These simultaneous strategies were not a contradiction to be resolved by declaring one faction authentic: they were choices under intense pressure, made by communities facing different dangers. This distinction prevents the convenient colonial term 'Chickamauga' from concealing the persistence of Cherokee kinship and nationhood across opposed political strategies.

Section 59.12

Muscogee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, and the Southern Imperial Borderlands

South and west of the Cherokee lay the Muscogee (Creek), Choctaw, and Chickasaw nations, each composed of numerous towns that retained considerable autonomy. Their region was an imperial borderland in which Britain, Spain, and now the United States competed for influence. Britain held East and West Florida until the later stages of the war, Spain held Louisiana and, after 1779 and 1781, conquered the Gulf Coast posts of Baton Rouge, Mobile, and Pensacola, and Georgia and the Carolinas pressed from the east. For these nations, as for others, trade was the principal link to outside powers and, consequently, the main lever of diplomacy.

The Muscogee Confederacy was not a single state. Its towns were conventionally divided into Upper Towns on the Coosa and Tallapoosa Rivers and Lower Towns on the Chattahoochee, with differing relations to neighbors. The Lower Towns, near Georgia, were more exposed to settler pressure and trade, while the Upper Towns had closer ties to British and later Spanish posts on the Gulf. These distinctions did not produce unified policies. Some towns favored the British, many sought neutrality, and some had dealings with the Americans, and leaders in each town acted on local calculations. When British and Spanish officials spoke of Creek policy, they often meant the actions of particular towns and leaders.

The Revolution brought several kinds of pressure. British Indian Department agents, including Superintendent John Stuart and his deputies, sought Creek, Choctaw, and Chickasaw support, and from 1778 they used it in operations along the Gulf Coast and in Georgia. Creek, Choctaw, and Chickasaw warriors participated in the defense of British-held Pensacola and Mobile in the early 1780s, and some Creek took part in the defense of Savannah. When Spanish forces under Bernardo de Gálvez moved against the British Gulf posts, Indigenous responses varied, and some Choctaw and other communities shifted their relations toward Spain. The Chickasaw, long allied to Britain, contested American efforts to establish forts on the Mississippi, and Chickasaw forces under leaders including members of the Colbert family harassed American positions.

Documentary Study

Creek Diplomacy and Competing Wartime Loyalties

Evidence type: British and Spanish Indian Department correspondence, Georgia and Continental records, and later biographical scholarship on leaders such as Alexander McGillivray · Period: 1776–1784

What the documents show: Correspondence between Indian Department officials and their superiors regularly reports Creek towns declining to commit warriors on demand, demanding presents as the price of cooperation, or attending American as well as British councils. Georgia officials, for their part, recorded repeated efforts to persuade Lower Town leaders to remain at peace. Different towns therefore appear in the documents as separate political actors rather than as parts of a single Creek policy.

Alexander McGillivray: Born of a Muscogee mother of the Wind clan and a Scottish trader father, McGillivray was educated in Charleston and worked for the British Indian Department as a commissary during the war. His father, a Loyalist, lost property in Georgia, and the family's circumstances connected Alexander to British interests. Toward the war's end, his combination of Muscogee kinship standing through his mother's lineage, bilingual and literate skills, and Indian Department experience made him an increasingly influential intermediary, particularly among Upper Creek towns, and in the mid-1780s he would negotiate with Spain. Historians continue to debate how fully his influence reflected the consent of Creek towns, and how far it depended on the support of outside powers.

Limitations: Nearly all surviving documents were written by European and American officials. They record Creek decisions largely as these officials understood them, often after the fact, and they can overstate the unity or the disloyalty of Creek towns according to the writer's purposes.

As the war ended, Creek leaders faced new neighbors with new claims. Georgia, which had suffered British occupation and Indigenous raids, sought land cessions as compensation. In 1783 it obtained an agreement at Augusta with a group of Creek and Cherokee representatives ceding a large tract, an agreement that many Creek towns rejected as made by individuals with no authority to sell. Spain's possession of Florida and the Gulf coast, confirmed in 1783, gave the Creek an alternative to American pressure, and McGillivray's growing influence would lead toward the Spanish treaty of 1784 and the diplomacy examined in Chapter 60. The Choctaw and Chickasaw, sharing the pattern of local autonomy, also faced new relations with Spain and the United States without having been party to the peace that determined their circumstances.

The record for Creek diplomacy is more specific than a simple division between British supporters and American sympathizers. In September 1775, headmen associated with several Lower Creek towns wrote to British superintendent John Stuart that they regretted the quarrel among white people and preferred friendship rather than participation. A further expression of neutrality followed in 1776. These statements survived through English-language official copies and must not be mistaken for votes by every Muscogee town, yet they show a serious strategy of avoiding a costly war while keeping commerce possible. The American commissioner George Galphin and British superintendent Stuart competed to influence those decisions through councils and goods. In 1777, Galphin's conferences helped discourage military participation that British officials hoped to secure. Georgia's frontier settlers, however, often demanded a harder policy than the commissioners believed prudent, revealing that American diplomacy was divided internally as well. For Choctaw and Chickasaw communities, geographic position and older military and trading relationships produced a different range of choices. Some warriors or headmen supported British interests, while other settlements calculated that remaining at home better protected hunting, agriculture, and relations with neighbors. The Chickasaw struggle for autonomy was not reducible to loyalty to a European flag, and the Choctaw should not be treated as a unified military force at Britain's disposal. After Spain entered the war, competition for Gulf Coast trade routes widened the field of negotiation. A serious southern history must therefore follow town-level interests and the changing supply of presents rather than describing an undifferentiated 'southern Indian alliance.'

Section 59.13

Shawnee, Lenape, Wyandot, and the Contest for the Ohio Country

North of the Ohio River lay a world of towns along the Scioto, Muskingum, Miami, Maumee, and Sandusky rivers, inhabited by Shawnee, Lenape (Delaware), Wyandot, Mingo, Miami, and other peoples, many of whom had moved there during the eighteenth century under pressure from colonial expansion further east. The region was contested in several ways: by Virginia and Pennsylvania, whose competing claims centered on Fort Pitt; by Indigenous nations whose territories overlapped and whose leaders disagreed about strategy; and by the British at Detroit, who sought to hold the region for the Crown. Its Indigenous communities were politically distinct, and their wartime choices diverged.

The Shawnee, most directly affected by Kentucky settlement, were deeply divided. Cornstalk, the principal peace leader, had agreed to terms in 1774 and tried through 1775 and 1776 to keep the Shawnee out of the war. Other Shawnee, including the war leader Black Fish and the younger generation, concluded that settlement would not halt and favored active resistance. In November 1777, Cornstalk, accompanied by his son Elinipsico and another Shawnee man, traveled to Fort Randolph at Point Pleasant to warn the American commander that many Shawnee were turning toward the British. The Americans detained them, and when a militiaman was killed nearby, soldiers broke into the room where the Shawnee were held and killed Cornstalk and the others. The murder of a leader who had come under conditions of diplomatic protection destroyed the credibility of Shawnee accommodationists and hardened Shawnee opposition. Virginia officials condemned the killing, but those responsible were not convicted.

Historical Vocabulary
Territorial Claims, Neutrality, Diplomatic Protection, and Settlement

Definition. A territorial claim is an assertion of rights to land, which may rest on occupation, use, treaty, conquest, or purchase. Diplomatic protection was the customary immunity of envoys and delegates traveling under a flag of truce or council invitation. Settlement, in this context, refers to the establishment of permanent colonial farms and towns, often without the consent of Indigenous owners.

Historical context. Indigenous and European diplomatic traditions both recognized envoy protection, and violating it carried heavy political costs. Claims to territory in the Ohio Country were layered: colonial charters granted vast lands on paper, the Haudenosaunee claimed lands through conquest, and Shawnee, Lenape, and other nations occupied and used them.

Why it matters. The murder of Cornstalk, an envoy under protection, and the settlement of Kentucky beyond any Indigenous cession show why many Native leaders doubted that American promises would be kept.

Potential misunderstanding. A paper claim by a colony or a treaty with one Indigenous nation did not extinguish the rights of others. The American habit of treating a treaty with any representative as binding on all claimants is an example of how treaty language can record a government's claim without proving its validity.

The Lenape (Delaware) were divided into three groupings: the Turtle, Turkey, and Wolf divisions, associated with different leaders and towns. Their principal leaders in the early war years were White Eyes (Koquethagechton), an advocate of friendship with the Americans; John Killbuck (Gelelemend) of the Turtle division; and Captain Pipe (Hopocan), the Wolf division leader who initially shared neutrality but moved toward Britain as American commitments failed. Pennsylvania and Virginia authorities at Fort Pitt courted them, and the Lenape's central location, between the Americans and the Great Lakes nations, made their choice significant. Their wartime history, the subject of Section 59.14, shows how a policy of cooperation with the Americans broke down.

The Wyandot, who held a leading position in the Sandusky country and whose council fire among the western nations carried weight, aligned more clearly with the British. So did most Mingo and many Shawnee after 1777. British commanders at Detroit, especially Lieutenant Governor Henry Hamilton, worked to maintain this alignment through presents and supplies, and sent war parties against Kentucky and the Pennsylvania and Virginia frontiers. American propaganda labeled Hamilton the hair buyer, a charge that circulated widely but rests on little reliable evidence and served political purposes. In practice, British support was uneven, and Indigenous war parties made their own decisions about targets and timing.

The war in this region also involved a contested politics of authority. Virginia and Pennsylvania each claimed jurisdiction over the upper Ohio, and Congress's Indian commissioners sometimes contradicted state officials. Indigenous leaders, trying to determine which authority could make binding promises, found a bewildering patchwork. At the same time, within Indigenous nations the authority of civil chiefs to restrain warriors was always partial, and young men eager to defend land or avenge killings acted as they saw fit. The combination of fractured colonial and Indigenous authority made diplomacy fragile, and a single incident, such as the murder of Cornstalk, could undo months of negotiation.

The murder of Cornstalk at Fort Randolph in November 1777 illustrates a particularly destructive problem in frontier diplomacy: a leader could attempt to mediate with colonial authorities while remaining unable to control every warrior, and a militia might nevertheless punish him for acts he did not authorize. Accounts differ about the precise sequence of events, but they agree that Cornstalk and accompanying captives or visitors were killed while in colonial custody. The incident undermined trust in the protection normally due to diplomatic visitors. As war spread, Detroit's significance to Shawnee, Wyandot, and other Ohio communities lay partly in access to arms and supplies, yet reliance on British stores did not mean surrendering local objectives. Towns along the Scioto, Sandusky, and Muskingum had distinct interests in protecting fields, routes, and political authority. A British officer's report that 'the Indians' were at war consequently tells historians little about who had decided to take part, why, and for how long.

Section 59.14

The Treaty of Fort Pitt, 1778: The Lenape and the United States

On September 17, 1778, at Fort Pitt, representatives of the United States and of the Lenape (Delaware) nation signed the first treaty between the United States and an Indigenous nation. The American commissioners, Andrew Lewis and Thomas Lewis, negotiated with the Lenape leaders White Eyes, John Killbuck, and Captain Pipe. The treaty emerged from a moment of mutual need. The Americans wanted to secure the western frontier, gain access across Lenape country for an expedition against Detroit, and neutralize a nation positioned on their flank. The Lenape wanted trade goods, protection from encroaching settlers and militia, and recognition of their political standing, and White Eyes in particular hoped that a close relationship with the United States would give the Lenape a future within the new political order.

The treaty's provisions reflect these aims. It declared peace and friendship between the two parties and canceled earlier grievances. It granted American troops passage through Lenape territory and permitted the construction of a fort, later built as Fort Laurens, to protect the Lenape and to supply the expedition. It arranged for trade, with the United States promising to supply goods, and for the Lenape to provide warriors and scouts to assist American forces. It addressed the procedure for trying offenders against either side. And it included a notable provision on territory and political standing: the United States promised to guarantee the Lenape their territorial rights as established by earlier treaties, and the treaty contemplated that other friendly Indigenous nations might join the Lenape in forming a state with the Lenape at its head and with representation in Congress, if Congress approved.

The treaty is also a document of military access: it allowed American forces onto Lenape lands.

The treaty relationship broke down quickly. Fort Laurens, built in the autumn of 1778 on the Tuscarawas River under General Lachlan McIntosh, was poorly supplied, and in the winter of 1779 it was besieged by British-allied Indigenous forces and its garrison nearly starved before relief arrived. The Americans failed to deliver promised trade goods and, more seriously, to restrain militia who attacked Indigenous people regardless of treaty. White Eyes died in November 1778 under circumstances disputed in the sources: American accounts attribute his death to smallpox while he accompanied an American force, but Lenape contemporaries suspected that he had been killed, and the question cannot be settled with certainty. His death removed the treaty's principal Lenape advocate.

Over the next two years, the Lenape divided. Captain Pipe and the Wolf division, disillusioned with American failures and facing pressure from the Wyandot and British, withdrew to the Sandusky country and aligned with the British. John Killbuck's Turtle division, centered at Coshocton, tried to keep a position of friendship with the Americans while remaining out of the fighting. In April 1781, Colonel Daniel Brodhead led an American force against Coshocton, destroying the town and killing a number of Lenape captives, including prisoners taken from a group that had been regarded as friendly. The treaty relationship, already damaged, collapsed. The statehood proposal, never ratified, became a lost possibility that Lenape and later historians have remembered as evidence of the road not taken, though how seriously the American leadership ever intended it remains uncertain.

The treaty's importance does not depend on its fate. It established the form of the nation-to-nation treaty as the vehicle of United States relations with Indigenous nations, a form that would be used for the next century. It also exposed the central problem that would persist: American officials could sign a treaty, but the country's governments, militias, and settlers did not act as a single authority bound by it. The Lenape experience in 1778 and 1781 taught many Indigenous leaders that treaty guarantees from the United States were only as strong as the institutions that could enforce them.

A reading of the treaty by article is more revealing than a summary of its promises. The earlier provisions placed peace and friendship alongside a proposed military relationship; the agreement also provided for travel through Lenape territory and the possibility of American military facilities. Article VI then tied an unusually broad promise of territorial protection to continued Lenape fidelity, and raised the possibility that friendly Indigenous nations might join in forming a state led by the Lenape and represented in Congress. Its final condition was indispensable: nothing about that plan was conclusive without congressional approval. The text recorded a proposal requiring a future political decision, not the creation of a state or an unconditional American pledge to create one. This distinction matters because the treaty can be read simultaneously as evidence of Lenape diplomatic ambition, American wartime dependence on Indigenous cooperation, and the limits of congressional enforcement. The later failure of those provisions should be studied alongside the treaty's words, rather than retrospectively attributing identical motives to all its signatories.

Section 59.15

War in the Ohio and Wabash Valleys, 1778–1781

The war in the West was a war of raids, small expeditions, and sieges rather than of grand campaigns, and it fell heavily on civilians. Its principal theaters were Kentucky, the upper Ohio, and the Ohio and Wabash valleys, and its participants included American militia and Continental troops, British officers and Loyalist rangers based at Detroit, and Indigenous war parties drawn mainly from the Shawnee, Wyandot, Mingo, Miami, and other nations, with the Lenape divided.

Kentucky was the focal point. Settlement began in earnest in the mid-1770s, with stations at Harrodsburg, Boonesborough, and elsewhere, and from 1777 Shawnee and other warriors besieged the stations and attacked travelers. In February 1778, Black Fish captured Daniel Boone and a party of salt makers, adopted Boone into a Shawnee family, and later, in September, led a prolonged siege of Boonesborough that failed to take the station. The Kentucky campaigns were driven by Indigenous objectives, to drive settlers from lands the Shawnee regarded as theirs, and Black Fish's capture and adoption of prisoners followed Shawnee practices of integrating captives, a subject treated in Chapter 53.

George Rogers Clark, a Virginia militia officer, took the war across the Ohio. In 1778 and 1779 he led a small force of Virginia militia to the Illinois country, where he secured the surrender of Kaskaskia and Cahokia and, in February 1779, after a winter march, of Vincennes, capturing Lieutenant Governor Hamilton. Clark's success relied on diplomacy as well as arms: he negotiated with Indigenous leaders of the Illinois country and the Wabash, alternating assurances with threats. In 1780 and 1782 he led raids against Shawnee towns on the Miami and Scioto rivers, burning towns and fields. These campaigns were intended in part to deprive Indigenous communities of their food and to keep them from supporting British operations, and their effects resembled those of the campaigns in the East.

British commanders and their Indigenous allies fought back. In 1780, a British and allied force under Captain Henry Bird, with Indigenous warriors, swept into Kentucky and captured Ruddell's and Martin's Stations, taking prisoners. In 1781, a Virginia party under Archibald Lochry traveling to join Clark was ambushed and destroyed on the Ohio by Mohawk and other warriors under Joseph Brant, a reminder that northern Haudenosaunee fighters operated in the Ohio region. American raids on Indigenous towns and Indigenous raids on settlements followed each other in a cycle in which each side cited the other's acts as justification.

Historians distinguish carefully between direct Indigenous participation and the objectives of European and American commanders. British officers at Detroit wanted to hold the Ohio Country and distract American forces, but they did not command their Indigenous allies and often could not control their actions. Indigenous war parties made their own decisions about when to fight, whom to target, and when to withdraw. Americans, too, cited British direction in explaining Indigenous raids, a convenient interpretation that downplayed the Indigenous grievances and objectives behind them. The sources reflect both the genuine collaboration between Detroit and its allies and the persistent tendency of outside observers to attribute Indigenous military actions to European instigation.

Section 59.16

Gnadenhutten, Crawford's Expedition, and the Violence of 1782

Among the Lenape lived a group who had embraced Christianity under the Moravian missionaries David Zeisberger and John Heckewelder. These Christian Lenape, together with Mahican and other converts, lived in mission towns on the Tuscarawas River in Ohio, including Gnadenhutten, Schoenbrunn, and Salem. The Moravian communities practiced pacifism and farmed, and they attempted to remain neutral. Their position pleased neither side: American frontier people suspected them of aiding the British, and British-allied Indigenous communities, notably the Wyandot and Captain Pipe's Lenape, suspected them of aiding the Americans. In September 1781, a British and allied force removed the Moravian Indians from their towns, relocating them to Captive Town on the Sandusky River, where they suffered from hunger and cold during the winter.

In early 1782, hungry and with crops standing unharvested in their old towns, a number of the Christian Lenape returned to Gnadenhutten and nearby sites to gather corn. On March 8, a force of Pennsylvania militia under Colonel David Williamson arrived. The militia, who had come in part in response to recent raids in which settlers had been killed, accused the Christian Lenape of involvement, and they rounded up the residents. After debate among the militia, whose members voted to kill their captives, the Christian Lenape were confined in two buildings and, over the following hours, killed with mallets and other weapons. More than ninety people died, among them women and children. The Moravians' reported last hours included prayer and hymn singing; these descriptions come largely from the testimony of survivors and later Moravian accounts, and while they are consistent with the community's religious life, they reflect the interpretive framing of their sources. A small number of people escaped to carry the news.

The militia then burned the town. The perpetrators later justified the killings by claiming that the Christian Lenape had taken part in raids and had possessed items belonging to settlers, claims for which the evidence is unconvincing and which the survivors and Moravian witnesses contested. The National Park Service and most historians describe the event as a massacre of unarmed noncombatants. No one was punished. The victims were, by their own commitment and by the testimony of observers, pacifists who had been trying to avoid the war.

The killings triggered further violence. In May and June 1782, Colonel William Crawford, a Pennsylvania officer and acquaintance of Washington, led roughly five hundred militia toward the Sandusky towns, with the intention of destroying them. Many of the volunteers, historians have argued, were motivated by a desire for revenge. Warned of the approach, the Wyandot, Lenape, and British forces, together with Shawnee and others, met the expedition near the Upper Sandusky and defeated it in a battle on June 4 to 6 after a retreat turned into a rout. Crawford was captured. Among his captors were Lenape who had suffered from American raids, and according to the account of the American surgeon Dr. John Knight, who was a witness before escaping, Captain Pipe told Crawford that he was being punished in retaliation for Gnadenhutten. Crawford was tortured and burned to death on or about June 11. Knight's narrative is the principal source for the incident, and while its general outline is accepted, its details were published with the intent to inflame American opinion.

Historical complexity: The violence cannot be reduced to a simple cycle of revenge. The Christian Lenape were victims of a militia acting without effective oversight, and the Crawford expedition was a militia undertaking shaped by the same atmosphere. Captain Pipe's Lenape, who had their own grievances, made their own decision about Crawford. The responsibility of particular actors differed, and the sources written by Americans and by Moravians reflect their own interests.

The events of 1782 must not be told as a story in which all Lenape were combatants. Many Lenape had sought neutrality and paid with their lives. The Moravian converts, committed to nonviolence, show that Indigenous responses to the war included principled refusal as well as alliance or resistance, and the massacre underscored that neither neutrality nor religious conversion offered protection from frontier militias. The consequences extended beyond the Ohio Country: Indigenous leaders who observed American officials failing to punish the killers drew conclusions about the reliability of the United States as a treaty partner. In the following years, surviving Moravian Lenape moved to Canada, where they founded communities such as Fairfield on the Thames River, while other Lenape moved west, beginning the series of migrations that would carry the nation across the continent over the following century.

The written evidence of the Crawford expedition is strongly mediated by survivors who sought to explain defeat and terrible retaliation to American readers. John Knight's later account is important, but it is a participant's narrative with an identifiable point of view; it cannot reproduce every exchange between prisoners, captors, and those deciding punishment. The same caution applies to Christian Lenape survivors of Gnadenhutten, whose testimony was often transmitted through Moravian missionaries. Recognizing the limits of these records does not obscure responsibility for the slaughter of unarmed people. It clarifies which details are documented, which were reconstructed later, and how a sequence of atrocities could become arguments for yet more collective violence.

Section 59.17

War, Refugees, Women, Households, and the Survival of Indigenous Communities

Behind every campaign were households. Across the regions examined in this chapter, the war's most widespread effect was to destroy the conditions of ordinary life: to burn the towns where people lived, to ruin the fields and stores that fed them, to scatter families, and to leave communities dependent on outside supply. The Haudenosaunee after 1779, the Cherokee after 1776 and 1780, the Lenape after 1781 and 1782, the Shawnee after the raids on their towns, and the Oneida and Stockbridge communities that had supported the Americans all experienced versions of this loss, and their experiences shared common features even as their politics differed.

Food was the central problem. Most of these peoples were farmers as well as hunters, and agriculture, organized and performed largely by women, provided the bulk of their diet. A campaign that burned a town and its fields in the late summer did more than destroy property: it removed the next year's seed and the winter's food. Communities that survived the invasion faced hunger, and hunger weakened people against cold and disease. Refugees concentrated at Fort Niagara, Detroit, and other British posts and depended on British rations that were often inadequate. The winter of 1779 to 1780 in particular is remembered as a time of exceptional suffering. Smallpox, which moved widely across the continent in these years, struck some displaced communities, and its effects compounded those of war and hunger, a subject examined more generally in Chapter 40.

Civilian History Study

Refugees, Food Systems, Captivity, and Reconstruction

Food systems. Towns in the Finger Lakes and Genesee Valley had substantial permanent houses, orchards, and fields, documented by the very soldiers who destroyed them. The loss was of a developed agricultural landscape, built and maintained through generations of women's labor and land stewardship, not of improvised camps.

Refugee settlements. Displaced Haudenosaunee, Lenape, Shawnee, and other families clustered near Niagara, Detroit, and on the Sandusky and Maumee rivers. Within them, women resumed gardening where land allowed, rebuilt households, and reestablished kin ties across the new settlements. Communities that had been neighbors before the war sometimes shared refuge, and new political relationships formed.

Captivity. Captive-taking was practiced by all parties. Indigenous communities took captives to replace lost relatives, and some were adopted into families; settlers and militia took Indigenous captives, and in some cases sold or enslaved them. Captivity could mean adoption, forced labor, ransom, or death, and its meanings differed by nation and circumstance, a topic treated more fully in Chapter 53.

Kinship responsibility and reconstruction. Kin networks distributed the burdens of care, taking in orphans and widows and redistributing food. Women's councils and clan matrons continued to guide decisions about where to settle and whom to support. The rebuilding of the Haudenosaunee, Cherokee, and Lenape communities after the war was substantially an achievement of women's labor and kinship organization, though the documentary record, dominated by officials, rarely names them.

Source note: The evidence for civilian experience is uneven: soldiers' journals record destruction, officials record rations and numbers of refugees, and oral tradition preserves memory of suffering. Statistical estimates of deaths are rarely reliable and should be treated with caution.

Indigenous communities rebuilt as best they could. Cherokee towns replanted and reconstructed houses after 1776 and again after 1780 and 1781. Haudenosaunee refugees at Niagara and elsewhere sowed what ground they could and, when the war ended, began deciding where they would settle permanently, a process that led to the Grand River community in Canada and to the persistence of communities in western New York. The Oneida, though allied with the Americans, found their towns raided and burned in 1780 and many of their people sheltering near Schenectady, depending on American rations, while still contributing to American military efforts. The Stockbridge and Lenape sought new homes. In every case, the work of reconstruction fell largely on women and elders, and its pace was set by what the surrounding political circumstances would permit.

The war also altered Indigenous demography and geography. Communities that had lived in long-established towns moved, merged, or divided. New settlements formed in Ohio, at the Grand River, and on the Tennessee, and the movement of peoples westward and northward that had been underway since the early eighteenth century accelerated. The Revolution did not create these patterns, but it intensified them, and the displacements of 1776 to 1783 were among the first of many forced and semi-forced movements that would characterize the following century.

At Fort Niagara and other places of refuge, survival depended on logistics that traditional military narratives rarely count. Families needed shelter, firewood, blankets, and food at precisely the moment when farming villages and stored provisions had been destroyed. British officers could issue rations, but an army's supply structure was not designed to replace the annual production of numerous Indigenous households indefinitely. People relocated with surviving livestock, implements, children, and elderly relatives where possible, while others arrived with almost nothing. Refugee communities were not merely passive recipients of imperial charity: they organized work, maintained kin connections, sought news of missing relatives, and attempted to influence decisions about returning to damaged homelands. Women had particular responsibilities for restoring seed supplies and preserving family relationships across settlements. Captivity created further complexity, since returning a prisoner who had been incorporated into a household might disrupt new relationships as well as repair old ones. Displaced Haudenosaunee and Cherokee families experienced different local conditions, but both confronted the challenge of recovering political institutions and food production after the ruin of towns. A military campaign's end could therefore mark the beginning, rather than the conclusion, of its most prolonged social consequences.

Section 59.18

The Treaty of Paris, 1783: Independence Without Indigenous Consent

The fighting in the East effectively ended with the British surrender at Yorktown in October 1781, but peace negotiations dragged on and were conducted in Paris among representatives of Britain, the United States, France, and Spain. No Indigenous nation took part. The American delegation, John Adams, Benjamin Franklin, and John Jay, negotiated directly with British representatives, and the preliminary articles of November 1782 were converted into the definitive Treaty of Paris, signed on September 3, 1783. By that treaty, Britain acknowledged the United States as free, sovereign, and independent, and the two governments agreed on boundaries that ran from the Atlantic to the Mississippi River, bounded on the north by a line through the Great Lakes and on the south by the thirty-first parallel.

The boundaries encompassed an enormous territory, including most of the Ohio Country, the Great Lakes shore, and large parts of the Southeast, that was occupied and governed by Indigenous nations. The treaty did not mention them as parties, and it made no provision for their consent to the transfer of whatever sovereignty or title Britain claimed to have had. Britain, for its part, ceded the territory without consulting the Indigenous allies who had fought for it, and in some cases had been assured that their lands would be protected. In practice, Britain continued to hold posts at Detroit, Niagara, Oswego, and Michilimackinac within the American boundary, using Britain's own interpretation of treaty obligations to justify retention, a matter that carried consequences examined in Chapter 60.

The distinction between European territorial claims and Indigenous sovereignty is the central point. A boundary drawn in Paris defined the limits of what Britain and the United States would recognize in dealing with each other; it did not alter the fact that Indigenous nations occupied and governed the lands in question. Many Indigenous leaders understood this. Britain's former Native allies, informed of the peace, expressed anger and a sense of betrayal to British officials, who responded with assurances and with supplies while avoiding commitments to renewed war. Joseph Brant and others pressed the British for support and for land on which their people could settle, which led directly to the arrangements of 1784.

The United States, for its part, developed a theory to justify its claims. Many American leaders argued that Britain's cession and the American victory entitled the United States to treat Indigenous nations that had fought for Britain as conquered peoples, free to dictate terms and to claim their lands by right of conquest. Congress's actions in 1783 reflected a more complicated position, asserting that only the national government could deal with Indigenous nations over land, and urging a policy of negotiated purchase to avoid war. In practice, the conquest argument shaped the treaties of 1784 and 1785, whose negotiators treated Indigenous representatives as defeated enemies. Indigenous leaders, who regarded themselves as undefeated or as independent nations who had never been conquered, rejected the premise.

The treaty's sequence of articles helps explain its silence. Article I acknowledged the United States as independent; Article II defined boundaries reaching through the Great Lakes toward the Mississippi and south to Spanish Florida. Other provisions addressed debts, property, Loyalist claims, the withdrawal of British troops, and navigation. None established a council with the Haudenosaunee, Shawnee, Cherokee, or other Indigenous peoples whose territories fell within the internationally described boundaries. That omission did not mean Indigenous governments had ceased to exist; it meant that the negotiators treated their territories as matters of Anglo-American diplomacy. Even the clause requiring British forces to withdraw from American territory did not immediately transfer the interior posts: Britain maintained positions around the Great Lakes after the peace, further complicating Indigenous diplomacy. The treaty therefore created overlapping orders of obligation. The United States asserted international title against Britain; Britain retained relationships with Indigenous allies; and Indigenous nations continued to claim land under their own laws and treaties. Explaining these orders separately is essential if students are to understand why peace between governments could coexist with continuing conflict along the Ohio frontier.

Section 59.19

Fort Stanwix and Grand River, 1784: Competing Postwar Settlements

In the autumn of 1784, two settlements shaped the postwar world of the Haudenosaunee. In October the United States held a treaty council at Fort Stanwix with representatives of the Six Nations, and within days the British governor of Quebec, Frederick Haldimand, issued a proclamation granting land on the Grand River to Haudenosaunee who had adhered to the Crown. The two arrangements were separate and governed different peoples, and the contrast between them illuminates the divided political landscape that the war had produced.

The American commissioners at Fort Stanwix, Oliver Wolcott, Richard Butler, and Arthur Lee, arrived with instructions to treat the Six Nations as a defeated people. They demanded the surrender of hostages as a guarantee that prisoners would be returned, and they pressed for large land cessions. The Indigenous delegation, which included Seneca leaders such as Cornplanter and Red Jacket (Sagoyewatha), as well as Onondaga, Oneida, and some Cayuga and Tuscarora representatives, was not unified and was negotiating from weakness after the devastation of 1779 and the years of dislocation that followed. Joseph Brant and most of the Mohawk, now settling in Canada, did not attend. The treaty signed on October 22 established peace, required the return of prisoners, took hostages, and obliged the Six Nations to cede their claims to lands in the west, including the Ohio Country, and a strip near Niagara. It guaranteed the Oneida and Tuscarora in their lands, reflecting their support for the Americans, and it set a boundary within which the remaining Six Nations lands in New York lay.

The American document and the British proclamation belong in comparison precisely because neither could be understood simply as the unchallenged transfer of empty territory.

Section 59.20

Indigenous North America After the Revolution: Sovereignty, Survival, and the New Republic

By the end of 1784 the Revolution's immediate consequences for Indigenous nations had become visible, and they varied sharply. The Oneida and Tuscarora, who had sided with the Americans, found that the alliance did not protect them from losses, from raids during the war, or from the pressure of New York land buyers afterward, though the Fort Stanwix treaty did guarantee their lands. The Mohawk, Cayuga, and others who had fought for Britain lost their homelands in the Mohawk Valley and the Finger Lakes country and became a community in exile in Canada. The Seneca and Onondaga retained land in western New York but under conditions of weakness and pressure. The Cherokee, Creek, and other southern nations retained their homelands, though with reduced territory and under growing pressure from Georgia and the Carolinas. The Lenape, Shawnee, Wyandot, and other peoples of the Ohio Country had not been defeated in the way American officials claimed, but they faced an expanding republic that asserted rights over their lands.

Diplomatic leverage shifted. The British Empire's withdrawal from the thirteen colonies removed the principal rival that Indigenous nations had been able to play against colonial governments, and with the French gone since 1763, the old strategy of balancing powers narrowed. Nations such as the Creek, who could turn to Spain, and those of the Ohio and Great Lakes, who retained access to British posts that Britain kept in defiance of the peace, preserved some room to maneuver. Others, such as the Haudenosaunee remaining in New York, had little. American authority was itself divided between a weak national government and assertive states, which gave Indigenous leaders both opportunities and dangers.

Indigenous nations rebuilt their governments and economies. Councils reconvened in new settlements, clan structures continued to organize kinship, and women's agricultural work resumed. Many communities adopted new economic strategies, including commerce with American and British traders, farming adjusted to new locations, and, at Grand River, the creation of a settlement under a British land grant. The Haudenosaunee continued to conduct their ceremonial and political life under the Great Law, and the Confederacy's council fire, though covered during the war, was later rekindled, with the Six Nations at Grand River maintaining its own council. Some nations maintained British connections for pragmatic reasons: British posts continued to supply trade goods and presents, and British officials, through the Indian Department, continued to deal with allies as political partners.

Historical Synthesis

Indigenous Sovereignty Before and After American Independence

Before 1775

  • Indigenous nations governed their territories and dealt with European empires through diplomacy that recognized their political standing
  • The British Crown asserted the Proclamation principle of negotiated cession while permitting practices that undermined it
  • Indigenous nations could play European powers against one another

After 1784

  • Indigenous nations still governed themselves and held land, but a new republic claimed rights over the continent's interior
  • The conquest theory and the treaty as an instrument of land cession entered American practice
  • The balance of European powers was reduced, though not eliminated, in the Ohio Country, Great Lakes, and Gulf South
  • Many communities were displaced, divided, or rebuilt in new places

Why the pattern matters: American independence was not Indigenous independence. The Revolution replaced a distant imperial government that had often restrained, imperfectly, the western expansion of colonists with a republic whose citizens and states directly pursued that expansion, while Indigenous nations retained functioning governments and rejected claims that they had been conquered.

The American claim of conquest and Indigenous insistence on sovereignty collided most directly over the western lands. The Fort Stanwix cessions, the treaties negotiated with Ohio nations beginning in early 1785, and the stream of settlers moving down the Ohio River meant that the unresolved question of who owned and governed the Ohio Country would define the next phase of Indigenous history. Indigenous nations did not disappear from American politics after 1783; on the contrary, they were central actors in the diplomacy and warfare of the following decade, as Chapter 60 and the chapters on the Northwest Indian Confederacy and later resistance movements will show.

The chapter's conclusion is therefore that Indigenous peoples neither disappeared from the political life of the continent nor surrendered their sovereignty with Britain's defeat. They emerged from the Revolution with their governments intact but their territories contested, their populations disrupted by war, hunger, and disease, and their diplomatic options narrowed. The unresolved conditions they confronted in 1784, an expansionist republic, contested land claims, British posts on American soil, and divided Indigenous communities seeking new foundations, form the starting point for Chapter 60.

Comparison across the continent shows why there was no uniform Indigenous result in 1784. Communities that had fought for Britain faced relocation or new American demands; some that had supported the United States discovered that wartime alliance supplied little protection against private land purchases and state governments; and others preserved a strategic distance from both. The distribution of wartime suffering was equally uneven: displaced Haudenosaunee families around Niagara and the Grand River, Christian Lenape survivors after Gnadenhutten, and southern communities rebuilding burned towns faced different obstacles. Yet they shared a recurring political problem. The new republic often interpreted imperial boundary cessions as authority over land, while Indigenous communities understood treaties as contingent relationships between governing peoples. Because those interpretations could not be reconciled by signatures alone, the years after 1784 saw new councils, confederation initiatives, and conflicts. Those are the next chapter's history; the point here is that the Revolution produced a new set of negotiating conditions rather than a terminal judgment on Native self-government.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has reconstructed the American Revolution as a conflict that Indigenous nations experienced as a struggle over land, sovereignty, diplomacy, and survival. It has argued that the Revolution was at once a colonial rebellion, an imperial war, a regional rivalry among Britain, Spain, and the United States, and an Indigenous crisis, and that the Patriot–Loyalist framework cannot capture the political reasoning of Native nations.

The roots of Indigenous responses lay in the years before 1775. The cessions of 1768, the pressure on Kentucky and the Ohio frontier, Lord Dunmore's War, and the Sycamore Shoals purchase showed that colonial governments and speculators were willing to treat Indigenous land as negotiable, and that Indigenous nations disagreed among themselves over who could cede what. These experiences made Native leaders skeptical of the assurances offered by both Britain and the Continental Congress in 1775 and 1776.

Neutrality was an active strategy. Councils, treaty negotiations, and attempts to restrain warriors were the means by which many nations tried to stay out of the war. Its failure, in the Haudenosaunee case, was not a failure of Indigenous politics but a consequence of that politics' consensus-based structure under the pressure of armies, missionaries, supply shortages, and settler hostility. Indigenous alliances never represented national unanimity: the Six Nations, the Cherokee, the Lenape, the Shawnee, and the Creek all divided among towns, leaders, and generations.

The northern war showed what that division cost. At Oriskany in 1777, Haudenosaunee fought Haudenosaunee, and the Confederacy's council fire was covered. British-allied leaders such as Joseph Brant and the networks sustained by Molly Brant conducted raids on the borderlands in 1778, while Stockbridge-Munsee and Oneida participants served the Americans at heavy cost. In 1779, the Sullivan–Clinton campaign deliberately destroyed Haudenosaunee towns, orchards, and food stores, produced a refugee crisis at Fort Niagara, and affected all six nations.

Outside New York, the war was geographically extensive and politically varied. The Wabanaki, Mi'kmaq, and Wolastoqiyik negotiated with Massachusetts at Watertown in 1776 and with British officials in Nova Scotia. The Cherokee divided between accommodation and resistance, suffered the destruction of dozens of towns in 1776, ceded land in 1777, and saw Dragging Canoe lead resisting communities to the Tennessee River. The Creek, Choctaw, and Chickasaw pursued town-level strategies in an imperial borderland contested by Britain, Spain, and the United States.

In the Ohio Country, the murder of Cornstalk in 1777 and the failure of the Treaty of Fort Pitt of 1778 showed how fragile treaty diplomacy was in a landscape of competing colonial authorities and militias. The treaty, the first between the United States and an Indigenous nation, promised territorial guarantees and contemplated a Native-led state, but neither was realized. Raids, counterraids, and campaigns, including Clark's operations and the destruction of Coshocton, devastated communities on all sides.

The violence of 1782 revealed the danger faced even by those who sought neutrality. The killing of more than ninety Christian Lenape at Gnadenhutten, and the retaliatory killing of Crawford after his expedition's defeat, show a frontier in which official authority was weak and reprisal cycles were self-reinforcing. They also confirm that Lenape communities cannot be treated as uniformly combatants.

Throughout, civilians bore the greatest costs. Destroyed settlements, ruined fields, hunger, disease, captivity, and displacement fell especially on women, children, and the elderly, and the rebuilding of communities depended on women's agricultural labor and kinship networks. The chapter has treated these experiences as central to the history, not as incidental details of campaigns.

The peace settlements brought independence on unequal terms. The Treaty of Paris of 1783 recognized American independence and boundaries without consulting the Indigenous nations whose lands were involved. In 1784, the United States treated the Six Nations as defeated at Fort Stanwix, while Britain provided land at the Grand River under the Haldimand Proclamation. Treaty language recorded the claims of governments without proving their validity or Indigenous consent.

The chapter's conclusion is that the Revolution did not end Indigenous sovereignty or political life. Indigenous nations emerged with intact governments, contested territories, disrupted communities, and narrowed diplomatic options, facing a new republic that asserted expansive claims. Chapter 60 takes up the continuing struggle that followed.

A Haudenosaunee metaphor for the place and process of a nation's or Confederacy's deliberation; its covering signified that a council could no longer act as one.

The network of treaty relationships linking the Haudenosaunee and British colonial governments, examined in Chapter 56.
Indian Department
The British imperial agency of superintendents, deputies, and interpreters that managed relations and trade with Indigenous nations.
Superintendent of Indian Affairs
A Crown official, in the north and south, responsible for diplomacy and trade with Indigenous nations.
Treaty of Fort Stanwix (1768)
A cession negotiated with Sir William Johnson that moved the Proclamation line west and covered lands claimed by several Indigenous nations.
Lord Dunmore's War
The 1774 conflict between Virginia and the Shawnee and allies over Ohio River lands, ending in negotiation without resolving land disputes.
Clan matron
A senior woman in Haudenosaunee society who nominated and could remove chiefs and held authority over land and household resources.
Beloved Woman
A Cherokee title for a woman honored with a voice in council on matters of war, peace, and captives.
Battle of Oriskany
The August 6, 1777 ambush of an American militia column near Fort Stanwix, in which Haudenosaunee fought on both sides.
Loyalist
A colonist who supported the Crown during the Revolution; a label that does not describe Indigenous allies of Britain.
Patriot
A colonist who supported the revolutionary cause; a label that does not describe Indigenous allies of the Americans.
Scorched-earth campaign
A military operation designed to destroy the material base of a community, including towns, fields, and food stores, as in 1776 and 1779.

The 1779 American invasion of Haudenosaunee country that destroyed dozens of towns and food supplies.
Refugee
A person displaced by war and dependent on others, as at Fort Niagara after 1779.

A name used by Americans for Dragging Canoe's followers and towns on Chickamauga Creek; the towns were Cherokee, and the term does not denote a separate people.

Conventional divisions of Muscogee towns on the Coosa-Tallapoosa and Chattahoochee rivers, with differing exposure to neighbors; not a division into separate nations.

The 1777 agreements by which Cherokee delegates ceded land after the American campaigns of 1776.

The customary immunity of envoys, violated in the murder of Cornstalk.

The first treaty between the United States and an Indigenous nation, with the Lenape, contemplating a Native-led state subject to congressional approval.

A pacifist Christian community of Lenape and others under missionaries Zeisberger and Heckewelder, including Gnadenhutten.

The March 1782 killing by Pennsylvania militia of more than ninety unarmed Christian Lenape.
Treaty of Paris (1783)
The Anglo-American peace recognizing United States independence and boundaries without Indigenous participation.
Conquest theory
The American argument that Indigenous nations allied with Britain had been defeated and could be treated as having forfeited land.
Hostage (in treaty practice)
A person held as a guarantee of compliance, as at Fort Stanwix in 1784.
Haldimand Proclamation
The October 25, 1784 grant of land along the Grand River to Haudenosaunee and other Indigenous allies of the Crown.
Grand River settlement
The community in present-day Ontario formed by British-allied Haudenosaunee and others after the war.
Sovereignty
The inherent authority of a people to govern itself and its territory, which Indigenous nations retained after the Revolution though contested by the United States.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has argued that the American Revolution looks different when its Indigenous participants are placed at the center. Instead of a war between colonists and a king, it becomes a many-sided contest in which Indigenous nations made decisions grounded in their own governments, histories, and interests, and in which the choices of Oneida, Mohawk, Cherokee, Lenape, Shawnee, Creek, Mi'kmaq, and others helped shape the war itself.

It also reveals a larger process: the transformation of the relationship between Indigenous nations and the powers around them. The Revolution removed Britain from most of the territory south of the Great Lakes and east of the Mississippi and created a republic whose citizens and governments pursued western land directly. The tools that would define the following decades, the conquest argument, the treaty as an instrument of cession, the pressure of settlement and state governments, were already visible in the treaties of 1783 and 1784.

What survived is as important as what was lost. Indigenous governments continued to function, councils continued to meet, kinship and women's labor rebuilt communities, and nations such as the Haudenosaunee reconstituted themselves in new places while maintaining their political traditions. The refusal of many Indigenous leaders to accept that they had been conquered would shape the resistance that Chapter 60 and the chapters on the Northwest Indian Confederacy, Tecumseh, and the War of 1812 examine.

Within the larger textbook, this chapter marks a hinge. Volume V has traced Indigenous power in an age of empires, in which Native diplomacy could balance rival European states. Volume VI will trace nations facing expanding settler states. Chapter 59 shows how the first gave way to the second, not through a single defeat but through a war that left Indigenous nations politically intact and strategically more exposed than before.
