Chapter Opening

In the first days of September 1886, a small group of Chiricahua Apache men, women, and children surrendered to the United States Army in the borderlands of Arizona Territory. Within days, they were placed on a railroad bound for Florida. The campaign had ended. For the Chiricahua people, a longer ordeal was only beginning.

The surrender of Geronimo, Naiche, and their followers is often remembered as the final scene of the American conquest of the Southwest: a famous warrior brought to bay, a general's triumph, a frontier closed. That version of events is not false, but it is badly incomplete. It centers a single man, treats the end of the shooting as the end of the story, and leaves almost unexamined what the United States did next. In the weeks after the surrender, the federal government transported to the eastern states not only the people who had fought in the final campaign but also hundreds of Chiricahua families who had remained at peace near Fort Apache, and even men who were then serving in the United States Army as scouts. The military campaigns of 1885–1886 ended in September. The imprisonment of the Chiricahua people continued until 1913 and 1914.

This chapter therefore asks a question that the surrender itself cannot answer. Why did the last Chiricahua resistance continue despite overwhelming American and Mexican military pressure, and why did surrender lead not to peace in the Apache homeland but to the imprisonment and exile of an entire people? Answering it requires attention to three levels of history at once. The first is the political and military history of 1884–1886: the unstable reservation settlement that followed George Crook's 1883 expedition into the Sierra Madre, the departure of May 1885, the pursuit across Arizona, New Mexico, Sonora, and Chihuahua, the negotiations of March 1886, and the final surrender. The second is the history of captivity: Fort Marion and Fort Pickens in Florida, Mount Vernon Barracks in Alabama, and Fort Sill in Oklahoma Territory, together with the removal of Chiricahua children to Carlisle Indian Industrial School in Pennsylvania. The third is the history of survival and memory: how Apache families preserved kinship, leadership, ceremony, and community across twenty-seven years of custody, and how later generations have told the story.

A governing interpretive principle of the chapter concerns Geronimo himself. Known to the Chiricahua as Goyaałé, he was a Bedonkohe-born man of great spiritual reputation and military experience, and he became one of the best-known Indigenous figures in the history of the United States. His fame, however, has often obscured the structure of Chiricahua political life. He was not the supreme chief of all Apache peoples, a description that circulated widely in newspapers and later popular culture. The Fort Sill Apache Tribe, whose citizens descend from Chiricahua prisoners of war, describes him as an important medicine man and leader rather than as such a chief. Naiche, a son of Cochise, held a distinct and in some respects more formal position of leadership, and other leaders, among them Chihuahua, Loco, Chato, Mangus, and Nana, made different decisions for reasons that deserve to be understood on their own terms. The chapter gives proportionate attention to each.

The chapter also depends on a distinction that the events of 1886 make unavoidable: surrender and captivity are separate historical events. The agreement that ended the campaign, and what each side understood it to promise, is a matter of recorded and conflicting testimony. The decision to deport the whole Chiricahua population was made by federal and military authorities, was extended to people who had not taken part in the final campaign, and was not the product of any individual trial. Understanding why those who had cooperated with the Army were nonetheless imprisoned requires examining the security reasoning of officials, the political pressures on commanders, and the limited power that Apache people possessed to shape the outcome.

The evidence for this history is unusually rich and unusually uneven. Military reports, telegrams, and diplomatic correspondence survive in large quantities. So do transcriptions of conferences conducted through interpreters, newspaper coverage that frequently exaggerated and invented, memoirs by officers, and a mediated life history published under Geronimo's name in 1906. Apache testimony, recorded decades later in interviews and preserved in family and tribal histories, offers essential perspectives that the official record does not contain. Each kind of source was produced under particular conditions, for particular audiences, and none can be read as a neutral transcript of events. The chapter's source windows are designed to teach students to read these records critically and against one another.

This chapter also has a defined place in the larger textbook. Chapter 79 treats the Apache Wars through Crook's 1883 expedition, including the Bascom Affair, the Camp Grant Massacre, Victorio's War, and Cibecue Creek, and this chapter does not retell them. Chapter 51 treats Apache worlds before the nineteenth-century wars. Chapter 81 turns to the destruction of the buffalo economy on the Plains. The present chapter closes the major Southwestern military narrative, and it deliberately avoids turning into a general history of western conquest or of reservation policy, which Chapter 84 compares across the United States and Canada. The boarding-school experience of Chiricahua children is examined here only as one family-centered case within the wider history of assimilation taken up in Chapters 87 and 88.

The central argument can be stated briefly. The final Chiricahua campaigns emerged from unresolved conflicts over homeland, security, political authority, and the reliability of government promises. American military pressure and negotiation brought about the surrender of Geronimo, Naiche, and their followers, but the United States extended punishment to the wider Chiricahua community through deportation and prolonged imprisonment. The campaigns ended; Apache society survived, through the leadership, family networks, cultural continuity, and political determination of people whose history extends far beyond 1886. Today the descendants of those prisoners belong to the Fort Sill Apache Tribe in Oklahoma and the Mescalero Apache Tribe in New Mexico, among other communities, and their own historical accounts are treated in this chapter as indispensable evidence rather than as a supplement to military records.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the reservation settlement that followed Crook's 1883 expedition remained unstable, distinguishing Chiricahua expectations from the expectations of military and agency officials.
  2. Distinguish among spiritual authority, military leadership, and formal political authority in Chiricahua communities, and apply the distinction to Geronimo, Naiche, and other leaders.
  3. Reconstruct the departure of May 1885 and evaluate the competing explanations offered by participants and officials.
  4. Analyze how mountains, water, borders, and distance shaped the capabilities of small mobile Apache groups and much larger military forces.
  5. Describe the experiences of Apache women, children, and elders during the campaigns, and explain why casualty figures must be attributed to their sources.
  6. Evaluate the role of Apache scouts in the American campaigns and explain why scout service should not be equated with disloyalty to Apache communities.
  7. Compare the approaches of George Crook and Nelson A. Miles to pursuit, negotiation, and the use of Apache scouts.
  8. Interpret the records of the Cañon de los Embudos negotiations and the Crawford diplomatic correspondence as evidence, identifying the limits imposed by interpretation, transcription, and institutional purpose.
  9. Distinguish the surrender agreement of September 1886 from the subsequent deportation and prisoner-of-war custody of the Chiricahua.
  10. Trace the geography of captivity from Arizona to Florida, Alabama, and Oklahoma, and the division of survivors between Mescalero and Fort Sill in 1913–1914.
  11. Assess how Chiricahua families maintained kinship, leadership, and cultural practice under prolonged confinement.
  12. Evaluate competing historical representations of Geronimo and the Apache Wars in tribal history, archival documents, scholarship, and popular culture.

Historical Context

The Chiricahua Apache were the Apache people whose homelands lay in the mountain and basin country of southeastern Arizona, southwestern New Mexico, and northern Sonora and Chihuahua. They were not a single political unit. Historians and tribal historians commonly distinguish several bands or regional groups, including the Chihenne (often called Warm Springs or Mimbres), the Chokonen, the Nednhi, and the Bedonkohe, each with its own territories, leaders, and kin networks, though marriage, ceremony, and shared language linked them closely. Their economies combined gathering, hunting, farming in some locations, trade, and raiding, with seasonal movement among mountain ranges and water sources. This background is developed in Chapter 51, and the nineteenth-century conflicts with Mexico and the United States are treated in Chapter 79.

By 1883, those conflicts had changed the conditions of Chiricahua life. Mexican military campaigns, bounties on Apache scalps, and violence across the borderlands had killed many Apache people and made the Sierra Madre both a refuge and a place of danger. American settlement, mining, and railroad construction had expanded in southern Arizona and New Mexico. The United States had confined most Apache peoples to reservations, and the San Carlos Reservation in Arizona, established in the 1870s, had become a place where groups from different regions, with different histories and sometimes mutual hostilities, were concentrated under military and civilian supervision. In 1883, Crook led a force of Apache scouts and soldiers into the Sierra Madre, and the leaders and families he met there agreed to return to the reservation. Chapter 79 treats that expedition in full. The present chapter begins with its aftermath.

Two features of the situation after 1883 are essential. The first is the overlapping authority of different parts of the United States government. The Army, led in Arizona by Crook, claimed responsibility for the Chiricahua; the Interior Department's Indian Service, through its agents, administered the reservations; and the territorial press and political leaders pressed both for harsher measures. Crook, who had considerable experience with Apache peoples, often favored arrangements that gave Apache leaders some role in policing their own communities, and he used Apache scouts extensively. Civilian officials, settlers, and many officers distrusted that approach. The second feature is the international border. Mexican and American forces operated under an agreement permitting reciprocal crossing in pursuit of hostile Indians, but the agreement was narrowly drawn, depended on local cooperation, and did not prevent bitter disputes over jurisdiction and conduct.

Students should also keep in view a methodological caution. Much of what has been written about the final campaigns relies on the reports and memoirs of American officers, on contemporary newspapers, and on interviews conducted with Apache people long afterward. The first group had institutional reasons to present events favorably; the second was often sensational; and the third reflects memory, family transmission, and the conditions under which interviews took place. This chapter treats all of them as evidence to be weighed.

Section 80.1

The Fragile Settlement after Crook's Sierra Madre Expedition

When Chiricahua groups returned to the reservation after Crook's 1883 expedition, many Apache families and many American officers believed that the long war had reached a settlement. The belief was sincere on both sides, but the two sides held different understandings of what the settlement meant. For Chiricahua leaders who came north from the Sierra Madre, returning was a decision to try life under supervision on the condition of safety for their families and some continuing control over their own affairs. For the Army and the Indian Service, return meant submission to a system of rations, counting, movement restrictions, and rules that would be enforced by officers and by the agency staff. The history of 1884–1885 is largely the history of the distance between those expectations.

The reservation arrangements adopted after 1883 placed Chiricahua people in several locations. Many, including groups under Chihuahua and Naiche and, after his return, Geronimo, were settled at Turkey Creek on the White Mountain Reservation near Fort Apache, under military rather than civilian supervision. Others, notably followers of Loco and of Chato, lived closer to San Carlos. Crook wished the Chiricahua to become farmers and stock raisers, and officers such as Lieutenant Britton Davis, who supervised the Turkey Creek community, tried to organize cultivation, issue rations, and maintain order. Davis later wrote an account of this period that remains an important source but that, like all officer memoirs, reflects the author's institutional position and his later purposes.

Daily life under this system was difficult in ways that went beyond material hardship. The Chiricahua had been mobile peoples whose subsistence and social calendar depended on movement among mountains and water sources. Farming on assigned land, the issuing of rations, and the requirement to be present for counting and supervision altered the rhythm of family and community life. The reservation also brought together people who had followed different strategies in the preceding decade. Some had fought alongside Victorio or Juh, others had lived at peace at San Carlos, and some had served as scouts. Old grievances, new rivalries, and differing views about Army authority existed within the same camps, and the Army's tendency to treat the groups as one population obscured them.

A particularly sensitive point concerned self-government. Crook's approach relied on Apache leaders and scouts to police the community, which gave some leaders real standing, but which also placed them in the position of enforcing American rules on their own relatives. The Army prohibited the brewing and drinking of tiswin, a fermented corn drink with a long place in Apache social life, and attempted to regulate certain family practices that officers regarded as unacceptable. Chiricahua leaders understood these measures as intrusions on private and ceremonial life. Officers understood them as necessary elements of discipline. The result was repeated friction over matters that neither side regarded as trivial.

Surveillance and rumor compounded these tensions. Chiricahua leaders were aware that some civilians in Arizona and New Mexico demanded the punishment of Apache men for earlier raids and killings, and that territorial newspapers urged the arrest or execution of prominent leaders. Geronimo and others who had fought in the borderlands knew that they were vulnerable to such demands, whatever Crook had said in Mexico in 1883. Their fears did not require that any specific plan to arrest them existed. They required only that the Army's authority over them be strong, that civilian pressure be intense, and that Apache men had good reason to doubt official assurances. The settlement of 1884 thus contained, from its beginning, a structural weakness: the security of the people it governed depended on promises that those people had limited ability to verify or enforce.

It is important not to read the departure of May 1885, discussed in §80.4, as inevitable. Many Chiricahua families remained at Fort Apache and San Carlos throughout the crisis, and many men chose to serve the Army. The breakdown of the settlement resulted from the interaction of particular decisions by particular leaders, particular rumors, and particular failures of communication. Historians continue to debate how much weight to give each, and the chapter returns to that problem in §80.4 and in the Cause and Effect feature in §80.13.

Section 80.2

Geronimo: Spiritual Authority, Personal History, and Resistance

Geronimo was born, by most accounts, around 1829 in the upper Gila country, within the Bedonkohe band of the Chiricahua. His Apache name is rendered Goyaałé, commonly glossed as "one who yawns," and the name by which he became famous seems to have originated among Mexicans, who rendered it Jerónimo. He grew up in a society in which kinship, ceremonial knowledge, and skill in raiding and warfare opened paths to influence. In adulthood he married into Chihenne kin networks and developed close ties with leaders of other Chiricahua groups, connections that later gave him access to followers beyond his small natal band.

The experience that shaped his reputation, and that Geronimo himself placed at the center of his own life story, was the violent attack on an Apache encampment near Janos, Chihuahua, commonly dated to March 1851 in later historical scholarship (although Geronimo’s edited 1906 account places it in 1858), in which Mexican troops killed many Apache people, including, according to his account, his mother, his wife, and his children. The attack belonged to a long cycle of raiding, reprisal, and Mexican military campaigns in the borderlands, and its effects extended beyond his personal grief. It confirmed for him and for many others that Mexican forces represented an implacable threat. The rest of his career, including his raids in Sonora and Chihuahua, must be read against that background, though the background does not explain every decision he made.

His authority rested on a distinctive combination of qualities. Geronimo was widely regarded as a person of spiritual power, one whose ceremonial knowledge and reported ability to foresee events gave him standing as a medicine man. He was also an experienced raider and war leader, valued for his knowledge of the Sierra Madre and for his reputation for reading danger. These forms of influence were real, but they differed from formal political authority. In Chiricahua communities, leaders typically held their positions through the consent of followers, family ties, demonstrated judgment, and success in protecting their people. A man whose spiritual or military reputation attracted followers could lead a raid or guide a group, but he did not thereby gain command over every Chiricahua family. Followers could and did choose to leave him.

This distinction is essential for the history of 1885–1886 and has been obscured by a century of American media. Newspapers in the 1880s described Geronimo as the chief of the hostile Apache, a phrase that gave readers a single person to fear and a single person to defeat. Army officers adopted similar language partly because it simplified reports and partly because it made the campaign comprehensible to Washington. Later films and popular histories enlarged the image further. The Fort Sill Apache Tribe's account, by contrast, describes him as an important medicine man and leader while distinguishing his role from the supreme chief of popular imagination. Scholars such as Edwin Sweeney, Angie Debo, and Robert Utley have documented the gap between his public image and his actual position, though they differ in emphasis and in their assessments of his character.

The records concerning Geronimo are of several distinct kinds, and each answers different questions. Military reports describe his movements and decisions from the viewpoint of officers who sought him. Newspapers published rumor, rhetoric, and sometimes invention. Conferences with Crook and others were transcribed through interpreters and therefore represent his words as translated and recorded by others. His life history, published in 1906 as Geronimo's Story of His Life, is a first-person narrative dictated through an interpreter and edited by S. M. Barrett, a point examined in §80.18. Apache recollections preserved in later interviews and in tribal histories provide yet another perspective, often attentive to family and ceremonial dimensions the other sources neglect. A responsible account compares them rather than choosing one.

Finally, students should resist two opposite errors. One portrays Geronimo as a romantic freedom fighter whose decisions were always wise, a view that ignores the real violence of his raiding and the disagreements his choices produced within Apache communities. The other portrays him as a bloodthirsty outlaw, the view of many contemporary newspapers, which ignores both the circumstances of borderland war and the fact that his people had legitimate reasons for fearing arrest. The historical Geronimo was a man of formidable skill and conviction, shaped by a world of continuous violence, whose significance lies in what he did and in what others have made of him, not in the title he never held.

Section 80.3

Naiche, Chihuahua, and the Politics of Chiricahua Leadership

To understand the final campaign, one must place Geronimo among other leaders whose positions, strategies, and followings differed. Chiricahua political life was organized through local groups led by men and sometimes women whose influence rested on kinship, reputation, and the consent of followers. The widely used band names (Chihenne, Chokonen, Nednhi, and Bedonkohe) describe regional and kin-based clusters rather than rigid political units, and individuals moved among them through marriage, residence, and alliance.

The surviving record also requires care when identifying who could speak for whom. Army officers often used labels such as “chief” as if an order from one man would bind a single, centralized Chiricahua nation. In practice the relatives and local groups associated with Naiche, Chihuahua, Geronimo, and other figures could make different decisions even during the same negotiation. A person's influence might come from inherited standing, judgment in war, religious responsibilities, or the ability to maintain relationships. These forms of authority overlapped without becoming identical. The importance of this distinction is visible in 1886: people associated with Chihuahua remained with the surrender party in March, while Geronimo, Naiche, and others departed. Neither course can be described simply as a nation voting for peace or for war. To reconstruct the choices, historians must compare military reports with later Apache accounts and ask which families were actually present, which individuals were quoted, and which people the recorder left anonymous. This makes political pluralism a historical fact rather than an excuse to treat Apache decisions as incoherent.

Historical Vocabulary
Chiricahua and the Band Names

Definition. "Chiricahua" is the name commonly applied by outsiders to a group of related Apache peoples whose homelands included the Chiricahua Mountains and surrounding ranges. It is not an Apache political label of the period. The principal regional groups usually distinguished are the Chihenne, Chokonen, Nednhi, and Bedonkohe, and modern descendant communities often use more specific names alongside the broader one.

Historical context. The Army and the press treated "the Chiricahua" as one population, which simplified administration and campaign planning. Apache communities, however, were organized through local kin networks, and decisions to stay, to flee, to scout, or to negotiate were made by different leaders and families.

Why it matters. The end of the campaigns in 1886 affected all Chiricahua groups, but it did not affect them as a single unit, and the later deportation of the entire population erased distinctions that Apache people had maintained.

Potential misunderstanding. Treating a band as a "tribe" with a single chief repeats the Army's own simplification.

Naiche, born around the mid-1850s, was a son of Cochise, the Chokonen leader whose war and peace with the United States dominated the 1860s and early 1870s. After Cochise died in 1874, his elder son Taza led the group, and after Taza's death in 1876 during a visit to the eastern United States, Naiche assumed a leading position among the Chokonen. He did not inherit unlimited authority, and his standing depended on his own conduct and on the consent of his followers. Nonetheless, his lineage, his place within the Chokonen network, and his participation in the key decisions of 1885–1886 make him a figure of the first rank, and the Fort Sill Apache Tribe's historical accounts give him sustained attention. He and Geronimo were close allies in the final campaign, but they were not interchangeable. Geronimo's authority was spiritual and military; Naiche's was inherited and political, in the Chokonen sense of leadership.

Chihuahua, a Chokonen leader of experience, had fought in the earlier wars and left the reservation in 1885, but he later took part in the March 1886 surrender and afterward chose to go with Crook's party rather than flee again. His decision illustrates that departure from the reservation did not commit a leader to fight to the end. Loco, a Chihenne leader who had lived through Victorio's war and its aftermath, was associated with a more conciliatory path, and he and his followers remained near the Army through the crisis. Chato had fought in earlier raids, including a notable raid in 1883 treated in Chapter 79, but afterward served as a scout and cooperated with Crook. Mangus, a son of Mangas Coloradas, and the elderly Nana, a veteran of Victorio's war, each had followers and each made decisions according to his own assessment of family safety.

These differing strategies reflected genuine disagreement about how Apache people could best survive. For some leaders, remaining on the reservation offered the best chance of keeping their families together and avoiding Mexican and American violence, even at the cost of obeying rules they disliked. For others, the experience of broken promises, the threat of arrest, and the memory of earlier betrayals argued that only mobility and the Sierra Madre guaranteed safety. Some leaders shifted between these positions as circumstances changed. The disagreement was not a contest between "loyal" and "hostile" Apache, as officers sometimes framed it. It was an argument within Apache communities about how to survive under conditions of extreme pressure and uncertain information.

The Army's reports often named Geronimo and Naiche together as leaders of the "hostiles," and it frequently referred to followers as though they formed a single fighting force. Apache testimony, including family histories preserved at Fort Sill and Mescalero, emphasizes instead the many separate households and kin networks that moved together under shifting alliances. Understanding the campaign requires keeping those networks in view, because they explain both the group's resilience and its vulnerability. A party composed mainly of kin could move together and share risk, but it could also be traced through the family ties that connected its members to the reservation.

Section 80.4

The Departure of May 1885 and the Renewal of War

On the night of May 17, 1885, a group of Chiricahua men, women, and children left the Turkey Creek community and moved south. The party included Geronimo, Naiche, Chihuahua, Nana, and Mangus, together with many of their relatives. The events leading to the departure have been reconstructed from the reports of Lieutenant Davis and other officers, from newspaper accounts, from Geronimo's later statements, and from Apache family recollections. They agree on the broad sequence but diverge on motive and detail.

In the days before the departure, a number of Chiricahua men drank tiswin in violation of the rules imposed by the Army. According to several accounts, some of the men feared punishment after the drinking, and rumors circulated that Geronimo or others were to be arrested or killed. One frequently cited account concerns a garbled telegram or rumor that reached the Apache community and was understood as evidence of an impending arrest. Whether any actual order of that kind existed, whether a message was mistranslated, or whether rumor alone supplied the fear cannot be settled with the surviving evidence. What is clear is that Chiricahua leaders believed that arrest or punishment was possible, and that they had reasons, drawn from long experience, to take such threats seriously.

Official explanations emphasized alcohol and the unruliness of particular men. Geronimo's later accounts emphasized fear for his life and his dissatisfaction with the conditions imposed on the people at Turkey Creek, including restrictions on ordinary social practice and the constant surveillance. Other Apache recollections stress the accumulated distrust of the Army and the reality that the people who left did so because they judged the reservation unsafe. These explanations are not mutually exclusive. Alcohol and rumor may have been the immediate occasion, while longer-term grievances over restrictions, supervision, and security supplied the conditions in which the occasion became a departure.

The number of people who left is itself a matter of estimate. Army reports at the time spoke of roughly thirty-five to forty men capable of fighting and about a hundred women and children, producing a total of approximately 140 persons, though the figures vary among sources and some reports include boys of fighting age in different categories. These numbers originate in official counts compiled under pressure and from imperfect information. The point of greater importance is the composition. The group was not an army. Most of its members were women, children, and older people, which shaped its speed, its vulnerability, and the decisions its leaders could make. Treating the departing party as a homogeneous force of "hostiles" misrepresents both its size and its character.

Not everyone who left remained with the group, and not all leaders agreed about what to do next. Chihuahua, Naiche, Geronimo, and others made independent decisions, and the party divided and reunited over the following months. In the summer and autumn of 1885, small groups raided in Arizona and New Mexico, drawing violent responses from settlers and soldiers, and the Army deployed columns and Apache scouts to intercept them. By the end of the year, the main body of the Chiricahua party was operating in Mexico, and Crook had decided to send a scout-based expedition after it. This transition from reservation departure to renewed war, covered in the next division, represented a hardening of positions on both sides. The longer the campaign lasted, the more difficult it became for Apache leaders to return without punishment and for American officers to accept anything less than complete submission.

Division II

The Final Campaign across the Borderlands, 1885–1886

§§80.5–80.8
Section 80.5

The Geography of Resistance: Sierra Madre, Sonora, and Arizona

The final campaign was fought across a landscape that neither Mexican nor American planners treated as a single space, but that Chiricahua families knew as one. From the Chiricahua and Dragoon ranges in southeastern Arizona, through the Peloncillo Mountains and the bootheel of New Mexico, into the Sonoran valleys of the Bavispe and Aros rivers and the high country of the Sierra Madre Occidental, travelers moved along routes of canyon, ridge, spring, and cienega that predated both nations' claims. The international boundary, drawn in the mid-nineteenth century and marked by few visible features, mattered chiefly as an administrative and legal fact. For Apache people it offered at times a practical advantage, because pursuing forces could not cross it without regard to the other nation's sovereignty.

Knowledge of water was the foundation of mobility. In arid country, a group of a hundred or more people with horses and mules could travel only between reliable springs, tinajas, creeks, and seasonal pools, and the leaders of the party carried detailed memory of where water could be found in each season and after each rain. The Sierra Madre, with its deep barrancas, pine forests, and defensible camps, offered cover, game, mescal, and sheltered places to rest. Chiricahua families also drew on long-established relationships with the landscape: camps used by earlier generations, caches, and trails remembered through oral tradition. Soldiers entering the same country, even with Apache scouts, depended on pack trains that were slow, limited in range, and exposed to ambush.

Seasonal conditions imposed their own constraints. Summer heat and the monsoon rains of July and August altered the availability of water and forage; winter in the high Sierra brought cold and snow that made travel hard for families with small children. Horses, which provided speed and carrying capacity, also needed grazing and water and were vulnerable to exhaustion and loss. The party obtained mounts and supplies by raiding settlements and ranches on both sides of the border, an economic necessity that also made them enemies of civilians who had no part in the political disputes that had driven the departure. The relationship between survival and raiding shaped the campaign and its human cost, a theme developed in §80.6.

For the Army, the geography of the borderlands presented almost opposite problems. Columns operating in Sonora and Chihuahua stretched supply lines across rough country, depended on animals that broke down quickly, and had to negotiate the political complexities of operating in a foreign nation. Under the reciprocal crossing arrangements in force, American troops could pursue hostile Indians into Mexican territory under defined conditions, but cooperation with Mexican authorities was uneven, and local Mexican commanders and civilian militias had their own priorities and their own bitter history with Apache raiders. These constraints help explain why a force much larger than the Chiricahua party could not simply overwhelm it. The advantage of numbers was offset by the demands of distance, supply, terrain, and divided jurisdiction.

Geography did not determine the outcome, however. The same mountains that protected the Chiricahua also confined them, and the endurance required to evade pursuit over months took a cumulative toll on food supplies, horses, and the health of the old and young. The loss of safe places, the growing capacity of Mexican forces to harass the party in Sonora and Chihuahua, and the Army's improving ability to maintain pressure all narrowed the options of Apache leaders over time. Students should therefore treat geography as a set of constraints and opportunities that each side learned to exploit, rather than as a mechanical explanation of success or failure. The Geography Study in §80.14 returns to these routes and contrasts them with the geography of captivity.

Section 80.6

Warfare, Raiding, Civilians, and the Experience of Apache Families

The campaign of 1885–1886 was violent, and its violence fell on many communities. Chiricahua raiding parties killed settlers, ranchers, travelers, and soldiers in Arizona, New Mexico, Sonora, and Chihuahua, seizing horses, cattle, and supplies. Mexican and American civilians and military forces killed Apache men, and at times Apache women and children, in attacks and reprisals. The people whose farms, ranches, and villages lay along the paths of movement experienced the campaign as a series of sudden attacks, and their fear and grief are part of the historical record. Territorial newspapers amplified those experiences, sometimes with accuracy and sometimes with rumor, and demanded severe punishment of Apache people generally.

Casualty figures for this campaign require particular care. Contemporary reports gave numbers of settlers and soldiers killed that were compiled from local accounts, official dispatches, and newspaper stories, often without independent verification and frequently attributed to the "hostile" party as a whole even when several different groups might have been responsible. Apache casualties are even less well documented, because the Army and Mexican forces reported what they chose to report and because Apache accounts were not systematically recorded at the time. Students should avoid repeating a single total as established fact. Any number given in a textbook, a memoir, or a monument inscription originates in a particular report, produced by a particular institution, for particular purposes, and it should be attributed to that source. It should also not be combined with casualty counts of other kinds, such as combatants and civilians, as though they were comparable.

The experiences of the women, children, and elders traveling with the mobile groups are among the least documented aspects of the campaign and among the most important. They carried the burdens of daily survival: gathering and preparing food, preserving water, caring for the sick and injured, tending horses and packs, and making the continuous small decisions that kept a large party moving. Women also took part in decision-making and, in some cases, in diplomacy. Apache testimony, especially that collected from descendants, describes children raised on the move, elders who could not keep up and had to be left or carried, and the constant anxiety of pursuit. These accounts reflect memory transmitted through families and should be read as such, but they supply a perspective that military reports omit.

The Fort Sill Apache Tribe's historical account preserves the story of Tahdeste, a Chiricahua woman who served as an intermediary between the Army and Apache people during the campaign. Her role illustrates that women were not merely passive companions to male leaders. They could carry messages, assess risks, and negotiate between groups, and officers on the American side sometimes relied on women's contacts to open communication with leaders who would not otherwise approach. The surviving documentation about individual women is thin, and students should be cautious about reconstructing details beyond what tribal and archival sources support. The broader point is secure: Apache women contributed to the diplomacy and the survival of their communities in ways that the standard military narrative has largely ignored.

The mixed composition of the party also affected its tactics. A group encumbered by children and elders could not travel as quickly as a raiding party of men alone, and its leaders had to weigh every action against the safety of its dependents. Some men detached themselves to raid or to scout while families sheltered in secure camps. The Army and Mexican forces, in turn, learned that attacking a camp could produce large numbers of casualties among noncombatants, a fact that shaped both the effectiveness and the moral weight of those attacks. When we read official tallies of "hostiles" killed or captured, we should ask who was counted and who was not.

Finally, the violence of the campaign belongs within a longer history. Mexican states had for decades offered payments for Apache scalps, a practice that made killing of Apache people, including noncombatants, a source of income for some, and Apache raiding of Mexican communities was embedded in a cycle of retaliation. The United States and its territorial authorities had its own history of violence against Apache people, treated in Chapter 79. None of this excuses attacks on civilians, but it explains why Chiricahua leaders regarded the Mexican borderlands as a theater of existential danger, and why many Mexican communities regarded Apache raiders with fear and hatred.

Section 80.7

Apache Scouts, Military Intelligence, and Divided Political Loyalties

The American campaigns against the Chiricahua could not have been conducted without Apache scouts. These men, enlisted in Army scout companies for short terms, knew the terrain, could read tracks, understood the language and customs of those they pursued, and often had kin or political connections that gave them information about the movement of hostile groups. Officers from Crook downward regarded them as indispensable and repeatedly argued that regular soldiers alone could not catch Apache groups in their own country. Many scouts were drawn from White Mountain, San Carlos, and other Western Apache communities, and some, including a number of Chiricahua men, came from the very population under pursuit.

Why did Apache men enlist? The motives were varied and need not be reduced to a single explanation. Army service offered pay, rations, and, for some, status and a measure of protection for their families. Some scouts had long-standing enmities with the Chiricahua or had suffered from earlier raids. Others judged that cooperation with the Army was the best way to secure safety for their communities, or that capable service might earn influence and concessions. Some scouts were related to people in the hostile party and used their position to open communication and, they hoped, to shorten the war. To interpret service as simple betrayal of fellow Apache people imposes a modern and oversimplified notion of loyalty on a situation in which Apache communities were politically divided and in which individuals chose among poor options.

The scouts served under officers who developed close working relationships with them, among them Emmet Crawford, Marion P. Maus, Charles Gatewood, and Crook himself. Crawford and Maus led companies that were composed almost entirely of Apache soldiers, and Gatewood had long experience with White Mountain scouts. These officers varied in their attitudes. Some, like Crook, respected the scouts' abilities and argued for treating them fairly, while others regarded them as useful instruments. The scouts, for their part, understood that their service depended on the goodwill of particular officers, who could not necessarily protect them from higher authorities. That dependence would become tragically clear in September 1886.

Scouts also served as interpreters, intermediaries, and messengers, although the formal interpreters at conferences were often non-Apache men with varying levels of skill, a limitation discussed in §80.9. Their knowledge of the political relationships among Apache leaders helped officers decide whom to approach and how. At the same time, scout companies were not uniformly reliable instruments of Army policy. They included men who maintained contact with relatives in hostile camps and who might refuse to attack them, and officers recognized that a scout's dependability was never guaranteed. Commanders who trusted scouts, such as Crook, tended to emphasize their loyalty; commanders who distrusted them, such as Miles, emphasized the risks.

Understanding the scouts is important preparation for the later history. When the campaign ended, the United States did not distinguish between men who had served the Army in the field and those who had been pursued. Scouts who had risked their lives for American commanders were disarmed, arrested, and deported along with the people they had helped pursue. The injustice of that decision, which Crook and a number of other officers and civilian reformers protested, is taken up in §80.13. Their story reminds us that the end of the Apache Wars was not a victory for those Apache people who had chosen to cooperate with American forces.

The diplomatic record of Captain Crawford's death provides unusually direct evidence that the scouts were not auxiliary figures on the margins of the campaign. In the dispatch published in the United States diplomatic correspondence for 1886, Secretary of State Thomas F. Bayard argued that Apache scouts were enlisted, paid, armed, and commanded as regular components of an American force. He used that legal status to insist that Mexico treat the encounter as an attack upon United States soldiers, not as a clash with an unregulated Apache raiding party. In military terms, therefore, scout service could bring formal recognition. The same recognition did not provide lasting security for Chiricahua scouts or their relatives. Their subsequent confinement alongside people the Army had pursued makes the contradiction exceptionally clear. A scout might help the Army interpret a trail, carry a message, or obtain a surrender while remaining exposed to a federal policy that classified his community collectively. This is a strong reason to examine enlistment and deportation together: the former records individual service; the latter reveals how the government subordinated that service to a decision about group identity.

Section 80.8

Captain Emmet Crawford and the January 1886 Expedition

In the autumn of 1885, Crook organized an expedition into Mexico under Captain Emmet Crawford of the Third Cavalry, a capable officer with long experience commanding Apache scouts. The force consisted largely of Apache scouts, with a small number of white officers and attendants, and its mission was to locate the Chiricahua camps in the Sierra Madre and either compel a surrender or defeat the party. Crook's reliance on a scout-based force reflected his conviction that regular troops could not pursue Apache people in the mountains. It also reflected a political calculation: a column of Apache soldiers might be able to approach Apache leaders in ways that white troops could not.

The expedition's crossing into Mexico was authorized under the agreement that permitted reciprocal crossing of the border by troops in pursuit of hostile Indians. That agreement did not give American forces freedom to operate at will. It was intended for pursuit in unsettled country and assumed notice to Mexican authorities, and its use inevitably raised questions of sovereignty. Mexican officials and military commanders were not uniformly enthusiastic about American columns in their territory, and local militias and irregular forces operated with considerable independence. The expedition therefore moved through an environment of political as well as military uncertainty.

In early January 1886, Crawford's scouts located a Chiricahua camp in the Sierra Madre, and on January 10 the expedition struck it, with the Apache party withdrawing and leaving behind supplies. Soon afterward, on January 11, near the Aros River (spelled "Haros" in some American documents), Crawford's camp was approached by a force of Mexican irregular troops. Accounts of the next events differ in important respects. American reports described an attack on Crawford's command by Mexican forces that should have recognized the scouts as American soldiers working with the United States Army. Crawford, according to those reports, climbed onto a rock to signal and was shot in the head. Mexican accounts, preserved in diplomatic correspondence, emphasized that the Mexican force had mistaken the Apache scouts for hostile Indians and that the encounter was a tragic error.

Crawford died of his wound on January 18, 1886, and Lieutenant Marion P. Maus assumed command. The death of a popular officer produced anger in the American press and military, and diplomatic exchanges between the United States and Mexico followed. The contemporary correspondence, preserved in the volumes of the United States Foreign Relations series, allows students to compare the American account with the explanations offered by the Mexican side and to see what can and cannot be established independently. In particular, the record does not conclusively resolve whether the Mexican commander knew whom he was attacking, and historians have differed on how to weigh the American claims of deliberate attack against the Mexican claims of mistaken identity.

The correspondence requires a closer separation of what the participants saw from what later officials inferred. Maus's report describes an attack on a Chiricahua camp on January 10, followed by the encounter with Mexican forces on January 11. Supporting depositions by officers, scouts, and an Army hospital steward offer partially overlapping sequences of warnings, gunfire, attempts to halt the fight, and the shooting of Crawford. They were assembled as part of an American effort to establish responsibility for a fatal cross-border incident. Bayard then used those statements in a diplomatic demand. Students should distinguish an eyewitness's account of a visible action from an inference about whether a Mexican soldier knew Crawford was an American officer. That difference matters because the charge of a deliberate killing was stronger than the claim that the two forces had collided in confusion. The documents are valuable precisely because they permit comparison among witnesses and reveal how military testimony became international argument; they do not remove the need to consider Mexican records independently.

Division III

Negotiation, Military Pressure, and the Surrender of 1886

§§80.9–80.12 · March–September 1886
Section 80.9

The Cañon de los Embudos Negotiations, March 1886

From March 25 to 27, 1886, General Crook met Chiricahua leaders at Cañon de los Embudos, a canyon in northern Sonora south of the international border. The conference brought together Crook and a small party of officers, scouts, and interpreters with Geronimo, Naiche, Chihuahua, and other Chiricahua leaders and their followers. It was the most direct and best recorded negotiation of the campaign, and it is one of the few occasions on which Geronimo's statements were transcribed at some length. The record was produced by military recorders working through interpreters, so students should treat it as a translated and officially filtered account rather than as a verbatim transcript.

Geronimo spoke first and at length about the history that had led to the present crisis. According to the surviving record, he described his grievances against reservation conditions, his fear that he would be killed or imprisoned, and the rumors and accusations that had circulated about him. He also raised allegations made against him by other Apache people, and he asked Crook to believe that he wished to be at peace. The speeches show a man making a case about trust: he insisted that the Army's treatment of him and his people had given him reasons for distrust, and he asked for reassurance. Crook's responses were blunt. He told the Chiricahua that they must decide whether to surrender or to resume the war, and that if they chose war, he would hunt them until the last was dead, a position consistent with his long-standing practice of coupling pressure with the offer of peace.

The interpreters at the conference were important mediators and potential sources of distortion. The principal interpreters were not neutral instruments; they translated among Apache, Spanish, and English, each translation introducing the possibility of error and of shading. Some participants later disputed what had been said. Apache leaders believed that they had agreed to certain terms, and American officers later reported different understandings. The ambiguity of translation, the high stakes of the discussion, and the absence of any written agreement signed by both sides all contributed to the disputes that followed.

The terms discussed at the conference are crucial and contested. According to Crook's own report, the Chiricahua leaders agreed to surrender, to be taken to the United States, and to be sent east for a period, after which they would be returned to the reservation. He described the arrangement as a form of temporary exile, which he believed would satisfy both Apache concerns about safety and the political demand in Arizona for punishment. The Chiricahua participants appear to have understood that they would be reunited with their families, would not be executed, and would eventually return to Arizona. Crook communicated these terms to his superiors, but the leadership in Washington, including General Philip Sheridan and the president's administration, found them unacceptable and demanded unconditional surrender, with the stipulation that only the lives of the prisoners would be spared. The difference between what the Apache participants thought they had agreed to and what Washington was prepared to approve opened a gap that would not be closed.

The competing authorities at the conference also mattered. Crook was the senior commander in the field and could negotiate the practical act of bringing people back under guard. But he could not unilaterally guarantee what national civilian and military authorities would subsequently approve. For Chiricahua leaders, this division of power may not have appeared in the same way: an Army general had traveled to meet them, heard their concerns, and stated a proposed path to safety. The problem was therefore not only whether words had been translated accurately but whether the speaker had the authority to make those words binding. A student's reconstruction should separate four stages: a proposal expressed in the canyon, the understanding held by those who heard it, the report Crook forwarded, and the response from Washington. Combining these stages produces a misleading document that appears to be a single settled treaty. Keeping them distinct explains why the March episode could be described by different participants as a genuine attempt at peace, an incomplete surrender, or a promise that was subsequently narrowed.

Section 80.10

The Collapse of the March Surrender and Crook's Removal

Within days of the conference, as the Chiricahua party and its escort moved north, Geronimo and Naiche left, taking with them a group that included a number of men and some women and children. Most of the other participants, including Chihuahua and his followers, continued toward the United States with the column. Howard’s September 1886 military report states that the return march began March 28 and that Geronimo and Naiche departed during the night of March 29; other recollections differ about the immediate sequence. The departing party numbered a few dozen people, including approximately twenty men, although estimates differ.

Why did they leave? The evidence permits several explanations, none of which can be wholly excluded. One concerns alcohol. According to American reports, a trader who had accompanied the party supplied mescal to the Chiricahua, and the drinking that followed heightened fear and suspicion. A second concerns rumor. Some accounts say that Geronimo and Naiche were told, or came to believe, that they would be killed or imprisoned for life as soon as they crossed the border, or that American civilians would attack them. A third concerns mistrust of the Army more broadly: given their experiences of arrest threats, broken promises, and violence, they may simply have concluded that the risk of surrender was too great. A fourth, emphasized in some historical accounts, is the cumulative effect of a long and tense journey in which discussions within the party continued to divide its leaders. The most defensible conclusion is that several of these factors interacted, and that the surviving sources do not allow historians to determine precisely which was decisive.

The political consequences were immediate. Crook had reported the surrender as a success and had communicated terms that his superiors found inadequate. When Geronimo and Naiche left, the agreement appeared to have collapsed, and Crook found himself in a position in which the surrender he had arranged was both politically unacceptable in Washington and incomplete in the field. Sheridan and other senior officers expressed dissatisfaction, and critics in Arizona and in the press attacked his methods, including his use of Apache scouts. In early April, Crook asked to be relieved, and Nelson A. Miles was appointed to command the Department of Arizona.

The assignment of blame in the decades after has often been personal. Some officers and writers blamed Crook for trusting the Apache, others blamed the trader, others blamed Geronimo's alleged treachery, and still others blamed the policies of Washington. A careful reading distinguishes what is documented from what is inferred. It is documented that the party left and that Crook asked to be relieved. It is documented that Washington rejected his terms. It is not clearly documented that a single decision or person caused the collapse. Treating the episode as a story of one man's betrayal oversimplifies a situation in which fear, miscommunication, institutional disagreement, and chance all played roles.

The consequences for the Chiricahua who remained with the column were severe. Chihuahua and his followers, who had followed the terms they understood and who continued to the United States, were taken into custody and, as later sections show, were soon sent east. The decision to surrender, which they had made in good faith, did not protect them from the policy that followed. For the Chiricahua still at the reservation, the news that those who had turned themselves in had been confined must have confirmed their fears about the reliability of official promises.

Section 80.11

Nelson A. Miles, Henry Lawton, and the Final Military Pursuit

Nelson A. Miles, who had fought Plains peoples in the 1870s, took command of the Department of Arizona in April 1886 with a reputation for energy and a different philosophy from Crook's. Miles distrusted the extensive use of Apache scouts, believing that they could not be fully relied upon, and he emphasized the use of regular troops organized into mobile columns, supported by a system of observation and communication. He also had political ambitions and a keen sense of the public importance of capturing Geronimo.

Miles's strategy combined several elements. He reorganized the forces in the department, established posts and camps at key locations, and used patrols to monitor water sources and likely routes in southern Arizona and New Mexico. He extended an existing network of heliograph stations, using mirrors to flash signals across long distances, which allowed commanders to share information about the movements of the Chiricahua party and the positions of troops. And he organized a special expedition under Captain Henry W. Lawton of the Fourth Cavalry, composed of picked officers and men, which crossed into Mexico in the spring and pursued the party through the summer. Lawton's column, which included a young army surgeon, Leonard Wood, endured extraordinary hardships, including long marches in intense heat, but it did not catch the Chiricahua.

It is tempting to attribute the eventual surrender to American technological superiority, and some contemporary accounts promoted that idea, but the evidence does not support so simple an explanation. The heliograph provided useful communication but did not allow the Army to catch a mobile party in rugged country. What it and the other measures did do, in combination with other pressures, was to make life steadily harder for the Chiricahua. Constant pursuit by columns that did not give up exhausted the party's horses and its people. Access to supplies and safe water was increasingly contested, and Mexican forces in Sonora and Chihuahua added their own pressure. Meanwhile the party's leaders could not be certain what the Army or the Mexican government would do next.

Political uncertainty was as important as military pressure. The collapse of the March agreement had left the question of what terms the Chiricahua could expect unresolved, and Washington's insistence on unconditional surrender, combined with the rumors that circulated among the Apache, made leaders wary of any new negotiation. Miles had to decide whether to continue the military effort alone or to open a new channel. He chose to do both. He kept the pressure on while also authorizing Lieutenant Charles B. Gatewood, who had experience with Apache scouts and who was respected by many of the Chiricahua, to seek out Geronimo's camp with a small party. The decision shows that even a commander who emphasized force recognized the need for diplomacy.

The contrast with Crook is instructive but should not be overstated. Crook believed that the scouts and negotiated surrender were the keys to ending the war, and he had tried to build a political settlement. Miles believed in mobile columns and observation, and he was more skeptical of the scouts and of conditional terms. Yet in the end the surrender was achieved through a combination of both approaches: sustained military pressure, the role of Apache intermediaries, and negotiation led by an officer who had been a Crook subordinate. The campaign cannot be reduced to the vindication of either philosophy.

Section 80.12

Charles Gatewood, Naiche, and the Final Surrender at Skeleton Canyon

In July 1886, Miles sent Lieutenant Charles B. Gatewood, who had served with Apache scouts in earlier campaigns, to find Geronimo and Naiche and to offer them terms. Gatewood was accompanied by two Chiricahua men, Martine and Kayitah, who knew the party and could approach it, and by an interpreter, George Wratten, along with a small escort. The role of Martine and Kayitah was critical. They were not simply guides; they were Chiricahua men with family and political ties to those they approached, and their willingness to take the risk made contact possible. Apache intermediaries of this kind were the means by which the final negotiation occurred.

In late August, Gatewood's party made contact with Geronimo and Naiche in northern Sonora, in the vicinity of the Bavispe River. The meeting took place on or about August 24–25. Gatewood conveyed Miles's message: that the Chiricahua who had surrendered in March, including Chihuahua's people, had been taken to Florida, that the other Chiricahua at Fort Apache were also being removed, and that Geronimo and Naiche could surrender and be reunited with their families in the East. The news that other Chiricahua had been sent away was not good news, and it may have added to the leaders' sense that the reservation was no longer a refuge. At the same time, their own party was weary and pursued, and the prospect of reunion with kin carried powerful weight.

Naiche's role in the decision deserves emphasis. The conventional telling gives Geronimo the central place in the final surrender, but accounts, including Apache accounts, indicate that Naiche's participation and agreement were essential, both because of his standing among the Chokonen and because the people with Geronimo included many of his relatives and followers. The decision was not a solitary act by one man. It followed discussion among the leaders and the families, in which women's views also mattered, and it reflected a collective judgment about survival.

After the conversation with Gatewood, Geronimo and Naiche agreed to meet Miles and to cross toward the United States, and they moved with Lawton's column toward Arizona. The surrender to Miles took place at Skeleton Canyon in Arizona Territory, where the principal meeting occurred on September 3–4, 1886. The official National Register documentation identifies September 4 as the final surrender date. Miles then sent the party under escort toward Fort Bowie. In this phase, Miles reportedly told the Chiricahua what would happen to them, and the Chiricahua stated what they believed had been promised. The two accounts are different.

What the Chiricahua understood is central. In his October 22 report, General D. S. Stanley recorded Geronimo's and Naiche's claim that Miles had promised they would be reunited with relatives in Florida; his report also describes how they understood the assurances given at Skeleton Canyon. Miles and other officers offered differing accounts of the commitments attached to surrender. The September discussion must not be confused with the proposal for temporary eastern exile and eventual return considered during Crook's March conference. Geronimo's later edited autobiography, official reports, and eyewitness recollections illuminate the disagreement without establishing a jointly signed written treaty. What is established is that the Chiricahua expected reunification, that the government separated members of the party, and that the surrender did not produce the durable peace or freedom from confinement that some participants had anticipated.

Students should be careful to distinguish the surrender from what followed. The agreement of September 1886 concerned the end of the campaign and the immediate disposition of the party. The decision to imprison the Chiricahua as prisoners of war, to deport them to Florida, and to include in that custody people who had not taken part in the final campaign was a separate series of decisions made by military and civilian authorities in Washington. The next division takes up that second history.

The history of the last negotiations illustrates the limits of a purely command-centered account of warfare. Military correspondence naturally emphasizes which officer gave an order and which general received a surrender. Yet the contact itself depended on Apache intermediaries whose knowledge of language, family connections, and the conduct of negotiations was not something the Army could manufacture through superior numbers. Gatewood's previous service made him a recognizable envoy, but Martine and Kayitah helped make an approach possible. Naiche's participation then mattered because the people in the camp were not a detachment that Geronimo could transfer at will. In this sense the final surrender was simultaneously a military event, a negotiation among people with different standing, and a decision taken under concern for the survival of families. The result should not be romanticized as an agreement freely chosen without constraints; neither should the remaining Chiricahua be portrayed as lacking agency merely because the alternatives had become desperate. The difference between making a decision and controlling its consequences is central to what followed.

Section 80.13

The Removal of the Chiricahua: Collective Punishment and Broken Promises

The decision to deport the Chiricahua was extraordinary even by the standards of nineteenth-century Indian policy. After the surrender, federal and military authorities did not limit confinement to Geronimo, Naiche, and the people who had taken part in the final campaign. They extended it to the Chiricahua families who had remained at Fort Apache, many of whom had cooperated with the Army, and to men who were then serving as Army scouts. In the first part of September 1886, hundreds of Chiricahua people were gathered, disarmed, and taken by rail from Arizona to Florida. Earlier detachments, including people associated with Chihuahua's March surrender, had already been sent east. Sources differ on the exact timing and manifests of particular groups, and students should consult the Source Register for the documentary basis of each count.

The government's reasoning combined several concerns. Military officers argued that Chiricahua people, whatever their individual conduct, represented a continuing security risk, because the kin ties among them meant that peaceful families could shelter or supply those who had fled. Territorial officials and civilian leaders in Arizona and New Mexico demanded that the entire population be removed from the region so that no further outbreak could occur. Officials in Washington, having rejected Crook's conditional terms, sought a demonstration that would satisfy public demands. These concerns produced a policy that treated membership in a people as grounds for confinement, rather than individual conduct.

Prisoner-of-war status was the legal device by which the removal was accomplished. The Chiricahua were not tried and convicted of crimes. They were held as prisoners of war under military custody, a status that allowed the Army to confine them without individual proceedings and without fixed terms. The arrangement was legally and morally anomalous. The Chiricahua were not citizens of a foreign state at war with the United States, and many had never fought it. The device allowed the government to avoid constitutional questions about confinement of people who had committed no offense, and it reflected the assumption, common in federal Indian policy, that Indigenous communities could be governed by executive and military authority without the procedural protections owed to other residents.

For students of government power, the most revealing comparison is between three groups taken into the same detention system: the relatively small final party that surrendered near Skeleton Canyon, Chiricahua people who had remained under reservation supervision, and Apache scouts who had served in the Army. Their military records, recent decisions, and relationships to the fighting were not the same. The government's policy nonetheless treated the categories as interchangeable when it decided that all should leave the Southwest. Officials defended this as a preventive measure, arguing that future resistance could not be separated from community support networks. The logic made family ties themselves into evidence of dangerousness. The distinction between a military commander's ability to accept an enemy's surrender and the executive government's authority to confine entire families must therefore remain visible. This chapter does not assume that the legal doctrines of 1886 were identical to modern doctrines of due process. It asks a historically narrower and better-supported question: how did government officials justify holding people without individual trials, even after particular individuals had complied with federal demands?

Historical Vocabulary
Prisoner of War

Definition. In ordinary military usage, a prisoner of war is a combatant held by an opposing power until the end of hostilities. In 1886 the term was applied to the Chiricahua as a legal and administrative label for confinement without trial.

Historical context. The label let the Army hold men, women, and children, including scouts in United States service and families who had not fought, under military supervision for twenty-seven years.

Why it matters. The status explains why captivity continued long after fighting ended and why release required executive and congressional decisions rather than the end of a sentence.

Potential misunderstanding. The term suggests individual combatants captured in battle. Most Chiricahua prisoners were noncombatants or had not fought in the final campaign.

Historical Vocabulary
Surrender: Conditional and Unconditional

Definition. A conditional surrender ends fighting on stated terms; an unconditional surrender leaves the victor free to decide what follows, subject only to whatever commitments it chooses to honor.

Historical context. Crook negotiated what he understood as conditional terms in March 1886. Washington demanded unconditional surrender. Apache participants and American officers later described the September surrender differently.

Why it matters. Whether the Chiricahua were promised anything is central to the later moral and historical judgment of their imprisonment.

Potential misunderstanding. The word "surrender" implies a single, shared understanding. Here, the parties' understandings were different, and the record preserves them separately.

For Chiricahua people, the removal was experienced as a profound betrayal. Families that had complied with American demands, farmed under supervision, and in some cases fought alongside American soldiers were told that they would be sent away from their homeland. The scouts, some of whom had been present in the campaign that ended in the surrender, were disarmed, discharged from service or held despite it, and placed on the same trains. Crook, who regarded the scouts' service as honorable, protested, and civilian reformers such as members of the Indian Rights Association also criticized the policy. Their protests did not reverse it.

The railroad journey was not a return home but the beginning of exile. Prisoners were transported in rail cars across the continent, in many cases without clear knowledge of their destination, and families were divided at the point of departure. Geronimo and a small number of men were separated from the others and held apart. The separation of families, which had been among the leading concerns of the Chiricahua at the surrender, became a defining feature of the policy.

Cause and Effect

From Reservation Dispute to Collective Deportation

Long-term causes

Decades of war and reprisal in the borderlands, loss of homeland and mobility, mutual distrust, and a federal system that placed Apache people under military and agency control with few legal protections.

Short-term causes

Rules and surveillance at Turkey Creek, disputes over tiswin, rumors of arrest, divisions among Chiricahua leaders, and civilian demands for punishment.

Immediate trigger

A drinking episode and fear of arrest in May 1885, followed by the failure of the March 1886 agreement and the rejection of Crook's terms.

Transformation

The departure became a renewed war; the final surrender became a policy of removal affecting the entire Chiricahua population.

Immediate effects

Separation of families, imprisonment of scouts and noncombatants, and the removal of the Chiricahua from the Southwest.

Long-term consequences

Twenty-seven years of captivity, deaths from disease, the division of descendants between Oklahoma and New Mexico, and a lasting contest over memory.

Historical complexity: No single cause produced this outcome. Chiricahua decisions, Army policy, civilian pressure, political disagreement in Washington, and the unequal power of the parties interacted. The transformation of a reservation dispute into collective deportation depended on choices that were not inevitable.

Section 80.14

Florida Captivity: Fort Marion, Fort Pickens, and Family Separation

The Chiricahua prisoners were taken to Florida and divided between two military posts. Most, including women, children, and the many men who had not taken part in the final campaign, were held at Fort Marion in St. Augustine, an old Spanish stone fortress on the Atlantic coast that had earlier been used to confine Plains prisoners in the 1870s. Geronimo and fifteen other men were held separately at Fort Pickens, on Santa Rosa Island near Pensacola on the Gulf coast, as the National Park Service records. The decision to separate the men of the final party from their families, many hundreds of miles away, was a deliberate act of punishment that also served the Army's wish to isolate those it regarded as most dangerous.

The experience of confinement was shaped by the environment and by the institutions that held the prisoners. Fort Marion was a stone fortress with limited space, and the prisoners were housed in rooms and in tents within and near it. Fort Pickens was likewise a coastal fortification. Both posts were in a subtropical climate, humid and hot, utterly unlike the high deserts and mountains in which the Chiricahua had lived. Prisoners were assigned labor, including work at the fort, and were given little control over their daily lives. Visitors, drawn by curiosity about the famous captives, came to look at them, and the Army and the press treated the prisoners as a spectacle.

Illness and death were pervasive. Unfamiliar disease environments, crowding, inadequate diet, and the psychological burden of exile contributed to high mortality, especially among children and the elderly. Tuberculosis, which would remain a major killer in later years, took many lives, and other diseases added to the toll. The records of the period describe deaths but do not always permit exact counts, and students should be cautious about repeating a single total without attributing it. What is clear is that the Chiricahua population declined sharply during the early years of captivity, a loss that was both demographic and cultural, because it removed elders who carried ceremonial and historical knowledge.

Family separation was especially painful. Husbands and wives, parents and children, were divided across distance, and the effort to reunite them became a central concern of Chiricahua leaders and of reformers who took up their cause. The men at Fort Pickens knew that their families were at Fort Marion, and the families knew that the men were held elsewhere. These separations persisted for many months and in some cases years, and the transfers and reunifications did not occur at the same time for everyone, a matter taken up in §80.16.

The documents from Fort Marion permit historians to go beyond a general statement that captivity was unhealthy. An Army final report dated April 27, 1887, now catalogued by the Carlisle Indian School Digital Resource Center, accounts for births, deaths, and movements between Fort Marion, Fort Pickens, Carlisle, and Mount Vernon Barracks. Such a record gives a date-specific administrative view of a changing prisoner population. It is not, however, a complete life history of every prisoner: categories changed when people were transferred, and a death recorded at one place need not tell us the eventual fate of someone moved elsewhere. Nor should conditions at a coastal fort be collapsed into the conditions of an Indian school or of the Alabama barracks. For an accurate mortality calculation, the researcher would need a defined cohort, dates of observation, age categories, and a method for following transfers. The human significance is not diminished by refusing an unsupported single-number death toll. It becomes clearer when students understand that bureaucratic records counted people who had lost control over where their families could live.

Section 80.15

Apache Children, Carlisle, and the Educational Consequences of Captivity

In November 1886, soon after the deportation, federal authorities selected Chiricahua children from among the prisoners at Fort Marion and sent them to Carlisle Indian Industrial School in Pennsylvania, founded in 1879 by Richard Henry Pratt on the principle that Indigenous children should be separated from their communities and educated in the language, habits, and religion of the dominant society. Pratt had earlier supervised Plains prisoners at Fort Marion and brought his educational ideas with him. The documented November 1886 transfer comprised 37 young people (29 boys and 8 girls), with additional Chiricahua transfers in 1887; their parents were themselves prisoners and had constrained power to refuse.

Government correspondence preserved by the Carlisle Indian School Digital Resource Center documents the recruitment and transfer. Officials framed the removal as an opportunity for education and civilization. Army officers and Interior officials discussed which children would be sent, how parental consent would be treated, and who held authority over children whose families were in military custody. These records show that officials discussed parental consent and recruitment even as they organized transfers from a population held in military custody. The existence of consent-seeking procedures does not, by itself, establish that parents had a meaningful ability to refuse. Apache parents' resistance and anguish appear in the record chiefly through officials' descriptions, a reminder that the surviving documents were written by those who held power.

The early Carlisle cohort can be studied through several kinds of evidence that should not be confused. The November 2, 1886 departure notice counted 29 boys and eight girls traveling from Fort Marion; a descriptive enrollment statement names and characterizes students in an administrative format; and a photograph identified as taken on arrival in November shows the group assembled at the school. The photograph records the existence and public presentation of the group, not what the children felt or whether their parents agreed. The lists supply a starting point for tracing names, ages, school experiences, transfers, and deaths, but variant spellings and institutional renaming make such tracing difficult. It is particularly important not to turn the whole history of Native schooling into a single, unverified statistic about this group. The larger boarding-school system will be studied later in the textbook. Here the defining issue is the transfer of children from a captive community at a moment when federal officials simultaneously controlled the parents' location, the terms of travel, and access to an institution designed to reshape family and cultural life.

Life at Carlisle imposed major changes on the children. They were given new clothing, assigned English names, placed in a regimented schedule of classes and labor, and forbidden or discouraged from speaking Apache. They received instruction in Christianity and in trades considered appropriate for Indigenous students. Separated from their families by thousands of miles, they faced loneliness, illness, and in many cases death. Records of the school and of Chiricahua testimony indicate that a significant proportion of the Chiricahua students died during or soon after their time at Carlisle, in many cases of tuberculosis and other diseases, though exact figures vary by source and should be verified in the archival record. Some children survived and returned, and a few, such as Asa Daklugie, later became leaders and interpreters, using the education they had received in service to their communities while retaining their Apache identity.

The Chiricahua case is one example of a broader pattern. The full continental history of boarding and residential schools belongs to Chapters 87 and 88. What the Chiricahua experience adds is the particular situation of children taken while their families were prisoners of war, a situation in which the government's authority over the family was nearly total and the possibility of resistance almost nil.

Section 80.16

Mount Vernon Barracks, Alabama: Disease, Confinement, and Community Survival

In 1887, the Army moved most of the Chiricahua prisoners from Florida to Mount Vernon Barracks, a former arsenal and military post north of Mobile, Alabama. Geronimo and the men at Fort Pickens were moved separately and later, joining their families in 1888, so the transfers and reunifications did not happen at the same moment for everyone. The move was intended to improve health conditions, which had been alarming in Florida, and to consolidate the prisoners in a single location.

Mount Vernon Barracks was not a healthy place. The prisoners lived in log cabins and tents on the grounds, in a damp, forested environment very different from their homeland. Tuberculosis and other infectious diseases continued to take lives, and mortality, especially among children and the young, was severe. The reports of Army surgeons and other officials document these deaths and the poor conditions, while Apache accounts emphasize grief and the loss of relatives. Prisoners were kept under military guard and subject to regular counts and restrictions on movement.

Apache families nonetheless worked to maintain community life. They preserved kinship relations, naming practices, and obligations among relatives. They continued to recognize leaders such as Naiche, Chihuahua, Loco, and Nana, and the Army sometimes dealt with these leaders as intermediaries, which gave them real if limited authority. Ceremonial life persisted within the constraints of confinement, and older people transmitted language, stories, and ceremonial knowledge to the young. Some prisoners worked for wages or in assigned tasks, and the community organized itself under conditions of continuous uncertainty.

The sequence of transfers should be read as a changing administrative process rather than as a single rescue from unhealthy conditions. Fort Marion's own final report helps identify how many people were still there at a particular moment in 1887 and who had been moved elsewhere. Later correspondence and family histories must then be used to establish the staggered reunification of separated relatives. Even where officials presented relocation as a sanitary improvement, a change of site could not solve the basic problems of coercive custody, restricted movement, and prolonged separation from homelands. The choice of Mount Vernon also placed a highland and desert people in an unfamiliar lowland environment, with disease risks that differed from those they had known. Researchers should distinguish the documented occurrence of tuberculosis and other illnesses from a precise causal allocation among climate, housing, nutrition, exposure, and stress: the sources do not support reducing every death to one factor. For Apache families, the policy's duration made the consequences cumulative. A child who survived one move could still spend formative years far from parents, and a family reunited in Alabama remained unable to choose its home.

Everyday Life and Social History

Families under Pursuit and Imprisonment

The ordinary experiences of Chiricahua families changed radically between 1885 and 1894, but certain tasks and responsibilities persisted. During the campaign, women and elders managed food, water, shelter, and the care of children and the sick under conditions of constant movement, and children learned to travel quietly and to be silent when danger approached. In captivity, families lived in crowded quarters, adapted to unfamiliar foods and rations, and faced illness without access to the medicinal plants and ceremonial specialists of their homeland. Mothers mourned children who died in confinement or at distant schools. Men who had been warriors or scouts were reduced to assigned labor and daily counting.

The sources for these experiences are mixed. Military and medical reports record deaths and rations but rarely describe emotional experience. Later Apache testimony preserved by descendants, in tribal histories and in interviews collected in the twentieth century, supplies more intimate detail but was recorded long after the events, shaped by family memory and by the circumstances of each interview. Historians use both, and they take care not to invent specific scenes that the evidence does not support. What the sources show clearly is that Chiricahua families did not merely endure captivity. They worked continuously to keep kin together, honor the dead, teach children, and sustain the practices that made them a people.

Why did military custody continue after the campaigns had ended? Officials in Washington and the Army argued that the prisoners remained a danger, and civilians in Arizona and New Mexico continued to oppose their return. Reformers, military officers who had served with Chiricahua scouts, and some members of Congress argued that the confinement was unjust and unhealthy and that the prisoners should be allowed to settle in a place where they could support themselves. Proposals to move them to different locations occupied the attention of officials for years. By the early 1890s, the question of the prisoners' conditions had become a political issue, and the decision to move them to Fort Sill in 1894 reflected the growing pressure to improve their circumstances while still keeping them in custody.

Division V

Survival, Freedom, and Historical Memory, 1894–1914 and After

Section 80.17

Fort Sill, Oklahoma: Life as Prisoners of War, 1894–1909

In October 1894, the surviving Chiricahua prisoners, roughly three hundred people, were moved from Alabama to Fort Sill in Oklahoma Territory, on lands shared with the Kiowa, Comanche, and Plains Apache. The move was a compromise. It brought the prisoners to a drier, more open country somewhat closer to their homeland, and it allowed them to farm and raise livestock, but it did not end their status as prisoners of war. They remained under Army control and continued to be forbidden to leave without permission.

At Fort Sill, the Chiricahua established villages on the post reservation, built houses, and began farming and raising cattle. Officers supervising them, especially Hugh L. Scott, who supervised the prisoners from 1894 to 1897, encouraged livestock raising and supported some of the community's requests, and the prisoners developed herds and gardens that provided a measure of economic stability. Some men enlisted as Indian scouts attached to the Army, receiving pay and a form of service that did not end their captivity. Children attended schools, some at the post and some away. The community remained subject to counts, passes, and restrictions, but within those limits its members rebuilt a rhythm of work, ceremony, and family life.

Leadership continued to operate within and beneath the Army's structure. Naiche, Chihuahua until his death in 1901, Loco until his death in 1905, and other older leaders remained important, and younger men educated at Carlisle, such as Asa Daklugie, took on roles as interpreters and advocates. The Army dealt with these leaders as spokesmen, but the community also relied on kin networks and ceremonial authority that officers could not fully see or control. Different groups within the prisoner population, shaped by their earlier band affiliations and by the divisions of the war years, maintained their own identities even as they shared a common fate.

The prisoners repeatedly petitioned for release and for permission to return to their homelands in Arizona and New Mexico. The requests were supported by some officers and reformers, who argued that the original justification for confinement had long since disappeared, and opposed by civilians in the Southwest and by officials who feared renewed conflict or who held that the prisoners had forfeited any right to return. Geronimo himself, as discussed in §80.18, asked Theodore Roosevelt in 1905 for permission to return and was refused. The Fort Sill Apache Tribe's own historical account of these years, which emphasizes continuity of family and community and the persistent hope of return, is central to understanding this period and should be read alongside the Army's records, not beneath them.

The conditions of life remained severe even as they improved. Deaths from tuberculosis and other diseases continued, and the knowledge that they were prisoners in a land not their own shaped the community's outlook. At the same time, the births of children at Fort Sill, some of whom would later become tribal leaders, and the gradual rebuilding of herds, houses, and institutions show a community that was not merely surviving but reconstituting itself. Understanding this duality is essential: the story of the Fort Sill years is both a story of continued injustice and a story of determined collective effort.

Fort Sill's relative opportunity to raise cattle should not be mistaken for an uncomplicated transfer of sovereignty over the land. The Fort Sill Apache Tribe recalls that the prisoners were led to expect a permanent home on the military reservation and that many acted on that expectation by establishing homes and productive farms. At the same time, land use at the post depended on the Army's continuing control and on decisions taken in Washington. The community's investments and the government's promises therefore raised a new political question: would a people who had improved a place while held under guard be permitted to remain there when the Army later wanted the land for other purposes? This tension links the 1890s experience to the disputed arrangements of 1913–1914. It also explains why economic achievement did not cancel imprisonment. The ability to keep livestock, educate children, and petition through recognized leaders was important, but the prisoners still lacked control over the location of their own community and over when custody would end.

Section 80.18

Geronimo as Prisoner, Public Figure, and Author

While remaining a prisoner, Geronimo became one of the most famous Indigenous people in the United States. In the late 1890s and early 1900s, Army authorities permitted him to appear at expositions and public gatherings under guard, including the Trans-Mississippi and International Exposition in Omaha in 1898 and the Louisiana Purchase Exposition in St. Louis in 1904, where he was displayed and sold souvenirs. On March 4, 1905, he rode in Theodore Roosevelt's inaugural parade in Washington, D.C., together with other prominent Indigenous leaders. Newspapers covered these appearances with a mixture of fascination and condescension, presenting him as a relic of a vanished frontier.

His celebrity was a form of both agency and constraint. He used public appearances to earn money and to press for the return of his people to their homeland. After the inauguration, he asked Roosevelt to allow him and his people to go back to Arizona, and the request was refused. The incident shows that fame did not translate into political power. Geronimo was a celebrity and a prisoner at the same time, and the American public that gathered to look at him was largely uninterested in his people's actual claims.

In 1906, Geronimo's Story of His Life appeared, taken down by S. M. Barrett, a school superintendent in Lawton, Oklahoma, who had obtained permission from federal authorities to record it. The narrative was dictated in Apache and translated by Asa Daklugie, who interpreted for Geronimo, and Barrett then edited and arranged the text. Army and government oversight shaped the conditions of the project, and Barrett added introductory material, notes, and structure. The book is therefore best understood as a mediated first-person account rather than an unedited verbatim autobiography. It is nonetheless a precious record, preserving Geronimo's own version of his early life, family, and wars, and his claims about injustice, though it must be read with attention to translation, editing, omission, and the political risks of speaking critically while in custody.

The production of the autobiography can itself be reconstructed as a negotiation over the right to tell history. Barrett's introduction records his efforts to secure permission from military authorities and ultimately presidential approval for the project; he also describes the difficulties of recording a narrative that Geronimo delivered through Asa Deklugie rather than dictating in the form preferred by an English-language editor. Those circumstances should be included in an assessment of the book's voice. Geronimo chose to recount particular wrongs and encounters, while Barrett selected the chapters, mediated the English presentation, and added government documents. The result is neither a fabrication to discard nor an untouched transcript to quote without context. It is a historical artifact created by several people whose access to authority was unequal. Comparing the account with the Army's letters on surrender terms and with tribal historical teaching reveals how readers can learn from Geronimo without assuming that his version represents every Chiricahua perspective.

Geronimo died at Fort Sill on February 17, 1909, of pneumonia, after a period of illness. He was buried in the Apache prisoners' cemetery at the post. He never returned to Arizona. Section 80.19

The End of Captivity, 1913–1914: Mescalero and Fort Sill

The release of the Chiricahua came after years of petitions, official inquiries, and political pressure. Chiricahua leaders and their supporters continued to press for freedom, and by the early 1910s, federal authorities and Congress moved toward ending prisoner-of-war status. The Army's own interest in the Fort Sill land, which it wished to use for artillery training, also played a role, as the post's expansion made the continued settlement of the prisoners there awkward. Officials eventually presented alternatives to the Chiricahua, but a return to their former homelands was not among the options.

The choice was between moving to the Mescalero Apache Reservation in New Mexico, where they would be among kin and fellow Apache in a landscape closer to their homeland, and remaining in Oklahoma, where they would receive individual allotments of land near Fort Sill. It was a difficult decision. Mescalero offered community, a familiar region, and the prospect of reunion with relatives, but it meant becoming part of another Apache nation's reservation, with its own government and resources. Oklahoma offered land but separation from the Southwest and from many relatives. Families considered ties of kinship, the memory of their homeland, the condition of their land, and the practical possibilities of livelihood.

The two outcomes should be distinguished chronologically. In 1913, about two hundred Chiricahua people moved to the Mescalero Apache Reservation, as the Mescalero Apache Tribe records. The Mescalero Apache community received them, and the arrival created a new community of Chiricahua descendants within the Mescalero Apache Tribe. In 1914, the Fort Sill Apache Tribe identifies approximately eighty-one individuals from about twenty families who remained in Oklahoma and were released and received allotments. These are separate events with different arrangements, and treating them as a single release obscures the choices families made and the different communities that resulted.

The consequences were lasting. Families were divided between the two places, and kinship ties stretched across the distance. At Mescalero, leaders such as Naiche, who died in 1921, and Asa Daklugie played roles in community life, and the Chiricahua became part of a larger tribal population whose history includes the Mescalero and Lipan peoples. In Oklahoma, the Fort Sill Apache community, living on allotments scattered among other residents, maintained a separate identity and, later in the twentieth century, organized a tribal government and sought federal recognition and a land base. Each community preserved its own historical memory of the captivity and exile.

The two eventual destinations cannot be described as a choice made on equal terms. As the Fort Sill Apache Tribe explains, federal officials and local interests pressed to remove the prisoners from Fort Sill's military lands, while some Apache families insisted on the land they had been told would be their permanent home. Mescalero leaders extended an invitation to the Chiricahua, but the decision was made against the background of land allotment, Army requirements, and the continuing exclusion of a return to the old Chiricahua homeland. The Mescalero Apache Tribe's own history records the arrival of approximately 200 Chiricahua in 1913; the Fort Sill Apache Tribe describes the different path of roughly eighty-one people from twenty families settled on allotments near Apache and Fletcher and released in 1914. These tribal accounts explain why 'the Chiricahua were freed in 1913' is an insufficient summary: the official and practical end of custody unfolded differently for the two groups, while neither regained collective possession of the old homeland through the settlement.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Political and Social Authority before Surrender, in Captivity, and after Release

What changed

  • The Chiricahua lost mobility, homeland, and the capacity to move among mountains and water sources
  • Formal leadership operated through the Army, which dealt with certain leaders as spokesmen and ignored others
  • Many elders and children died, altering the transmission of knowledge
  • After 1913–1914, descendants were divided between two communities in two states with different legal and political frameworks

What stayed the same

  • Kinship networks and obligations remained the structure of community life
  • Recognition of leaders by consent, kin ties, and judgment continued beneath Army supervision
  • Language, ceremony, and historical memory were transmitted, despite schooling and confinement
  • The claim to homeland and the hope of return persisted across generations

Pattern. Change was abrupt in 1886, when removal ended mobility and divided families, then gradual and uneven in captivity, as communities adapted to farming, wage labor, and military supervision. It fell most heavily on children, elders, and the families removed despite having taken no part in the final campaign. Continuity was strongest in kinship and memory, and it matters because it explains how a people survived a policy designed to disperse and subordinate it.

Section 80.20

The End of the Major Southwestern Wars: Survival, Sovereignty, and Historical Memory

What did the surrender of 1886 actually end? It ended the last large-scale Chiricahua military campaigns against the United States. It did not end Apache political life, cultural continuity, or claims to homeland, and it did not end the consequences of removal. It also did not end all Indigenous resistance or military conflict elsewhere in North America. Struggles over land, treaty rights, schooling, and sovereignty continued across the continent, as later chapters show, and Apache peoples in Arizona, including the White Mountain and San Carlos Apache, continued to live on and to shape the reservations that remained in the Southwest.

Representations of the Apache Wars have changed repeatedly. In the late nineteenth century, newspapers and officers cast Geronimo as a savage villain and the Army as a heroic civilizing force. In the twentieth century, films and popular histories reversed or complicated that image, sometimes presenting him as a tragic or noble figure, but often continuing to focus on a single man and on a frontier narrative in which Indigenous people were obstacles or victims. Soldiers and parachutists adopted his name as a shout of daring, and in later years the use of his name for military purposes drew objections from Apache leaders who regarded it as offensive. Monuments and place names across the Southwest commemorate officers and episodes of the campaign, often in terms that omit the experience of the people deported.

Tribal histories offer a different narrative. The Fort Sill Apache Tribe and the Mescalero Apache Tribe, among others, tell the story as a history of a people, centered on families, leaders including Naiche and women such as Tahdeste, the scouts' betrayal, the long captivity, and the survival and return. These accounts do not deny the importance of Geronimo but place him within a community history in which he was one of many significant figures. Archival documents allow historians to test and enrich these accounts, while scholarly works by historians such as Edwin Sweeney, Angie Debo, Robert Utley, and Paul Andrew Hutton differ in emphasis, in judgments about the leaders, and in their attention to Apache sources. Reading these works against one another and against tribal histories is part of the historian's task.

The life of Mildred Imach Cleghorn gives this longer chronology a concrete human scale without extending the military campaign into another chapter. The Fort Sill Apache Tribe records that she was born at Fort Sill on December 11, 1910, while her family remained under prisoner-of-war control. She later became an educator, artist, and a leader of the Fort Sill Apache Tribe, serving as chairperson from 1976 to 1995. Her work preserving and teaching cultural history illustrates what a narrative ending only in September 1886 cannot show. Someone born decades after the last campaign could still begin life inside its system of confinement, yet go on to help govern and represent a living Indigenous community. Cleghorn's life should not be treated as a symbolic substitute for the experiences of other prisoners. Rather, it points students toward sources produced by descendants and tribal governments and reminds them that the history of the Apache Wars includes institutional continuity, public leadership, and family memory well beyond military surrender.

Historical Debate

Was the End of the Apache Wars a Negotiated Peace, a Military Defeat, or an Imposed Exile?

The question: How should historians characterize the events of 1886? The label chosen shapes how the surrender, the promises, and the imprisonment are understood.

Interpretation A — A negotiated peace: Some accounts emphasize that Geronimo and Naiche chose to surrender after talks with Gatewood and Miles, that Crook's March conference had produced an agreement on terms, and that the final surrender involved discussion and Apache agency. On this view, the end of the war was a settlement, even if its terms were later violated.
Interpretation B — A military defeat: Others stress the cumulative military pressure of the pursuit, the exhaustion of the Chiricahua party, the loss of safe refuges, and the Army's insistence on unconditional surrender. On this view, the surrender was the end of a campaign the Chiricahua could no longer sustain.
Interpretation C — An imposed exile: Tribal histories and many modern scholars emphasize what followed: the collective removal of people who had not fought, the imprisonment of scouts, and the twenty-seven years of captivity. On this view, the central historical fact is not the surrender but the exile imposed on a whole people.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the Embudos conference record, officers' reports, the surrender testimony, deportation orders, mortality records, and Apache oral and tribal histories.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns terminology, interpretation of the surrender terms, and the weight given to Apache versus official sources, rather than the basic sequence of events.

Why it matters: If the end of the war was a negotiated peace, the imprisonment looks like a breach of agreement. If it was defeat, it looks like an expected consequence. If it was exile, the campaign's end is only the beginning of the story. These interpretations are not mutually exclusive, and many historians combine elements of each.

Student task: Using the primary sources in this chapter, assess each interpretation. Identify what additional evidence could help decide among them.

The historical significance of 1886 cannot be reduced to one man's surrender. The event brought together the military pressure of two states, the decisions of many Apache leaders and families, the work of scouts and intermediaries, and the policy of a government that chose collective removal over individual accountability. Its consequences extended through a generation of captivity and into the formation of the Mescalero and Fort Sill Apache communities. The people who survived those years are the main historical outcome of this chapter: they carried language, kinship, ceremony, and memory through exile, and their descendants continue to govern communities and to tell the story in their own terms. In that sense, the end of the major Southwestern wars was not the end of an Indigenous history but a turning point within one.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the final Chiricahua campaigns of 1885–1886 emerged from unresolved conflicts over homeland, security, political authority, and the reliability of government promises. After Crook's 1883 expedition, Chiricahua families returned to reservation supervision under conditions that combined farming, rations, surveillance, and rules that many experienced as intrusive. The settlement was fragile because the security of the people it governed depended on promises they could not verify, and because the Army, the Indian Service, civilian settlers, and Apache communities understood peace differently.

Chiricahua leadership was plural. Geronimo, known as Goyaałé, was an influential medicine man and war leader whose reputation was spiritual and military rather than that of a supreme chief. Naiche, a son of Cochise, held a distinct position within the Chokonen leadership network, and leaders such as Chihuahua, Loco, Chato, Mangus, and Nana made different decisions about reservation life, negotiation, and flight. The departure of May 1885 involved a mixed party of fighting men, women, children, and elders, set off by rumor, alcohol, fear of arrest, and accumulated grievance.

The campaign unfolded across a landscape that Chiricahua families knew as a single space, from Arizona and New Mexico into Sonora and Chihuahua and the Sierra Madre. Knowledge of water, terrain, and seasons gave small mobile groups real advantages against larger forces, while distance, supply, and divided jurisdiction hindered American and Mexican columns. The violence of the campaign fell on many communities, and casualty figures must be attributed to their sources. Women and elders carried the burdens of survival and sometimes acted as intermediaries.

Apache scouts were indispensable to the American campaigns. Their motives were varied, and their service reflected political division and survival strategy rather than simple disloyalty. Crawford's expedition and his death in January 1886 illustrated both the reliance on scouts and the fragility of cross-border cooperation, and it opened a path to the conference at Cañon de los Embudos, where Crook and Chiricahua leaders reached a conditional understanding that Washington rejected.

The collapse of the March surrender, the departure of Geronimo and Naiche, and Crook's replacement by Miles led to the final pursuit, in which sustained pressure, exhaustion, political uncertainty, and the work of Apache intermediaries such as Martine and Kayitah, together with Lieutenant Gatewood, produced the surrender at Skeleton Canyon on September 4, 1886. Naiche's participation was essential, and the terms the participants believed they had received are recorded in testimony that conflicts with officers' accounts.

Surrender and captivity are separate events. The decision to deport the entire Chiricahua population, including families who had remained at peace and men serving as scouts, was a policy of collective punishment carried out through prisoner-of-war status without individual trials. It transformed a military surrender into exile and separated families.

Captivity in Florida, Alabama, and Oklahoma brought disease, mortality, and the removal of children to Carlisle Indian Industrial School, which linked the Chiricahua case to federal assimilation policy. Yet families preserved kinship, leadership, language, and ceremony, rebuilt farming and herding at Fort Sill, and repeatedly petitioned to return home.

Geronimo's celebrity during his captivity, including his appearance at Roosevelt's inauguration and the 1906 publication of his mediated life history, did not secure his return or his people's freedom. He died at Fort Sill in 1909. The release of 1913–1914 divided survivors between Mescalero and Oklahoma, creating two descendant communities with distinct but related histories.

The historical debate over whether 1886 was a negotiated peace, a military defeat, or an imposed exile shows that interpretation depends on which evidence is privileged. Official records, Apache testimony, and tribal histories each illuminate different dimensions, and none alone suffices. Popular memory has often reduced the story to one man, whereas tribal histories place him within a community and emphasize survival.

The final lesson is that the end of the major Southwestern wars did not end Apache history. The Fort Sill Apache Tribe, the Mescalero Apache Tribe, and other Apache communities continue as political and cultural nations, and the significance of 1886 lies in the survival of the people, not in the capture of one leader.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows how the end of a war can be the beginning of a different and longer history. The surrender of 1886 closed the last large Chiricahua military campaign, but the federal government's decision to deport the entire people, including those who had cooperated, turned a campaign's end into a generation of captivity. Understanding that distinction is essential for reading the history of conquest accurately.

The chapter also reveals how historical narratives are built. A single famous figure can stand in for a whole people, and a dramatic surrender can overshadow its aftermath. By attending to Naiche and other leaders, to scouts, women, and families, and to tribal histories alongside military records, we see a more complete and more accurate picture.

The experience of the Chiricahua connects to larger processes taken up elsewhere in this textbook: the reservation order examined in Chapter 84, the assimilation policies of Chapters 87 and 88, the Indigenous struggles for recognition and sovereignty in later volumes, and the continuing contests over memory examined in Chapter 132. What survives of the Chiricahua story is not a closed chapter of frontier history but a continuing community history, carried by the Fort Sill Apache Tribe, the Mescalero Apache Tribe, and their citizens.
