Chapter Opening

What did Indigenous military service mean once peace returned? For Miguel Trujillo, an Isleta Pueblo Marine veteran, the question was posed at a county registration office in New Mexico in 1948.

In 1948, Miguel Trujillo, a member of Isleta Pueblo in New Mexico and a veteran of the United States Marine Corps in the Second World War, went to register to vote and was refused. The refusal rested on a provision of the New Mexico constitution excluding "Indians not taxed" from the franchise. Trujillo sued, and a federal court in Trujillo v. Garley ruled that the exclusion could not stand. The episode is documented in court records and in the later accounts of New Mexico and Pueblo institutions, and it poses the question that organizes this chapter: what did Indigenous military service mean once the war was over?

Trujillo was not alone. Across the United States and Canada, Indigenous veterans returned to communities and to legal systems that treated them differently from other veterans. Many used education and training benefits, and some built careers in teaching, administration, and the professions. Others found that benefits designed for a mainstream population did not work on trust land or reserve land, that offices and lenders were distant, or that officials still held authority over their housing, land, and personal status. Veterans were not the only people who returned. War workers, nurses, and family members came home to a postwar economy that offered some new jobs and closed many old ones.

Beneath these individual experiences lay broader change. Indigenous populations were beginning to grow, though the figures are hard to read because governments counted people by their own categories. Health conditions, especially tuberculosis and infant mortality, were improving unevenly as antibiotics and public-health services arrived. Wage work, migration, and federal development programs such as the Navajo-Hopi Rehabilitation Act of 1950 altered household economies, while uranium mining and large public works brought risks that would become clear later. In Mexico, a new Instituto Nacional Indigenista, created in 1948, began regional programs of education and development.

Education became a principal postwar aspiration. Veterans used the GI Bill and its Canadian counterparts; parents pressed for schools; and governments expanded facilities while continuing assimilationist policies, English-only or Spanish-oriented instruction, and boarding schools that separated children from families. The question was not whether Indigenous people wanted schooling, since many plainly did, but who would control it and on what terms.

Political expectations rose accordingly. Court challenges in Arizona and New Mexico in 1948 removed major state barriers to Native voting. The Indian Claims Commission Act of 1946 created a forum for historical claims, though only for money, not land. In Canada, a parliamentary committee heard Indigenous testimony from 1946 to 1948, and the Indian Act was revised in 1951, repealing bans on ceremonies and on claims fundraising while preserving ministerial authority. In Alaska, the Native Brotherhood and Sisterhood secured an anti-discrimination law in 1945. These measures had different purposes and institutions, and they should not be merged into a single story.

Indigenous organizations grew in strength. The National Congress of American Indians, founded in 1944, the North American Indian Brotherhood in Canada, provincial associations, tribal councils, and veterans' groups articulated demands for services and equal rights. Their positions were varied, but a recurring theme was that improved living conditions need not and should not come at the price of tribal governments, treaty rights, land, language, and ceremony.

By the early 1950s, governments and Indigenous communities were advancing competing visions of the postwar future. For many officials, progress meant integration into national institutions and, in the United States, the winding down of federal responsibilities. For many Indigenous communities, it meant better schools, health care, housing, and employment while preserving their continuing political existence. This chapter, which treats the period from 1945 to about 1953, explains how those visions took shape, using legislation, court decisions, census data, agency and parliamentary records, oral histories, and the work of historians. It prepares the ground for Chapter 104, on termination, and it builds on Chapters 101 and 102, which cover Indigenous military service itself.

Historical Context

Postwar developments built on earlier institutions. In the United States, the Meriam Report of 1928 had criticized federal Indian administration, and the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 ended allotment, authorized tribal constitutions and corporations, and provided limited credit. Commissioner John Collier's administration promoted community schools and cultural tolerance, but also imposed measures such as livestock reduction on the Navajo Reservation that many Diné remembered with bitterness. The Depression brought relief work, including the Indian Division of the Civilian Conservation Corps. During the war the Indian Office moved its headquarters to Chicago, and Collier resigned in 1945.

In Canada, the Indian Act of 1876 and its amendments gave the federal government authority over status, reserves, band funds, and education. Residential schools were run by churches with federal support. Enfranchisement was available as a path out of status, and amendments had banned certain ceremonies and, from 1927, fundraising for land claims. The Indian Affairs Branch moved into the Department of Mines and Resources in 1936. Indigenous organizations, including the Native Brotherhood of British Columbia and, from 1943, the North American Indian Brotherhood, pressed for change. Inuit were recognized as within federal jurisdiction by a 1939 Supreme Court decision, and Métis people were largely outside federal Indian administration.

In Alaska, the Alaska Native Brotherhood and Sisterhood had campaigned since the early twentieth century against segregation and for citizenship and voting. In Mexico, the revolutionary land reform, the ejido, and the Cárdenas government's Departamento de Asuntos Indígenas formed the background to the Pátzcuaro Congress of 1940 and the development of indigenista institutions.

The war itself produced social change. Tens of thousands of Indigenous people in the United States and thousands in Canada served in the forces, and many others took war jobs. Wages, travel, and exposure to new institutions altered expectations, while communities coped with absence, casualties, and the strains of wartime administration. These developments, treated in Chapters 101 and 102, are the immediate background to the postwar period, and this chapter picks up as demobilization begins.

Section 103.1

Indigenous North America at the End of the Second World War, 1945

The Second World War ended in stages for Indigenous communities, as it did for other North Americans. Germany's surrender in May 1945 and Japan's in August and September of that year began a demobilization that stretched across many months and, for some personnel, into 1946 and beyond. Indigenous servicemen and servicewomen came home by train, bus, truck, and on foot to reservations, reserves, pueblos, villages, towns, and cities. Many had spent years away from their communities; others had left only briefly and returned to find the places they had known changed by the war economy. They came back to a continent in which the central question was no longer how to win a war but what the peace would be for.

The communities that received them had been shaped by the preceding decade and a half. The Great Depression had reduced already limited incomes on reservations and reserves, and in the United States the Indian New Deal had introduced tribal constitutions, revolving credit, and new land policies whose results were uneven and contested. On the Navajo Reservation, livestock reduction programs imposed in the 1930s had altered the economy of herding families. In Canada, the Indian Act continued to give federal officials wide authority over reserve life. Wartime brought changes of its own: military allotments and wages sent home by relatives, defense-industry jobs for those who left, labor shortages that created work for others, and the absence of a large share of working-age men and some women from their home communities. The war also left grief. Some families received telegrams or notices of death, missing service members, or wounded men whose disabilities would shape household life for decades.

The policy environments to which veterans returned were not the same. In the United States, Indigenous people were citizens under the Indian Citizenship Act of 1924 but remained subject to a distinctive body of federal Indian law, to the supervision of the Bureau of Indian Affairs over trust lands and many services, and, in several states, to restrictions on voting and access to public programs. In Canada, status Indians lived under the Indian Act, which governed reserves, band membership, and aspects of personal status, and since 1936 the Indian Affairs Branch had been located within the Department of Mines and Resources. Inuit, who were recognized by a 1939 Supreme Court of Canada decision as falling within federal jurisdiction though not subject to the Indian Act, and Métis, who had no reserves and were generally treated as ordinary citizens by federal law, occupied different administrative positions. Alaska was a territory of the United States with its own Native Service, territorial legislature, and Native rights organizations. Mexico had a different history again, rooted in the revolutionary land reforms of the early twentieth century and in an official indigenismo that treated Indigenous peoples as a distinct social problem for national policy.

It would be a mistake to describe the returning veterans only as individuals. They came back as citizens of nations, members of clans, societies, and extended families, and participants in ceremonial and political communities that had their own expectations of them. A Diné veteran returning to a sheep camp, a Haudenosaunee veteran returning to Six Nations, a Lakota veteran returning to a reservation where leasing of allotted land provided much of the local income, a Tlingit veteran in southeast Alaska, and an Anishinaabe veteran from the Great Lakes were each re-entering a community with specific obligations and specific histories. Their service had been performed for the United States or Canada, but the meaning communities gave to it often drew on older traditions of warrior societies, protective ceremony, and obligations to kin.

The practical problems of return were immediate. Employment was the first. Reservation and reserve economies offered limited wage work, and veterans seeking jobs often discovered that the skills they had gained in the military, in mechanics, communications, construction, administration, or medicine, had few local outlets. Housing and household economies were strained by overcrowding and by the presence of returning family members. Medical needs, whether for war wounds, tuberculosis, or ordinary care, ran into the scarcity of hospitals and the distances to reach them. And community responsibilities, from farming and herding to ceremonial duties and political service, awaited those who had been absent. For some veterans the return was a homecoming; for others it was a period of disorientation, and for many it was both.

This section therefore frames the chapter's central problem. Indigenous veterans and war workers came back with expectations shaped by service, travel, wages, and exposure to institutions and ideas beyond their communities. They found governments, still operating from older assumptions, that were beginning to reconsider Indigenous policy but were not yet prepared to recognize the aspirations that had developed during the war. The following sections trace how those expectations were tested in veterans' benefits, health care, economic development, schooling, voting, land claims, and administrative reform between 1945 and about 1953.

Section 103.2

American Indian Veterans, the GI Bill, and Unequal Access to Benefits

The Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944, universally known as the GI Bill, was signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt on June 22, 1944, and became the central instrument of American veterans' readjustment. Its provisions were organized in titles: hospitalization and claims procedures; education and training; government-guaranteed loans for homes, farms, and businesses; employment services; and readjustment allowances for unemployed veterans. For most Americans these provisions became a gateway to college, vocational training, home ownership, and business formation. The statute did not distinguish veterans by race or by tribal status, and American Indian veterans were eligible under its terms.

The education and training provisions allowed eligible veterans to receive tuition and fees up to a stated annual limit and a monthly subsistence allowance, for a period tied to the length of their service and subject to an overall cap. Veterans could attend colleges, but also vocational schools, business and technical institutions, and on-the-job and agricultural training programs. The loan provisions did not provide money directly but allowed the Veterans Administration to guarantee a portion of a loan made by a private lender, initially up to a modest ceiling that Congress soon raised. The unemployment allowance, in the amount of twenty dollars a week for a limited period, was meant to support veterans while they looked for work. Veterans with service-connected disabilities could also qualify for medical treatment, disability compensation, and, where applicable, pensions through the Veterans Administration.

These benefits were real, and many Native veterans used them. Documentary and oral-history evidence shows American Indian veterans enrolling in colleges and technical schools, taking part in training programs on farms and in trades, receiving disability payments, and using the unemployment allowance. But the formal availability of a benefit did not guarantee that it could be used. The most consequential obstacle concerned the home and farm loan provisions. A lender making a guaranteed loan needed collateral that it could foreclose upon in the event of default. Much Indian land was held in trust by the United States and was legally restricted against sale and mortgage, which meant that a veteran living on a reservation often could not offer the land or a house built upon it as security. The loan guarantee therefore had limited use in precisely the places where many Native veterans lived and where housing needs were greatest. Some veterans could use loans to buy property off the reservation, but doing so required them to leave their communities and to face local housing markets in which discrimination was common.

Other obstacles were practical. Reservation communities were frequently distant from banks, mortgage lenders, and local Veterans Administration offices. Information about benefits spread unevenly, often through the Bureau of Indian Affairs, tribal officials, veterans' organizations, and word of mouth. Many veterans had interrupted or limited schooling, which complicated admission to colleges and training programs, and some faced language barriers. Lenders and employers in towns bordering reservations sometimes exhibited prejudice. In several states, county and state agencies that administered programs connected to the federal system, such as public assistance and some employment services, treated reservation residents as ineligible or as the responsibility of the federal government, and the reasoning applied to benefits that were meant to be national.

Historians therefore distinguish between eligibility and access. Eligibility is established by the statute and by the Veterans Administration's rules; access is determined by whether a veteran could actually reach a program, satisfy its conditions, and obtain the service in practice. For Native veterans, access varied by region, by tribe, by the presence or absence of local institutions, by the vigor of Bureau of Indian Affairs and tribal assistance, and by the veteran's own education and family resources. Some veterans, particularly those with prior schooling, found the education benefit transformative. Others received almost nothing beyond disability compensation. The history of the GI Bill for Indigenous veterans is thus not a story of either exclusion or equal participation, but of a national program passing through local conditions that sorted people differently.

The benefits also had consequences for communities. Cash from disability compensation and subsistence allowances entered local economies. Veterans who completed training returned as skilled workers, teachers, tribal administrators, and officers of tribal governments. At the same time, the loan guarantee's limited usefulness exposed a structural feature of trust land that would continue to restrict Indigenous economic development long after the 1940s. The question of why a program designed to promote ownership could not readily reach people living on land the United States itself held in trust leads directly to the later sections on economic development.

Primary Source Window

Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944 (the GI Bill)

Creator: United States Congress; signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt · Date: June 22, 1944 (Public Law 78-346) · Source type: Federal statute

Context: Congress wrote the act to ease demobilization of millions of service members and to avoid the unemployment and unrest that followed the First World War. It was written for veterans, administrators of the Veterans Administration, educational institutions, and private lenders. It was not written specifically for Indigenous veterans, and its text is race-neutral.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): The act is organized in titles. One title deals with hospitalization and claims; another with education and training, offering tuition, fees, and subsistence for a period linked to length of service; another with government-guaranteed loans for homes, farms, and businesses; another with employment services; and another with readjustment allowances for unemployed veterans. The loan provisions work by guaranteeing part of a loan made by a private lender.

Analysis: The statute shows what was formally available to Indian veterans: education, training, medical care, unemployment allowance, and loan guarantees on the same terms as other veterans. Read alongside the legal status of trust land, it also shows how a neutral text could have unequal effects, since a guarantee presupposed collateral that a lender could claim.

Limitations: A statute states entitlements, not outcomes. It does not record how many Native veterans applied, how local offices treated them, or why lenders declined. Those questions require Veterans Administration and Bureau of Indian Affairs records, tribal histories, and oral testimony.

Perspective: The perspective is that of federal lawmakers and administrators. Native veterans, tribal councils, and reservation lenders or their absence are not represented in the text; their perspectives survive in letters, agency reports, and later interviews.

Verified documentary access: National Archives: Servicemen’s Readjustment Act of 1944 (original enrolled act). Citation: Public Law 78-346, June 22, 1944, 58 Stat. 284. The text establishes formal eligibility, not individualized records of veteran access.

Historical Vocabulary
Trust Land and Reserve Land (Inalienability)

Definition. In the United States, trust land is land whose legal title is held by the federal government for the benefit of a tribe or an individual Indian, generally restricted against sale, taxation, and mortgage without federal approval. In Canada, reserve land is held by the Crown for the use and benefit of a band, and the Indian Act restricts its sale and the seizure of property on reserves.

Historical context. These protections arose in the nineteenth century, partly to shield Indigenous land from loss, and were retained as protective features of federal and Crown responsibility.

Why it matters. The same protections that guarded land from alienation prevented its use as collateral. That limited access to mortgage credit, including veterans' loan guarantees, and shaped the economic history of reservations and reserves.

Potential misunderstanding. Inalienability is not simply a disadvantage imposed on Indigenous peoples. Many nations value it as protection of homelands, and later termination and allotment policies show the dangers of removing it. The historical problem is the combination of protection with federal control and limited credit.

Section 103.3

First Nations, Métis, and Inuit Veterans in Postwar Canada

Canada's postwar program for veterans, often called the Veterans Charter, combined several pieces of legislation passed during and after the war: provisions for reinstatement in civilian employment, rehabilitation grants and training, war service gratuities and re-establishment credits, pensions for disability and death, and the Veterans' Land Act of 1942, which offered veterans a route to farm or small-holding ownership. Education assistance allowed veterans to attend universities and vocational schools. Together these measures constituted one of the most extensive demobilization programs in the country's history, and they were available to veterans of Indigenous ancestry as well as others.

First Nations veterans, however, returned to a legal situation in which these programs intersected with the Indian Act. On reserves, land was held by the Crown for the use and benefit of the band, and individual members held rights to land through allotments recorded by location tickets. This structure meant that a status Indian veteran on a reserve did not own land in the way assumed by the Veterans' Land Act, which was designed around veterans acquiring title or secured interests in land. Veterans Affairs Canada's own historical summaries describe how the arrangements for reserve veterans differed: the Department of Indian Affairs, rather than the veterans' land administration, held title and administered funds and land transactions for these veterans, and the resulting benefits were in practice structured differently from, and often less advantageous than, those received by other veterans. A veteran who wished to use land-based benefits as other veterans did could do so off the reserve, but this might mean leaving the community and, depending on circumstances, could raise questions about status.

This was not simply a matter of paperwork. Indian Affairs officials had broad powers over reserve life, including approval of land transactions, administration of band funds, and the supervision of individual veterans' progress. First Nations veterans, who had served as soldiers in a national army and in some cases risen to non-commissioned rank, found themselves again dealing with agents who had authority over their housing, farming, and personal affairs. The Royal Canadian Legion and other veterans' associations became sites where some of these grievances were voiced, and the Special Joint Committee of the Senate and House of Commons on the Indian Act, which held hearings from 1946 to 1948, heard testimony from Indigenous delegations about veterans' treatment alongside broader criticisms of the Indian Act.

Métis and Inuit veterans occupied different administrative positions. Métis people did not have a reserve base or status under the Indian Act and were generally treated as ordinary civilians for purposes of veterans' benefits. That could mean eligibility on paper under the same rules as other veterans, but Métis communities, many of them rural and poor, in some cases lacked the land and credit that benefits presupposed, and the provincial governments of the Prairie provinces, not the federal Indian Affairs Branch, handled many Métis welfare questions. Inuit military service in the Second World War was limited, and Inuit were administered through the northern branch of the federal government rather than through the Indian Act. The Canadian Rangers, established in 1947 to provide a local military presence in remote regions, would later become an important institution for Inuit and other northern residents, but that development lies largely beyond the chapter's period.

The career of Tommy Prince, an Ojibwe man from Manitoba who served in the First Special Service Force and received the Military Medal and the American Silver Star, is often used to illustrate the position of First Nations veterans. His postwar difficulties in finding steady employment, and his later reenlistment for the Korean War, are documented in biographical accounts and show how a decorated soldier could struggle in civilian life. The case needs to be read with care. Prince's experience was distinctive in its recognition and public attention. It reflects broader institutional problems, such as the limited local economies of many reserves and the difficulties of discrimination in towns and cities, but it does not stand for every First Nations veteran. Many returned to farms, trapping lines, and fishing operations, or to wage work on and off the reserve, with quieter outcomes that left fewer records.

Historians of Canadian veterans' policy emphasize that the issue was not only fairness in benefits. Veterans' experience fed a growing argument that the legal framework of the Indian Act was incompatible with the claim that Indigenous people had fought as citizens. Veterans, band leaders, and Indigenous organizations used the language of service to press for changes to the Act, an argument that would feature in the parliamentary review and the revisions of 1951 discussed in section 103.19.

Section 103.4

Veterans, Indigenous Women, Families, and Community Reintegration

The return of service personnel and war workers was a social event as well as an economic one. Families that had been separated for years had to reconstitute daily life. Spouses who had managed households and farms during the war had developed habits of independent decision-making, and children had grown up with absent fathers. Some veterans returned with disabilities, with the effects of wartime trauma that contemporaries did not yet have a developed vocabulary to describe, or with illnesses acquired overseas. Others came home to find that parents, siblings, or children had died in their absence of tuberculosis, influenza, or other causes. Education that had been interrupted by enlistment was resumed or abandoned, and many young people who had expected to follow a certain path found it closed.

Women's wartime roles shaped the postwar period in distinctive ways. Several hundred American Indian women served in the Army Nurse Corps, the Women's Army Corps, the Navy's women's reserve, and other units, and First Nations women served in Canadian women's services. Others worked in defense plants, shipyards, aircraft factories, laundries, and offices in cities where their presence had seldom been seen before the war. After 1945, many of these women encountered the same narrowing of employment that affected women generally as returning servicemen reclaimed jobs, though Indigenous women also faced discrimination based on ancestry. Some used their experience to enter nursing, teaching, clerical work, and the Bureau of Indian Affairs, where the Indian preference in hiring established in the 1930s created openings. Others returned to home communities and brought skills that were absorbed into local institutions, from community clinics to tribal offices and schools. Their experience complicates any account that treats veterans as exclusively male or that regards wartime change as affecting only the men who served.

Communities responded to returning veterans through ceremony and association. Among the Diné, ceremonies such as the Enemy Way, traditionally intended to restore balance for those who had been in contact with the dead of an enemy, were performed for returning soldiers. Pueblo communities and many Plains nations honored veterans through ceremonies, naming practices, dances, and the reestablishment or expansion of warrior societies. On the southern Plains, veterans' associations and dance traditions expanded in the years following the war, laying part of the foundation for the powwow culture that would grow in subsequent decades. In both countries, veterans joined the American Legion and the Royal Canadian Legion, formed their own posts and associations, and used these as sites for fellowship, mutual aid, and political discussion. The extent to which they were welcomed varied by community and region; some Legion posts included Indigenous members as equals, while others were slow to do so.

Veterans also entered local leadership. Tribal councils, band councils, school boards, cooperatives, and local branches of national organizations drew on men who had acquired experience in military organization, literacy in dealing with government paperwork, and exposure to different regions and ways of life. In the United States, the National Congress of American Indians, founded in 1944, drew members and officers who included veterans, and its early advocacy emphasized the relationship between service and equal rights, a subject developed in Chapter 107. In Canada, veterans were among those who testified, wrote letters, and joined provincial and regional associations pressing for reform. Yet it would be inaccurate to portray all returning veterans as political reformers. Some resumed farming or ranching and avoided public life; some left their communities for good; some embraced the integrationist optimism of the postwar years; others became skeptical critics of federal programs; and still others held to the authority of traditional leaders. Veterans did not form a single political bloc.

The central historical question, then, is how wartime experience altered relationships within Indigenous communities. It changed expectations about wages, schooling, and mobility, gave some returning service members new standing, and introduced new tensions between generations, between those who had left and those who remained, and between supporters and critics of federal programs. In many communities, elders continued to hold primary authority on ceremonial and land matters, while veterans gained influence in dealings with outside institutions. In others, particularly those with elected councils created under the Indian Reorganization Act, veterans entered political life through ordinary elections. The evidence for these shifts is uneven and local, and generalization requires caution.

The first division of the chapter thus shows that military service had practical and political consequences after the war, and that those consequences were shaped by legal status, geography, gender, and community institutions. The chapter's attention now turns from the veterans themselves to the broader conditions of population, health, housing, and household life in which they and their families lived.

Historical Debate

Did Postwar Veterans' Programs Deliver Substantive Equality to Indigenous Service Members?

The question: Veterans' programs in the United States and Canada were formally open to Indigenous veterans. Did they deliver substantive equality in practice?

Interpretation A: Formal eligibility produced real, if uneven, gains. Scholars emphasizing this view point to documented use of education, training, medical, and disability benefits, to the careers of veterans who used them, and to the growth of Indigenous professionals. They stress that many veterans did obtain benefits and that to portray universal exclusion would erase their experience.
Interpretation B: Structural barriers sharply limited access. Other scholars emphasize trust and reserve land that blocked loans, distance from offices and lenders, discrimination, limited schooling, and the intermediary role of Indian Affairs. They argue that benefits designed for a mainstream population did not reach many Indigenous veterans on equal terms.

Evidence: Statutes, agency reports, program participation records (inconsistently categorized by race), parliamentary testimony, tribal histories, and oral interviews.

Points of disagreement: Mostly interpretation and scale, because participation data are incomplete. The positions are not mutually exclusive, and the most defensible accounts distinguish benefits by type, region, and legal status.

Why it matters: The answer affects whether the postwar period is read as a moment of inclusion, of continued exclusion, or of differentiated outcomes that fed political demands for reform.

Section 103.5

Indigenous Population Change and the Problem of Historical Measurement

Historians who want to measure Indigenous population change after 1945 face a problem that is as much institutional as statistical. Population figures are produced by governments, and governments define the categories they count. The United States Census Bureau recorded 333,969 people in its American Indian category in 1940 and 343,410 in 1950. Taken at face value, the change is modest, an increase of less than three percent over a decade. But the figures cannot be read as a simple measure of births and deaths, because the enumeration methods and definitions that produced them changed and were applied unevenly.

In 1950, as in earlier census years, enumerators generally assigned race by observation or local reputation rather than by asking respondents to identify themselves, with special rules for people living on reservations and for those of mixed ancestry. A person's classification depended on who the enumerator was, where the person lived, and how the community regarded them. Indigenous people who had moved to cities, who lived in towns near reservations, or who were of mixed heritage might be counted as white, Black, or something else. Beginning with 1960, the census relied more heavily on self-identification, and the American Indian count rose to 523,591. That increase far exceeded what births and deaths could explain and reflected changes in classification, in willingness to identify, and in enumeration, as well as demographic growth. A historian who compared the 1950 figure with a modern self-identification statistic would draw wholly misleading conclusions about the mid-century population.

This does not mean that the underlying demographic processes were negligible. Evidence from the Bureau of Indian Affairs, health records, and local studies indicates that Indigenous birth rates were high in the postwar years and that death rates, especially infant and tuberculosis mortality, began to decline as antibiotics, improved sanitation, and expanded medical services reached more communities. The natural increase of many Indigenous populations was therefore positive and, over time, would become pronounced. But the strongest acceleration in census-counted American Indian population growth lay after the immediate 1940 to 1950 period, and the chapter should not exaggerate the speed of postwar recovery. Life expectancy and infant survival remained markedly worse than for the general population throughout the period, and the improvements that did occur were uneven among regions and communities.

Migration complicated the picture further. Wartime and postwar movement to cities and industrial centers meant that a growing number of Indigenous people lived away from the reservations and reserves where agencies kept their records. In the United States, those who left and were not enumerated as Indian might disappear from official counts of Indian population even though they remained members of their nations. In Canada, the situation was different in structure but similarly difficult to measure. Federal records of people legally recognized under the Indian Act must be distinguished from the national census. Statistics Canada’s historical yearbook tabulated 125,521 people in 1941 and 165,607 in 1951 under the period category “Native Indian and Eskimo,” an ethnic-origin classification that included Inuit; these totals were not counts of registered status Indians. The centralized Indian Register was introduced under the revised Indian Act in 1951, and registration remained governed by rules concerning descent, status, marriage, and enfranchisement. Changes in census enumeration, community population, and legal registration should therefore be analyzed separately. Métis people were recorded inconsistently under externally imposed categories, and northern enumeration of Inuit populations was uneven.

Mexico’s censuses used a different criterion again. The 1940 and 1950 counts included speakers aged five and older of Indigenous languages, recording both those who spoke only an Indigenous language and those who also spoke Spanish. Approximately 2,490,909 such speakers were counted in 1940 and 2,447,609 in 1950—about 14.8 and 11.2 percent, respectively, of the population aged five and older. In 1940, roughly 1.24 million were recorded as monolingual and 1.25 million as bilingual; by 1950 the monolingual figure had fallen to about 795,000 while the bilingual figure stood near 1.65 million. A bilingual speaker was therefore not excluded merely for speaking Spanish. These totals still cannot be equated with Indigenous identity: people who no longer spoke a counted Indigenous language, children below the census age threshold, and communities whose speech varieties were categorized inconsistently could be omitted from the linguistic total. Changes in the denominator, census practice, migration, schooling, and language transmission all affected the apparent percentages.

The lesson is that demographic history is inseparable from the institutions that record populations. Each of the three states in this section produced different kinds of statistical categories: racial classification and reservation-related enumeration in the United States, ethnic-origin census categories distinct from legal Indian registration in Canada, and Indigenous-language use (including bilingual speakers) in Mexico. None corresponded to Indigenous nations' own conceptions of citizenship, kinship, and belonging, which were usually determined by community recognition. Students should therefore treat numbers as evidence about both Indigenous populations and the governments that counted them.

Section 103.6

Public Health, Medical Infrastructure, and Unequal Access to Care

Health conditions in postwar Indigenous communities were among the clearest indicators of the distance between formal government responsibility and actual services. Tuberculosis remained the most serious disease in many communities. Trachoma, a bacterial eye infection associated with crowded living and limited water, affected communities in the Southwest and elsewhere. Gastrointestinal illness, pneumonia, measles, and other infectious diseases contributed to high rates of infant mortality, and maternal deaths remained a concern where women gave birth at home or far from hospitals. Poor nutrition, limited access to clean water, inadequate sewage disposal, and overcrowded housing created conditions in which disease spread easily, and each of these conditions was linked to poverty and to the economic marginalization of Indigenous communities.

In the United States, medical services for American Indians were provided principally through the Bureau of Indian Affairs, which operated hospitals, clinics, and field nurses, with some contract arrangements with private and public providers. Funding was limited, facilities were frequently old, and staff recruitment was difficult. Wartime had drawn physicians and nurses into the military, leaving shortages that persisted into the postwar years. The responsibility for Indian health remained within the Bureau until the 1954 transfer legislation moved it to the Public Health Service as the Indian Health Service, effective in 1955. That development lies beyond the chapter's principal period and is foreshadowed here only to emphasize that the services of 1945 to 1953 were delivered by a different and more constrained system.

Canada's arrangements differed. In 1945, responsibility for the health care of status Indians and Inuit was transferred from the Indian Affairs Branch to the Department of National Health and Welfare, which established Indian Health Services. The department operated Indian hospitals, nursing stations, and tuberculosis sanatoria, many in or near reserves and some in distant cities. Treatment for tuberculosis often required long stays away from home, which separated patients from families and communities and left lasting memories of fear and loss in Indigenous families. For Inuit, medical evacuation for tuberculosis to southern hospitals and the Eastern Arctic patrol with its X-ray equipment became important parts of northern health policy, a history treated more fully in Chapter 100.

In Alaska, the Alaska Native Service, a branch of the Bureau of Indian Affairs, ran hospitals, sanatoria, and health programs for Alaska Native communities, supplemented by territorial and military facilities. Tuberculosis was an acute problem, and the geography of the territory, with its villages accessible only by boat, small aircraft, or seasonal trails, made case-finding and treatment difficult. Health aides, public health nurses, and sometimes teachers played crucial roles in the absence of physicians. In Mexico, the Secretaría de Salubridad y Asistencia, created in 1943, extended clinics and campaigns against infectious diseases into rural areas, and the Instituto Nacional Indigenista, established in 1948, later made health services a component of its regional coordinating centers. Indigenous communities in remote highlands and forests, however, continued to depend heavily on local healers, midwives, and family care, and public medicine was often seen by communities as unfamiliar, distant, or coercive.

New medical technology began to change outcomes. Streptomycin became available in the late 1940s and isoniazid in the early 1950s, offering the first effective chemotherapy against tuberculosis. Sulfa drugs and penicillin helped control other infections. Vaccination and sanitation campaigns expanded. But these tools required infrastructure to deliver: hospitals, trained staff, laboratories, transport, and follow-up care. Where those were lacking, as in many isolated communities, the technological advances had slower and more uneven effects. The historical relationship between treatment and distance is especially important. A hospital located a day's journey away, on a road that might be impassable in winter or spring, was not meaningfully accessible to a family with a sick child.

Nurses, community health workers, and Indigenous staff were central to the delivery of what services existed. Field nurses visited homes and schools; Indigenous women served as interpreters, aides, and informal health educators; and community members organized transport and care. Their work often went unrecorded in official histories, but it appears in agency reports, memoirs, and oral histories. It also raised questions about who controlled health care. Many communities welcomed medical services but resisted aspects of how they were provided, including long separations for tuberculosis treatment, school-based inspections, and the dismissal of traditional medicine. Communities frequently combined treatments rather than choosing between them, and the distinction between acceptance of services and acceptance of external authority again proves essential.

The analysis of health in this period thus links poverty, housing, sanitation, and institutional capacity. Disease was not simply a natural consequence of Indigenous status; it was produced by identifiable conditions, including the limited investment of governments in infrastructure on reservations and reserves, and it could be reduced by identifiable measures, as the declines in mortality began to show.

Section 103.7

Housing, Household Economies, and the Postwar Indigenous Family

Housing was the most visible expression of the economic conditions of many Indigenous families. On many reservations, families lived in one- or two-room log, frame, or adobe houses, in hogans, in tents or temporary shelters, or in dwellings built from scavenged materials. Overcrowding was common, with multiple generations sharing small spaces, and few homes had running water, indoor plumbing, or electricity. Rural electrification programs in the United States had reached much of rural America during the 1930s and 1940s but frequently stopped at reservation boundaries, in part because of the cost of extending lines across sparsely populated areas and the uncertain legal status of rights-of-way. Roads, telephones, and public transportation were similarly limited. In Canada, reserve housing was largely the responsibility of families themselves, with limited federal assistance, and many reserve houses were small, poorly insulated, and lacking services.

The household economy that these dwellings supported was almost always diversified. Cash income from wage work, seasonal labor, military allotments, disability payments, craft sales, and public assistance was combined with subsistence activities: gardening, herding, hunting, fishing, gathering, and trapping, according to region. The arrival of wage income did not simply replace older economic practices. Many families used cash to buy supplies that supported subsistence, such as seed, tools, ammunition, fishing gear, and feed. Extended-family networks pooled resources and distributed food, labor, and care, and ceremonial obligations required families to share goods and time in ways that cash economies did not always recognize. The result was a mixed economy in which the dividing lines between employment, subsistence, and kinship support were permeable.

Public assistance and social programs entered this mixed economy unevenly. In Canada, the Family Allowances Act of 1944, which began payments in 1945, was extended to status Indians and Inuit, with administration through Indian Affairs and, in some cases, delivery in kind in the Arctic rather than in cash. Old Age Security, introduced in 1951, similarly extended to Indigenous elders. Family allowance payments in Indian communities were sometimes tied administratively to school attendance, an arrangement that gave officials new leverage over parents' decisions. In the United States, general assistance and categorical aid programs such as old-age assistance depended on state administration, and some states refused to extend them to reservation residents until federal pressure and litigation changed practices in the late 1940s. Where aid was available, it provided real support for families, and where it was not, hardship was greater.

The postwar household cannot be understood without women's work and decision-making. Women were responsible for much food production and preparation, textile and craft production, childcare, and care of the sick, and in many nations held significant authority within kinship and property systems. Among the Diné, women owned livestock and managed herds; among Hopi and other Pueblo peoples, women's lineages held houses and fields; among Haudenosaunee nations, clan mothers had a defined role in selecting leaders. Wartime had expanded women's responsibilities as men were absent, and postwar conditions forced many to balance these roles with wage work, new schooling demands, and interactions with government agencies. In Canada, women's authority was constrained by Indian Act provisions on status and band governance, a subject taken up in section 103.19.

Children and elders connected the household to the community's future and past. Children's schooling increasingly separated them from households for long periods, whether in boarding schools, day schools, or distant public schools, and parents weighed the benefits of education against the loss of children's presence and language. Elders remained the principal transmitters of language, history, ceremony, and ecological knowledge in most communities. Their roles were not diminished by wage work or schooling in the way that policy-makers often assumed, although the circumstances of transmission changed as young people spent less time in community settings.

Comparison between locations shows variation. Households in smaller towns and industrial centers had better access to electricity, running water, and wages, but often faced discrimination in housing and employment, higher living costs, and isolation from kin. Reservation and reserve households had lower cash incomes and fewer services, but stronger kinship support, land access, and community institutions. Many families maintained ties between the two, with members moving back and forth, sending money home, and returning for ceremonies and seasonal work. This circulation, rather than a one-way movement from community to city, was characteristic of the postwar years.

Section 103.8

Postwar Life in Alaska and the Northern Indigenous Homelands

Alaska's situation at the end of the war was shaped by its territorial status and by the massive military buildup of the early 1940s. The construction of airfields, bases, the Alaska Highway, and other infrastructure had brought tens of thousands of military and civilian workers to the territory and increased wage employment, including for some Alaska Native workers. The Alaska Territorial Guard, organized in 1942, enlisted many Alaska Native men, particularly Iñupiat and Yup'ik residents of the western and northern coasts, to watch for invasion, and it was disbanded in 1947. After the war, the military presence continued and then grew with the Cold War, reinforcing Alaska's strategic importance and generating continuing demand for labor, transportation, and services.

Alaska Native communities had their own political institutions. The Alaska Native Brotherhood, founded in 1912, and the Alaska Native Sisterhood, founded in 1923, were organizations of Tlingit, Haida, and Tsimshian people in southeastern Alaska that had campaigned for citizenship, voting rights, schooling, and an end to segregation. Their work culminated in the Anti-Discrimination Act of 1945, signed by Governor Ernest Gruening in February of that year. The act prohibited discrimination in public accommodations in the territory, and it is often cited as one of the earliest such laws in the United States. Elizabeth Peratrovich, a Tlingit leader and Grand President of the Alaska Native Sisterhood, and her husband Roy Peratrovich, who held office in the Brotherhood, were prominent in the campaign, which also drew on the experience of Alaska Natives in the war. The act did not end discrimination, but it provided legal grounds for challenging it and showed the effectiveness of organized Indigenous political action.

The Alaska Native Service, a branch of the Bureau of Indian Affairs, ran schools and health programs for Alaska Native villages, while the territorial government operated schools for non-Native children and, increasingly, took part in the education of Native children. This dual system produced inconsistent standards and fostered debate about consolidation. In 1947, the Bureau opened Mount Edgecumbe boarding school in Sitka, using a former naval air station, to provide secondary education for Alaska Native students from across the territory. The school expanded educational opportunity at the cost of sending adolescents far from their communities, a trade-off that families and officials evaluated differently. Elementary schooling in villages remained limited, with many communities served by one-teacher schools and some by none.

Economic opportunities were diverse. Alaska Native households combined subsistence hunting, fishing, and gathering with wage labor in canneries, mining, construction, and military-related work. The salmon fishery, in particular, was central in the southeast and southwest, and Alaska Natives opposed the fish traps operated by large canning interests, which they argued depleted the resource on which villages depended. Territorial status limited Alaska Natives' political leverage, since residents had no voting representation in Congress and the territory's resources were controlled by distant corporate and federal interests. The unresolved question of Native land title, which lay behind these conflicts, would become decisive later, but it was already a concern for Native organizations and communities in the 1940s.

Public health presented the same challenges as elsewhere, intensified by geography. Tuberculosis was widespread, and sanatoria in Alaska and the Pacific Northwest provided treatment, with patients often separated from their families for long periods. Transport by air became increasingly important for medical evacuation, and wartime airfields and aircraft helped link remote communities to hospitals. Teachers, nurses, and local aides again carried significant burdens, and Native Service staff and community members improvised in conditions of scarcity.

A brief comparison with the Canadian and Greenlandic Arctic shows both parallels and differences. Inuit in northern Canada experienced expanding federal activity in the late 1940s, including the extension of family allowances, the growth of health and education programs, and a rising federal presence, but their administration remained distinct from that of Alaska Natives, and the specific histories of settlement and relocation belong to Chapter 100. In Greenland, Denmark reconsidered its colonial administration after the war. A commission appointed in 1950 produced a modernization program, often called G-50, and in 1953 a Danish constitutional revision made Greenland an integral part of the Kingdom of Denmark, steps that expanded services and schooling while leaving questions of Greenlandic self-government to later decades.

The northern homelands thus illustrate that postwar change was uneven and shaped by different state structures. Wartime military expansion created new economic and transport opportunities, Indigenous political organizations used these changes to press for equal treatment, and communities continued to rely on subsistence economies and local institutions. The common thread was that expanded services arrived through governments that controlled their terms, a theme taken further in the chapter's treatment of economic development.

Section 103.9

Reservation and Reserve Economies in the Postwar Years

After 1945, reservation and reserve economies rested on a limited set of resources whose character differed greatly from region to region. Agriculture and ranching were important wherever land and water allowed, but on many Plains reservations the allotment of land in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries had been followed by leasing of Indian lands to non-Indian farmers and ranchers, so that rental income rather than direct operation provided much local cash. Timber supported communities such as the Menominee in Wisconsin, whose tribal sawmill and forest management were widely noted. Fishing was central in the Pacific Northwest and in coastal British Columbia, where Native fishers, cannery workers, and boat owners were deeply involved in the commercial salmon economy. Trapping and fur trading continued across the Canadian Subarctic and parts of Alaska, though fur prices fluctuated with markets. Mining, small businesses, crafts, and wage labor filled the remaining space.

The preceding years left a legacy. The Depression had reduced prices and employment, and federal programs of the New Deal had provided relief jobs, including the Indian Division of the Civilian Conservation Corps, as well as credit through the revolving loan fund authorized by the Indian Reorganization Act. The war then created labor shortages and higher demand for agricultural products, raising incomes for some farmers and ranchers and giving wage work to those who left. After 1945, wartime demand declined, prices fluctuated, and a significant share of workers who had gone into defense industries returned. Transportation difficulties, distance from markets, and lack of capital limited what reservation producers could do. Credit remained scarce. In the United States, the trust status of land prevented its use as collateral; in Canada, the Indian Act's provisions on the property of Indians on reserves, which protected it from seizure, had the corollary effect of making lenders reluctant to extend credit. Protection and restriction were two sides of the same legal structure.

Tribal and community economic initiatives were a notable feature of the period. Cooperatives and enterprises combined commercial goals with collective responsibilities. The Navajo Arts and Crafts Guild, founded in 1941, marketed Diné weaving, silverwork, and other crafts. Pueblo and Zuni artisans developed marketing networks for pottery and jewelry. Tribal cattle associations and herds on several Plains reservations aimed to keep income within communities. Native fishing organizations in British Columbia, such as the Native Brotherhood of British Columbia, represented Indigenous fishers in dealings with canners and the government. These ventures varied in size and success, and they operated under restrictions: the Bureau of Indian Affairs or Indian Affairs Branch could approve or veto decisions, and limited capital constrained expansion. They nonetheless show that communities sought economic means consistent with their own priorities.

Regional comparison guards against the assumption that reservations and reserves shared a single economic condition. The Diné Reservation, vast and arid, depended on a combination of livestock, wage work, crafts, and, increasingly, mineral royalties and federal programs, with extremely limited infrastructure. Plains reservations such as Pine Ridge and Rosebud relied heavily on leasing, small-scale ranching, and federal employment, and suffered from distance and drought. Great Lakes communities used timber, wild rice, fishing, tourism, and seasonal work. In the Pacific Northwest, treaty-protected fishing rights and the commercial salmon industry shaped livelihoods, although tensions over access to fisheries were growing. Canadian First Nations in the Prairie provinces confronted similar issues of small land bases and limited capital, while those in the Subarctic depended on trapping, fishing, and seasonal wage labor, and British Columbia First Nations were more closely tied to the commercial resource economy. The degree of dependence on federal programs, wage labor, and subsistence differed accordingly.

The common feature was the limited control that communities had over their resources. Officials held the power to lease land, approve contracts, manage funds, and supervise development, and decisions about the use of timber, minerals, grazing land, and water were often made in Washington or Ottawa. Where tribal governments or band councils had authority, it was usually subject to approval. Many Indigenous leaders therefore argued for economic development that would increase community control rather than merely raise incomes, a distinction that would become sharper in the debates examined in section 103.12 and in the Historical Debate on development.

Gender and age also shaped economic life. Women's craft production was important to many households, often as the largest steady source of cash, and it connected communities to national markets while reinforcing artistic and cultural traditions. Young men were more likely to seek wage employment outside, while elders held the knowledge and often the land rights that sustained subsistence. The mixed economy that resulted, drawing on wages, rents, credit, crafts, and subsistence, was resilient, but it left communities vulnerable to market swings and policy changes.

Section 103.10

Federal Development Programs and the Navajo–Hopi Rehabilitation Act of 1950

The Navajo–Hopi Long-Range Rehabilitation Act of 1950 offers a detailed case of postwar development policy. It combined a large federal appropriation with a comprehensive plan for two nations whose reservations in northeastern Arizona, southeastern Utah, and northwestern New Mexico were among the poorest in the United States. Its background lay in several developments. Livestock reduction in the 1930s had undermined the economic base of many Diné families and left deep resentment. Wartime service and employment had given thousands of Diné and Hopi people experience of wages and distant places. Returning veterans, together with tribal leaders, pressed for schools, roads, jobs, and hospitals. Then came the severe winter of 1946 to 1947 and the extreme winter of 1948 to 1949, when blizzards isolated families and the military and civilian agencies mounted airlifts of supplies to stranded communities, an effort commonly called Operation Snowbound. National press coverage of the emergency made the conditions on the reservations visible to Americans who had known little about them.

Congress considered rehabilitation legislation across several sessions, and the hearings and debates revealed competing aims. Supporters in Congress and the Department of the Interior argued that a ten-year program of investment would raise living standards and reduce the long-term cost of federal responsibility. Others saw an opportunity to encourage Diné and Hopi people to leave the reservation for employment elsewhere, and still others to reduce federal obligations. Navajo Tribal Council leaders, including Chairman Sam Ahkeah, and Hopi leaders testified and negotiated, contending over the extent of tribal authority, the content of the program, and provisions that affected their governments. President Harry S. Truman signed the act on April 19, 1950. It authorized approximately $88.57 million over the long-range program, an amount that indicates the ambitions of postwar federal development planning.

The act authorized investment in education, roads, irrigation, soil and water conservation, health and sanitation facilities, housing, industrial development, and other infrastructure, to be administered by the Secretary of the Interior. It also provided for the resettlement of Navajo families on irrigable lands along the Colorado River, a measure that tested the idea that population could be redistributed to match resources, and for employment-oriented programs aimed at helping workers find jobs off the reservation. Over the following decade, the program contributed to school construction, road building, water development, and other projects that expanded the reservations' physical infrastructure. Navajo and Hopi workers were employed in construction, and tribal governments gained experience in managing programs. In that sense, the act delivered the kind of investment that communities had long said they needed.

Alongside these benefits, the act reflected a structure of federal control. The program's priorities were set largely in federal planning, and the Interior Department held the administrative authority. Tribal leaders sought a greater role in decisions about spending and project selection, and disputes over control accompanied the program's implementation. The act also unfolded in a context of long-standing tension between the Diné and Hopi over land, since the 1882 executive order reservation for the Hopi and surrounding areas had left a shared area whose use was contested, a dispute that would produce litigation and legislation in later decades. The development program intensified such tensions by raising the stakes of land use without resolving them.

The postwar years also saw the expansion of uranium mining on and near Navajo lands. Uranium and vanadium mining in the Colorado Plateau had begun earlier, and the creation of the Atomic Energy Commission in 1947 and its program of ore purchases from 1948 stimulated prospecting and mining. Diné men found employment in mines and at mills, an important source of wages in a region with few jobs. At the time, the dangers of radon exposure were not communicated effectively to workers, and ventilation in many mines was poor. The health consequences, including elevated rates of lung cancer among miners, were documented in later decades and became the subject of federal compensation legislation and long-term environmental concern. This section introduces the subject only to emphasize a pattern: jobs and income offered immediate gains that were real to the families who received them, and the costs emerged over time and fell heavily on the same communities.

For historians, the significance of the Navajo–Hopi Rehabilitation Act lies in what it reveals about postwar federal development. It treated Indigenous poverty as a problem requiring large-scale public investment, which was a notable shift from earlier retrenchment. It also treated development as a program to be designed and administered by the federal government, with Indigenous governments as consultants and partners of uncertain authority. Whether this pairing of investment and control was a path toward autonomy or a form of paternalism is the subject of the Historical Debate later in this division.

Section 103.11

Postwar Employment, Labor Markets, and Indigenous Geographic Mobility

Between 1945 and the early 1950s, Indigenous workers moved in significant numbers between reservations, reserves, rural towns, and industrial centers. This mobility had deep roots. Seasonal agricultural work, railroad labor, mining, logging, and fishing had long taken Indigenous workers away from home communities for part of the year, and wartime migration to defense industries enlarged those patterns. After 1945 the movement continued for a mix of reasons: veterans with training sought jobs unavailable at home; families wanted higher incomes; young people sought education or adventure; and for some, economic necessity left no alternative. Indigenous people also moved in response to the loss or decline of local resources, as when drought, livestock reduction, or closed mines eliminated work.

Employment opportunities took many forms. Railroads, such as the Santa Fe, employed Diné, Pueblo, and other Indigenous workers on track gangs, and Laguna Pueblo families had maintained a community of railroad workers in Richmond, California, for decades. Defense plants, shipyards, and military installations continued to provide jobs in the postwar period, though many wartime positions disappeared. Agricultural labor drew workers to farms in the Southwest and Pacific Northwest. Cities such as Los Angeles, Denver, Chicago, Minneapolis, and Seattle offered manufacturing, service, and construction work. In Canada, First Nations workers took jobs in logging, mining, farming, fishing, and, increasingly, in towns and cities, though the Indian Act's restrictions on movement, which had included the informal pass system on the Prairies in earlier decades, were weakening.

Movement carried costs. Indigenous workers faced discrimination in hiring and wages, often being the last hired and first dismissed. Housing discrimination limited choices, and rents consumed a large share of income. Migrants encountered unfamiliar institutions, lacked kin support, and sometimes were isolated by language and cultural differences. Maintaining relationships with home communities required money and time that were not always available, and the loss of workers weakened some communities' capacity for ceremonial and political life. Yet many migrants maintained strong ties, returning for ceremonies, sending money to family, and keeping membership in their nations. Mobility did not necessarily mean departure.

New associations emerged among Indigenous people in cities and work sites. Intertribal clubs, social organizations, churches, athletic teams, and veterans' groups brought together people from different nations. These associations offered sociability, mutual aid, and sometimes political voice. They were precursors of the more extensive urban institutions that would develop later, a history reserved for Chapter 105. At this stage the associations were typically small and informal, and their significance lies in showing that Indigenous people created community in new settings and that their identities were not dissolved by movement.

It is important to distinguish this voluntary and economically pressured migration from the later organized federal relocation program. In the late 1940s, the Bureau of Indian Affairs began to experiment with placing workers in jobs outside reservations, particularly for Diné and others from the Southwest, and its employment assistance efforts expanded in the early 1950s. But the formal and large-scale relocation program, with its funding for transportation, training, and settlement in designated cities, was a policy of the 1950s and belongs to Chapter 105. The circumstances described here, including the economic pressures of reservation life, the spread of information through kin networks, the effect of veterans' training, and the pull of urban wages, created the conditions in which that program could be proposed. They did not make it inevitable, and many Indigenous people who moved in these years did so independently of any federal plan.

The Canadian case developed along parallel lines but within a different legal setting. Status Indians who left reserves for employment kept their status unless they were enfranchised, and the Indian Act gave officials power to enfranchise individuals who met certain conditions or applied. Leaving the reserve did not itself end status, but length of residence abroad and other conditions could affect it, and some who moved lost contact with band lists. The Prairie provinces also saw the growth of Métis and First Nations populations in small towns and cities, where discrimination in housing and employment was common.

The significance of mobility is therefore double. It expanded the range of economic choices and experiences available to Indigenous people, and it created new sites of Indigenous community, but it also exposed workers to market insecurity and discrimination, and it gave policy-makers a reason to argue that reservations and reserves could be reduced in importance. Whether mobility was a path to opportunity or a symptom of economic marginalization depended on the individual, the community, and the policy framework, and both interpretations had advocates.

Section 103.12

Indigenous Mexico and the Postwar Politics of Economic Integration

Mexico belongs in this continental history because its postwar Indigenous policy addressed the largest Indigenous populations in North America north of Guatemala and because its policy models circulated across the Americas. Under President Miguel Alemán, who held office from 1946 to 1952, the government pursued industrialization, infrastructure construction, and agricultural modernization, with large public works including dams, roads, and irrigation. The national development agenda was frequently expressed in terms of integration of the entire population into a modern national economy and culture. For Indigenous communities, this agenda meant both possible access to services and pressure to conform to national patterns.

The intellectual framework for official policy was indigenismo, a set of ideas and practices developed in the aftermath of the Mexican Revolution that treated Indigenous peoples as a distinctive part of the national population needing special attention. Earlier institutions included the Departamento de Asuntos Indígenas created under President Lázaro Cárdenas, and the First Inter-American Indigenist Congress, held at Pátzcuaro, Michoacán, in 1940, which led to the founding of the Inter-American Indigenist Institute. Anthropologists such as Manuel Gamio, Alfonso Caso, and Gonzalo Aguirre Beltrán contributed to the design of programs. Indigenismo was never a single doctrine. Some of its proponents emphasized respect for Indigenous cultures and community institutions; others emphasized assimilation into mestizo national culture; and policy often combined the two.

In December 1948, the Mexican government established the Instituto Nacional Indigenista, or INI, by law. Alfonso Caso, an archaeologist and official who had been involved in Indigenous policy for years, became its first director and led it into the 1970s. The institute's mission was to study Indigenous problems, advise the government, and promote and carry out programs to improve Indigenous communities' conditions, with attention to education, health, communications, agriculture, and economic development. Its design drew on the idea that Indigenous regions were linked to surrounding Mestizo towns in relationships of dependency and exploitation, and that integrated regional development could change these structures.

The first Centro Coordinador Indigenista, the institute's regional model, was established in 1951 in San Cristóbal de las Casas, Chiapas, to serve the Tzeltal and Tzotzil Maya communities of the highlands. Its founding director was the anthropologist Gonzalo Aguirre Beltrán, who directed the center in 1951–1952; Alfonso Villa Rojas contributed earlier ethnographic research in the region but should not be identified as the center’s founding director. The center coordinated schools, clinics, road construction, agricultural extension, and other work, and it trained bilingual promoters, young Indigenous men and some women, who taught and acted as intermediaries between communities and the institute. Bilingual promoters were an innovation intended to use Indigenous languages to introduce Spanish literacy, and they became influential figures in many communities. The center also faced local resistance and conflict, since it operated in a region where landholders and traders held considerable power and where Indigenous communities had their own governance and religious institutions.

The central historical problem is the difference between expanded public services and government-sponsored integration. Many Indigenous communities wanted schools, clinics, roads, and secure land, and some actively sought the institute's help. At the same time, the institute's programs aimed to transform Indigenous economic and cultural life in ways that communities might not have chosen. Land tenure raised some of the sharpest issues. The revolutionary agrarian reform had distributed lands through ejidos and recognized communal property, but disputes over boundaries, titles, and access persisted, and development projects could threaten community land. The construction of the Papaloapan Commission's projects, begun in the late 1940s, and the later Miguel Alemán Dam in Oaxaca, which displaced Mazatec communities in the 1950s, illustrate how national development could impose heavy costs on Indigenous peoples. Many such cases went beyond the 1945 to 1953 period, but the planning that produced them began in these years.

Indigenous communities responded in varied ways. Some collaborated with INI programs, using them to obtain schools and clinics while retaining local institutions. Others resisted or were suspicious, particularly where the programs threatened land or religious practice. Linguistic diversity, with dozens of Indigenous languages in Mexico, meant that no single approach served all communities, and local political organization, including the civil-religious hierarchies of many communities, shaped how institutions were received. The relationship between Indigenous communities and indigenismo was therefore negotiated, not simply imposed.

Students should resist equating Mexican indigenismo with U.S. termination or Canadian Indian Act administration. The institutions had different legal foundations, aims, and histories. Mexico did not maintain reservations in the U.S. sense, treated Indigenous peoples as citizens within a national project, and relied on cultural and developmental rather than treaty-based concepts. Yet there are parallels in the tension between public services and external control, which makes comparison productive.

Historical Vocabulary
Indigenismo

Definition. Indigenismo is a set of ideas, policies, and institutions in Latin America through which non-Indigenous intellectuals and states have defined and addressed what they called the Indigenous problem.

Historical context. In Mexico it grew after the Revolution of 1910, through anthropologists and officials, and it took institutional form in bodies such as the Departamento de Asuntos Indígenas, the Pátzcuaro Congress of 1940, and the Instituto Nacional Indigenista of 1948.

Potential misunderstanding. Indigenismo is not the same as Indigenous movements. It was developed largely by non-Indigenous experts, though Indigenous people participated, and it should be distinguished from later Indigenous organizing for autonomy.

Division IV

Education, Youth, Language, and Institutional Change

Sections 103.13–103.16. Education became a major arena of change, with growing Indigenous aspirations operating alongside institutions that restricted Indigenous control over curriculum, language, and schooling.

Section 103.13

Indigenous Veterans and the Expansion of Educational Opportunity

For many Indigenous veterans, education was the most important benefit of service. The GI Bill and, in Canada, the education provisions of the Veterans Charter gave eligible veterans the means to attend colleges, universities, trade schools, technical institutes, and professional programs that had previously been inaccessible to most Indigenous people. Veterans used these benefits to study agriculture, engineering, teaching, nursing, law, business, and the trades, and some pursued graduate study. Among the Native veterans who moved into public life through advanced study was Ben Reifel, a Brulé Lakota who served in the army, earned a master's degree at Harvard, and later held senior positions in the Bureau of Indian Affairs and served in Congress. His career was exceptional, but it illustrates the possibility that education opened.

Veterans' training was not limited to colleges. Vocational schools and on-the-job and on-farm training programs allowed veterans to learn mechanics, welding, carpentry, electrical work, and agricultural methods while receiving subsistence allowances. Because many Indigenous veterans lacked the high school credentials required for college admission, these programs were often more accessible. Adult education classes, correspondence courses, and programs sponsored by tribes and agencies also served veterans seeking to complete their schooling. The effect was to increase the skilled workforce within Indigenous communities and among Indigenous workers in cities.

Barriers were substantial. Many veterans had attended poorly funded schools, some only to the sixth or eighth grade, and could not meet admission requirements or compete with other veterans in classrooms. English-language proficiency was an issue for some, particularly in communities where Indigenous languages predominated. Colleges and universities were often distant, and costs of living, transportation, and housing could exceed the subsistence allowance, especially for veterans with families. Discrimination in college towns and housing markets affected some students. Information about benefits was uneven, and some veterans did not know about the programs or the procedures for enrolling. In Canada, the Indian Act's provisions on enfranchisement and the control of Indian Affairs officials over funds meant that some veterans' education involved additional administrative steps.

Despite these obstacles, veterans entered education in larger numbers than any previous cohort of Indigenous adults. Some became teachers and school administrators, returning to serve in federal, mission, and public schools. Some entered government service, working for the Bureau of Indian Affairs, the Indian Affairs Branch, state and provincial agencies, or tribal governments. Others became nurses, engineers, lawyers, ministers, and business owners, and some became community leaders. In the United States, Indian preference in Bureau hiring, established in the 1930s, gave Native people advantages in federal employment, though in practice these opportunities were often confined to lower-level positions.

The significance of educational attainment was twofold. For individuals, education could mean higher income, greater security, and wider choices. For communities, it could expand administrative and political capacity. Tribal governments and band councils needed people who could read and draft legal documents, manage budgets, negotiate with agencies, and understand law. Educated veterans filled these roles, and the growing number of Indigenous professionals contributed to a developing pool of leaders who understood both community and state institutions. This capacity would be important in the political contests over termination and, later, over self-determination.

Education also generated tensions. Some communities worried that young people who left for school would not return, or would return with attitudes that challenged elders' authority. Some graduates felt caught between worlds, valued by outsiders as examples of assimilation and by their own communities as resources and sometimes as potential critics. These tensions did not mean that education was unwelcome. Most Indigenous families and leaders sought education as a tool, and many argued that it should be available on terms that respected community identity and did not require abandoning language, land, or political institutions.

It remains difficult to measure the overall scale of veterans' education benefits for Indigenous people because records categorize recipients by race inconsistently, and many Indigenous veterans were counted under general headings. Historians therefore rely on a combination of agency reports, school records, tribal histories, oral interviews, and biographical evidence. The pattern that emerges is of significant but unevenly distributed gains, concentrated among those with more preparation and access to institutions, and of a lasting influence on Indigenous leadership in the following decades.

Section 103.14

Federal Indian Schools, Boarding Schools, and the Expansion of Public Education

The education of Indigenous children in the immediate postwar years took place in a patchwork of institutions. Federal boarding schools, operated by the Bureau of Indian Affairs, enrolled thousands of children, often far from their homes. Federal day schools served some reservation communities. Mission schools, operated by Catholic and Protestant churches, continued in many regions, some with federal support. Public schools, increasingly, enrolled Indigenous children under arrangements funded in part through the Johnson-O'Malley Act of 1934, which allowed the federal government to contract with states for the education of Indian children. In some regions, no school was available at all. The system's diversity means that no single description fits all, and historians rely on specific school settings to illustrate the range.

The Navajo Reservation provides a stark example of the shortage of schooling. At the end of the war, a large proportion of Navajo children, many thousands, were not enrolled in any school, because of the shortage of classroom space and the dispersed settlement pattern of the reservation. The federal government launched a Special Navajo Program in 1946, intended to provide older children and adolescents who had never attended school with an intensive education of several years, often including instruction in English and vocational skills, at boarding schools on and off the reservation, such as Sherman Institute in California and Chilocco in Oklahoma. In 1950, the Bureau opened Intermountain School in Brigham City, Utah, on the site of a former military hospital, to house and teach Navajo students. The Navajo–Hopi Rehabilitation Act included funds for school construction, and the Navajo Tribal Council urged expansion of education. These facts illustrate both the scale of unmet demand and the federal responsibility for it.

In Alaska, the opening of Mount Edgecumbe in 1947 provided a boarding secondary school. In the Great Plains, Pacific Northwest, and elsewhere, parents and tribal leaders pressed for better facilities. Teacher shortages, a legacy of wartime and low salaries, affected rural and reservation schools, as did inadequate buildings, scarce supplies, and limited transportation. School buses, where available, allowed day attendance in some communities, reducing the need for boarding. The expansion of public school attendance, supported by Johnson-O'Malley contracts, brought some Indigenous children into schools with non-Indigenous students, which could offer better facilities but also exposed them to discrimination and curricula that ignored their communities.

The tension between the demand for education and the policies of schools was at the center of the period. Parents and leaders wanted schools that would equip children for a changing economy, and the Navajo Tribal Council, for example, repeatedly asked for more schools. But the federal government's assumptions remained assimilationist. Boarding schools separated children from families for months or years. Many schools, though practices varied, discouraged or forbade the use of Indigenous languages, emphasized English and vocational training, and imposed discipline that included regimentation and, in some cases, corporal punishment. Curricula seldom incorporated Indigenous history or knowledge. Efforts to reform federal Indian education under John Collier in the 1930s, including support for community day schools and bilingual materials, had lost momentum, and wartime budget limits and staff turnover reduced their reach.

Historians therefore distinguish parents' efforts to obtain better schools from approval of every practice used by school administrators. Parents might send children to boarding school because there was no alternative, because they wanted them educated, or because economic hardship made it difficult to support them at home, while still disliking the loss of language and the harshness of some schools. They could also exercise agency by choosing among schools, requesting transfers, visiting, withdrawing children, or organizing to press for improvements. Sources, including school records, agency correspondence, oral histories, and student writings, show a wide range of experience, from appreciation of educational opportunity to deep distress at separation and mistreatment.

The federal government's educational responsibility was grounded in treaties, statutes, and the trust relationship, but it was exercised through a bureaucracy with limited resources and a mandate rooted in the goal of assimilation. During the late 1940s and early 1950s, officials increasingly encouraged public school enrollment as a means of integration and as a way of reducing federal obligations, a tendency that foreshadowed the termination-era emphasis on transferring responsibilities to the states. For Indigenous families, the choice between federal and public schools involved judgments about quality, access, and the degree of control by their own communities, and the options were rarely ideal.

The postwar boarding school period is thus not a simple continuation of earlier practice, nor a break from it. The mixture of expansion, new investment in some areas, persistent assimilationist assumptions, and Indigenous demand for schooling created a contested field in which the future of Indigenous education would be fought out in the following decades, including in the investigations of the boarding school system examined in Chapter 128.

Section 103.15

First Nations and Métis Education in Postwar Canada

In Canada, the education of status Indian children was governed by the Indian Act and administered by the federal government, in partnership with the churches. The residential school system, in which Roman Catholic, Anglican, United Church, and other denominations operated schools funded by the federal government, remained in operation across the country, alongside federal day schools on reserves. Provincial schools enrolled a smaller number of Indian children in some regions. The quality of education varied considerably, and many residential schools combined limited academic instruction with half-day labor in farm and domestic work, overcrowded dormitories, and inadequate staffing. Inuit education in the North was provided through a small number of federal and mission schools. Métis children attended provincial public schools where they were accepted, or went without, in communities without schools.

Postwar critics pointed to these conditions. Parents objected to the separation of children from families and to the neglect and sometimes abuse that children experienced. Veterans who had served in the forces argued that their children deserved better than the education they had received. Indigenous organizations, including the North American Indian Brotherhood, the Native Brotherhood of British Columbia, the Indian Association of Alberta, and others, submitted briefs and testimony. Teachers and some missionaries and Indian Affairs staff also criticized overcrowding and inadequate facilities. The Special Joint Committee on the Indian Act, which heard from many Indigenous witnesses between 1946 and 1948, concluded that Indian children should be educated alongside other Canadian children wherever practicable, and it recommended an increase in the number of day schools and the integration of Indian students into provincial systems.

The link between education and the Indian Act was direct. The Act defined compulsory attendance, authorized the Minister to establish schools and to commit children to them, and gave church-operated schools a place in the system. Revisions in 1951 allowed the federal government to make agreements with provincial governments, school boards, and religious and charitable organizations for the education of Indian children, creating a legal basis for the integration of Indian students into provincial schools. Over the following years, the proportion of Indian children attending provincial schools rose and the federal government shifted its emphasis from residential schools, though many continued to operate for decades, sometimes serving as child-welfare residences as well as schools. These later developments are traced in Chapters 87 and 88.

The policy of integration had Indigenous supporters and critics. Some families welcomed provincial schools, believing that they offered better instruction and that children would be able to attend while living at home. Others feared that integration would expose children to prejudice, erase cultural identity, and reduce community influence over schooling, and they pointed out that federal and provincial authorities had not consulted them on the terms. Indigenous leaders consistently argued that they should participate in decisions about education. Their requests included local control of day schools, hiring of Indigenous teachers, and instruction in Indigenous languages, and these demands reflected a conviction that education should serve community goals and not only state ones.

Family Allowance payments added another dimension. Because payments to Indian families were administered by Indian Affairs and could be linked to school attendance, parents sometimes found that income depended on compliance with schooling requirements, an arrangement that some regarded as coercive. For Inuit families in the Arctic, the extension of family allowances went along with growing federal pressure for children to attend school. Education therefore became a lever of broader administrative influence.

Differences among communities were significant. In British Columbia, where the Native Brotherhood and other organizations were well established, community members engaged in sustained advocacy. In Ontario and Quebec, First Nations such as the Six Nations of the Grand River had established histories of both federal and local institutions and of debate over education. On the Prairies, residential schools were widespread and often overcrowded, and the Métis lacked the federal system altogether. In the Arctic, schools were few, and a fuller expansion of federal education occurred in the 1950s and 1960s. The analysis of this period therefore requires attention to regional particulars and to the distinction between policy as written and as experienced.

The main thread for the immediate postwar period is that Indigenous communities increasingly demanded educational access and participation while the federal government moved toward integration on its own terms. Later developments, including the growth of Indigenous-controlled education, are discussed elsewhere in the textbook; they are mentioned here only to clarify the direction of change.

Section 103.16

Indigenous Languages, Young People, Women, and New Educational Aspirations

Schooling, military service, and widening contact with outside institutions had intergenerational effects. Young people who had spent years at boarding schools, in the armed forces, or in wage work in cities often returned to their communities with new skills, new expectations, and sometimes new difficulties relating to elders. Parents and grandparents faced decisions about what to teach and what to entrust to schools. In many communities, elders continued to hold primary responsibility for language, history, ceremony, and ecological knowledge, and families developed strategies for transmission that worked around the demands of schooling, such as teaching during summers, ceremonial seasons, and household work.

Language was at the center of these concerns. Most Indigenous languages were still widely spoken in the late 1940s, and in many communities they were the languages of home and ceremony. But the pressures on them were growing. English-only classrooms, military service in English-speaking units, wage work, and radio and film all expanded English's role, and in Mexico the same was true of Spanish. Parents sometimes made deliberate choices to speak English or Spanish with children in order to ease their path in school and work, and in other cases children learned both. Community-based teaching, through storytelling, ceremony, and daily work, sustained languages, but the scarcity of written materials, trained teachers, and official recognition limited institutional support. The more extensive language revitalization movements of later decades, examined in Chapter 123, drew on knowledge preserved by this generation of speakers.

The emergence of Indigenous educators and professionals was a notable development of the period. Teachers, nurses, social workers, interpreters, clerks, and community aides acted as intermediaries between communities and institutions. Some worked in federal and church schools; others in public schools, hospitals, and tribal offices. Indigenous teachers could bring knowledge of community language and culture to classrooms, though they worked within systems that often prescribed curricula and discouraged the use of Indigenous languages. They also formed a growing professional class whose members would take leading roles in educational reform movements later.

Women were central to these developments. Indigenous women worked as teachers and aides, health workers, interpreters, and clerical staff, and they were often responsible for sustaining language and ceremonial practices in the home. They made decisions about family economy, including whether children attended school, whether family members migrated for work, and how cash income was used. In political life, women participated in tribal councils, community organizations, church groups, and national bodies, though constraints varied among nations and legal systems. Under the Indian Act, women's formal participation in band governance was limited until the 1951 revisions, which granted women the right to vote in band council elections, and women's status was affected by marriage rules. In the United States, women held office in some tribal governments established under the Indian Reorganization Act and played prominent roles in community advocacy. Mexican Indigenous women, though often excluded from formal civil-religious offices in their communities, were central to household and market economies and to the transmission of language, and in INI programs a number became promoters and school workers.

Comparison among the three states shows that schooling could serve both Indigenous aspirations and integration policies. In the United States, schooling in English at federal and public schools offered pathways to jobs and further study while undermining Indigenous languages. In Canada, residential and day schools served the government's aim of assimilation, even as Indigenous parents and leaders sought schooling for their children's future. In Mexico, INI's bilingual promoters used Indigenous languages as a bridge to Spanish literacy, a method that aimed at national integration but also created Indigenous educators with a stake in local institutions. In each case, education was not simply imposed; communities negotiated, adapted, and sometimes subverted it.

The question this section poses is whether expanded educational opportunity necessarily required weakening Indigenous cultural and political autonomy. The record of the postwar years suggests that it did not, as a matter of necessity, but that the institutional framework in which schooling was offered tended to link access with assimilation. Indigenous families and leaders who sought schools without surrendering language, land, or governance were asking for a different arrangement than the one on offer. The later development of community-controlled schools, tribal colleges, and language programs was in part a response to this unresolved problem, and the chapters on those developments return to it.

Section 103.17

Returning Veterans, Voting Rights, and Indigenous Political Participation

The relationship between military service and political rights was sharply posed in the United States after 1945. The Indian Citizenship Act of 1924 had declared Indians born in the United States to be citizens, but the Constitution leaves the regulation of voting largely to the states, and several states with substantial Indigenous populations used constitutional provisions, statutes, and administrative practices to keep Native people from voting. The justifications included that Indians were not taxed, that they were wards of the federal government, that they lived on federal land and so were not residents of the state, or that they lacked English literacy. Veterans returning from a war fought in the name of democracy found that these barriers remained, and their experience made the contradiction difficult to ignore.

In 1948, two state-level challenges succeeded. In Arizona, Frank Harrison, a Yavapai man from the Fort McDowell reservation, was refused registration by a county official. The Arizona Supreme Court's decision in Harrison v. Laveen, issued in July 1948, held that Native people living on reservations were not "persons under guardianship" within the meaning of the Arizona constitution's disqualification of persons under guardianship, and it overturned the reasoning of an earlier decision, Porter v. Hall, from 1928, that had supported exclusion. The decision did not eliminate every obstacle, since Arizona's English literacy requirement continued to affect voters, but it removed the principal legal basis for denying the franchise to reservation residents.

In New Mexico, Miguel Trujillo, an Isleta Pueblo man who had served in the Marine Corps, attempted to register in Valencia County and was refused on the ground that the state constitution excluded "Indians not taxed." He sued, and in 1948 a federal three-judge court in Trujillo v. Garley ruled that the exclusion could not stand, holding it inconsistent with the federal Constitution. The decision invalidated New Mexico's bar on Native voters. As with Arizona, formal victory was followed by the continuing work of registration, outreach, and overcoming practical and social obstacles.

Historians stress that these cases drew on more than military service. They rested on constitutional arguments about equal protection and citizenship, on legal interpretations of terms such as guardianship and taxation, and on years of preparation by Indigenous litigants, attorneys, tribal leaders, and allies. Pueblo leaders and Native organizations supported Trujillo's suit, and the National Congress of American Indians, founded in 1944, and its legal advisers took an interest in the cases. Service gave the claimants a powerful public argument and an emotionally compelling narrative, and newspapers and politicians used it. But the argument that Native people should vote because they had fought could be a double-edged one, since it implied that rights depended on military sacrifice, and Indigenous advocates also argued from citizenship and equality as such. Moreover, many Native people voted or did not vote for their own reasons, and some Indigenous traditionalists, such as members of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy, rejected U.S. citizenship and voting as incompatible with the sovereignty of their nations. The expansion of voting rights was thus not uniformly regarded as an unmitigated gain.

Obstacles persisted. Registration practices could be intimidating or inaccessible, with registrars located far from reservations, limited hours, and requirements for documents that some voters lacked. Literacy tests in English, or in some states tests that were administered selectively, disadvantaged those who had little formal schooling. Polling places were distant, and the political influence of Native voters was diluted by the structure of districts and the reluctance of non-Native officials to respond to their interests. Other states acted later: Maine, where Penobscot and Passamaquoddy residents had been excluded from voting in state elections, extended the franchise in 1954, and Utah's restriction was ended in 1957. These dates show that the changes of 1948 opened a longer process rather than concluding one.

Voting rights also connected to the work of national organizations. The National Congress of American Indians, an intertribal body founded in Denver in 1944, made voting rights, protection of treaty rights, and opposition to proposals that would diminish tribal authority central to its work. Its early history, leadership, and structure belong to Chapter 107, and the discussion here is confined to its immediate postwar advocacy. What matters for this chapter is that Native political participation was growing at the same moment as federal policy-makers were reconsidering the government's relationship to Indian nations, so that expanded individual political rights coexisted with debates about collective political status.

That coexistence raises an important distinction between individual citizenship rights and tribal sovereignty. A veteran who won the vote in state elections gained a voice in the state's politics, but this did not alter the political status of his nation, which held a government-to-government relationship with the United States. Some policy-makers in the late 1940s and early 1950s argued that full citizenship made special federal responsibilities unnecessary, a line of reasoning that fed the termination policies discussed in Chapter 104. Indigenous leaders more often argued for both: equal rights as citizens and the retention of tribal governments and treaty rights. Understanding this duality is essential to the political environment of the period.

Section 103.18

The Indian Claims Commission Act of 1946 and Indigenous Land Claims

The Indian Claims Commission Act, signed by President Truman on August 13, 1946, created a federal forum in which Indigenous nations could bring claims against the United States for historical wrongs. The background was long. Over the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the United States had acquired most of its territory through treaties, agreements, and legislation, and many Indigenous nations argued that the land had been taken without adequate payment, through fraud or coercion, or in violation of treaty terms. Because the United States enjoyed sovereign immunity from suit, tribes could not bring such claims in court without its consent, and Congress had granted that consent only through special jurisdictional acts, which authorized individual nations to sue in the Court of Claims on a case-by-case basis. The process was slow, expensive, and dependent on political support.

Proposals for a general claims commission had been discussed for years, and several had foundered. In the 1940s, momentum grew from several sources. The Indian New Deal's emphasis on tribal governments created institutions that could pursue claims. Members of Congress and officials expected that clearing the backlog would allow the government to close old accounts, an aim that fit later termination thinking. Indigenous organizations and attorneys saw an opportunity to obtain compensation. The postwar mood of fairness toward Indian veterans contributed as well. The commission was established with three members, to hear claims against the United States by any Indian tribe, band, or other identifiable group, with a filing period of five years from the date of enactment.

The act defined several categories of claims, including claims arising under the Constitution, laws, treaties, and executive orders; other claims in law or equity, including those sounding in tort; claims that would result if treaties were revised on grounds such as fraud, duress, or unconscionable consideration; claims arising from the taking of lands by the United States without payment of the agreed compensation; and claims based on fair and honorable dealings that were not recognized by any existing rule of law or equity. The last category was significant because it allowed the commission to consider moral claims beyond strict legal rights. The commission could determine the extent of a tribe's title to land, the value of land at the time it was taken, and the offsets to which the government was entitled for payments and certain expenditures. It was served by an investigative division and operated through hearings with attorneys, expert historians and anthropologists, and extensive documentary research.

The crucial limit was that the commission could award money but could not restore land to Indigenous nations. Its authority was to determine liability and compensation, in the form of monetary judgments against the United States. It could not return territory, recognize jurisdiction, or alter the political status of any nation. This limit shaped how the process worked and what it meant. A nation that proved that its lands had been taken unfairly would receive a sum calculated on the value of the land at the time of the taking, not at present value and generally without interest, and the government could deduct offsets. The process thus treated land loss as a financial injury to be compensated, rather than as a loss of homeland, relationship, and political authority.

Indigenous responses reflected this tension. Many nations filed claims, often through attorneys, and the process engaged communities in research, testimony, and discussion of their histories. For some, the prospect of compensation was welcome, as money could fund community programs. Others were uneasy. Some leaders objected that accepting money for land might be taken as consent to its loss, and that no sum could address the loss of territory or the destruction of political and spiritual relationships to land. Some nations, notably the Lakota in the case of the Black Hills, placed emphasis on the return of land rather than payment, and the later history of the Black Hills claim illustrates the long-term conflict between monetary compensation and restoration. Disagreements also arose within communities about attorneys' fees, the use of awards, and the distribution of payments among members.

The commission's work lasted through 1978, and it adjudicated hundreds of dockets, but this chapter does not survey its decisions. For the 1945 to 1953 period, what matters is its creation and its framing of the issue. The act was one of several measures of these years that reflected a policy of settling accounts with Indigenous peoples while moving toward reducing federal responsibilities. Officials hoped that the settlement of claims would remove obstacles to ending federal supervision. Indigenous advocates hoped that it would vindicate treaty rights. Because the commission could not restore land, the two aims were not aligned, and the tension between them runs into the termination period.

The act also shows how historical research and law intersected with Indigenous politics. The need to prove title, occupancy, and the circumstances of land cessions generated extensive historical and anthropological research, including by expert witnesses whose reports became part of the scholarly record. This work preserved evidence of Indigenous land use and political organization, while also shaping the legal and historical categories by which nations presented themselves, often requiring them to demonstrate exclusive occupancy of defined areas in ways that fit poorly with overlapping or shared use.

Section 103.19

Canada, Indigenous Political Advocacy, and the Indian Act Revision of 1951

In Canada, the postwar years produced a sustained reconsideration of Indian policy. During the war, Indigenous service and war work, the example of the Atlantic Charter's language of self-determination, and the growth of Indigenous organizations such as the North American Indian Brotherhood, founded in 1943, increased pressure for reform. Veterans and their families spoke of the contradiction between military service and legal restrictions. Churches, humanitarian groups, and some members of Parliament joined calls for change. In 1946 Parliament appointed a Special Joint Committee of the Senate and House of Commons to examine the Indian Act, and it held hearings through 1948. Indigenous delegations from across the country testified, often at length, about education, enfranchisement, land, treaty rights, taxation, health, voting, and the powers of Indian agents. This process marked a significant occasion for Indigenous voices to be heard in Parliament, though the committee was composed of non-Indigenous parliamentarians and its recommendations were advisory.

The committee's reports recommended a revision of the Act with greater attention to Indigenous opinion, including the removal of provisions that restricted religious ceremonies and the raising of funds for claims, the expansion of education and social services, and measures to extend the rights of citizenship. Legislation followed in 1951, after further consultation. The revised Indian Act, which received royal assent in 1951 and took effect that September, consolidated and rewrote the earlier statute. The administration of Indian Affairs moved in 1950 to the new Department of Citizenship and Immigration, reflecting the government's integrationist emphasis.

The 1951 revisions removed some of the most criticized provisions. The prohibition on ceremonies such as the potlatch and Sun Dance, which had been in the Act since the nineteenth century and had been used to suppress central practices of Indigenous religion and governance, was repealed. The 1927 ban on soliciting funds to pursue land claims, which had hampered Indigenous political organization, was also removed. The Act created a centralized Indian Register to record status, extended to women the right to vote in band council elections, loosened some restrictions on the consumption of alcohol, and authorized agreements with provinces for education and other services. It also incorporated provincial laws of general application into the Act's scope on reserves, a change that expanded provincial involvement.

The 1951 Act nonetheless left intact the core structure of federal control. The Minister retained broad authority over the definition and registration of Indian status, the administration of band funds and reserve lands, the approval of band council decisions, and the supervision of wills and estates. Enfranchisement, the legal process by which an individual or family lost Indian status and became subject to general Canadian law, remained, and in some respects policy continued to encourage it as an objective. The amendments did not establish Indigenous self-government, and they did not give status First Nations people the unrestricted right to vote in federal elections, which came only in 1960. Whether the Act was reform or restatement is the subject of lasting disagreement among historians, with some emphasizing the repeal of repressive provisions and others the persistence of paternalism.

The treatment of Indigenous women under status and registration rules deserves emphasis. The Indian Act continued the rule that an Indian woman who married a man without Indian status lost her own status and band membership, while an Indian man who married a non-Indian woman retained his and conferred status on his wife. The 1951 revisions added the so-called double mother rule, under which a child whose mother and paternal grandmother both had status only through marriage would lose status at the age of twenty-one. The Act thus continued to define Indigenous women's belonging through their relationships to men, a discrimination that Indigenous women challenged for decades, leading to the amendments of 1985 and later litigation.

It is also essential to distinguish First Nations legal status from the circumstances of Métis and Inuit communities. The Indian Act applied to status Indians. Métis people had no status under the Act and, in the postwar years, were largely addressed through provincial policies, such as Alberta's Métis colonies and the efforts of Métis organizations in Saskatchewan and Manitoba. Inuit were within federal jurisdiction but outside the Indian Act and were administered through separate northern institutions. Any statement about the 1951 revisions must therefore be specified as applying to status Indians and their communities.

Indigenous reactions to the 1951 Act were mixed. Some leaders welcomed the repeal of ceremonial bans and the removal of the claims restriction, which opened space for ceremony and organization. Others were disappointed that the Act did not recognize treaty rights more fully or provide for self-government, and they criticized the continuing powers of the Minister and the enfranchisement provisions. Many continued to organize, forming provincial and national associations in the decades that followed.

Section 103.20

Postwar Indigenous America at the Threshold of Termination and New Activism

The five transformations traced in this chapter, returning veterans, demographic and social change, economic development, expanding education, and rising political expectations, were interwoven rather than sequential. Veterans' service shaped the educational and political claims of their communities. Population growth and health improvements raised demand for schools, housing, and jobs. Development programs introduced new money and new forms of control. Education created leaders who could challenge as well as administer those programs, and political expectations gave all of these developments a vocabulary of rights, equality, and responsibility. At the same time, each transformation operated within institutions, such as trust status, the Indian Act, territorial administration, and indigenismo, that limited Indigenous autonomy.

Indigenous communities pursued greater access to public services while defending treaty rights, landholding, cultural institutions, and political authority. This dual pursuit is the chapter's central pattern. Requests for schools, clinics, roads, and jobs were not requests to surrender governments or lands. Veterans, tribal leaders, band councils, Native organizations, and community members repeatedly distinguished between services that improved life and policies that required giving up political status. The distinction mattered because some federal officials proposed to treat service equality as a reason for ending special relationships, as though equal citizenship made treaties and trust obligations obsolete.

The policy environments of the countries differed in ways that should not be blurred. The United States combined citizenship with a continuing trust relationship, tribal governments recognized in law, and a bureaucracy under growing political pressure to reduce its role. Canada maintained the Indian Act regime, with its status system and ministerial authority, while moving toward integration through provincial services and citizenship measures. Alaska was a territory whose Native peoples were pressing for equal treatment and for recognition of land interests under territorial administration. Mexico advanced a state-led indigenismo that treated Indigenous peoples as a distinct sector of a national population, using institutions designed to promote integration and development. These were not variants of a single continental administration. They had different legal foundations, different institutions, and different possibilities for Indigenous action, and comparison is valuable precisely because of these differences.

By the beginning of the 1950s, competing visions of Indigenous development, political equality, and government responsibility were becoming explicit. In the United States, officials in the Interior Department and Congress increasingly discussed ending federal supervision of Indian affairs. The Hoover Commission on government organization had recommended in 1949 that responsibility for Indian programs be transferred where possible to states and other agencies, and the Bureau of Indian Affairs under Commissioner Dillon S. Myer, who took office in 1950, pursued policies of reduced federal responsibility and encouraged off-reservation employment. These tendencies had antecedents in the Bureau's own earlier planning for the withdrawal of services from tribes judged ready. In 1953, Congress adopted House Concurrent Resolution 108, announcing the policy of ending federal supervision as soon as possible, and Public Law 280, which extended state criminal and some civil jurisdiction over Indian country in several states. These measures belong to Chapter 104.

Introduced here, they should be understood as the culmination of a conflict that the chapter has traced. On one side stood proposals to end certain government-to-government responsibilities, often justified in the language of equality, efficiency, and freedom. On the other stood Indigenous efforts to defend their continuing political institutions and treaty relationships, expressed through tribal councils, delegations to Washington, and organizations such as the National Congress of American Indians. Many Indigenous leaders accepted that integration into the wider economy and society was inevitable and, in some respects, desirable. What they resisted was integration that required dissolving their nations. The same distinction appears in Canada in the responses to the Indian Act revisions, and in Mexico in community engagement with indigenista institutions.

The chapter's analytical studies bring these threads together. The Cause and Effect study that follows examines how service, employment, population change, administrative reform, and education fed expanding expectations. The Continuity and Change study compares Indigenous political authority, rights, economic conditions, and educational access in 1939, 1945, and 1953, in order to show how much changed and how much did not. Together they support the chapter's closing claim: that postwar Indigenous peoples and governments advanced competing interpretations of progress.

For many officials, progress meant integration into national institutions. For many Indigenous communities, it meant improved living conditions without surrendering sovereignty, treaty rights, cultural continuity, or political authority. This disagreement did not arise from rejection of modernity by Indigenous peoples, who pursued education, wages, medicine, and political participation with energy. It arose over who would define the terms of change and whether the nations would continue to exist as political communities. That question, sharpened in the early 1950s, is the foundation for the history of termination in Chapter 104.

Cause and Effect

Why Political Expectations Expanded after 1945

Long-term causes

Longstanding Indigenous political traditions and organizations, treaty rights, the Indian New Deal's tribal governments, and decades of protest against exclusion and administrative control.

Short-term causes

Military service, wartime wages and migration, education benefits, population and health changes, parliamentary review in Canada, and federal development programs.

Immediate trigger

Return of veterans to legal restrictions and unequal services, public attention to conditions such as the severe winters of 1947 and 1949, and creation of forums such as the claims commission.

Event or transformation

Court challenges to voting bars, claims filings, testimony to Parliament, new Native organizations, and demands for services without loss of political status.

Immediate effects

Some barriers removed, new federal programs, and public discussion of Indigenous status.

Long-term consequences

Intensified conflict over integration and sovereignty, leading into termination and later self-determination movements.

Historical complexity: No single factor produced rising expectations. Military service was one among several; expectations rested on prior struggles, and their outcomes were contingent on the choices of governments and Indigenous organizations.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the years from 1945 to about 1953 as a period of transition in which Indigenous peoples moved from wartime mobilization to peacetime life. Its central argument is that the war accelerated changes already under way, bringing new opportunities for education, employment, public services, and political participation, while the institutions that delivered them continued to restrict Indigenous sovereignty, economic independence, and equal access to resources.

The chapter began with returning veterans. Indigenous service members and war workers came home as members of nations and communities, to economies and legal systems that differed among the United States, Canada, Alaska, and Mexico. The Servicemen's Readjustment Act and the Canadian Veterans Charter offered education, training, medical care, and, in principle, loans and land assistance. The distinction between eligibility and access explains why outcomes varied: trust land and reserve land limited loan benefits, distance and discrimination limited services, and Indian Affairs officials mediated Canadian benefits.

Veterans' return also had social meaning. Families reunited, grieved, and adapted; women who had served or worked in war industries encountered narrowing opportunities; and communities received veterans through ceremony, associations, and leadership. Veterans did not form a single political bloc, and their influence varied by community and institution.

Demographic evidence shows both change and the limits of measurement. The U.S. census counted 333,969 American Indians in 1940 and 343,410 in 1950, but categories and enumeration methods changed, and later increases reflect reclassification as well as growth. Canada and Mexico counted Indigenous populations by legal status and language respectively. Population history is inseparable from the institutions that recorded it.

Health conditions reveal the gap between formal responsibility and actual services. Tuberculosis, infant mortality, and poor sanitation reflected poverty, overcrowding, and limited infrastructure. New antibiotics and expanded services began to reduce mortality, but their effect depended on hospitals, staff, and transport that remained scarce. The transfer of U.S. Indian health to the Public Health Service came after the period.

Household economies were diversified, combining wages, subsistence, crafts, and public assistance. Women's work and decision-making, children's schooling, and elders' roles sustained community continuity. Alaska and the northern homelands showed the effects of military expansion, territorial administration, and Indigenous political organizing, as in the Anti-Discrimination Act of 1945.

Economic development in these years was marked by large federal programs, such as the Navajo-Hopi Rehabilitation Act of 1950, and by mobility among reservations, reserves, towns, and cities. Development brought roads, schools, and jobs, but also federal control, new risks such as uranium mining, and pressure to leave home communities. Mexico's Instituto Nacional Indigenista, created in 1948, offered a different institutional model, with similar tensions between services and integration.

Education became a principal arena. Veterans used educational benefits, Indigenous parents sought better schools, and governments expanded facilities while retaining assimilationist assumptions. Canadian policy moved toward integration in provincial schools, and Mexican policy used bilingual promoters. Across the three countries, schooling served both Indigenous aspirations and state integration, and the question of control remained open.

Political expectations expanded. The 1948 cases in Arizona and New Mexico removed major state barriers to Native voting, the Indian Claims Commission Act created a forum for historical claims limited to monetary remedies, and the Canadian Indian Act revision of 1951 repealed some restrictions while preserving federal authority and discriminatory status rules for women. These measures had different aims and effects and should be distinguished.

The chapter ends at the threshold of the termination era. Indigenous communities pursued services and equality while defending treaty rights, land, culture, and political authority, whereas many officials equated progress with integration and reduced federal responsibility. This conflict between competing interpretations of progress provides the foundation for Chapter 104.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows that the end of the Second World War was not a simple return to normal for Indigenous North America. Veterans, workers, families, and communities brought new experiences and expectations into institutions that changed slowly and often resisted Indigenous participation. The postwar years are important because they reveal a pattern that would recur: services and rights expanded, but on terms set by governments.

The chapter also shows how much depended on legal frameworks. Trust land, the Indian Act, territorial administration, and indigenismo each created distinct possibilities and constraints. Comparing them reveals that Indigenous peoples across the continent faced related problems of poverty, schooling, health, and recognition, but through different legal and political systems.

Several developments of these years shaped later history. The 1948 voting decisions opened the way for greater Native participation in state politics. The claims process established a model of monetary settlement whose limits later became contested. The Canadian revisions of 1951 set the stage for further decades of organizing against the Indian Act, including on women's status. Educational expansion produced a generation of Indigenous professionals who would lead in later movements.

Finally, the chapter prepares students for Chapter 104. The distinction between equality of services and preservation of political status was already explicit by 1950. The termination era would test it directly, and Indigenous responses, informed by the experiences described here, would help produce the activism, treaty-rights struggles, and national organizations examined in the chapters that follow.
