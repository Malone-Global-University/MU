Chapter Opening

Washington, November 2, 1972. Indigenous travelers who had crossed the United States to present a national program for treaty rights and governmental reconstruction entered the headquarters of the Bureau of Indian Affairs. Their planned delegation was becoming something very different.

The Trail of Broken Treaties began before the confrontation that made it famous. During the autumn of 1972, Indigenous organizers assembled caravans from the Pacific Coast, Oklahoma, and other communities to demand serious federal consideration of treaty obligations, land rights, and the political status of Indigenous nations. Travelers carried experiences of reservations and cities, of schools and courts, of federal assistance and its failures. They intended to meet officials during a presidential election and to demonstrate that Indigenous people could speak together without surrendering the sovereignty of their individual nations.

The original strategy was not the permanent occupation of a federal headquarters. Organizers held meetings on the Rosebud Reservation and in Denver, gathered donations, arranged transport, and sought the cooperation of Indigenous associations and religious supporters. The caravans passed through communities whose histories differed profoundly. The forced removal of southeastern nations, the legal struggles of Northwestern fishing peoples, and the politics of Plains nations could not be collapsed into a single story. The journey was designed to create a shared political audience out of that diversity.

In the Twin Cities, the travelers and their advisers turned accumulated recommendations into a detailed Twenty-Point Position Paper. Coordinated and finalized by Hank Adams from contributions made by many participants, the paper proposed renewed treaty-making authority, land restoration, judicial remedies, changes in jurisdiction, and a successor to the Bureau of Indian Affairs. It also addressed Indigenous membership, religion, education, employment, housing, and health. The coalition was asking the government to reconsider the structure of political authority, not merely to increase the budget of existing programs.

The arrival in Washington revealed how difficult it would be to translate a manifesto into federal action. Accommodation arrangements failed to meet the needs of travelers. Expected meetings were canceled, disputed, or reduced in scope. An earlier federal instruction had prohibited assistance from the BIA, the very agency on which many visiting delegations had historically relied. Disagreement over an intended ceremony honoring Indigenous veterans added to frustration. As demonstrators gathered in the BIA auditorium, a dispute over shelter and political recognition became a confrontation about federal control of the building.

By November 3, a barricaded occupation had begun. Participants declared the building a Native American embassy, negotiated with Interior officials, and later communicated through a White House channel. The situation demanded daily decisions about food, children, safety, communication, and access to records. Reports of damaged rooms, removed files, and threats of force dominated national coverage, while the original Twenty Points received less attention. Some Indigenous leaders supported the underlying political demands but strongly criticized destruction of records crucial to land and enrollment claims.

The occupation ended on November 8 through negotiations that included assistance for travelers and a commitment to a federal interagency review. Those arrangements were not equivalent to implementing the Twenty Points. The contrast between immediate settlement and long-term political ambitions became central to subsequent evaluations of the campaign. The federal government recovered the building, the caravan participants went home, and Indigenous advocates continued debating whether the confrontation had brought valuable attention or displaced their policy program.

The surviving evidence includes the original manifesto, contemporary correspondence, an Episcopal Church committee report prepared soon afterward, newspaper coverage, federal accounts, photographs, and Indigenous institutional records. These sources do not all say the same thing. A near-contemporary report may preserve a quotation from a memorandum whose original copy is not presently at hand; an official estimate of damage may not be a final audited cost; a participant’s account may clarify motives without proving that everyone agreed. This chapter identifies those limitations rather than treating all historical claims as equally established.

The story is bounded deliberately. Chapter 110 examined the American Indian Movement as an organization; this chapter considers a multi-organization political campaign whose final occupation AIM helped lead. Chapter 112 will address Wounded Knee in 1973, and Chapter 114 will examine the self-determination policies of the following decade. Here the question is how a caravan, a manifesto, and an occupied building became three connected stages of one effort to make treaty rights and Indigenous governmental authority central to American national politics.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the caravan, Twenty-Point Position Paper, and BIA occupation constituted one political campaign.
  2. Identify the Rosebud and Denver planning meetings and distinguish coalition organizers from endorsing organizations.
  3. Trace the October 1972 routes and explain the importance of community stops and Twin Cities workshops.
  4. Analyze the manifesto as a program of constitutional, treaty, judicial, land, and administrative reconstruction.
  5. Distinguish Congress’s 1871 end of new treaty-making from the continuing status of existing treaties.
  6. Explain the demands to restore a land base, reform jurisdiction, and protect Indigenous membership authority.
  7. Identify the proposed replacement of the BIA and distinguish it from ordinary administrative reform.
  8. Reconstruct the November 1–3 transition from arrivals to a barricaded occupation using conflicting sources.
  9. Analyze the community’s practical needs, the role of BIA documents, and competing accounts of damage.
  10. Compare the responsibilities of Interior, BIA, White House, courts, and caravan negotiators.
  11. Distinguish the Nine Demands, November 8 settlement, and interagency review from adoption of the Twenty Points.
  12. Evaluate Indigenous disagreement over the occupation’s tactics and its influence on later policy without assuming simple causation.

Historical Context

Indigenous nations entered the twentieth century with distinct treaties, governments, territorial relationships, and experiences of federal administration. The 1871 decision to cease making new treaties did not invalidate existing agreements, but it altered the forms through which federal policy was negotiated. Later allotment, assimilation, termination, and jurisdictional policies created overlapping grievances, discussed in Chapters 92, 104, and 106. The Trail brought those histories together around the question of whether the United States would treat existing obligations as enforceable commitments or as matters for discretionary administration.

The National Congress of American Indians and National Indian Youth Council had developed networks of legal and political advocacy before 1972. The American Indian Movement, created in Minneapolis in 1968, added direct action and neighborhood programs to this field; the Alcatraz occupation created a prominent example of land-based protest. The Trail was related to these developments but was not an action directed by one organization acting on behalf of all Native nations. Its participants included organizers with histories in tribal government, legal defense, youth organizing, churches, and intertribal activism.

President Nixon’s July 1970 message rejected termination as a policy direction and supported self-determination without abandonment of federal obligations. That announcement preceded the caravan. In 1972, activists judged existing reforms inadequate because many treaty disputes, land questions, and administrative practices persisted. The presidential election offered a moment when federal candidates might be pressed to state their positions, but the demands extended beyond one administration. The Twenty Points called for a comprehensive legislative and institutional settlement.

The chapter treats the October caravan and November occupation as connected events while preserving the distinction between participants’ intended plans and consequences that emerged in Washington. It uses Indigenous organizational statements, the Center for World Indigenous Studies reproduction of the manifesto, the National Park Service narrative, the February 1973 Episcopal report, and Smithsonian archival finding aids. Each source has its own perspective and limitations; the reader should distinguish verified chronology from attribution, interpretation, and later memory.

Section 111.1

The Crisis of Federal–Indigenous Relations in 1972

By 1972, termination, treaty violations, land loss, federal paternalism, and political exclusion had left many Indigenous communities with grievances that federal reform had not resolved. Nixon's July 8, 1970 message repudiated termination and endorsed self-determination. Activists nonetheless judged these steps insufficient, because the message did not bind Washington to concrete treaty, land, or jurisdictional commitments. The 1972 presidential election supplied the immediate political context: a national campaign offered visibility and leverage.

The political argument of the Trail of Broken Treaties began before a vehicle crossed the country. For more than a century the United States had treated Native nations as treaty partners while steadily restricting the political significance of those agreements. The change in federal treaty making in 1871 did not extinguish existing treaties, but Congress thereafter relied on statutes, appropriations, and administrative decisions that nations could not negotiate on equal terms. Federal agents continued to exercise extensive authority over education, trust land, business development, and social services. Campaign organizers understood these matters as connected: the administration of programs was inseparable from who possessed the right to make decisions about land and governments. Their proposed remedy accordingly reached far beyond improved appropriations or the replacement of a few officials.

The 1950s policy of termination brought the conflict into especially sharp relief. Congress had ended federal recognition of particular tribal relationships and reduced protections for lands and services, while relocation policies encouraged migration into cities. Resistance to termination was already substantial by the early 1960s, but its consequences endured in the Menominee and Klamath experiences and in the anxieties of nations threatened with similar action. Public Law 280 introduced further disagreement about state jurisdiction over reservation communities. An initiative promising to restore political equality could not avoid these histories: they supplied examples of federal choices made without consent and helped explain why activists distrusted invitations to participate in administrative programs that remained under outside direction.

The late-1960s Red Power movement offered new methods alongside established legal arguments. Fish-ins, campus organizations, national Indigenous associations, and the occupation of Alcatraz demonstrated that public action could make treaty questions visible to a national audience. Yet each campaign had a different constituency and objective. The Trail attempted something more integrative: to bring people concerned with rural land, urban welfare, schools, spiritual practices, policing, and treaty law into one national presentation. This did not imply that every tribal government endorsed the project. It meant organizers were attempting to show that different grievances shared a common structure of decision-making power that required congressional and executive attention.

President Richard Nixon's July 8, 1970 message became an important point of comparison. Nixon repudiated termination and described self-determination without ending federal responsibilities, a change welcomed in many Indigenous political circles. Yet an executive message was not the same as laws changing the handling of land, treaty enforcement, or federal jurisdiction. Legislators still had to act on many of the administration's proposals; agencies had their own established procedures, and the federal bureaucracy could resist changes in authority. The Trail's organizers interpreted the gap between policy announcements and day-to-day governance as precisely the problem that made an ambitious written program necessary. They were not simply asking officials to repeat the language of self-determination.

The presidential election gave the campaign a deadline and an audience. Richard Nixon and George McGovern were presenting competing visions of public policy, while participants in the caravan wanted both campaigns to explain how they would deal with Indigenous sovereignty. An electoral moment could attract journalists and require politicians to respond; it could not guarantee that either party would accept the demands. The organizers also recognized that many treaty obligations transcended party politics and presidential terms. Their strategy sought to make those obligations a continuing national question, not a temporary campaign promise contingent on which candidate won.

The name Trail of Broken Treaties worked at several levels. It evoked the forced migrations of the nineteenth century, especially the Trail of Tears, and it identified repeated treaty violations as a contemporary political condition rather than an episode safely confined to the past. The public could recognize the moral charge without knowing every relevant agreement; participants were responsible for supplying the legal and historical specificity that made the critique more than symbolism. Thus the central purpose of the 1972 journey was both public education and organized political pressure. It was not conceived as an assault on a government building, although the occupation that followed would profoundly affect how the campaign was remembered.

Historical Vocabulary

Self-determination and inherent sovereignty

Definition. Self-determination concerns the authority of Indigenous peoples to determine their political future and control institutions affecting their lives; inherent sovereignty refers to political authority that predates the United States.

Historical setting. The Nixon administration used self-determination as a policy direction, but the caravan demanded more enforceable treaty and governmental rights.

Misunderstanding to avoid. Federal permission to administer a program is not the same as full political control over jurisdiction, land, or treaty interpretation.

Section 111.2

The Formation of the Trail of Broken Treaties Coalition

Planning emerged at a gathering in August 1972 at Crow Dog's Paradise on the Rosebud Reservation, followed by meetings in Denver. Hank Adams, Dennis Banks, Russell Means, Robert Burnette, Reuben Snake, and organizers from participating organizations all played roles. Historians should distinguish founding organizers, endorsing organizations, and later participants, because later accounts often collapse the coalition into AIM alone. Contemporary organizational reporting documents the financial and logistical difficulties of assembling the caravan.

Accounts produced years afterward sometimes credit the caravan to a single famous AIM personality. The near-contemporary report prepared for the Episcopal Church provides a more complicated history. It identifies August 17, 1972, at Crow Dog's Paradise on the Rosebud Reservation as the moment when organizers began shaping a national convergence on Washington. The setting mattered: a gathering on Indigenous land offered an opportunity for political planning outside a federal conference room, and it brought together people whose previous experiences included community action, national advocacy, and confrontations with government agencies. The idea at this stage was to organize a delegation with enough geographic reach and public visibility to secure meetings with federal and party leaders.

Planning a national caravan required organizations, not only speakers. Someone had to negotiate meeting sites, arrange food and vehicles, collect contributions, contact churches and community centers, identify lawyers, and establish a plan for what delegates would actually present when they reached Washington. The scope was unusually demanding because the participants were dispersed across thousands of miles and could not rely on one permanent national office. At the same time, leaders wanted broad representation without letting funding arrangements or a central steering group silence local voices. This organizational tension shaped the campaign from the beginning. The more inclusive the undertaking became, the more complicated it was to coordinate its expectations and obligations.

The organizing meetings in Denver on October 1 and 2 brought representatives from AIM, the National Indian Youth Council, the Native American Rights Fund, the National Indian Brotherhood in Canada, and other Indigenous organizations into the same discussion. The Episcopal report also names the National American Indian Council, National Indian Leadership Training, the American Indian Commission on Alcohol and Drug Abuse, and the National Committee on Indian Work. Several additional organizations endorsed or assisted the venture, including women's groups, United Native Americans, educational associations, and the National Indian Lutheran Board. Endorsement was not identical to operating control. The coalition's diversity is most accurately described by identifying who attended planning, who supplied funding or moral support, who traveled, and who later publicly advocated its proposals.

Robert Burnette, a former Rosebud tribal chairman and former executive director of the National Congress of American Indians, and Reuben Snake were selected as co-chairmen during planning. Their roles demonstrate why the caravan should not be represented as a rebellion entirely detached from established Indigenous politics. People who had worked in tribal administration, national organizations, churches, and legal advocacy contributed alongside militants known for demonstrations. Nevertheless, the NCAI as an institution was not a principal sponsor of the caravan, and later disagreements with its officers should not be mistaken for proof that its individual members had no role. Organizational identities and individual political activity overlapped without being interchangeable.

The money problem appeared early. The Episcopal report records approaches to Lutheran Indigenous leaders concerning an ambitious funding request and their response that the resources and planning were insufficient for the proposal as framed. Churches nevertheless provided a portion of the assistance and facilitated contacts. This was a complicated relationship: religious institutions had historically participated in the assimilation of Indigenous people, yet some clergy and lay networks now supported Indigenous political and cultural self-determination. Their contributions could enable the caravan without entitling them to dictate its political content. A history centered only on speeches would miss the decisive effect of such practical negotiations on the journey's size and conditions.

An early seven-point program emerged at Denver, asking for treaty fulfillment, stronger protection of land and resource rights, changes to the administration of Indian affairs, protection against termination without a community referendum, adequate appropriations, higher educational opportunity, and meetings with presidential candidates. This program preceded the more detailed Twenty-Point Position Paper. The relationship reveals continuity rather than improvisation: the later document refined broad principles into particular institutional mechanisms. Organizers also publicly presented the caravan as a spiritual and peaceful undertaking and sought to exclude deliberate disruption and destruction. That original intention is essential evidence when evaluating the occupation, because later events did not erase what participants had set out to accomplish.

Section 111.3

The Caravan Routes: From the West Coast and Southern Plains to Washington

Caravans left Seattle, San Francisco, and Los Angeles on October 6, 1972, and an Oklahoma caravan retraced part of the Trail of Tears. Stops at reservations and urban Indigenous communities turned the journey into an organizing process that linked western reservation communities, urban organizations, and Plains nations. The route created a mobile national coalition.

Three main caravan routes departed from Seattle, San Francisco, and Los Angeles around October 6, 1972. Their departure points were significant centers of Indigenous political activism, but their routes ran through many different Indigenous geographies. California urban communities did not have the same treaty histories as Plains nations; Washington fishing-rights activists brought a distinct experience of state regulation and court conflict. By traveling to and through reservation and urban communities, the caravan linked these issues without assuming that one national statement could substitute for each nation's legal claims. The route made the project's wide geographical scope visible to the public and to participants themselves.

A further caravan originating in Oklahoma explicitly recalled the nineteenth-century forced removal of southeastern Native nations, an event long associated with the Trail of Tears. The analogy was historical and political, not an assertion that every route in 1972 physically retraced a single removal path. Organizers chose the symbolism to challenge the comfortable separation between an earlier era of removal and ongoing disputes over federal authority. The caravan's name thus operated as a mobile history lesson. It asked the public to see contemporary treaty and jurisdictional grievances as continuations of older political decisions, while the caravan's specific proposals made those connections actionable.

The travel itself depended on ordinary infrastructure. Cars and buses needed fuel, repairs, drivers, insurance, places to park, and reliable destinations where people could sleep. Participants with limited income needed help meeting expenses; families required arrangements different from those of unmarried students. When the number of travelers grew or a local destination changed, organizers had to communicate updates across multiple routes without modern mobile phones or digital registration systems. Some churches, Indigenous community organizations, and sympathetic individuals helped bridge those gaps. Their participation was not incidental: without transportation and food, even the strongest political declaration would never reach the capital.

Stops also provided opportunities for reciprocal exchange. Organizers gathered stories about schools, criminal jurisdiction, land leases, health services, tribal enrollment, and the treatment of Indigenous workers in cities. Local participants could question national figures about the aims and methods of the campaign, and travelers could learn that problems described in one region sometimes had different causes elsewhere. A reservation with a disputed resource lease did not resemble an urban center seeking health care access in every respect. Workshops and conversations nevertheless helped form a common agenda: decision-making authority, enforceable obligations, and public accountability. This process helps explain why the later Twenty Points extended into such diverse areas.

Media attention was uneven. A caravan passing through a local community could receive substantial newspaper coverage and little national notice, while a speech by a well-known AIM leader might circulate more widely. Images of automobiles moving toward Washington communicated scale but could not convey the significance of a particular treaty clause. Organizers therefore faced a communication task: transform physical movement into public understanding. Press releases, interviews, and stops at symbolic places served that purpose. The risk was that a visually compelling protest would become a story about prominent personalities or confrontation rather than the legal program participants were developing.

The western caravans converged in the Twin Cities late in October, where AIM had established an important urban organizing base. Minneapolis and St. Paul provided meeting space and a network of people familiar with urban Indigenous political needs. The gathering was also a transition point: the journey's accumulation of grievances now had to become a usable document. The caravan could not simply arrive with hundreds of separate requests and expect federal officials to respond. Participants needed a structure that converted regional testimony into proposals for congressional and executive action. That task belonged to the St. Paul workshops and to the drafting process led by Hank Adams, the subject of the next division.

Section 111.4

On the Road: Community Meetings, Solidarity, and Political Education

Workshops, ceremonies, and recruitment along the routes drew women, elders, families, students, veterans, and participants from many nations. The journey helped identify shared grievances while leaving distinct treaty histories and priorities intact. Evidence for these experiences is uneven; much survives through organizers' reports and press accounts rather than participants' own records.

It is tempting to imagine the caravan as a unified procession whose members agreed about every political question. The evidence suggests a more complex temporary community. Participants came from different nations and regions and had different prior experiences with federal agents, tribal governments, church assistance, urban service organizations, and public protest. Some saw the journey chiefly as an opportunity to defend treaties and specific territorial claims. Others were concerned about housing, educational access, policing, health, or Indigenous people whom federal programs did not recognize as eligible for services. A coalition became meaningful only when these priorities could be heard and translated into shared objectives without pretending they were identical.

The participation of women and families is particularly important because press accounts tend to concentrate on public spokesmen. Travelers needed food preparation, child supervision, communications, medical supplies, and coordination of sleeping arrangements. Those tasks were political labor: they made it possible for others to attend discussions and present testimony. Yet a historian should resist assigning individual actions or titles to unnamed participants without evidence. The more useful question is how organizing depended on work that was often less visible than speeches. Where surviving oral histories or organizational files identify particular women or families, their accounts should be incorporated with the same care given to better-known leaders.

The Denver planning statement described the caravan as a spiritual undertaking and assigned ceremonies a central role in the journey. Different communities possessed their own traditions and authority over practices, so a shared political ceremony should not be construed as one uniform Indigenous religion. Ritual observance could establish respect, discipline, and historical continuity for participants while also making a public claim that Indigenous ways of political assembly deserved recognition. Some supporters valued ceremonies as the heart of the caravan's purpose; others may have focused on concrete legal outcomes. The resulting tension is a subject for historical inquiry rather than a contradiction requiring an artificial resolution.

The relationship between urban and reservation participants also needs to be understood through actual experiences. Urban residents faced difficulties involving housing, welfare, legal representation, employment, and access to Indian Health Service programs. Reservation governments faced land management, treaty recognition, resource control, jurisdiction, and federal trust responsibilities. These concerns overlapped, especially for families maintaining ties to home nations while living in cities. The caravan's challenge was to describe a common political future without treating urban people as somehow less Indigenous or presuming that intertribal campaign organizers could replace governments chosen by specific nations.

Accounts written by movement supporters offer valuable evidence of aspirations and solidarity but cannot be treated as a census of opinion. An organizer might describe a successful meeting as expressing unanimous agreement when some participants merely accepted a temporary compromise. Conversely, newspapers might identify a clash as proof that the coalition was incoherent when the disagreement concerned an ordinary question of tactics. Researchers should compare correspondence, meeting notes, Indigenous press coverage, mainstream reporting, and later interviews. The differences among these records help explain how a political coalition could appear strong on the road and still face serious problems when confronting a tightly organized federal administration.

By the time travelers reached the Twin Cities, their most important accomplishment was not a completed revolution in federal policy. They had connected multiple Indigenous political communities and established a channel through which people could propose changes together. Their meetings turned personal and regional experience into questions of treaty jurisdiction, resource control, and institutional design. The next challenge was whether the resulting program would satisfy both local constituencies and the national audiences whose attention the caravan sought. That question became more urgent as the deadline for the Washington presentation approached.

The Twenty-Point Position Paper and the Program for Reconstruction

Drafting, treaty renewal, land restoration, and institutional reform

Section 111.5

Drafting an Indigenous Political Manifesto, October 1972

The caravans converged in the Twin Cities for workshops ending October 27. Hank Adams coordinated the final drafting from participants' recommendations, producing the Twenty-Point Position Paper. Its preamble moved beyond a statement of grievances toward constitutional, legislative, and administrative change.

The St. Paul workshops in late October confronted a problem of political translation. Organizers had accumulated demands from participants across many nations, but the federal government was organized into separate agencies, committees, and legal categories. A request for improved health services might go to one office, a land title dispute to another, and treaty enforcement to Congress or the courts. To challenge the overall relationship rather than seek isolated favors, the workshops needed a coherent statement of what institutional change should accomplish. Their resulting paper linked treaty-making, land, membership, jurisdiction, agency structure, cultural rights, and community development in a sequence that exposed the limits of narrow program reform.

Hank Adams, an Assiniboine-Sioux activist with deep experience in Northwest fishing-rights advocacy, played a central role in synthesizing recommendations. The Center for World Indigenous Studies identifies him as principal coordinator and final drafter, while emphasizing that the proposals originated with caravan participants, Indigenous authors, tribal leaders, and others who had contributed ideas. That distinction is crucial. The document was not simply a personal essay by Adams, nor should it be imagined as the formally ratified constitutional position of every Indigenous nation in the United States. It was a coalition manifesto designed to advance negotiations and organize political discussion across communities whose views could vary considerably.

The paper emerged from workshops during the week ending October 27, 1972, and is commonly dated October 1972. Its full title described a demand for renewal of contracts, reconstruction of Indigenous communities, and a secure Indigenous future. The language of contract and treaty made political status the foundation of material improvement. Roads, housing, schools, and employment mattered, but the authors argued that those issues could not be resolved durably while the United States held unilateral control over the decisions shaping Indigenous life. The manifesto therefore placed constitutional and treaty proposals before the more familiar list of social-service deficiencies. This was a deliberate arrangement of argument, not evidence that people lacked immediate concerns about poverty.

The Twenty Points should be read as twenty related recommendations, not twenty identical claims. Some proposed changes to existing federal statutes; others called for new commissions, altered judicial authority, or reorganization of congressional oversight. Some would have required long transitions and extensive negotiation with tribal governments, states, and federal agencies. Still others, such as improved health, education, and housing, were goals shared by broad segments of Indigenous political life but could be pursued through competing institutional models. A textbook should distinguish the policy mechanisms from the moral purposes they were intended to serve. The document reveals what the coalition was asking the federal government to consider, not what Congress had already agreed to do.

Its preamble presented the historical record as a series of failed promises and denials of nationhood. The authors wanted the federal government to acknowledge Indigenous nations as continuing political communities and to accept responsibility for violations of its prior agreements. By framing the relationship as one requiring reconstruction, the paper rejected the idea that marginal changes in bureaucratic procedure would be sufficient. Yet there were differences within that vision. Some proposals asserted the autonomy of particular nations, while others imagined national-level Indian institutions or referendums that would coordinate across them. Students should notice the potential tension between a united national movement and the authority of each Indigenous nation to speak for itself.

The workshop setting also helps clarify the role of time in the manifesto. Organizers were preparing for a presidential-election moment, but their proposed institutions could not be created by one meeting or executive promise. Adams later explained in negotiations that the Washington visit was intended as the beginning of an educational process among Indigenous communities and federal authorities. The Twenty Points should therefore be evaluated at two levels: as a program of long-term structural change and as an immediate political document that gave the caravan something definite to present. Its concrete demands made the coalition more accountable than a protest whose sole request was attention.

Section 111.6

Treaties, Constitutional Authority, and Indigenous Nationhood: Points 1–9

These points proposed restoring federal treaty-making, creating treaty commissions, examining violations, reviewing unratified treaties, establishing judicial remedies, and reforming congressional oversight. The legal distinction is essential: Congress ended new treaty-making in 1871 but did not invalidate existing treaties. The activists sought both renewed treaty-making and stronger enforcement of existing commitments.

Point 1 called for restoration of treaty-making authority by repealing the 1871 provision that ended the federal government's practice of negotiating new treaties with Indigenous nations. The original provision had appeared in an appropriations act; it did not erase treaties already concluded, even though later federal conduct could undermine their performance. Reopening treaty-making would have been politically significant because a treaty is an agreement between governments. Its proposed restoration asserted that Indigenous nations should again occupy a recognized position as parties to formal commitments rather than solely as recipients of administrative benefits. The legal feasibility and scope of such a change required congressional action, not merely a presidential expression of sympathy.

Point 2 envisioned a treaty commission to negotiate a new security-and-assistance arrangement for the remainder of the twentieth century. This proposal connected the authority to make agreements with a process for deciding their content. It suggested that an enduring national settlement could not be crafted without Indigenous participation in its design. Point 3 went further in questions of representation, proposing an address to the American people and a joint session of Congress by four Indigenous representatives selected by Indigenous people. The demand was significant because it challenged the convention that federal officials could adequately summarize Indigenous opinions while speaking on their behalf. Selecting the representatives would itself have raised questions about constituencies and political legitimacy.

Point 4 proposed a commission including Indigenous and non-Indigenous members to review treaty obligations and alleged violations, with recommendations for remedies. This was a response to the problem of unfulfilled commitments accumulating across generations without a regular, trusted forum for review. Point 5 concerned treaties negotiated in the past but never ratified by the Senate. The authors called for reconsideration where those proposed agreements were not already made irrelevant by subsequent events. Such a process would have required detailed historical and legal evaluation rather than a blanket determination that every proposed text remained binding. The manifesto asserted the importance of historical commitments while proposing a new mechanism for examining their status.

Point 6 sought congressional recognition that all Indigenous people stood within treaty relations, a broad political formulation that raised difficult questions about differing historical relationships and federally recognized status. The authors were asserting that federal obligations should not be made to disappear through administrative exclusions. Yet not every community had signed a treaty with the United States, and Indigenous peoples experienced very different legal histories. A careful reading distinguishes the manifesto's normative principle from the more complex legal record. The proposal is instructive precisely because it shows how a national movement could use treaty language to demand inclusion without resolving every question of nation-specific legal authority.

Points 7 and 8 addressed courts. Point 7 called for mandatory judicial relief against treaty violations through federal legislation. Point 8 proposed procedural and jurisdictional reforms recognizing Indigenous rights to interpret their own treaties and governmental interests in litigation. These proposals arose from frustration with costly and slow litigation in which courts often relied heavily on non-Indigenous legal conventions. Even where treaty protections existed, obtaining a remedy could require years of legal work and uncertain funding. The authors wanted an enforcement system that made violations easier to challenge and Indigenous interpretations harder to dismiss. Such changes would have required careful attention to federal jurisdiction, the roles of tribal courts, and the independence of the judiciary.

Point 9 proposed a joint congressional committee focused on reconstructing federal–Indigenous relations, concentrating jurisdiction that was otherwise scattered across multiple committees. This was a demand about the architecture of federal legislative power. When many committees exercise authority over appropriations, land, education, courts, and administration, responsibility can become diffuse. A specialized committee might improve coordination and accountability but could also concentrate decision-making within Congress rather than among Indigenous nations. The manifesto's institutional proposals should therefore be evaluated for both their promise and their limits. The section as a whole shows that treaty renewal was not a purely symbolic demand: it required negotiated agreements, a new review process, a voice in Congress, court remedies, and legislative oversight.

Section 111.7

Land, Termination, Membership, and Jurisdiction: Points 10–13

These points proposed restoring a 110-million-acre Indigenous land base, consolidating land and resource rights, reversing termination, protecting tribal membership authority, opposing Public Law 280, and expanding legal protections. Treaty enforcement was thereby linked to territorial jurisdiction, economic independence, and the recovery of political authority.

Point 10 proposed rebuilding an Indigenous land base reaching approximately 110 million acres under Indigenous ownership and control. It was one of the manifesto's most ambitious demands and should be read as a proposal for land reform rather than a claim that this area was actually restored. The authors envisioned priorities for returning lands, consolidating resources, and reducing arrangements that allowed non-Indigenous interests to control land through leases, condemnation, or other mechanisms. Their language tied land to political survival: governments without meaningful territorial resources could have nominal sovereignty but little capacity to exercise it. The proposal must be placed beside the history of allotment, land sales, resource development, and termination described in earlier chapters.

The reference to 110 million acres also raises questions of evidence and implementation. Which lands were being identified, through what process would they be transferred, and what claims might overlap? Different Indigenous nations possessed distinct treaties and territorial histories; a nationwide figure could frame a negotiating goal without providing a cadastral map or a lawful method for every transfer. Students should not assume that the paper settled all competing claims or proposed the same land tenure structure for each nation. The real historical importance lies in the scale of the proposal and in its insistence that economic development must rest on Indigenous-controlled land and resources rather than perpetual dependence on external approval.

Point 10 also demanded repeal of termination measures affecting nations such as the Menominee and Klamath. The authors regarded termination not as evidence of completed equality but as a means of dissolving political relationships without adequate consent. Reversing termination required more than restoring a name to a federal list. Governments would have to address land ownership, jurisdiction, funding, and the political institutions damaged or disrupted by prior legislation. The campaign's argument therefore linked a critique of federal policy from the 1950s with the practical tasks of reconstructing contemporary communities. Restoration of a legal relationship was a necessary step, but not necessarily a complete economic remedy.

Point 11 addressed tribal membership authority and the federal rules that could deprive people of rights or benefits. It challenged externally imposed enrollment restrictions, the effects of blood-quantum classifications, and prohibitions on obtaining certain benefits across multiple tribal relationships. The underlying sovereignty claim was that nations should determine their own membership through their own legitimate political institutions. At the same time, belonging is a sensitive matter in communities with distinct histories, family lines, and governing laws. A national manifesto could demand an end to federal restrictions without defining the appropriate membership rule for each nation. This distinction keeps the chapter from converting an assertion of self-government into another universal rule imposed from outside.

Point 12 demanded reversal of state laws enacted under Public Law 280 that had expanded state criminal and limited civil jurisdiction in portions of Indian country. The proposal addressed everyday experiences of state policing and courts as well as the legal standing of tribal governments. Supporters believed that externally imposed jurisdiction weakened community accountability and treaty-based relations. Yet the distribution of authority differed by state and by nation, and any transition would require provisions for criminal enforcement and civil adjudication. The chapter should explain that the manifesto opposed unwanted state jurisdiction without suggesting that all communities sought identical court structures or that repeal could resolve every public-safety question automatically.

Point 13 proposed renewed federal protection of Indigenous people against offenses, including a national Indigenous federal grand-jury mechanism, attention to crimes committed by non-Indians in reservation communities, and accelerated rehabilitation or release programs for Indigenous prisoners. These proposals demonstrate that advocates did not think sovereignty could be separated from practical questions of safety and justice. They also reflected the wider Indigenous critique of imprisonment and unequal treatment in federal and state courts. The land and jurisdiction points together were an effort to rebuild the conditions under which Indigenous governments could function, not simply to increase spending. They asked who controlled territory, who belonged to a nation, who adjudicated conflicts, and who could be held legally accountable.

Section 111.8

Replacing the BIA and Reconstructing Federal–Indigenous Relations: Points 14–20

The paper did not merely ask Washington to improve the BIA. It called for abolishing the existing bureau by 1976 and replacing it with an Office of Federal Indian Relations and Community Reconstruction. Further points addressed taxation, commerce, religious freedom, community decision-making, education, housing, health, employment, and economic development.

Point 14 called for abolishing the existing Bureau of Indian Affairs by July 4, 1976, and replacing it through a new Indian Reconstruction Act. Point 15 proposed an Office of Federal Indian Relations and Community Reconstruction. The pairing matters: the authors did not suggest that federal responsibilities should disappear along with the agency. They proposed to redesign the structure through which those responsibilities were carried out. This was an institutional challenge to the BIA's long history as a bureau that administered land, education, services, and regulations affecting Indigenous nations. The target date gave the proposal a political horizon rather than leaving reconstruction as an undefined aspiration.

Point 16 explained the purpose of the proposed office: to repair what the paper characterized as a breakdown in constitutionally prescribed relations between the United States and Indigenous nations. The language treated the problem as one of government-to-government legitimacy. Its implication was that consultation could not substitute for meaningful authority, and that a successor institution should be evaluated by how much control Indigenous communities exercised over its decisions. But any successor would have required careful design. Congress would have had to establish legal powers, allocate funding, transfer records, protect land interests, and prevent interruptions of essential services. The manifesto offered a political program; it was not itself a complete administrative transition manual.

Point 17 addressed commerce and tax immunities, seeking statutory protections against state interference in Indigenous trade, transportation, and economic activity. This proposal linked the right to govern with the capacity to sustain livelihoods. Tribal businesses and families often operated in environments where questions of state taxation, jurisdiction, and licensing were unsettled or contested. The authors wanted federal law to clarify Indigenous authority and prevent states from undermining it. Yet different commercial activities took place on and off reservation land, with implications for non-Indigenous customers and neighboring communities. A serious policy analysis must distinguish the manifesto's broad purpose from the details legislators and courts would have been required to resolve.

Point 18 sought enforceable religious freedom and cultural integrity. This demand addressed ceremonies, sacred sites, objects, burial grounds, and other forms of community life that had been subject to suppression or official disregard. Indigenous political identity in 1972 could not be reduced to spiritual practice, but cultural and religious freedom were central to many participants' understanding of autonomy. The proposal anticipated discussions that later became important in federal legislation, although the chapter must not claim that one caravan alone caused those laws. Different nations held different ceremonial responsibilities, so a useful legal framework had to protect freedom without defining a single standardized Indigenous religion.

Point 19 imagined referendums, local options, and other elective procedures through which Indigenous people could participate in decisions affecting shared resources and institutions. It raised a democratic problem internal to the movement's own ambitions: how could an intertribal program secure consent from the nations it purported to benefit? A national vote might demonstrate solidarity, but it could also conflict with the distinct political authorities and constitutions of individual Indigenous governments. The proposal is therefore valuable not only for its promise of participation but also for the questions it leaves open about representation, membership, and the legitimate scale of collective decision-making.

Point 20 returned explicitly to health, housing, education, employment, and economic development. In the authors' reasoning, these were obligations to be met through creative Indigenous community initiatives, not merely services to be delivered through established bureaucratic routines. The placement at the end of the manifesto was deliberate: constitutional change, land, justice, and institutional reconstruction were presented as the foundations for durable material improvements. The authors sought a government relationship in which resources were used for purposes determined by Indigenous communities, with financial responsibility and political accountability.

The debate over replacement versus reform of the BIA should not be framed as a contest between people who valued services and people who did not. Both sides could recognize the necessity of keeping records, financing schools, and protecting trust lands. They disagreed over the institutional authority capable of doing so legitimately. The Twenty Points challenged critics of radical restructuring to explain how an agency with a history of paternalism could become accountable; they challenged supporters to demonstrate how an alternative would avoid administrative collapse or new concentration of power. That tension made the manifesto a serious program for political debate rather than a list of slogans.

Section 111.9

The Caravan Arrives in Washington, November 1–2, 1972

The main caravan reached Washington on November 1. Lodging proved inadequate, meetings were canceled or disputed, and an October 11 federal instruction had restricted BIA assistance. That instruction helps explain why logistical problems were read as political rejection. A planned ceremony honoring Indigenous veterans also became disputed; accounts differ between Arlington National Cemetery and the nearby Arlington Ridge memorial area, so students should identify the actual site and the responsible authority in each source.

The arrival of the main caravan in Washington on November 1 brought the organizers into the political terrain they had spent weeks preparing to enter. Participants assembled at St. Stephen and the Incarnation Episcopal Church and elsewhere while arrangements for meetings, transportation, and accommodations were still uncertain. Accounts differ on precisely when particular groups arrived, which is unsurprising for travelers reaching the capital in several vehicles and from several routes. The National Park Service uses November 2 as the campaign's arrival date in its narrative of the occupation; the Episcopal report identifies a main-body arrival on November 1. The dates should describe different stages rather than be forced into a single supposedly universal moment.

Organizers had anticipated an orderly delegation to offices and political leaders. They wanted the Twenty-Point Position Paper to be received and discussed by people with authority to act, and they planned a ceremony honoring Indigenous military veterans. The Nixon administration was not prepared to offer all the meetings requested. A letter from Leonard Garment to Dennis Banks had already indicated that the president would not personally meet the caravan, although an administration representative would be available. Arrangements with other departments also became uncertain. Participants who had traveled across the country reasonably interpreted these developments in the context of longstanding experiences of being denied an effective hearing by federal agencies.

A further restriction complicated matters before they arrived. The February 1973 Episcopal report reproduces the substance of an October 11 memorandum from Assistant Secretary Harrison Loesch to BIA Commissioner Louis Bruce directing the bureau not to supply assistance or funding to the caravan. It also describes Bruce's October 12 telegram transmitting the instruction to field offices. This account is supported by identifiable correspondence cited in the report, but the present chapter should not claim that every manuscript original has been personally inspected. The practical effect was to constrain the very agency to which visiting Indigenous delegations often turned for help arranging meetings and lodging.

Poor accommodation proved to be more than a discomfort. The National Park Service reports that the travelers encountered inadequate space in a church building, which made it difficult to house a large group that included older adults and children. The mismatch exposed both planning limits within the coalition and the federal decision to avoid logistical responsibility. Neither factor alone explains the occupation. Activists had made political commitments they believed required a visible audience; the federal government possessed buildings and administrative resources it declined to deploy as the visitors expected. By November 2, the BIA's auditorium became a focal point where the unresolved questions of housing and political recognition converged.

The planned military-veterans ceremony added another dispute. The National Park Service describes a proposed gathering at Arlington Ridge Park near the Marine Corps War Memorial, where Ira Hayes is commemorated; the Episcopal report refers to permission to hold ceremonies at Arlington National Cemetery. These may reflect different descriptions of connected plans or distinct stages of an application, but the records should not simply be merged. The dispute concerned permission for an Indigenous ceremony in a highly symbolic federal commemorative space. Participants interpreted denial through the history of Indigenous military service and the contradiction of honoring Indigenous soldiers while resisting Indigenous political claims.

These circumstances did not make a building occupation inevitable. Negotiators could have found alternate rooms, delayed meetings, or returned to the caravan's broader organizing goals. Yet participants were under immediate pressure to protect travelers, preserve the campaign's credibility, and make clear that the Twenty Points would not vanish into an ignored correspondence file. The events of November 1 and 2 show a change in strategic conditions rather than a sudden abandonment of the original program. The decisions made in the following hours turned a demand for an official audience into a direct confrontation over the use of a federal building.

Section 111.10

November 2–3: From Delegation to Occupation of the BIA

The National Park Service dates the initial sit-in to November 2, with barricades appearing as demonstrators resisted removal; a February 1973 report describes more entrenched occupation by November 3. These accounts can be reconciled without assuming that every participant experienced the same sequence. Participants called the building a Native American embassy, which set competing political and legal claims against federal authority over government property.

The initial gathering at the BIA should be understood as a shifting sequence. On November 2, hundreds of participants assembled in the building, initially demanding assistance and a hearing. According to the Episcopal report, Interior negotiators and caravan leaders briefly agreed that people might remain overnight. The National Park Service describes a planned eviction that triggered a confrontation at the entrance, after which demonstrators barricaded doors with furniture. These accounts emphasize different aspects of the same breakdown, and neither can supply a minute-by-minute experience for everyone present. An accurate narrative should specify which account supports each turning point and avoid inventing dialogue between unnamed officials and participants.

As barricades went up, the building changed political meaning. It was a federal headquarters containing offices, records, and administrative equipment, not a vacant public square. But the occupiers increasingly presented it as an Indigenous diplomatic space, displaying a banner identifying a Native American embassy. This was a claim about nationhood directed at the American public: the people inside wanted to be addressed as political representatives whose treaties and governments deserved recognition. The banner did not create a legally recognized embassy under federal law. It made a political demand visible in the very institution that embodied federal supervision of Indigenous affairs.

The number of participants varied across the week and across historical accounts. The Episcopal report refers to approximately five hundred people behind barricades by November 3; the National Park Service describes a larger flow of people over the course of the occupation. Figures should be read as estimates for different moments, not contradictory exact censuses. Crowding affected everything from negotiation to sanitation. The building had corridors, offices, and meeting spaces but had not been designed to house hundreds of people for several days. Officials feared a prolonged standoff; residents had to decide how to remain organized without reliable access to ordinary domestic facilities.

The occupation was also a crisis of political representation. The coalition had convened to present proposals, not to establish a permanent governmental delegation with a fixed voting membership. Some people had authority from their organizations, some spoke as individual activists, and others joined the sit-in as supporters or travelers seeking shelter. Negotiating with federal officials required named representatives, yet the public demonstration depended on a larger group whose expectations could change. Disagreements about whether to maintain barricades or accept a partial settlement could not be solved by simply asking one famous activist to decide. Organizational ambiguity was an important element of the occupation's dynamics.

The federal government's legal position was straightforward in one sense: the building remained federal property, and occupants could not unilaterally acquire authority over it by declaring an embassy. The occupiers' argument operated at a different level, demanding that the United States reckon with a history of broken political agreements and inadequate administrative accountability. The two positions therefore sometimes spoke past one another. Officials discussed evacuation, safety, and control of offices; activists insisted on a political program that could not be reduced to questions of trespass. Successful historical analysis must hold both dimensions in view without treating either the government's property claim or the protesters' political language as sufficient explanation of the events.

The national press quickly found a dramatic story in barricades and threats of force. The demonstration offered powerful images, but those images encouraged shorthand descriptions of militants and a siege. The document that had motivated the journey became less visible than the occupied building. This transformation is critical to the chapter's central question: the occupation gave the coalition leverage and visibility while making it more difficult to keep attention on the twenty specific proposals. The consequences of that trade-off appeared inside the building, in later negotiations, and in the sharply divided reactions of Indigenous organizations after the departure.

Section 111.11

Inside the BIA: Families, Leadership, Food, Security, and Daily Life

Hundreds of participants had to organize sleeping space, food, childcare, medical needs, communication, security, and collective decisions inside the building. Women, elders, youth, organizers, and negotiators all had roles, though the surviving record documents them unevenly.

Living inside the BIA headquarters posed immediate problems of shelter, food, hygiene, and coordination. An office designed for daytime employees did not have the sleeping rooms, bathing facilities, cooking capacity, or medical support needed by a large mixed-age community. Travelers had already endured long journeys and uncertain accommodation. Some could rest on floors or in improvised spaces; others required places suitable for children or older adults. A careful narrative should avoid describing a detailed kitchen, hospital, or childcare center unless a contemporary source documents its existence. The verified circumstance was a crowded, changing occupation whose participants had to improvise to remain together.

Food and water depended on connections outside the barricaded building. Supporters could supply provisions, sympathetic religious institutions could help coordinate assistance, and negotiators could seek temporary accommodation for vulnerable residents. The February 1973 report identifies a decision to move children and older people to rooms at a nearby YMCA during the increasingly tense negotiations. This detail matters because it complicates images of the occupation as a uniformly hardened confrontation: participants and supporters also took steps intended to protect people from deteriorating conditions. Such actions were part of the practical politics of holding a collective demonstration under pressure.

The organizational roles were fluid. Negotiators needed contact with federal officials and the media, while others watched entrances, communicated developments, organized supplies, or tried to maintain order among residents. These functions were necessary but not equivalent to a formally ratified governmental system. Accounts frequently highlight male leaders who gave interviews or attended meetings. Women and younger participants could be responsible for essential logistical and political work without appearing in the same number of published photographs or official memoranda. Where the evidence does not establish named roles, the historian must describe the structural requirement and the limits of identification rather than invent a list of committee chairs.

Living amid government files introduced a further dilemma. Some occupiers believed the records offered evidence of federal mismanagement; others may have been motivated by practical needs, mistrust, or a desire to document treaty and trust claims. Files concerning enrollment, land, water, or economic development had potential importance far beyond the occupied offices. Moving them out of cabinets could create access for activists but also risk loss of order and long-term damage. A historian should distinguish what participants said they discovered from what an independent investigation established. Neither the existence of trust grievances nor the presence of documents in a disrupted office automatically proves a particular allegation about the contents of every file.

The building also produced psychological pressure. Occupiers confronted uncertainty about whether police would enter and whether negotiations would lead to an agreement. Rumors could circulate rapidly in a crowded space, while news reports outside the building sometimes emphasized the possibility of force. Families and elders might weigh the risks differently from younger people prepared to remain on barricades. A coalition that could cooperate during travel confronted a more immediate decision: how to preserve solidarity when the physical safety and freedom of its members appeared to be at stake. This was a form of collective decision-making under coercive circumstances, not an idealized experiment in settled self-government.

The daily experience of the occupation reveals why its outcomes cannot be assessed exclusively through the Twenty Points or a police report. Participants created a temporary community, depended on assistance from people outside it, and discovered how material needs could shape political choices. When a negotiation produced housing or transport assistance, that could be decisive even though it did not settle treaty law. When some residents departed before others, it might reflect concern for family safety, exhaustion, or a strategic decision rather than rejection of the manifesto. The inside history therefore helps explain why the occupation eventually ended through a combination of negotiation, material support, and unresolved political disagreement.

Section 111.12

Documents, Barricades, Media Coverage, and Competing Accounts

News reports described weapons, improvised defenses, damage, and the removal or destruction of documents. Historians must distinguish verified incidents from allegations, and official damage estimates from established losses. Land, enrollment, and treaty-related files could have direct consequences for Indigenous rights, which is why even supporters of the political demands were alarmed by their loss.

The most contested evidence about the BIA occupation concerns damage to property and records. Officials documented vandalism and disruption of offices, and contemporary photographs show broken furniture, scattered materials, and damaged interiors. Government estimates of monetary loss circulated in the press and in subsequent reports, sometimes reaching several million dollars. Such figures should be identified by the office making the estimate, the date, and whether the amount referred to repairs, missing records, equipment, or broader administrative costs. An estimate published during a politically charged dispute is evidence of an official assessment; it should not automatically become an independently verified final accounting.

The files matter because the BIA held records on matters of continuing consequence: land ownership and leases, water and resource rights, tribal enrollment, federal programs, and economic development. For many Indigenous communities, those documents were neither merely symbolic government paper nor replaceable office supplies. Their organization and survival could influence the ability of a nation to demonstrate a legal claim or administer a program. This helps explain why some tribal leaders condemned damage while endorsing the substantive grievances expressed in the Twenty Points. Opposition to destruction of records was not necessarily support for paternalistic federal administration; it could be defense of Indigenous interests dependent upon those records.

Accounts by occupiers and supporters introduced a different emphasis. Participants described encountering evidence of questionable federal administration of trust resources and argued that access to records revealed problems that official channels had obscured. Such allegations are historically important because they help explain the occupiers' conduct and political justification. But the fact that a participant reported finding incriminating material does not establish the contents, custody, or authenticity of each document. The chapter should distinguish an allegation made during the occupation from a finding supported by later audits or court proceedings. These evidentiary categories should be visible to students rather than quietly collapsed into a single authoritative narrative.

National journalists often gave priority to the most visually striking scenes. Barricades, damaged desks, and an official building under occupation could be represented quickly in a photograph or television report. The legal questions of treaty enforcement and jurisdiction were more difficult to explain in a short news segment. This imbalance was not entirely the journalists' invention: the occupation was genuinely disruptive, and officials had reasons to report damage and dangers. Nevertheless, the emphasis influenced how the general public encountered the campaign. The Twenty Points could become secondary even for readers who had initially been interested in the caravan's peaceful organizing work.

Contemporary religious and Indigenous organizational responses complicate a binary contrast between government denunciations and activist celebration. The Episcopal Church's near-contemporary report criticized the destruction and stated that the campaign had failed in some of its stated objectives, while also arguing that it forced serious consideration of federal policy. The NCAI's Charles Trimble expressed outrage about lost files and articulated substantial political common ground with caravan organizers. These interpretations demonstrate that support for sovereignty was compatible with sharp disagreement over protest methods. A responsible chapter must give these critiques intellectual weight rather than depicting them as evidence of insufficient commitment to Indigenous rights.

Historians should compare different kinds of sources with different evidentiary strengths. Photographs can establish visible damage but not always the person responsible. Inventory reports can estimate losses but may require later reconciliation. Participant recollections can reveal motivations but may be shaped by subsequent controversy. Newspaper reports show public framing as well as events. Archival finding aids show that survey teams and correspondence existed, but cannot by themselves establish the conclusions of every document in a collection. Bringing these sources together allows a defensible account of the occupation's costs without manufacturing certainty or denying the importance of genuine political grievances.

Division IV · §§111.13–111.16

Federal Response, Negotiations, and the End of the Occupation

Officials, political demands, legal action, and settlement

Section 111.13

The Nixon Administration, the Interior Department, and the BIA

Officials involved included Interior Secretary Rogers C. B. Morton, BIA Commissioner Louis Bruce, Assistant Secretary Harrison Loesch, White House adviser Leonard Garment, and Office of Management and Budget official Frank Carlucci. The BIA, Interior Department, White House, courts, and police did not necessarily share one strategy or voice.

The federal response to the occupation was not the work of one undifferentiated authority. The Bureau of Indian Affairs administered many of the programs and records at issue, but the Interior Department supervised the bureau, the White House managed its own political relations, and courts and law-enforcement agencies addressed questions of legality and safety. Understanding these distinctions is essential. BIA Commissioner Louis Bruce could favor one course while an assistant secretary or the Interior secretary imposed another; a negotiator sent by the White House could promise consideration of demands without possessing authority to enact congressional legislation. The apparent inconsistency of federal messages reflected institutional division as well as the speed of the crisis.

Harrison Loesch's October 11 instruction to Louis Bruce is a particularly clear example of bureaucratic power. The near-contemporary Episcopal report identifies Loesch as Assistant Secretary for Public Land Management and cites a surviving copy of his directive not to provide caravan assistance. Bruce then sent instructions through BIA channels. These actions constrained an agency whose normal contacts with Indigenous delegations might have helped arrange meetings or accommodation. They also placed Bruce in a difficult position when activists arrived seeking help. The episode shows that an administrative directive about resources could have major political consequences, even when its words did not explicitly address treaty policy.

Interior Secretary Rogers C. B. Morton operated above the BIA in the departmental hierarchy. The Episcopal report states that he ordered Bruce to stay out of the building during negotiations, limiting the commissioner's direct role. Such intervention demonstrates why institutional titles alone do not explain who controlled a decision. A commissioner who oversaw Indian affairs could be excluded from talks about the building his agency occupied. Senior officials also had to consider preservation of federal property, the ability of employees to resume work, and the danger that forcible removal might cause injuries or a larger political crisis. These concerns were real but did not settle the underlying question of how Indigenous demands would be heard.

At the White House level, Leonard Garment provided a channel for political contact, while Frank Carlucci of the Office of Management and Budget participated in later talks. Their presence mattered because the immediate dispute could no longer be managed as a routine matter of office security. The caravan's demands involved presidential initiatives, legislative proposals, and interagency coordination. OMB's involvement emphasized the relation between promised reforms and the resources needed to carry them out. Yet it would be misleading to suggest that the participation of high-ranking officials meant the White House accepted the manifesto. They could negotiate conditions for departure and establish a review process without committing Congress or federal courts to the proposed changes.

The federal government also confronted a public-relations problem. Nixon had previously announced a policy of self-determination and could not easily depict all Indigenous demands as illegitimate without contradicting that rhetoric. On the other hand, officials were unwilling to surrender control of the headquarters or to appear to reward destruction. They sought a way to end the occupation without a violent confrontation while maintaining that federal property rules applied. This tension helps explain the eventual emphasis on an interagency task force and practical assistance for travelers. Such mechanisms could offer a path out of the crisis while postponing the deeper arguments about treaties, land, and jurisdiction.

For historians, the response illustrates the importance of comparing departmental correspondence, statements to newspapers, the record of judicial proceedings, and the participants' descriptions of meetings. Each institution left a different paper trail and had reasons to emphasize its own authority. The completed chapter should avoid inventing private conversations among Morton, Bruce, Garment, or Carlucci. What the records support is a fragmented federal response that eventually converged around negotiation, limited commitments, and departure—not a unified acceptance or rejection of all Twenty Points at a single meeting.

Section 111.14

Hank Adams and the Negotiations over Political Demands

Adams negotiated with federal officials, including Garment and Carlucci. The Twenty Points were accompanied by nine supplementary demands, and discussions included an audit of Johnson–O'Malley education funds. Immediate negotiations to end the occupation must be distinguished from longer-term proposals needing legislation or restructuring.

Hank Adams's role in the final negotiations reflected his prior work coordinating the caravan's principal document. A negotiator who understood the Twenty Points could distinguish the long-term legal demands from practical terms capable of ending the occupation. The Episcopal report identifies Adams as a representative named by caravan participants in talks involving Leonard Garment and Frank Carlucci. This was an exercise in political translation under pressure. Officials wanted a settlement they could implement quickly; the coalition wanted assurance that months of organizing had produced something more substantial than a return ticket home. Agreement was difficult partly because the two sides were measuring success on different time scales.

During the occupation, demonstrators adopted nine supplementary demands. The near-contemporary Episcopal report reproduces them from a November 3 document cited in the National Committee on Indian Work files. They began by pressing for executive and legislative action on the Twenty Points, then addressed specific federal personnel, including Deputy Commissioner John Crow, Assistant Secretary Harrison Loesch, and BIA Commissioner Louis Bruce. One demand sought to restore Bruce's authority during implementation. These requests were linked to how participants interpreted the officials' earlier handling of the caravan. The personnel questions were not the same as the manifesto's long-term proposal to replace the BIA altogether.

Other supplementary demands responded directly to the occupants' circumstances: adequate temporary housing, fulfillment of stated commitments involving Johnson–O'Malley educational funds, better services for urban and landless Indigenous people, and the return of ancestral remains and cultural objects for reburial. A final demand called for replacement of National Council on Indian Opportunity director Robert Robertson with an Indigenous person. In paraphrasing the list, the chapter must preserve its specificity without reproducing the report's apparent transcription error in the name of the Johnson–O'Malley program. These demands show how the crisis connected immediate accommodation, administrative authority, cultural rights, and continuing federal responsibilities.

The difference between the Twenty Points and the Nine Demands matters analytically. The former proposed a reconstructed political relationship over years, involving commissions, legislation, courts, land, and new institutions. The latter combined insistence on the broader program with requests generated by conflict in Washington. Accepting a hotel room would not amount to restoring treaty-making authority. Nor would agreement to review the manifesto mean that a new office had been established. Leaders had to explain these distinctions to participants whose personal circumstances might make an immediate improvement decisive and to critics who would measure the campaign against its most ambitious public statements.

According to the Episcopal report, Adams and other representatives emphasized that the caravan had been intended as the beginning of a broader educational and political process. This retrospective explanation, made shortly after the events, describes the takeover as an obstacle that had emerged rather than as the original strategic goal. It should be treated as a participant negotiating position, not an objective fact about every individual's intentions. Even so, it is consistent with the recorded planning statements calling for orderly visits and hearings. The point is historically important because it challenges the assumption that all previous travel and drafting had been preparation for an occupation.

The talks also concerned an audit of Johnson–O'Malley educational funds. This was an example of a demand more concrete than a general pledge to improve policy yet narrower than a total reconstruction of federal administration. It involved questions of financial accountability, federal obligations, and the practical operation of Indigenous education. The final agreement emerged from the search for such achievable interim commitments, alongside broader promises to examine federal policy. A careful account must identify which commitments were announced, which required further administrative steps, and which remained proposals with no established implementation in November 1972.

Section 111.15

Court Orders, Religious Freedom, and the Limits of Federal Authority

Restraining orders, eviction disputes, and a challenge to restrictions on the veterans' ceremony ran alongside negotiations. Participants framed their actions through religious freedom, treaty obligations, and sovereignty, while officials invoked ordinary federal-property and public-order authority.

The occupation raised questions of federal property law and public order at the same time as it generated litigation over Indigenous religious expression. A federal building can be opened for public business without becoming a place that any group may control indefinitely. Officials relied on that distinction when seeking eviction and restoration of normal operations. The occupiers, by contrast, argued that the government could not invoke neutral office rules while refusing to recognize the political rights of the nations whose affairs were administered there. These were different kinds of claims: one concerned control of premises, the other the legitimacy of the federal–Indigenous relationship. Courts could address particular injunctions without resolving every underlying treaty dispute.

The religious-ceremony controversy added another dimension. Some participants intended to honor Indigenous veterans near prominent military memorials at Arlington. The National Park Service describes Arlington Ridge Park and the Marine Corps War Memorial; the Episcopal report refers to Arlington National Cemetery and a legal challenge to the denial of permission. Rather than flattening these descriptions, the chapter should identify the physical location named in each source and the authority responsible for granting access. The discrepancy matters because different sites may be governed by different agencies or rules. It is not evidence that the veterans' ceremony was unimportant or that one account is necessarily fabricated.

The Episcopal report identifies litigation by Daniel Satiacum and others against Secretary of Defense Melvin Laird and reports that an appellate court reversed a lower-court restriction on the proposed religious ceremony. The legal record should be cited specifically and the disposition described no more broadly than the available documents support. A court's intervention in an access dispute would not establish that Indigenous nations had won all their treaty demands, just as a property injunction would not determine the merit of the Twenty-Point Position Paper. The distinction between temporary judicial relief and underlying sovereignty questions is a useful way to teach the limits of litigation during political protest.

The prospect of removal created additional tension among negotiators. Police intervention could reestablish access to offices but might injure demonstrators, employees, or bystanders. Continued occupation threatened documents and facilities while making it difficult to arrange the originally requested hearings. The existence of legal authority to order removal did not necessarily make immediate forcible action the most prudent choice. Similarly, willingness to discuss a task force did not mean that federal officials surrendered control of the building. This interaction among courts, police, executive departments, and participants shaped the practical choices available during the final days.

The treatment of legal issues must be symmetrical in its evidentiary standards. A movement's claim to religious freedom should be analyzed within the applicable law and the actual permit dispute, not simply accepted as a blanket exemption from public rules. An official allegation of criminal behavior should likewise be supported by evidence and distinguished from a later conviction or finding. Court orders have precise parties, dates, and scopes; they should not be paraphrased as proof that one side prevailed on every issue. The immediate litigation shows a conflict over access and authority unfolding alongside a much larger political campaign, not a comprehensive judicial resolution of that campaign.

Section 111.16

November 8, 1972: The Agreement and Departure

The final agreement established a federal interagency task force and a commitment to review Indian policy, with transportation assistance for departure; the last participants left on November 8. The settlement was neither an unconditional victory nor a capitulation: the participants secured direct negotiations and review commitments, but not enactment of the Twenty Points.

By November 7, departures from the occupied building had begun, and the final participants left by the evening of November 8. The immediate settlement emerged from negotiations involving Hank Adams and federal representatives, with others working to manage accommodation and transport. The Episcopal report identifies an agreement to establish a special federal interagency task force to review Indian policy and needs, with a process intended to receive views from a broad range of Indigenous communities. It also reports arrangements for examining educational funding. The National Park Service additionally describes return-travel funding and assurances concerning prosecution. These were concrete parts of an exit strategy, but their scope and long-term implementation must be stated with precision.

A task force was not the treaty commission proposed in Point 2, nor the successor government office envisioned in Points 14 and 15. It was a federal review mechanism created in response to immediate political pressure. The difference could seem small in a newspaper account that described both as government action, but it was fundamental to the manifesto's authors. A new commission to negotiate treaties or restore land would require political authority and potentially legislation. A task force could receive submissions and recommend changes, but could not by its mere existence bind Congress to repeal termination laws or transfer 110 million acres. Students should learn to distinguish an administrative promise from an enforceable policy outcome.

Transport assistance also deserves attention. Hundreds of travelers were far from home after weeks on the road, and some lacked the means to return. A settlement that supplied transportation or resources could help end a dangerous standoff without settling the wider political disagreement. The National Park Service gives an approximate figure of $66,500 for return travel; this should be attributed to its source rather than presented as a universally verified accounting of every payment. Critics interpreted assistance as a government payment for ending an occupation; organizers could interpret it as necessary support for participants whose journey had been frustrated. The same practical measure could therefore carry different political meanings.

The final negotiations did not amount to an unconditional endorsement of the occupiers' actions. Federal officials continued to assert the right to control the building and to condemn damage, while participants maintained that their grievances and proposals deserved serious consideration. Public statements at the end could emphasize either compromise or victory depending on which result the speaker valued. The documentary record supports the conclusion that negotiators secured access and policy review commitments while leaving the broader program unimplemented. It does not support saying that the federal government accepted all Twenty Points or that Indigenous activists abandoned them.

The timing of the departure was also politically consequential. The presidential election occurred while the occupation was still unfolding, and the attention of officials and journalists was divided among national concerns. Returning home did not mean the coalition had become a permanent, unified political organization capable of directing all subsequent campaigns. Members reentered their home communities, where local treaty disputes, organizational disagreements, and economic pressures remained. Some continued working with AIM and other groups; others took different paths. The Trail's national moment ended, but its written demands could circulate long after the physical occupation was over.

A useful final measure of the settlement is to ask what changed immediately, what was promised, and what remained unresolved. The building was returned to federal control and travelers received assistance to leave. Federal officials committed to review policy through an interagency process. Indigenous organizations continued to debate the tactics and costs of the occupation. The manifesto's proposed treaty mechanisms, jurisdictional rules, land reform, and successor agency did not become law in November 1972. Understanding those categories prepares students to assess the aftermath without accepting either a triumphalist or a purely dismissive account.

Indigenous Reactions, Political Consequences, and Historical Legacy

Section 111.17

The Aftermath: Damage, Records, Public Opinion, and Federal Investigation

Official accounts of damage and missing documents competed with activists' accounts and with the concerns of tribal governments, BIA employees, and communities whose records were affected. Claims reported in 1972–1973 should be kept distinct from independently verified findings.

The aftermath was shaped by evidence of disruption and by sharply differing attempts to interpret it. Federal officials publicized damage to the BIA headquarters, missing or disturbed files, and the cost of restoring offices to use. The Episcopal report records an Interior estimate of approximately $2.3 million. That number belongs to the history of official responses, but it should not be treated as an independently audited final figure without the underlying inventory and accounting. Contemporary statements also differed about the nature and extent of documents removed. The existence of damaged offices is not in doubt; precise responsibility for individual losses and the long-term consequences for particular Indigenous communities require more specific records.

For tribal governments, the issue of destroyed records could be urgent. Enrollment files, land and water claims, resource leases, and program accounts were not simply agency property. They could contain evidence needed to protect families' interests or pursue treaty rights. A government that wanted the BIA replaced could still depend on its records while a successor system was being designed. This practical concern helps explain the force of Indigenous criticism after the occupation. It also complicates the suggestion that condemnation of damage was equivalent to support for every aspect of federal administration. A person could demand reconstruction of the system while insisting that documentary evidence be preserved.

The BIA's own operations became a subject of inquiry. The Episcopal report cites a letter from Commissioner Louis Bruce to NCAI executive director Charles Trimble requesting a representative impact survey. The Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian now catalogs files from the NCAI Trail of Broken Treaties Impact Survey Team, documenting a serious organizational effort to assess consequences. A finding aid identifies the existence and broad subjects of those files; it should not be mistaken for an inspection of each individual report. The archive nevertheless offers a route for testing claims about records, damages, and Indigenous institutional reactions through contemporary documents rather than later summaries alone.

National reporting often emphasized the occupation's cost and the apparent contradiction between a sovereignty program and damage to records affecting Indigenous interests. Activists responded that the confrontation had exposed the neglect and unaccountability of federal agencies. Both arguments could contain important truths without being complete accounts. Historical judgment requires attention to the campaign's earlier months, the substance of the Twenty Points, and the experiences of people inside the building. Only then can the destruction and the settlement be understood as parts of a political sequence rather than as substitutes for that sequence.

The immediate effect was reputational as well as administrative. Some organizations worried that an image of disorder would weaken public support and make substantive federal reform more difficult. Others thought that a large-scale challenge to the BIA had forced officials to take demands seriously after years of delay. Neither interpretation should be assumed to represent every Indigenous person. The aftermath was also uneven across communities: some organizers pursued national alliances, while tribal governments continued working on particular land, education, and resource matters with or without the caravan coalition. The Trail left a new body of documents and disagreements that became part of the political record of the 1970s.

Section 111.18

Indigenous Disagreement: NCAI, Tribal Leaders, and the Caravan Coalition

Indigenous reactions did not divide neatly into conservative officials and radical activists. In a November 10, 1972 statement, NCAI executive director Charles Trimble rejected property destruction and the loss of records while acknowledging substantial common ground with the campaign's demands. The NCAI Trail of Broken Treaties Impact Survey Team records at the National Museum of the American Indian preserve institutional Indigenous responses beyond AIM's own accounts.

Charles Trimble's November 10, 1972 statement for the National Congress of American Indians is especially valuable because it refuses a simple division between supporters and opponents of Indigenous sovereignty. The statement denounced disruption and damage, particularly the destruction or loss of records concerning land, water, enrollment, economic development, and legal matters. At the same time, Trimble recognized that NCAI and the caravan organizations traveled along substantial portions of the same political path and might agree with many elements of the Twenty-Point program. The statement shows an institutional leader assessing tactics and policy demands separately, even at a moment of public controversy.

The NCAI was organized around member tribal governments and established advocacy relationships. Its officers had reason to value records, legislative access, and the ability to maintain working contacts with federal officials. Caravan participants sought to challenge a relationship they believed had been too deferential or ineffective. Those approaches were not necessarily incompatible. Both could seek preservation of treaties and federal accountability, but they placed different weight on disruption as a tactic. The statement thus provides a way to teach political disagreement within Indigenous movements without describing one side as authentic and the other as compromised.

The Episcopal report also records concern from Webster Two Hawk of the National Tribal Leaders Association and a response from Navajo chairman Peter MacDonald, who emphasized that the grievances raised crossed the supposed boundary between urban and reservation Indians. Those statements do not establish a single reservation position. They show leaders attempting to define what was at stake after the occupation and resist outside efforts to portray Indigenous society as divided into mutually exclusive factions. Different nations and councils could object to damage while sharing demands for stronger land rights, jurisdiction, and educational control.

A further complication is that individuals could hold roles in more than one political setting. Robert Burnette's history in national advocacy and tribal leadership did not make the NCAI an institutional sponsor of the caravan. Likewise, an AIM organizer participating in national coalition meetings did not give AIM authority over the governments of every nation represented. Historical accounts become misleading when they convert individual participation into blanket institutional endorsement. The chapter should explain who signed statements, who attended meetings, and what authority each group possessed rather than attributing one position to all Indigenous people.

The NCAI Impact Survey Team files provide an opportunity to study reactions at their own level of detail. They include organizational records and materials concerning the occupation and its aftermath, but their very existence should not be used to invent particular conclusions. Students can ask whose testimony was gathered, which material losses were prioritized, and whether the survey addressed both the substantive Twenty Points and the damage. The central conclusion is that Indigenous political response was neither unified celebration nor unanimous repudiation. It included efforts to defend important common demands while holding the protesters' methods to account.

Section 111.19

Did the Trail of Broken Treaties Change Federal Indian Policy?

Nixon announced self-determination in July 1970, so the occupation did not cause it. The stronger argument is that the Trail pressed that rhetoric toward concrete commitments. Reforms already under way must be distinguished from policies the caravan may have influenced, and particular legislation should not be attributed to one protest.

Whether the Trail of Broken Treaties changed federal Indian policy depends first on what kind of change is being measured. The campaign unmistakably created a national political event and prompted high-level negotiations. The government agreed to an interagency review process, and the Twenty Points circulated as an identifiable program. Yet immediate enactment of treaty-making reform, major land restoration, and abolition of the BIA did not occur. These are different outcomes and should not be combined into the assertion that the campaign either succeeded or failed in every respect. Political visibility and lawmaking are connected, but they are not the same historical variable.

Chronology limits simple causal claims. President Nixon had issued his major self-determination message in July 1970, more than two years before the Trail. Tribes, attorneys, national organizations, and community activists had already been pressing for changes in federal policy. Later reforms arose through legislative negotiations, tribal advocacy, litigation, executive decisions, and many local efforts. A chapter may plausibly argue that the caravan intensified public debate and contributed to the political climate, but it should not credit the 1972 occupation alone for policies that had independent histories. Evidence of causation would require tracing particular proposals through later testimony, drafting, hearings, administrative memoranda, and statements by decision-makers.

The federal task force is an appropriate test case. Its establishment indicated that officials could not simply ignore the demands; the National Park Service reports that the review ultimately rejected the manifesto's proposals. Students should examine the difference between an opportunity to be heard and a mechanism capable of transferring political power. A task force might collect views and produce recommendations without overcoming the obstacles of congressional opposition or entrenched bureaucratic interests. Its existence was a negotiated outcome, but not a constitutional reconstruction. That distinction was central to what the organizers had argued about the limitations of consultation alone.

The Twenty Points also endured as a political vocabulary. Their combination of treaties, land, jurisdiction, cultural freedom, and material development offered a reference point for advocates who wished to connect legal sovereignty with everyday community institutions. Their influence should be evaluated through specific later citations and organizing documents rather than the assumption that any subsequent Indigenous campaign must have derived from them. A document may exert influence even when its provisions are not enacted; it can shape political questions, provide language for coalitions, and mark the demands that officials have refused to meet.

The relationship between publicity and policy remains the chapter's central historical debate. The occupation ensured that the caravan would be remembered nationally, yet it also gave journalists compelling images that displaced discussion of the manifesto. One interpretation is that dramatic confrontation was necessary to force a serious hearing. Another is that the cost to documents, public trust, and coalition relationships overshadowed the program's objectives. Students should weigh these interpretations against both the November 1972 settlement and the longer history of Indigenous advocacy, recognizing that a moral judgment about tactics is not a substitute for measuring political results.

Section 111.20

The Historical Legacy of the Trail of Broken Treaties

The campaign influenced political organizing, treaty-rights advocacy, and national and international Indigenous networks, and it preserved the Twenty Points as a sovereignty program. Its experiences also contributed to the political environment preceding the February 1973 Wounded Knee occupation, taken up in Chapter 112. Chapter 114 treats the self-determination framework in detail.

The Trail of Broken Treaties brought together people who normally operated in different political environments. Reservation governments, urban organizations, college activists, rights lawyers, and religious support networks encountered one another through planning, travel, workshops, and a confrontation in Washington. These connections were real even if the coalition did not remain a single permanent body. Participants could return home with new contacts and a broader sense of shared grievances. That is an important institutional legacy, but it should be measured through later organizing and correspondence rather than treated as automatic proof of national unity.

The Twenty-Point Position Paper offers a more durable measure of the campaign's intellectual contribution. It described Indigenous political status through treaties and continuing nationhood while insisting that land, courts, religious practice, schools, and employment were connected to the distribution of governing authority. It challenged federal officials to explain why policy messages invoking self-determination had not resolved practical disputes over power. This program was broader than the occupation that became the campaign's most famous episode. Preserving the full text and teaching its twenty provisions permits historical discussion to move beyond photographs of the confrontation.

The occupation also generated enduring arguments about responsibility. Some Indigenous leaders believed the building takeover had exposed the failures of government, while others believed the damage to records had endangered the very rights the caravan sought to protect. The two judgments could coexist in one community, family, or organization. Neither side should be reduced to a caricature. Historical memory gains accuracy when it retains both the serious grievances that motivated the journey and the demonstrable human, documentary, and political costs of its final days.

The route from the Pacific Coast and Plains to Washington also enlarged the geography of Indigenous national politics. The march crossed territory shaped by removal, treaty negotiations, allotment, urban relocation, and twentieth-century community activism. The federal capital became one stop in a much larger network of Indigenous places and institutions. The caravan's lasting geographic lesson is that Washington decisions affected communities separated by enormous distances, but the methods of responding to those decisions were built through local relationships. Its national form did not dissolve nation-specific histories or local political responsibilities.

Chapter 112 will examine the 1973 occupation of Wounded Knee, where sovereignty conflicts, local Oglala politics, and federal intervention produced another highly visible confrontation. Chapter 111 should end before that narrative begins. The Trail of Broken Treaties left an unresolved program, a contested record of protest, and a direct challenge to federal claims of self-determination. Its historical importance lies in the attempt to make treaty rights and Indigenous political authority the central terms of national debate—and in the difficult question of how a movement can preserve those objectives when crisis transforms its strategy.

Chapter Summary

The Trail of Broken Treaties was conceived in 1972 as a national Indigenous campaign for enforceable treaty obligations and a reconstructed federal–Indigenous political relationship. Its organizers drew on older Indigenous advocacy, newer direct-action movements, and grievances intensified by termination, relocation, and federal paternalism. The campaign’s purpose cannot be inferred solely from the building occupation that became its best-known episode.

Planning began at Crow Dog’s Paradise in August and developed through Denver meetings in October. AIM was a major organizing force, but the coalition also involved Indigenous legal advocates, national organizations, religious supporters, and people with experience in tribal government. Endorsement, direct participation, and formal sponsoring authority were not interchangeable.

Three major caravans departed from Seattle, San Francisco, and Los Angeles, with an Oklahoma route invoking the forced removals remembered as the Trail of Tears. Travel through Indigenous communities provided political education, contacts, and evidence of different local grievances. Difficulties of financing, transportation, and communication were part of the campaign’s history rather than incidental inconveniences.

In St. Paul, participants’ recommendations were transformed into a Twenty-Point Position Paper, with Hank Adams coordinating and completing the final draft. The manifesto linked renewed treaty-making and treaty enforcement with land restoration, jurisdiction, tribal membership, administrative reconstruction, cultural freedom, and community development.

Points 1–9 concerned treaty authority, review commissions, congressional participation, court remedies, and legislative oversight. Points 10–13 addressed land, termination, enrollment, state jurisdiction, and protection of Indigenous people through federal justice systems. The paper made a political argument about nationhood while proposing specific legal institutions that would require further legislation.

Points 14–20 sought abolition and replacement of the Bureau of Indian Affairs by 1976, clearer protection for commerce and religion, democratic procedures, and investments in health, housing, education, and employment. This was not simply a call for expanded government services; the authors wanted Indigenous communities to control more of the institutions shaping their lives.

The caravan’s arrival in Washington coincided with inadequate accommodation, disputed meetings, and a prior federal instruction restricting BIA assistance. By November 2–3, a delegation had become a barricaded occupation of federal headquarters. The ensuing crisis involved families, logistical needs, records, political representation, federal property rights, and judicial proceedings.

Hank Adams and other representatives negotiated through channels involving Interior and White House officials. Occupiers also adopted nine supplementary demands addressing immediate assistance and administrative personnel alongside the broader manifesto. On November 8, participants left after commitments including an interagency review and return-travel arrangements. The settlement did not enact the Twenty Points.

The aftermath divided Indigenous opinion over methods and evidence. Charles Trimble and other national leaders condemned damage and missing records while recognizing substantial agreement with the demands for rights and reform. The NCAI Impact Survey Team produced an additional archival record of Indigenous institutional concern. Historians must distinguish allegations, estimates, and verified consequences when reconstructing the event.

The Trail did not initiate Nixon’s July 1970 self-determination policy, nor did it by itself secure later legislation. It did, however, publicize an ambitious sovereignty program and bring together activists across communities. Its legacy lies in both the Twenty Points and the contested history of the occupation. The unresolved conflicts over sovereignty and federal authority lead into Chapter 112 on Wounded Knee.

Chapter Reflection

The Trail of Broken Treaties showed that Indigenous political organizations could transform widely separated concerns into a shared national demand without erasing the distinct histories of the nations involved. Its caravan crossed the geography of removal, treaty-making, reservation administration, and urban migration, while its manifesto connected those histories to practical institutional change.

The Twenty-Point Position Paper deserves attention as a policy program in its own right. Its authors proposed renewed treaty-making, enforceable remedies, land restoration, changes in jurisdiction, and reconstruction of the agency responsible for Indian affairs. They insisted that economic and educational improvements would be insecure without a stronger political foundation.

The occupation of the BIA raised the visibility of those demands but also caused damage and controversy. Distinguishing the legitimacy of a grievance from the consequences of a tactic is a central task of historical judgment. Indigenous leaders themselves disagreed over methods while often sharing concerns about sovereignty and the federal trust relationship.

The November 1972 settlement ended the occupation without implementing the manifesto. Yet the document continued to circulate and the campaign’s organizing relationships mattered beyond Washington. The historical problem left unresolved was how Indigenous nations could compel enforceable political change when even direct access to federal officials did not guarantee action. That question carries forward into the next chapter.
