The Great Sioux War and the Battle of the Little Bighorn

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

In the summer of 1876, thousands of Lakota and Northern Cheyenne people, joined by a smaller number of Arapaho families, lived in a great camp along the Little Bighorn River, in country the United States had recognized in 1868 as unceded Indian territory. They were hunters, mothers, grandparents, children, ceremonial leaders, and warriors. They were not a military installation, and the battle that took place beside their lodges was, for them, a defense of their families.

The Battle of the Little Bighorn, fought on June 25 and 26, 1876, is among the most famous engagements in American history, and also among the most distorted. For well over a century, popular memory, film, and commemoration placed Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer at the center of the story and treated the Lakota and Cheyenne who defeated him as a backdrop to his final hours. This chapter reverses that arrangement. It places the Indigenous communities of the northern Plains at the center of the Great Sioux War of 1876–1877 and treats the battle as one engagement, important but not isolated, within a wider military campaign that began months before Custer's regiment reached the Little Bighorn and continued long after.

The war grew from the collapse of treaty protection. In the Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868, examined in Chapter 76, the United States ended Red Cloud's War (Chapter 75) by abandoning its forts along the Bozeman Trail, recognizing the Great Sioux Reservation, and designating the Powder River country as unceded Indian territory closed to non-Indians. Within six years a military expedition entered the Black Hills, gold was reported, and prospectors flooded onto treaty land. The federal government did not remove them effectively. It tried instead to purchase the Hills, failed, and in late 1875 chose a course that treated Lakota and Cheyenne people who remained outside the agencies as hostile and subject to military force.

The communities that resisted were not a single army under one commander. They were independent camps joined by kinship, ceremony, shared hunting, and common danger, and they included people with very different relationships to the agencies. Some families lived near agencies and drew rations. Others hunted bison in the Powder, Tongue, Rosebud, and Bighorn country and refused to accept confinement. Many moved between these worlds seasonally. Political leadership was distributed among councils, headmen, soldier societies, ceremonial leaders, and battlefield leaders, and this chapter takes care to distinguish the political and spiritual leadership of Sitting Bull from the battlefield decisions of Crazy Horse, Gall, Two Moons, and others.

The American campaign was equally complex. General Alfred Terry, General George Crook, and Colonel John Gibbon moved in separate columns with poor intelligence and weak communication. Each also relied on Indigenous scouts and allies, notably Crow, Shoshone, and Arikara, who had their own histories of conflict with the Lakota and their own reasons for joining the army. Their participation was a matter of Indigenous politics as much as American strategy, and the chapter treats them accordingly.

The campaigns of 1876 produced two major Indigenous victories: at the Rosebud on June 17, where Lakota and Cheyenne warriors halted Crook's advance, and at the Little Bighorn eight days later, where Custer's immediate command of five companies was destroyed. Yet these victories did not end the war. The United States expanded its forces, struck villages in order to seize horses, food, and shelter, took military control of agencies, disarmed and dismounted agency communities, and pursued the free-roaming camps through the winter. By the spring of 1877, most communities had come into the agencies; Crazy Horse was dead by September; and Sitting Bull had led followers into Canada. In February 1877 the United States had also legislated its acquisition of the Black Hills, a measure whose legal dimensions belong to Chapter 76 but whose military and coercive context belongs here.

The evidence for this history is unusually varied and unevenly distributed. Army reports, telegrams, letters, newspaper accounts, and courts of inquiry document the American side. Indigenous accounts survive in interviews recorded years or decades later, in pictographic drawings, in Cheyenne and Lakota oral history, and in testimony collected by writers, soldiers, and ethnologists. Archaeology has added material evidence, especially cartridge cases, bullets, and human remains, that allows historians to test both traditions. No soldier of Custer's immediate battalion survived to describe its end, so reconstructing that fight depends on Indigenous testimony and the physical record. The chapter emphasizes that Lakota and Cheyenne witnesses are not supplementary to the military record; they are central to it.

The chapter's central question is this: How did Lakota and Northern Cheyenne resistance produce the major military victories of 1876, and why did those victories fail to prevent the United States from imposing reservation confinement, weakening Indigenous military independence, and acquiring the Black Hills? The answer lies neither in a single decisive battle nor in the inevitability of defeat, but in a sequence of political decisions, logistical realities, and escalating federal pressure examined across twenty sections. The war's end did not mean the end of Lakota or Cheyenne sovereignty, culture, or claims, a point the chapter's final section takes up directly and that connects forward to Chapters 84, 90, and 119.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the failure of the United States to protect the territories recognized in 1868 contributed to the outbreak of the Great Sioux War.
  2. Distinguish among Lakota divisions, Northern Cheyenne and Arapaho communities, and agency and nonagency families, and explain why residence outside an agency did not extinguish Indigenous political rights.
  3. Trace the sequence of federal decisions from the 1874 Black Hills expedition to the ultimatum of December 1875 and the transfer of the conflict to the army.
  4. Analyze the leadership of Sitting Bull, Crazy Horse, Gall, Two Moons, and others, distinguishing political and ceremonial authority from battlefield command.
  5. Reconstruct the American three-column strategy of 1876 and explain its logistical and intelligence weaknesses.
  6. Evaluate the independent political interests of Crow, Shoshone, and Arikara participants in the campaign.
  7. Assess the significance of the Battle of the Rosebud for the larger campaign and for the later events at the Little Bighorn.
  8. Describe the Little Bighorn encampment as a community of families and explain why its defenders fought as they did.
  9. Interpret the evidence for the destruction of Custer's battalion, comparing Indigenous testimony, army testimony, and battlefield archaeology, and identify what cannot be known.
  10. Distinguish categories of casualties and explain why Custer's immediate command is not the same as the whole Seventh Cavalry.
  11. Explain how the United States responded to Little Bighorn through reinforcement, attacks on subsistence resources, agency control, and winter campaigning.
  12. Evaluate why tactical victory did not produce strategic success, and distinguish the end of armed resistance from the end of Indigenous sovereignty.

Historical Context

The Lakota, or Teton, are the western division of the Oceti Sakowin, the Seven Council Fires, a larger Dakota, Nakota, and Lakota political and linguistic world. By the mid-nineteenth century the Lakota had expanded westward from the Missouri River into the northern Plains, a movement shaped by the horse, the bison economy, trade, and warfare with neighboring nations, including the Crow, Arikara, and Shoshone. The seven Lakota divisions were the Oglala, Sicangu or Brulé, Hunkpapa, Miniconjou, Sans Arc (Itazipco), Sihasapa or Blackfeet, and Oohenunpa or Two Kettles. Northern Cheyenne communities, whose political separation from the Southern Cheyenne deepened in the decades before the war and after the Sand Creek massacre of 1864 (Chapter 74), were close allies of many Lakota bands. Arapaho communities were also part of this regional world.

Red Cloud's War, examined in Chapter 75, ended in 1868 when the United States agreed to close the Bozeman Trail forts. The Fort Laramie Treaty of that year recognized the Great Sioux Reservation, which included lands west of the Missouri River in present-day South Dakota and the Black Hills, and also designated a region north of the North Platte River and east of the Bighorn Mountains as unceded Indian territory, closed to non-Indians without Lakota consent. The treaty also protected certain hunting rights. Its terms and legal afterlife are analyzed in Chapter 76; this chapter takes them as the background to the war.

Two features of the postwar northern Plains matter especially. First, the treaty created a system of agencies where food and goods were distributed, and some leaders, including Red Cloud and Spotted Tail, chose to live near them and negotiate from that position. Many other Lakota and Cheyenne families continued to hunt in the unceded territory, and many people moved between agency and hunting grounds as seasons and opportunities changed. Second, the buffalo economy remained the foundation of life for the free-roaming communities, making mobility, horses, and access to hunting grounds matters of political survival. These conditions framed the crisis that followed.

The United States in the mid-1870s was also under pressure of its own. The economic depression that followed the Panic of 1873 intensified interest in mining and railroad expansion. The Northern Pacific Railroad's surveys through Yellowstone country had already provoked clashes in 1873. Military leaders such as Philip Sheridan, William T. Sherman, Crook, and Terry regarded the unceded territory and the free-roaming communities as obstacles to settlement and a source of potential instability. Many federal officials believed that confinement to agencies was both inevitable and desirable. The chapter does not treat this outcome as inevitable. Lakota and Cheyenne leaders pursued varied strategies, and the outcome depended on decisions, on logistics, and on the ability of each side to sustain its position.

Section 77.1

Lakota, Northern Cheyenne, and Arapaho Political Life after 1868

The northern Plains after 1868 was not divided between a peaceful agency population and a hostile hinterland. It was a landscape of overlapping communities, each making decisions about food, safety, kinship, and political rights under changing conditions. The Oglala, Hunkpapa, Miniconjou, Sans Arc, Brule, Sihasapa, and Two Kettles Lakota each maintained their own councils and leaders, and each division included people who chose different paths. Northern Cheyenne communities, with their own council chiefs and soldier societies, lived and hunted in the Powder River country and often camped alongside Lakota relatives. Arapaho families moved within the same regional world, though their strategies in the war were varied and their participation as a body was limited.

Political authority in these societies was dispersed. Councils of respected men, headmen known in Lakota as itancan, camp police and soldier societies (akicita), ceremonial leaders, war leaders, and the kin networks of extended families all shaped decisions. No single person or body could command every camp. A leader's influence rested on reputation, generosity, ceremonial standing, and the willingness of families to follow. This structure had real strengths: it allowed communities to disperse, regroup, and make decisions suited to local conditions. It also meant that the coalition that formed in 1876 was voluntary and fluid, and that no leader could compel a camp to remain or fight.

The agencies created by the 1868 treaty added a new political dimension. Red Cloud, the Oglala leader who had led the successful resistance on the Bozeman Trail, and Spotted Tail, the Brule leader who had long pursued a policy of accommodation, chose to live near agencies and bargain for rations, goods, and the protection of what they could. Their choices were strategic, not simply submissive. They were attempting to hold their peoples together under mounting pressure. Dull Knife (Morning Star) and Little Wolf were Northern Cheyenne leaders whose communities moved between hunting grounds and the agency region. Two Moons, another Cheyenne leader, would become prominent at the Little Bighorn.

Sitting Bull, Crazy Horse, Gall, and others pursued a different strategy: remaining in the hunting grounds and refusing confinement. Sitting Bull, a Hunkpapa, was regarded by many as a respected political and ceremonial leader whose influence extended beyond his own people. Crazy Horse, an Oglala, had earned a formidable reputation as a war leader, and he was known for his reserve and his devotion to his people. Gall, also Hunkpapa, was an experienced leader and warrior. Each held a distinct role, and later American writers, who often spoke of "chiefs" commanding "tribes" in the manner of European armies, obscured those differences.

The line between agency and nonagency life was porous. Many families spent winters near the agencies, where rations were available, and left for the summer hunt, joining larger camps in the unceded territory. These "summer roamers" would swell the camps of 1876. Agency agents frequently counted such absences as signs of hostility, and military officers later treated them as evidence of coordinated rebellion. But the 1868 treaty itself had recognized hunting rights and designated unceded territory where Lakota and Cheyenne people were entitled to be. Residence outside an agency did not automatically mean that a community had abandoned its political rights or declared war. This distinction, and its erasure by federal officials, lies at the center of the origins of the war.

These communities also had to manage relations with neighboring Indigenous nations. The Crow, whose treaty lands lay to the west, and the Arikara and Shoshone had long-standing rivalries with the Lakota and Cheyenne. These tensions would shape the war, as the army recruited scouts from among them. The Lakota expansion westward had come partly at the expense of Crow hunting grounds, and the Crow had reason to see the Lakota as a threat. The northern Plains of 1876 was therefore not simply a field on which Indigenous people faced the United States; it was a region in which several Indigenous political communities pursued their own interests.

The 1868 treaty created a geographical distinction that federal officials later obscured: the reservation was not the same as the unceded Powder River country, and the treaty's recognition of hunting access did not make every seasonal absence from an agency illegal. This matters because the people later labeled nonreservation or hostile were not all residents of the same band, and their reasons for being in the hunting grounds varied. Some remained there year-round; some had never accepted residence under an agent; others traveled from agencies to visit relatives and hunt. A military roster of adversaries cannot substitute for a political account of the rights and choices of these communities.

Section 77.2

The Black Hills Gold Rush and the Collapse of Treaty Protection, 1874–1875

In the summer of 1874, a large military expedition under Custer left Fort Abraham Lincoln in Dakota Territory and marched into the Black Hills. The expedition, which included over a thousand soldiers, scientists, and miners, was officially described as a reconnaissance, but the Black Hills were part of the Great Sioux Reservation, and the expedition's entry violated the treaty. The Hills, known to the Lakota as Paha Sapa, were a place of deep religious and economic importance, a source of timber, game, and shelter, and the site of ceremonies. Lakota leaders did not welcome the intrusion.

The expedition's report of gold, and the press attention that followed, changed everything. Prospectors began entering the Hills in late 1874, and by 1875 hundreds, then thousands, had followed, despite army efforts to remove some of them. Temporary settlements appeared, and by 1876 Deadwood, in the northern Hills, had grown into a mining town. The Panic of 1873 and the economic depression that followed intensified the appeal of gold. Railroad promoters, merchants in Dakota and Nebraska towns, and local politicians pressed for opening the Hills. Military officers sometimes escorted prospectors out and sometimes did little. The federal government's response was inconsistent: it recognized that the treaty barred trespass, yet it did not enforce exclusion with the vigor the treaty required.

In 1875, a scientific expedition under geologist Walter Jenney, escorted by soldiers, investigated the Hills and confirmed the presence of gold. Meanwhile, the federal government turned to negotiation. A commission led by Senator William B. Allison met with Lakota leaders in September 1875 near Red Cloud Agency, offering either an annual payment for mining rights or a purchase. The gathering drew thousands of Lakota and allied people, and the discussions revealed deep divisions. Many leaders refused to sell at any price the commissioners would offer. Some spoke of the Hills as a place that could not be sold; others demanded sums so large that they signaled refusal; and the more cautious tried to find terms that would preserve rations and protect their position. The presence of young men who opposed any sale created an atmosphere of tension, and the commissioners were alarmed. The negotiations failed. Sitting Bull and the free-roaming camps took no part in them, and they would not have accepted a sale.

The failure of negotiation, together with continuing trespass, put federal officials in a position in which the treaty barred what they wanted. Historians generally cite a decision in the fall of 1875, at a meeting at the White House attended by President Ulysses S. Grant, Sheridan, Crook, and Interior Department officials, that the army should stop trying to keep miners out of the Hills while preparing to compel Lakota compliance with federal demands. The surviving record of this meeting is thin and has been interpreted in different ways, but the shift in policy is clear from what followed: the government ceased to enforce the exclusion provision of the treaty and began to pressure the Lakota who stayed outside the agencies.

The relationship among interests mattered. Miners wanted access; settlers and territorial officials wanted markets and land; railroad promoters wanted traffic; and military leaders wanted to remove the free-roaming communities from the unceded territory. None of these interests required war in itself, but together they produced a policy environment in which maintaining the treaty guarantees was less attractive than ending them. The consequences for the Lakota were stark. A treaty that the United States had signed less than a decade earlier, after military defeat in Red Cloud's War, had been quietly set aside. The legal aspects of the Black Hills taking, including the 1876 agreement, the 1877 act, and the later claims history, belong to Chapter 76; here the point is that by late 1875, the United States was unwilling to enforce the protections it had recognized.

A careful chronology also separates three developments frequently compressed into one story. First came the army-supported reconnaissance of 1874, which publicized mineral discoveries inside treaty-protected land. Next came the growing presence of prospectors and the largely ineffective efforts to remove them, while federal negotiators offered to acquire the Hills. Finally, after the failure of the Allison Commission, officials progressively abandoned meaningful exclusion and adopted policies aimed at nonagency Lakota. None of these steps was itself the February 1877 congressional taking. The distinction explains how pressure accumulated before the formal legal change and why agency families could experience the crisis even while opposing a new war.

Section 77.3

The Ultimatum of 1875–1876 and the Decision to Use Military Force

In November 1875, an Indian Bureau inspector, E. C. Watkins, submitted a report on the nonreservation Lakota that urged military action against Sitting Bull and others who remained outside the agencies. The report characterized these communities as defiant and suggested that a winter campaign would compel them to come in. Federal officials, including Interior Department leaders and the army, drew on such recommendations. On December 6, 1875, the Commissioner of Indian Affairs instructed agents to notify Lakota groups outside the agencies that they must report to an agency by January 31, 1876, or be treated as hostile. The notice stated that the army would then be authorized to act.

The ultimatum was nearly impossible to meet. The communities in question were scattered across the Powder River, Tongue River, Yellowstone, and Missouri River country, and messengers had to find them in the dead of winter, on horseback or on foot, across hundreds of miles of snow. Some camps received the message late; some may not have received it at all; and the deadline, set during the coldest part of the year, ignored the practical difficulty of moving families, elders, children, and horse herds to agencies. Communicating a deadline across winter distances was in itself a problem; complying with it was another. Many Lakota and Cheyenne leaders also saw no reason to obey. They were living in territory the treaty recognized, and they had not been accused of violating it.

Reports of how Sitting Bull and others responded survive in various forms, including accounts that Sitting Bull ignored the message or answered it with contempt. Historians treat many of these anecdotes with care, since they come from American observers or from later recollections. What is clear is that no significant number of nonagency camps relocated by the deadline. On February 1, 1876, the Interior Department informed the War Department that the deadline had passed and that the military should take over. General Philip Sheridan, commander of the Military Division of the Missouri, directed his departmental commanders, Crook in the Department of the Platte and Terry in the Department of Dakota, to prepare operations.

The designation of noncomplying people as hostile transformed their legal and political position. In the American view, they became enemies of the United States, subject to attack wherever found. In the view of many Lakota and Cheyenne, they were people exercising treaty rights in unceded territory. The gap between an administrative classification and Indigenous understanding of treaty rights is among the most important interpretive issues of the war. The government's decision shifted the burden of proof: communities that had not committed acts of war were reclassified as belligerents because they had not moved. The label also had practical consequences, since it allowed military commanders to treat camps as targets rather than as communities with rights.

The decision to use military force also reflected strategic assumptions. Sheridan and others believed that winter was the time to strike, when horses were weak, camps were stationary, and families could be caught in their lodges. The experience of previous campaigns on the Plains had suggested that attacking villages, seizing horses, and destroying supplies could force communities to submit. This logic, applied to the northern Plains in 1876, would define the opening phase of the war and continue through the following winter.

Primary Source Window I

The December 1875 Ultimatum and the Military Directives of Early 1876

Creators: The Commissioner of Indian Affairs, Interior Department officials, Indian Bureau inspector E. C. Watkins, and army officers of the Military Division of the Missouri · Dates: November 1875–February 1876 · Type: Official correspondence, inspector's report, and military directives

Provenance and audience: These documents were produced within the federal government for officials, agents, and military commanders. Many were later printed in congressional documents and annual reports of the Indian Bureau. They were not written for, or by, Lakota or Cheyenne communities, though they directly concerned them.

Context: The documents trace the shift from treaty enforcement to military coercion. Watkins's report, written in November 1875, urged action against nonreservation communities. The December 6 instruction directed agents to notify groups outside the agencies that they must return by January 31, 1876. Following the deadline, the Interior Department turned the matter over to the army, and military commanders prepared for operations.

Editorial paraphrase (not a quotation): The surviving instructions, as summarized here in the editors' words, told agents to inform the nonreservation people that failure to report by the stated date would lead to their being treated as hostile and to action by the military. Watkins's report, in the editors' paraphrase, portrayed the free-roaming communities as defiant and recommended that the army be sent against them, preferably during the winter.

Evidentiary value: The documents establish the sequence of federal decisions, the date and terms of the ultimatum, and the reasoning officials gave. They show how an administrative framework redefined people's legal status.

Limitations: They represent only federal perspectives. They do not show whether, when, or how the message reached particular camps, and they say little about how Lakota and Cheyenne leaders understood or answered it. Anecdotes about Sitting Bull's reaction come from other sources and require separate evaluation.

Questions for students:

  1. What do the documents say about the date and terms of the ultimatum, and who issued them?
  2. Who created them, and why would these officials have framed noncompliance as hostility?
  3. How might the practical conditions of a Plains winter have affected a community's ability to comply?
  4. What can historians learn from these documents about the federal decision-making process?
  5. What cannot be established from them alone, and what additional evidence would be needed to understand Lakota and Cheyenne responses?

The deadline also operated through a limited information network. The federal government could issue a fixed calendar date in Washington, but its Indian agents had to transmit that instruction to camps moving over a vast and cold region. An agent's report that notice had been sent did not prove that a particular leader received it, understood its legal implications, or had the means to move a population over snowbound ground. For students comparing official records and Indigenous testimony, this difference between issuing an order, delivering it, and being able to comply is a central source problem. Treating nonappearance as proof of intentional rebellion erased those distinctions before military operations began.

Section 77.4

Sitting Bull, Crazy Horse, and the Coalition of the Free-Roaming Camps

The communities that refused confinement were bound together by kinship, shared hunting, and common circumstance rather than by a central command. Sitting Bull's importance among the Hunkpapa and allied groups rested on his reputation as a political and ceremonial leader, as a man of spiritual authority, and as an advocate of keeping the Lakota way of life intact. Americans, seeking a single figure to blame or negotiate with, often described him as the head of the "hostile" Sioux. Lakota political organization did not work that way, and Sitting Bull himself could not direct the camps of the Oglala, Miniconjou, Sans Arc, or Cheyenne.

Crazy Horse's authority was of a different kind. An Oglala, he was known as a formidable war leader whose reputation for bravery and tactical skill was widely recognized, and he lived with a following of Oglala and Cheyenne families in the Powder River country. He was not a political chief in the sense that Red Cloud was, and he is remembered in Lakota tradition as a man who avoided display. Gall was a Hunkpapa leader with a record of experience in war and politics. Northern Cheyenne leaders, including Two Moons, Old Bear, Lame White Man, and others, made their own decisions for their own communities. After the March 1876 attack on Old Bear's village (Section 77.5), Cheyenne survivors joined Crazy Horse's Oglala camp and later the larger gathering.

The formation of the great camps in the spring of 1876 was a gradual process. As the weather warmed, bands that had spent the winter in smaller groups moved toward the buffalo herds and toward each other. People left agencies to join relatives, to hunt, and, in some cases, because of the army's attacks and the ultimatum. The great gathering drew families who wanted safety in numbers, and it grew by accretion across spring and early summer. Leaders met in council to decide where to camp, when to move, and how to hunt. Camps were organized in circles by division and band, and akicita societies enforced order during hunts and moves.

The economic foundations of these communities were bison hunting, trade, horse herds, and the labor of families. Buffalo supplied meat, hides for lodges and robes, sinew, bone, and tools. Women processed meat and hides, set up and took down lodges, and managed the camp's domestic economy, while men hunted and defended. Horses were the basis of mobility and wealth. Trade with agencies and with traders supplied cloth, metal goods, ammunition, and firearms. The Lakota and Cheyenne possessed a mixture of weapons, including bows, muzzle-loading guns, and repeating rifles of various types. Historians should avoid generalizing either that the Indigenous forces were poorly armed or that they uniformly possessed superior weapons; the evidence, including battlefield archaeology, shows variety.

Students must understand that the large encampments of 1876 were living Indigenous communities, not simply assembled armies. They contained elders, infants, and the sick, and their movements were governed by grass for horses, availability of game, water, and the safety of noncombatants. Decisions about whether to fight, to flee, or to negotiate were made in light of these responsibilities. When army columns approached, leaders had to weigh the risk to families against the possibility of defense. This is essential to understanding why Lakota and Cheyenne fought as they did at the Rosebud and the Little Bighorn: they fought to protect their camps.

Historical Vocabulary
Treaty Rights, Nonreservation Communities, and the Label "Hostile"

Definition. Treaty rights are the rights recognized in a treaty between nations, here the Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868, including residence and hunting in designated territory. Nonreservation communities are those who lived or traveled outside the agencies. "Hostile" was an administrative classification used by federal officials for people who did not comply with federal instructions.

Historical context. The terms were not equivalent. A community could be exercising treaty rights while living outside an agency, and the label "hostile" described a government's judgment of compliance, not a community's actual conduct. The ultimatum of December 1875 converted nonresidence into hostility by administrative decision.

Why it matters. The label shaped how the army treated camps, authorizing attacks on communities that had not attacked anyone. It also shaped historical memory, since "hostile" and "friendly" became shorthand in later American writing.

Potential misunderstanding. The words "hostile" and "friendly" suggest fixed identities. In practice, families moved between agency and hunting grounds, and political positions changed with circumstances.

Political alliances were reinforced by material relationships. Sharing horses, winter shelter, ammunition, and access to bison mattered as much as statements made in council. A family that welcomed refugees from an attacked camp took on a real obligation to feed and protect new relatives; the same relationship could increase the number of warriors available for defense. Yet a growing camp placed heavier demands on grass and game, which limited how long participants could remain concentrated. The coalition's size and mobility were therefore both strengths and vulnerabilities. Later descriptions of an army under Sitting Bull are inadequate to explain communities that could gather for mutual defense and then disperse independently when food or safety required.

Section 77.5

The March 1876 Powder River Campaign and the Attack on a Northern Cheyenne Village

The first American offensive of the war began in winter. In early March 1876, Brigadier General George Crook led a column north from Fort Fetterman, in present-day Wyoming, into the Powder River country, in severe cold and with guides including the scouts Frank Grouard and Louis Richard. The expedition's purpose was to locate and strike the camps of the free-roaming communities before spring brought grass and mobility. The march was difficult, and Crook, hampered by weather and uncertainty about where the villages lay, detached part of his force under Colonel Joseph J. Reynolds to move ahead.

On March 17, Reynolds's detachment attacked a village on the Powder River at dawn. The camp was identified by participants and later historians as principally Northern Cheyenne, under Old Bear, with some Oglala visitors, and it lay in winter quarters close to the camp of Crazy Horse's people. The inhabitants were families, not an assembled army. The soldiers drove people from their lodges, seized the village's horse herd, and burned lodges, robes, food, and equipment. Army reports described the attack as a success. The number of Indigenous dead is uncertain, and accounts differ; several American soldiers were killed. Noncombatants, including women and children, fled into the cold with whatever they could carry, and the loss of shelter and food at that time of year placed them at grave risk.

The operation then fell apart. Reynolds withdrew without securing his captures or his dead, and there are reports that a wounded soldier was left behind. Cheyenne and Oglala warriors counterattacked, and within a short time they recovered most of the captured horses. Crook, who had remained with the main force, was dissatisfied, and he returned to Fort Fetterman at the end of March. Reynolds and a subordinate officer were later court-martialed for their conduct. The army's own assessments varied: some saw the operation as a damaging blow, and others, especially Crook, saw it as a failure of execution that squandered an opportunity.

Indigenous experiences of the consequences differed from American military assessments. For the Cheyenne of Old Bear's village, the destruction of lodges and supplies in winter was a catastrophe. The survivors made their way to Crazy Horse's camp, where Oglala relatives sheltered and fed them, and the shared hardship cemented an alliance between the Cheyenne and the Lakota. The attack also demonstrated what the army was willing to do: strike camps that had not fought. It convinced many Cheyenne that they could no longer expect peace by staying out of the army's way. The Powder River attack therefore helped to produce the larger coalition of 1876, rather than breaking it.

The logic of attacking horses, food, lodges, and winter shelter, rather than seeking decisive battle, would recur through the war. A mobile community depended on its horses for movement and hunting, on stored food for survival until the next hunt, and on lodges for shelter. Destroying them could force people toward agencies without a victory in the field. Reynolds's attack was clumsy, but its purpose was consistent with a strategy that would become more effective in the months to come.

The March attack also illustrates the danger of relying exclusively on military after-action reports. A report that soldiers burned a village and seized stock might describe tactical success in the commander's vocabulary, yet for the people whose lodges were destroyed the central event was the immediate loss of heat, food, bedding, and mobility. Conversely, the recovery of horses by Cheyenne and Oglala warriors mattered not only as a reversal of the army's plan but as an attempt to restore the means of survival. When Indigenous recollections and army records differ about casualties, students should first establish whose dead were being counted and whether a report includes those who died later of exposure or wounds.

Section 77.6

The Three-Column Campaign: Crook, Terry, Gibbon, and the Search for the Indigenous Villages

The army's plan for the summer called for converging columns. Crook would march north again from Fort Fetterman in Wyoming; Colonel John Gibbon would move east along the Yellowstone from Montana, beginning from Fort Ellis; and Brigadier General Alfred Terry would advance west from Fort Abraham Lincoln in Dakota Territory, with the Seventh Cavalry under Lieutenant Colonel Custer as his principal striking force. The idea was to trap the villages between the columns. In practice, the plan relied on coordination that the army could not provide. The columns moved independently over vast, poorly mapped country, communication was by courier, and each commander operated with partial and often inaccurate intelligence about where the villages were and how large they had become.

Logistics constrained each column. Cavalry horses needed grain and grass, infantry needed supplies, and wagons and pack mules slowed movement. Terry's Dakota column left Fort Abraham Lincoln in mid-May. Gibbon's Montana column had been in the field since the spring and operated along the Yellowstone. Crook, delayed after the winter's failure, marched again at the end of May. Command relationships added uncertainty. Custer's regiment was part of Terry's column but had a record of independent action and a reputation for aggressive initiative, and the instructions Terry gave him allowed for discretion. Each commander also had incentives to find and defeat the villages before the others.

Indigenous scouts and allies were central to the campaign. Crook was joined in mid-June by a substantial body of Crow and Shoshone warriors, among them men who had fought the Lakota and Cheyenne for decades. Gibbon's column had Crow scouts, and Custer's regiment included Arikara scouts and several Crow scouts, along with interpreters and guides such as Mitch Bouyer. These men knew the terrain, could read trails and camps, and often understood Lakota and Cheyenne movements better than the army officers they served. Their reconnaissance was often decisive.

Their reasons for serving were their own. The Crow, whose lands lay between the Lakota hunting grounds and the Yellowstone, had suffered from Lakota and Cheyenne expansion into the Powder and Bighorn country, and had reason to see the army as a counterweight. The Shoshone, under leaders including Washakie, had also fought Lakota and Cheyenne for access to hunting grounds. The Arikara, whose communities had been devastated by epidemic disease and by Lakota pressure, lived on a reduced reservation and sought alliances that might protect them. To call these scouts traitors to Indigenous people, or to see them as simple instruments of American expansion, misreads their position. They acted within their own political histories and with limited choices, while the United States benefited from their knowledge and military strength.

By early June, the columns had begun to find evidence of large camps. Terry and Gibbon met on the Yellowstone, and Major Marcus Reno led a reconnaissance up the Powder, Tongue, and Rosebud in mid-June that located a large trail without confirming the location of the villages. Terry then devised a plan in which Custer would march up the Rosebud, and Gibbon would move up the Yellowstone and Bighorn toward the mouth of the Little Bighorn. A critical weakness was that Terry did not know of Crook's fight on the Rosebud on June 17 or of its consequences, so his plan assumed that Crook was still advancing from the south.

The columns operated on different timetables and with different sources of information. Gibbon knew parts of the Montana terrain and drew on Crow knowledge of the Bighorn country. Terry was responsible for coordinating the northern movement and relied on information delivered by mounted messengers and scouts. Crook advanced separately from the south, and even when his officers reported contact with large Indigenous forces, the news could not reach the other commanders promptly. This was not a single force approaching a known target with a continuous communications network. The difficulty of turning sightings, hoofprints, camp debris, and reports into reliable intelligence helps explain why the columns could converge in general geography yet fail to support one another at the critical moment.

Section 77.7

The Great Indigenous Gathering, Sitting Bull's Sun Dance, and the Defense of the Homeland

By late spring, the Lakota and Cheyenne camps in the Powder and Rosebud country were growing steadily. People arrived from the agencies and from smaller winter camps, bringing the news of the army's winter attack and the ultimatum. The gathering served several purposes at once: it offered safety in numbers, access to buffalo, the company of relatives, and the opportunity for ceremony and political consultation. Camp circles were arranged by division, with Lakota groups and Northern Cheyenne placing their lodges near one another.

In early June 1876, the gathered people held a Sun Dance, associated with Sitting Bull, on Rosebud Creek. The Sun Dance was the central annual ceremony of many Plains peoples, a time of prayer, sacrifice, and renewal that drew communities together. Accounts by participants and later writers say that Sitting Bull made offerings of his own flesh and then, after a period of dancing, experienced a vision in which soldiers fell upside down into the camp. This vision was understood as a promise of victory over attacking soldiers. Accounts of its details differ, and many were recorded years afterward, but the tradition of the vision is widespread and reflects the importance of Sitting Bull's spiritual leadership.

The significance of the Sun Dance lay in the political and spiritual encouragement it gave. A vision of this kind, shared with the camps, strengthened resolve and shaped how people understood coming events. But Sitting Bull's religious leadership did not make him the commander of the battles that followed. Lakota and Cheyenne warfare was conducted by warriors and war leaders acting through soldier societies, individual initiative, and consultation, not by a general directing tactics from a headquarters. Sitting Bull's role was to provide spiritual guidance, political authority, and protection for the families, and to hold together a coalition that no one could order.

The camps moved frequently, following grass and game. From the Rosebud valley, they moved up the creek and into the country between the Rosebud and the Little Bighorn. The buffalo hunt remained essential; a camp of thousands consumed enormous quantities of meat, and horse herds grazed down the grass in a short time. Preparations for defense were part of daily life: scouts watched for soldiers, camp police enforced discipline, and leaders considered how to protect families should an attack come. The movement of the camps thus reflected both subsistence needs and the awareness of danger.

Historical Vocabulary
Coalition Warfare, Military Intelligence, and Battlefield Reconstruction

Definition. A military coalition is an alliance of independent groups cooperating for a shared purpose without a unified command. Reconnaissance and scouting are the gathering of information about terrain and opponents. Battlefield evidence includes physical remains such as cartridge cases, bullets, and human remains. Participant testimony is the account of those who took part.

Historical context. Lakota and Cheyenne forces acted as a coalition, with leaders and societies making decisions within their own groups. The American columns used scouts to gather intelligence, and later historians use both testimony and archaeology to reconstruct the battles.

Why it matters. The evidentiary difference between testimony and archaeology is critical. Testimony records what people remember seeing and doing, shaped by position, memory, and purpose. Archaeology records patterns of material remains, which can test testimony but do not by themselves identify individuals or intentions.

Potential misunderstanding. A coalition is not a centralized army. Describing the Indigenous side as having a single commander or plan imposes a European model of command.

Primary Source Window II

Lakota and Northern Cheyenne Accounts of the Sun Dance and the Battle of the Rosebud

Creators: Lakota and Cheyenne participants and witnesses, interviewed or recorded by later writers, ethnologists, and military figures; American officers' reports · Dates: Accounts range from 1876 reports to interviews recorded years or decades later · Type: Oral testimony and recollection, alongside official military reports

Provenance and audience: Indigenous accounts of the Sun Dance and the Rosebud were generally recorded long after the events, often through interpreters, for audiences of writers, soldiers, and scholars. Many have been gathered and edited in later published collections. Official reports by Crook and his officers were written in 1876 for military superiors.

Editorial paraphrase (not a quotation): Participants' accounts, as paraphrased by the editors, describe the Sun Dance as a gathering of several peoples in which Sitting Bull gave offerings and received a vision of soldiers falling into camp, and they describe the Rosebud fight as a day of mounted attacks and counterattacks in which warriors defended their camps and families from an approaching force. Cheyenne accounts specifically describe a woman's rescue of her brother during the fight, remembered in the name often rendered as Where the Girl Saved Her Brother.

Evidentiary value: These sources preserve Indigenous understandings of the ceremony, the motives of the defenders, and details of fighting that army reports could not observe. They are essential for understanding the meaning of events to participants.

Limitations: Decades-later interviews are shaped by memory, by the interviewer's questions, by translation, and by the passage of time. They must be distinguished from documents produced during the campaign. Accounts may disagree on details, and accounts of a vision are matters of religious tradition as well as historical record.

Section 77.8

The Battle of the Rosebud, June 17, 1876: Crazy Horse and the Defense against Crook

On June 17, 1876, eight days before the Little Bighorn, Crook's column, advancing from the south, met Lakota and Cheyenne warriors in the valley of the upper Rosebud Creek. Crook had approximately a thousand soldiers and roughly two hundred and sixty Crow and Shoshone allies, who had joined him in the preceding days. They had marched north from Goose Creek and stopped to rest in the valley of the Rosebud on the morning of June 17, with limited security posted. The strength of the Indigenous force is uncertain, with estimates from several hundred to well over a thousand, and the precise numbers cannot be established.

The Lakota and Cheyenne warriors had left their camp when scouts reported soldiers approaching, with the aim of meeting the column before it reached the village. Crazy Horse was prominent among the leaders, as were Cheyenne leaders and warriors of several Lakota divisions. The battle began when Crow and Shoshone scouts, who were out ahead of the main column, encountered the warriors and gave warning. Fighting developed across the broken terrain of ridges, ravines, and valley floors, with a fluid series of mounted charges and counterattacks rather than a single line of battle. The Crow and Shoshone allies played a major role, engaging the warriors at critical moments, and army accounts acknowledge their contribution.

The terrain shaped the fight. The valley of the Rosebud narrowed into a canyon downstream, and the surrounding ridges allowed warriors to attack from several directions while limiting the ability of the army to concentrate its force. Crazy Horse and others struck repeatedly and withdrew, drawing and testing the soldiers. At one point Crook, believing that a village lay downstream, ordered a detachment of cavalry under Captain Anson Mills and Captain Henry Noyes to move on it, which divided his force. As warriors pressed the main body, Crook recalled the detachment. The conflict between his attempts to attack the village and the need to defend his own column illustrates the pressure the Indigenous attack exerted on his decisions.

One episode is remembered especially by the Northern Cheyenne. In the fighting, a young man, Comes in Sight, was in danger after his horse was struck, and his sister, Buffalo Calf Road Woman, rode into the fight and carried him to safety. The Cheyenne remember the battle as the fight where the girl saved her brother. Her action is preserved in Cheyenne tradition as an example of courage, and it also reminds students that women participated in the defense of their communities in diverse ways.

By late afternoon the warriors withdrew, and Crook's force held the field. A U.S. Army campaign atlas reports ten army personnel killed and twenty-one wounded; other accounts differ slightly in their classifications, and the National Park Service separately reports more than ten Crow and Shoshone scouts killed. Those figures should not be added or compared without checking whether a given report counts enlisted soldiers, officers, and Indigenous allies together. Losses among Lakota and Cheyenne combatants also remain uncertain, with accounts ranging from a handful to several dozen killed. In a narrow sense, Crook held the ground. In a strategic sense, the battle halted his advance. His column, short of supplies and burdened with wounded, withdrew to its base on Goose Creek and remained there for weeks awaiting reinforcement. It did not join Terry and Gibbon before the Little Bighorn, and Terry, unaware of the fight, continued to plan as if Crook were moving north. The Rosebud therefore removed one of the three American columns from the decisive days of the campaign and shaped the circumstances in which Custer's regiment would meet the village.

The Rosebud is often overshadowed by the Little Bighorn, but it was a significant Indigenous victory in its own right. A mobile coalition, outnumbered by the army column in total strength but defending its families, stopped a major American advance. It also showed that Crow and Shoshone participation had altered the shape of the fight, and that Indigenous politics, not only American strategy, structured the campaign.

Division IIIThe Battle of the Little Bighorn, June 25–26, 1876
Section 77.9

The Village at Greasy Grass: Lakota and Cheyenne Families on the Little Bighorn

After the Rosebud, the camps moved west across the divide into the valley of the Little Bighorn, a river known to the Lakota as the Greasy Grass. By the final week of June, a very large encampment lay along the western bank of the river, stretching for roughly three miles. The National Park Service estimates that approximately eight thousand people lived in the village, including roughly fifteen hundred to eighteen hundred warriors. These are reconstructed estimates, derived from the number of lodges reported by participants and observers, the traces of the camp circles, and reasoning about household size. No precise count has been established, and historians continue to debate the figures.

The village was a collection of camp circles. The Hunkpapa lodges were generally placed toward the southern end and the Northern Cheyenne toward the northern end, with the Oglala, Miniconjou, Sans Arc, Sihasapa, and smaller groups between. Accounts differ on the exact order of the circles, and the arrangement shifted as groups arrived. A small number of Arapaho families were present, as were some other related people. Each circle had its own leaders, its own soldier societies, and its own horses, yet all were linked by kinship and common purpose.

The village included women, children, elders, and the sick. It held thousands of horses, grazed in the surrounding hills and bottomlands, along with the stores of meat and the hides, lodges, and equipment of daily life. Women had been working to process meat and hides from the recent hunts, and children were playing, some in the river. Religious and political leaders were present, among them Sitting Bull, who was among those in camp rather than directing a battle plan. The village was a place where ceremonies were held, councils were convened, and marriages and friendships were renewed.

Families gathered there for several reasons. The buffalo hunt in the Rosebud and Little Bighorn country was productive, and the grass for horses was good. Kinship drew relatives together, and summer was the season of ceremonies and visiting. Diplomacy among the Lakota and Cheyenne, and with others, was carried on at such gatherings. Security was also a motive: after the attack on Old Bear's village, the ultimatum, and the Rosebud, people knew that soldiers were in the field, and a large camp offered a measure of protection. These motives were not separate. A camp of this size could not remain in one place for long, and its location reflected a balance among hunting, grass, water, and safety.

The important analytical point is that the defenders were protecting their families and camp, not occupying a military installation. Warriors who fought did so with the knowledge that their wives, children, and parents were within a short distance. Many of the choices made on June 25, to attack aggressively, to cover the flight of noncombatants, and to prevent the soldiers from reaching the camp, are intelligible only in this light. The scale of the village also explains why the American forces, who underestimated its size, were overmatched.

The geography of the encampment helps explain both the speed of its defense and the dangers to noncombatants. Separate camp circles allowed families to remain near kin and leaders, while the river and its tributary coulees offered water and shelter for people and horses. When the alarm came, a runner or a person on horseback could carry news through neighboring circles, but families far from the first clash had incomplete information about the direction of attack. Some hurried to save children and possessions while warriors moved toward Reno or northward toward Custer. The apparent size of the village, visible only in fragments from surrounding ridges, also explains why United States officers struggled to convert a trail of campsites into an accurate estimate of the people they were approaching.

Section 77.10

Custer's Advance: Intelligence, Terrain, and the Division of the Seventh Cavalry

On June 22, 1876, Custer's Seventh Cavalry left Terry's command on the Yellowstone and marched up the Rosebud. Terry's instructions to Custer, which survive in written form, directed him up the Rosebud while Gibbon's column moved toward the Bighorn, and left him a measure of discretion. Custer declined an offer of additional cavalry and also did not take the Gatling guns that had been available. On June 24, his scouts found a large Indigenous trail leading toward the Little Bighorn, a trail that suggested a much larger body of people than the army had anticipated.

Custer's Crow and Arikara scouts played a central role. From a high point called the Crow's Nest, on the divide between the Rosebud and the Little Bighorn, the Crow scouts reported seeing signs of a large village in the valley, and the guide Mitch Bouyer reportedly warned that it was among the largest he had seen. The scouts' reports reached Custer in the early morning of June 25. Custer, who feared that his regiment had been seen by Indigenous observers and that the village might scatter, decided to attack that day, rather than wait, as Terry's plan had implied, for Gibbon's column to approach the following day.

On the afternoon of June 25, near midday, Custer divided the regiment. Captain Frederick Benteen was sent with three companies to scout to the left, toward the south and west, a movement intended to prevent the village from escaping in that direction. Captain Thomas McDougall's company guarded the pack train, which followed behind. Major Marcus Reno took three companies, A, G, and M, with approximately one hundred and forty soldiers according to National Park Service accounts, and was directed toward the village. Custer kept five companies, C, E, F, I, and L, numbering about two hundred and ten men. The division of a regiment in the presence of an enemy of unknown strength has been criticized by many historians, though the circumstances Custer faced, including incomplete information and the risk that the village would flee, remain a subject of discussion.

Intelligence was incomplete. Custer did not know the size of the village or the number of warriors, and the broken terrain, with ridges and ravines that limited visibility, prevented him from seeing it as a whole. Communication between the separated battalions was by messenger and trumpet, and was slow and uncertain. After the battle, disagreements about Custer's intentions arose among surviving officers, and later historians have had to distinguish orders that are documented in testimony from plans reconstructed afterward. The surviving evidence, including testimony before a court of inquiry convened in 1879 to examine Reno's conduct, was given after the event and shaped by the interests of the witnesses.

Custer's decision and its consequences cannot be understood without recognizing that he was operating under assumptions about Indigenous behavior. He expected that the village might attempt to flee and that a rapid attack would catch it, an expectation drawn from earlier experience on the Plains. The reality was a very large village whose warriors, alerted by scouts and by the approach of soldiers, were ready to defend their families.

The intelligence problem should not be reduced to an accusation that scouts failed. Crow and Arikara men were working in difficult terrain and reporting what their experience allowed them to infer; the decisive judgments about whether to attack immediately, how widely to divide the regiment, and when to concentrate force remained those of the American command. The route from the Crow's Nest to the river did not provide an uninterrupted view of the village, and movements between ridges could be hidden within minutes. Afterward, the surviving written orders and testimony established some steps in Custer's plan but could not reconstruct every adjustment made by a battalion whose officers were killed. This is why a narrative that appears completely certain about Custer's final intentions exceeds its evidence.

Section 77.11

Reno's Valley Attack, Indigenous Counterattack, and the Retreat to the Bluffs

Reno's battalion crossed the Little Bighorn in the afternoon of June 25 and advanced down the valley toward the southern end of the encampment, with Arikara scouts moving ahead. Reno had been told that Custer's battalion would support him, according to the testimony of officers who carried messages. As his companies approached the camp, Reno halted and formed a skirmish line, with soldiers dismounting and horse-holders taking mounts to the rear. The advance had brought him close to the Hunkpapa circle, and the fire from his line was directed at the lodges and at warriors rushing out.

The village was awake and mobilizing. Women and children fled northward and into the hills as warriors, among them Hunkpapa under Gall and many others, rushed to meet the soldiers. Participants described a rapid and growing counterattack, with warriors riding forward from several circles and turning the flank of Reno's line. Gall, according to many accounts, lost members of his own family in the first moments of the attack, which accounts for the intensity of the Hunkpapa response. The defenders' movements were not directed by a single commander. They resulted from decisions made by individuals and groups acting on their own judgment, responding to what they saw, and the effect was a very powerful counterattack.

Reno, facing mounting pressure and the threat of being surrounded, withdrew his men into a stand of timber along the river. The position proved untenable, and Reno ordered a retreat across the river and up the bluffs to the east. The retreat was disorderly and costly. Soldiers and scouts were killed as they tried to cross the river and climb the steep banks, and some men were left behind in the timber. Bloody Knife, an Arikara scout, was among the dead. Some of the Arikara, Crow, and other scouts had separated from the command earlier, and some scouts did not take part in the retreat. The losses among soldiers and scouts in the valley fight and the retreat were substantial, and some of the men left in the timber later rejoined the command, while others were killed.

Benteen's battalion, after scouting to the south and finding nothing, had been recalled by a message from Custer's adjutant and arrived at the bluffs shortly after Reno's retreat. Benteen joined Reno, and the pack train arrived soon after. The combined force, with about a hundred and forty from Reno and approximately 125 from Benteen, along with the packers and the wounded, began to organize a defense on the hilltop. The arrival of Benteen's command, documented in the National Park Service's account, prevented the immediate destruction of Reno's battalion.

It is important to distinguish what participants knew during the battle from what historians later learned. On the bluffs, Reno and Benteen heard firing to the north and saw dust and smoke, but they did not know what had happened to Custer. Indigenous defenders in the valley understood the immediate fight against Reno, and then, as warriors moved north, the larger situation. Neither side had a complete picture. Later reconstructions, drawing on Indigenous testimony and archaeology, allowed a fuller understanding of the sequence of events than any participant had.

Section 77.12

The Defeat of Custer's Battalion: Indigenous Testimony, Battlefield Evidence, and Historical Uncertainty

The destruction of Custer's five companies is the most studied and most contested part of the battle. The central fact is that no soldier of Custer's immediate battalion survived to tell what happened. The few men who left the command earlier, such as messengers and some scouts, could describe only what they saw before the final fight. Reconstruction therefore depends on three bodies of evidence: Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho testimony; the physical evidence on the field; and indirect American evidence, such as the position of bodies as found and the observations of the soldiers who reached the field on June 27.

The broad outline is agreed. After sending his message to Benteen, Custer moved with his five companies northward along the ridges east of the river, toward the northern end of the village. Indigenous accounts describe soldiers approaching the river at points opposite the village, in the area of Medicine Tail Coulee, where some men moved toward a ford and were met by warriors. Many warriors, having dealt with Reno, rushed north along the river to meet this new threat. The soldiers then moved to higher ground to the north and east, along the ridges, in the area around Calhoun Hill and Last Stand Hill. The fighting spread across these ridges, with warriors converging from several directions, including from the north and east, where bands that had moved around the soldiers' flank attacked from behind.

Indigenous testimony names many participants. Crazy Horse, Gall, Two Moons, the Miniconjou Hump, the Oglala Low Dog, the Hunkpapa Crow King, and the Cheyenne Lame White Man, who was killed in the fight, are among those named, along with White Bull, a Miniconjou, and numerous others. Participants such as Wooden Leg and Kate Bighead among the Cheyenne, Iron Hawk and Moving Robe Woman among the Hunkpapa, and Red Horse, a Miniconjou whose drawings survive, gave accounts. The accuracy of these accounts varies, and historians distinguish between well-supported testimony, corroborated by multiple witnesses and physical evidence, and later heroic legend. Claims about who killed Custer, for example, cannot be resolved; many Indigenous witnesses said they did not know which soldier was Custer, and later claims by individuals are contested.

The evidence of the physical landscape and archaeology has transformed the field. After a grass fire in 1983 exposed the ground, archaeologists conducted systematic surveys beginning in 1984, mapping and recovering cartridge cases, bullets, equipment, and human remains. By analyzing the distribution of cartridge cases and identifying individual weapons through marks left on the cases, they could track the movement of particular firearms across the field. The results show that the battle was not a single compact stand. Soldiers moved in groups, formed skirmish lines, and fought in several locations, and some were found in positions suggesting flight or isolated resistance. The remains found in and near the ravine on the slope below Last Stand Hill suggest that some soldiers broke away from the main position. The archaeology also shows a wide variety of Indigenous weapons, including repeating rifles, and the fire the soldiers faced came from many directions.

The chapter rejects the assumption that all of Custer's soldiers fought and died in a single compact formation on Last Stand Hill. The evidence points instead to a series of engagements across the ridges, with different companies and groups of soldiers fighting, retreating, and dying in different places. The duration of the fighting is also uncertain; accounts and reconstructions suggest that the destruction of Custer's five companies took place in a comparatively short time, probably well under two hours, though no precise figure can be established.

A useful teaching distinction is that the defeat of Custer's five companies is not identical to the defeat of the entire Seventh Cavalry. Reno's and Benteen's companies survived on the bluffs to the south, and the regiment as a whole continued to exist. The destruction of Custer's immediate command was a catastrophe for the regiment, but the survival of the other companies, and the arrival of additional forces on June 27, shaped how the battle was understood. And the fighting against Reno and Benteen continued for another day, a subject taken up in the next section.

The reconstruction of this engagement illustrates the importance of taking Indigenous testimony seriously. For generations, American accounts relied on the army's limited knowledge and on imaginative reconstruction, centering Custer. Indigenous accounts, long collected but often treated as secondary, supply the perspective of those who fought and who saw the field from the other side. When tested against archaeology, they have often been shown to be reliable on many points, though no account is complete. The result is a history in which Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho witnesses are central to knowing what happened.

Archaeological interpretation is powerful but not self-explanatory. Matching firing-pin or extractor marks can associate cartridge cases with a weapon, yet a weapon might have been moved, captured, or used by more than one person. Surface collections can be affected by erosion, souvenir gathering, older burials, and decades of commemorative disturbance. Likewise, a line of military markers is evidence of where remains were believed to have lain, not a photograph of a company formation at the moment of death. The strongest reconstructions therefore compare artifact distributions with independent Indigenous accounts and surviving military observations, while acknowledging the places where the evidence permits competing sequences. The object is neither to defend a legend of a single last stand nor to replace it with a different story of unwarranted certainty.

Division IVThe Aftermath of Little Bighorn and the Renewal of American Military Power
Section 77.13

The Reno–Benteen Defense, June 25–26, 1876

While Custer's immediate command was being destroyed to the north, the surviving companies of the Seventh Cavalry fought for their own survival on the bluffs. After Benteen joined Reno and the pack train arrived, the combined command, together with the wounded and the packers, held a hilltop position. Captain Thomas Weir, who had moved north along the ridge in an effort to make contact with Custer, advanced to a high point from which officers could see dust and smoke and distant movement, but the Indigenous warriors who had defeated Custer soon turned their attention to the position on the bluffs, and the soldiers withdrew to the main position.

The afternoon and evening of June 25 saw sustained fighting. The soldiers formed a defensive perimeter on the hilltop, using their horses, mules, and packs for cover and digging shallow rifle pits with whatever tools and implements were available. Lakota and Cheyenne warriors, many of whom had just fought Custer, fired from surrounding ridges and ravines, and some attacked closely. The soldiers' ammunition was a concern, and the wounded needed care. Doctors and other soldiers tended them under fire as best they could. Officers including Benteen, whose steady conduct is widely noted in accounts, and Weir moved among the lines, though disagreements over responsibility, especially Reno's leadership and the question of whether the commands should have moved sooner to support Custer, divided the survivors and later historians.

The most pressing problem on the morning of June 26 was water. The hilltop had none, and soldiers, suffering from thirst and with wounded men in need of water, organized parties of volunteers to descend a ravine to the river and bring water back under fire. Several soldiers who took part were later awarded the Medal of Honor for this and related actions during the defense. Fighting resumed at dawn and was intense during the morning. The Indigenous attacks on June 26 tested the perimeter, and the soldiers' positions were precarious.

During the day, village leaders decided to withdraw. Scouts reported that additional soldiers, Gibbon's and Terry's column moving up from the north, were approaching, and the camp, with its thousands of noncombatants, horses, and possessions, could not remain in place. The village packed in the afternoon and began to move south along the valley toward the Bighorn Mountains in a long column that the soldiers on the bluffs watched from above. The decision was a judgment about the safety of the families, taken when the immediate military situation had been resolved. It was not a retreat from a defeat. The warriors left the field after destroying Custer's command and besieging the survivors.

On June 27, Terry's column reached the bluffs. The Crow scouts attached to the column had discovered the bodies of Custer's command on June 26, and the arrival of the soldiers confirmed the scale of the defeat. The Seventh Cavalry's wounded were cared for, the dead of Custer's battalion were identified and buried in shallow graves, and the news began its journey to the rest of the country. The National Park Service's history of the Reno-Benteen defense describes this sequence in detail and provides a foundation for studying the aftermath.

Survival on the ridge was itself a logistical problem. Section 77.14

Casualties, Indigenous Victory, and Reconstructing the Battle

Accounting for the human losses of the Little Bighorn requires distinguishing several categories. Custer's immediate command, the five companies he led north, lost approximately two hundred and ten men, all of whom died on June 25. This is the figure often misread as the total American loss. Total American deaths in the battle, including soldiers killed in Reno's and Benteen's commands in the valley fight, the retreat, and the hilltop defense, together with scouts and civilians attached to the regiment, are commonly given by the National Park Service as about two hundred and sixty-eight, with some dozens more wounded, a few of whom later died. Exact figures vary with how scouts, civilians, and the missing are counted, and students should treat all numbers as approximations.

Indigenous casualties are less securely established. The Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho did not keep rosters of the dead, and estimates have been drawn from oral histories, from army observations, and from later recollections. Estimates commonly range from a few dozen to roughly a hundred killed, with disagreement about whether to include those who died of wounds afterward, and the number of wounded is unknown. Some of the dead were buried or placed on scaffolds as the camp withdrew. Among the dead were women and children killed in the valley fight, and Cheyenne and Lakota warriors including the Cheyenne leader Lame White Man. Treating these figures with care means acknowledging both the limits of the evidence and the importance of Indigenous memory.

The engagement was an overwhelming tactical victory for the Indigenous defenders. A very large village, defended by warriors who repelled an attack on one end, destroyed a second force at the other, and besieged the survivors, defeated the Seventh Cavalry in a day and a half. Several factors combined: the size of the village and the warrior force; the knowledge of the terrain and the mobility of mounted defenders; the division of Custer's regiment into separated battalions; the underestimation of the Indigenous strength by the American command; and the determination of defenders fighting to protect their families. The victory did not rest on any single leader or tactic, though individual leaders and warriors distinguished themselves.

Battlefield archaeology has become a central tool for reconstructing the engagement and has changed older assumptions. It has also exposed the problems created when later narratives privileged Custer's experience while minimizing Lakota, Cheyenne, Arapaho, and allied Indigenous testimony. For many decades, popular accounts, paintings, and memorials treated the battle as a drama of Custer's last stand and the Indigenous participants as scenery. The long commemoration of the field as a monument to the soldiers, to which an Indian memorial was added only in the late twentieth century, reflected these priorities. The name of the park itself changed from Custer Battlefield National Monument to Little Bighorn Battlefield National Monument in 1991, a change that signified a broader recognition of the Indigenous history of the site.

The victory also had meaning within Lakota and Cheyenne communities. It confirmed the power of their collective resistance and the effectiveness of defending their territory and their families, and it entered oral tradition as a defining event. But its immediate consequences were mixed, as the following sections show. The same victory that proved their capacity to defeat the army also set in motion the American response that would end their independence.

The categories behind famous casualty figures need to be visible to students. The National Park Service commonly identifies 210 in Custer's immediate battalion and approximately 268 Americans killed or later dying of wounds across the battle, a broader category that includes losses beyond his five companies. Some tabulations separate the 263 killed at or immediately after the engagement from five who succumbed to wounds. Indigenous deaths are much less certain because no comprehensive roll survives and because accounts distinguish combatants from women and children killed near the lodges. An estimate should therefore be paired with its author, method, and population counted. Numbers without those qualifications can create the false impression that every community kept the same type of record.

Section 77.15

The American Response to Custer's Defeat: Public Reaction, Reinforcements, and Renewed War

The news of the defeat reached the American public in the first days of July 1876, in the midst of the centennial of the Declaration of Independence. Reports of the disaster were carried by couriers to settlements in Montana and telegraphed east, and the steamer carrying Terry's dispatch and the wounded arrived at Fort Abraham Lincoln on July 5. Newspapers across the country carried the story from July 6. The timing intensified the reaction: a nation celebrating its first hundred years received word that a celebrated cavalry commander and several hundred soldiers had been killed by Indigenous people whom many Americans regarded as obstacles to progress.

Public reaction combined grief, anger, and calls for revenge. Newspapers criticized the government's Indian policy, with some blaming the Grant administration, others blaming Custer, and others calling for decisive military action. Custer was rapidly transformed into a figure of national commemoration and mourning, a hero whose death was cast as sacrifice, though military and political disputes over responsibility began almost at once and continued for decades. The army conducted inquiries, and officers argued in print over who bore blame, with Reno and Benteen on one side and Custer's defenders on the other.

The military response was to send reinforcements. The army moved additional cavalry and infantry into the region, and Congress authorized additional troops. Posts were established in the Yellowstone country, including one at the mouth of the Tongue River, that would serve as bases for sustained operations. Officers such as Colonel Nelson A. Miles, Colonel Wesley Merritt, and Colonel Ranald S. Mackenzie brought their regiments north. The strategic aim shifted from the pursuit of a single decisive battle to the destruction of the economic and military capacity of the free-roaming communities.

The political consequences for the Lakota communities living at agencies were severe. The defeat of Custer was used to justify demands for disarmament, for surrender of horses, and for concessions of territory. Congress, in an appropriations act of August 1876, attached a condition that rations for the Lakota would be withheld unless they ceded the Black Hills and relinquished hunting rights, a measure whose legal meaning is examined in Chapter 76. Agency people who had not fought, and who in many cases had relatives in the camps, found themselves held collectively responsible. Military authority over the agencies expanded.

The central question is why a major Indigenous victory strengthened demands within the United States for more aggressive intervention. Several factors contributed. The scale of the defeat of a regiment shocked the public and the army and created a political demand for retribution. The existing interests in the Black Hills and in the opening of the northern Plains had not disappeared, and the defeat provided a rationale for pressing them. The army's institutional confidence in its ability to win through persistent operations remained intact. And the victory, by demonstrating that Indigenous communities could defeat the army in the field, led American leaders to conclude that they must be deprived of the means to do so.

The months after the battle reveal how a public narrative can influence policy without explaining policy by itself. The miners already occupying the Black Hills, congressional appropriations disputes, and military commanders' determination to restrict nonagency movement predated Custer's defeat. The news of the battle gave these interests political urgency. Army commanders, meanwhile, increasingly favored persistent pursuit and well-supplied operations rather than expecting one dramatic charge to end the war. Those shifts required transportation, food, remounts, interpreters, and Indigenous scouts. For Lakota families at agencies, the same administrative system that distributed rations could threaten to withhold them as leverage in land negotiations, placing people who had not fought within the coercive machinery of the postbattle campaign.

Section 77.16

The Pursuit after Little Bighorn: Warbonnet Creek, the Horsemeat March, and Slim Buttes

After the Little Bighorn, the large camp dispersed. The great gathering could not be sustained in one place, and the bands separated into smaller groups to hunt and to avoid the army. This dispersal was a rational Indigenous response, but it also meant that the coalition lost the concentration that had made it powerful. The American commanders, reinforced and learning from their experience, set out to pursue and break up the scattered camps.

On July 17, 1876, at Warbonnet Creek in northwestern Nebraska, troops of the Fifth Cavalry under Colonel Wesley Merritt intercepted a party of Cheyenne who had left the agency to join the northern camps. In a brief engagement a Cheyenne warrior known as Yellow Hair, or Yellow Hand, was killed by the scout William F. Cody, who later made the encounter part of his entertainment career. The episode illustrates the army's effort to prevent agency people from joining the free-roaming communities and the extent to which the war's meaning was quickly absorbed into popular spectacle.

In August, Terry's and Crook's columns, which had met on the Rosebud, followed trails northeast but soon lost them. Terry's command was largely broken up, while Crook, determined to continue, set out in late August with a column supplied for only a short campaign. Heavy rain, mud, and shortages of food followed. As supplies ran out, soldiers killed their exhausted horses and mules for meat, and the march became known as the Horsemeat March, a stage of the campaign that caused real suffering among the troops. The hardship of the army is part of the history, and it should be understood alongside the hardship its operations imposed on Indigenous families.

On September 9, 1876, an advance detachment of Crook's column under Captain Anson Mills, sent ahead to find food, came upon a Miniconjou village at Slim Buttes, in present-day South Dakota, led by American Horse. The soldiers attacked at dawn. The village, a settlement of families preparing to join relatives at the agencies, was overrun, and American Horse was mortally wounded. The troops seized the village's food, including dried meat, and other supplies, and found items taken from the Seventh Cavalry, which suggested the village's connection to the Little Bighorn. Crazy Horse and other warriors arrived and counterattacked on September 9 and 10 but could not retake the village. Crook's column, relieved by wagons sent from the Black Hills settlements, resumed its march.

Slim Buttes was a small engagement in numbers, but significant for what it revealed. It showed that the army could now find, attack, and plunder villages, and that the destruction of food and supplies had become central to its strategy. For the families of the village, the loss was devastating. In a season when families needed to build up winter stores, the seizure of their supplies, along with the death or capture of leaders and the loss of lodges, forced many to seek the agencies. The pursuit of the summer and early autumn did not produce a decisive battle, but it steadily reduced the means of Indigenous independence.

Division VMilitary Coercion, Surrender, Exile, and the Limits of Indigenous Victory
Section 77.17

Military Control of the Agencies, Disarmament, and the Black Hills Crisis

During the summer and autumn of 1876, the army extended its authority over the Lakota agencies. The agencies, where food and annuity goods were distributed, became sites of surveillance and control. Troops were stationed in and around them, and the movement of people in and out was monitored and restricted. Military officers, in coordination with Indian Bureau agents, treated the agencies as bases from which to prevent people from joining the free-roaming camps and from supplying them with weapons, ammunition, and food.

Disarmament and dismounting followed. In October 1876, Colonel Ranald S. Mackenzie's troops, supported by other forces, surrounded Oglala camps near Red Cloud Agency, including groups associated with Red Cloud, and seized weapons and horses. Military disarmament and livestock seizures also occurred at other agencies under separate commands; the operations and results must not be merged into a single seizure. The seizure removed means of hunting and mobility from people who, in many cases, had not fought. General Crook also declared that Red Cloud was deposed as a leader and elevated Spotted Tail as the principal chief of the Lakota, an assertion of American control over Lakota political leadership that did not reflect Lakota political practice. The use of food and annuities to influence political decisions was a central instrument: rations could be withheld, and the Congressional condition of August 1876 tied them explicitly to concession of territory.

Families and leaders who had not participated in the fighting experienced collective restrictions. They were counted, watched, and made dependent on federal rations, with horses and weapons taken, so that hunting on the open plains was no longer possible. For many, the seizure of horses was devastating in an economy built around them. Agency leaders had made choices to cooperate in hope of protecting their people, and now found that cooperation did not shield them from the consequences of the war.

Against this background, a federal commission headed by George W. Manypenny traveled to the agencies in the autumn of 1876 to obtain Lakota agreement to relinquish the Black Hills and hunting rights in the unceded territory. The commission negotiated under the conditions just described: rations tied to compliance, military force present, and communities disarmed. Many leaders signed, believing that refusal would worsen their situation, and the commission's work produced an agreement whose validity was later challenged. Congress confirmed the Black Hills taking by legislation in February 1877. The close legal analysis of the 1876 agreement, the 1877 act, and the later claims history belongs to Chapter 76. For this chapter, the essential point is the military context: the Black Hills were acquired while Lakota communities were under military occupation, disarmed, and dependent on rations, and the war's pressure was a central part of how the acquisition was achieved.

Specific agency operations should be kept distinct. In October 1876, Mackenzie's command targeted Oglala people associated with the Red Cloud Agency, while other army columns confiscated horses and weapons in the Missouri River agency districts. Crook did not simply conduct an identical disarmament at every agency at the same moment. Contemporary military descriptions of thousands of horses seized demonstrate the scale of the policy, but the practical consequences varied from camp to camp. A family with no weapons or riding animals could be unable to participate in a buffalo hunt even if its members had not fought in any engagement. The resulting dependence on issued supplies increased the government's bargaining power and weakened Indigenous leaders' ability to refuse demands framed as conditions for feeding their relatives.

Section 77.18

The Winter Campaigns: Dull Knife's Village and the Battle of Wolf Mountain, 1876–1877

As autumn advanced, Colonel Nelson A. Miles, operating from the Yellowstone and Tongue River country, pursued the Hunkpapa and other camps. In October 1876, Sitting Bull met Miles for a parley near Cedar Creek, but no agreement was reached, and fighting followed. After the encounter, some Hunkpapa and other lodges came in to surrender, while Sitting Bull and others withdrew. The episode showed that negotiation was possible but that the terms offered by the army, which required submission and the surrender of arms and horses, were unacceptable to many leaders.

In November, the army turned to the Northern Cheyenne. Colonel Ranald S. Mackenzie, with a force that included cavalry and a substantial contingent of Indigenous scouts and allies, among them Pawnee, Shoshone, and others, located a Northern Cheyenne village in the Bighorn Mountains, led in part by Dull Knife and Little Wolf. Following a difficult approach during the night of November 25, the soldiers and scouts attacked at dawn on November 26 in the narrow valley of the Red Fork of the Powder River. The Cheyenne, who had gathered a considerable village to prepare for winter, were taken by surprise, and after a day of fighting, in which many warriors fought to protect the retreat of families, the troops seized horses and burned the village, with its lodges, robes, food, and equipment. The number of Cheyenne killed is uncertain; the figure of roughly forty is recorded as a Cheyenne estimate and must not be misrepresented as a verified burial count. U.S. soldiers also died in the fighting.

The destruction of the village in the cold of late November was the central event. The survivors fled into the mountains with little clothing or shelter in severe winter weather, and some, including infants and the vulnerable, died of exposure during the following nights. They sought refuge with Crazy Horse's Oglala camp, whose people shared what they had, but food and shelter were scarce. The Cheyenne, ravaged and dependent, soon faced the decision to surrender. By the following spring, some Northern Cheyenne groups associated with Dull Knife and Little Wolf came in to Camp Robinson; other Cheyenne families followed different paths, and the subsequent forced removal south belongs to the later reservation-history chapters. The attack demonstrated that winter, which had once been a season of relative security for camped families, could now be turned against them.

Miles, meanwhile, remained in the field with infantry and artillery through the winter. In December, he skirmished with Lakota parties, and on January 8, 1877, at the Battle of Wolf Mountain, in the Wolf Mountains of Montana, his force engaged warriors associated with Crazy Horse in a snowstorm. The Lakota and Cheyenne attacked Miles's position, and the soldiers, using artillery and organized infantry fire, held it. Neither side suffered heavy losses, but Crazy Horse's following, short of food and shelter, could not sustain the struggle. For the army, the engagement showed that it could campaign in winter and hold its ground.

The winter campaigns reveal the strategy. The army sought to make the continued independence of mobile communities unsustainable, not by defeating them in a single battle but by striking their winter camps, destroying their shelter, seizing their horses, and forcing them to find food and safety at agencies. The Army's official historical account identifies the Dull Knife fight and Wolf Mountain as major components of the final campaigns, and Indigenous recollections of the cold and hunger describe the human cost.

Section 77.19

The Surrender of Crazy Horse and the End of Organized Resistance, 1877

By the spring of 1877, many Lakota and Cheyenne communities faced a bleak calculation. Horses were weak, food was short, ammunition was scarce, and the army was unrelenting. Agency leaders, including Red Cloud and Spotted Tail, sent messengers to the free-roaming camps, urging them to come in and offering assurances. Spotted Tail in particular traveled to meet with Crazy Horse's people, and army officers, notably Lieutenant William P. Clark, who was known to the Lakota as White Hat, played a role in negotiating the surrender. Indigenous intermediaries, interpreters, and army officers each worked within different assumptions.

In early May, Crazy Horse and his followers arrived at Camp Robinson, in northwestern Nebraska. The Nebraska State Historical Society dates his surrender to May 6 and records a band of 889, while one National Park Service biography uses May 7 and approximately 1,100 followers; another NPS chronology gives May 6 and more than 1,100. Those sources may differ in whether they count the formal surrender, the completion of an arrival, or accompanying followers. The chapter preserves the range rather than treating the figures as identical headcounts. The arrival was an event of ceremony and display, as the procession entered the post, but its significance was the end of the largest remaining organized Oglala resistance. Other groups were coming in at the same time. Miniconjou under Lame Deer had been attacked by Miles's troops on May 7, and Lame Deer was killed in that engagement, while various groups surrendered at other posts.

Within the agency environment, tensions grew. Crazy Horse's following had to adjust to confinement and ration dependency, while rivalries among Lakota leaders and suspicion by army officers increased. Rumors circulated that Crazy Horse planned to leave or to resume fighting, and the army's interest in recruiting Lakota scouts for service against the Nez Perce, a conflict taken up in Chapter 78, created further friction. Interpreters and intermediaries sometimes relayed statements in ways that deepened mistrust. Accounts of what Crazy Horse actually said and intended are contested, and the evidence for the rumors comes largely from sources with their own interests.

On September 5, 1877, officers at Camp Robinson, after concluding that Crazy Horse was a risk, attempted to arrest him and to place him in the guardhouse. In the struggle that followed at the guardhouse, he was fatally wounded by a soldier's bayonet and died that night. The broad sequence is well supported: the attempted arrest, the struggle, the wounding, and his death. The details are contested, including whether he drew a weapon, how he came to be wounded, the roles of individuals such as Little Big Man and the soldiers present, and the question of responsibility. Eyewitness accounts conflict, and the chapter does not resolve them. His family took his body, and his burial place was kept secret and remains so.

The death of Crazy Horse was a deep loss for the Oglala and for many Lakota people, and it became a symbol of the costs of confinement and the dangers of life under military control. It marked the collapse of a principal organized Oglala fighting coalition, not the end of all Lakota armed independence: Sitting Bull and other followers remained outside United States control in Canada, and other dispersed groups faced separate decisions. The Cheyenne, meanwhile, were sent in 1877 to join the Southern Cheyenne in Indian Territory, a displacement taken up in the chapters on the reservation order, and the Lakota agencies were soon moved to the Missouri River, as the United States consolidated control.

The events after surrender cannot be reduced to a single personal quarrel. Crazy Horse's followers needed food and security, army officers wanted predictable obedience, and agency leaders held different views of how to protect their communities under surveillance. Interpreters stood between incompatible political languages, and an ambiguous statement could be read as evidence of renewed resistance. Later accounts of the attempted arrest consequently reflect not only conflicting memories of the guardhouse struggle but also rival assumptions about whether Crazy Horse had accepted permanent military control. Even after his death, the political choices of other Lakota groups remained unresolved: some were living at agencies, some had surrendered separately to Miles, and Sitting Bull's followers remained outside U.S. jurisdiction across the border.

Section 77.20

Sitting Bull's Canadian Exile and the Meaning of the Great Sioux War

In 1877, Sitting Bull made a different decision. Rather than surrender, he led a group of followers north across the international border into Canada, to the region around Wood Mountain in what is now Saskatchewan. The decision was political, made under worsening circumstances. It was not a universal movement of all Lakota participants. Other leaders, including Crazy Horse, chose to come in to agencies, and still others later joined Sitting Bull. Gall and others who had followed him across the border would eventually return, some before Sitting Bull did.

In Canada, Sitting Bull's people encountered officers of the North-West Mounted Police, including Major James M. Walsh, who dealt with them as foreign visitors who had to obey Canadian law. Canadian officials, mindful of relations with the United States and with Canadian Indigenous nations, did not offer them a permanent homeland or rations. In October 1877, a commission sent by the United States met Sitting Bull at Fort Walsh and offered terms for return, which he refused. The Canadian borderlands were part of a region of Indigenous diplomacy and movement, as Chapter 82 on Canada's numbered treaties and Chapter 83 on the Red River and North-West resistances show, and the Lakota were entering a region where Canada was extending its own treaty system.

Life in exile was hard. The buffalo herds were shrinking in the northern plains on both sides of the border, and the Lakota in Canada struggled to maintain a hunting economy. Food became scarce, especially after 1879, and the pressure from Canadian officials and the United States increased. Groups gradually returned to the United States, and in July 1881, Sitting Bull himself returned to the United States and surrendered at Fort Buford, on the Missouri River. Published sources give July 19 or July 20 for stages of the return and formal surrender. This epilogue is a limited one; his later life, including his time as a prisoner of war, is not the subject of this chapter.

The war's outcomes were contradictory. Lakota and Cheyenne communities achieved major military victories, at the Rosebud and the Little Bighorn, that demonstrated the strength of their collective defense. Yet federal military expansion, attacks on subsistence resources, the occupation of agencies, the confiscation of horses and weapons, and the dispossession of the Black Hills profoundly restricted their independence. The army had not won by decisive battle. It had won by sustained pressure, applied to the foundations of mobile life.

The end of the war must be distinguished from the end of Indigenous sovereignty. Lakota, Northern Cheyenne, and other communities preserved political identities, kinship networks, ceremonies, and languages, and they continued to assert rights even after defeat and displacement. The Lakota refusal to accept payment for the Black Hills, examined in Chapter 76, is one expression of this continuity, and the later history of resistance and revitalization, including events treated in Chapters 90, 112, and 119, shows that the war's end was a transition to a different phase of Indigenous politics rather than an end to it.

Historical Vocabulary
Tactical Victory, Strategic Defeat, and Military Confinement

Definition. A tactical victory is success in a particular engagement. A strategic defeat is failure to achieve or maintain the larger aims of a conflict. Military confinement is the restriction of a people's movement and subsistence through force and control of resources.

Historical context. The Lakota and Cheyenne won the Rosebud and the Little Bighorn, yet the army's attacks on villages, supplies, and agencies reduced their capacity to remain independent.

Why it matters. The distinction explains how a decisive battlefield victory could coexist with eventual loss of independence, since the conflict was decided by the sustainability of mobile life rather than the outcome of single battles.

Potential misunderstanding. Strategic defeat does not mean that victories were meaningless or that defeat was inevitable. The outcome depended on choices and circumstances, and the victories preserved lives, shaped negotiations, and remain central to Indigenous memory.

Canadian exile did not remove Sitting Bull's followers from imperial state policy. The North-West Mounted Police sought to prevent cross-border fighting, the Canadian government was managing its own treaty and reserve program, and Indigenous nations north of the border had preexisting rights and relationships affected by newcomers' use of buffalo grounds. Lakota families could seek temporary refuge, but they could not assume unrestricted supplies or an independent reservation would be granted. The decline of northern bison herds made these constraints more severe over time. This helps explain why some groups returned before Sitting Bull without treating return as a change in personal courage or loyalty. The differing choices were responses to food scarcity, family obligations, and the narrowing possibilities for independent hunting.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Lakota and Cheyenne Win at Little Bighorn but Lose the Wider War?

The question: Historians agree that the Lakota and Cheyenne won major victories in June 1876 and that the free-roaming communities had lost their independence by the end of 1877. They differ on how to explain the relationship between these facts and on which factors weighed most heavily.

Interpretation A — Material and logistical pressure: Some historians emphasize that mobile bison-hunting communities depended on horses, food, and shelter, and that the army's attacks on these resources, together with the decline of the buffalo and the army's capacity to reinforce and supply its forces, made continued independence unsustainable regardless of battlefield outcomes.
Interpretation B — Political fragmentation and federal coercion: Others emphasize the political dimension: the divisions between agency and nonagency communities, the coercive control of agencies, the use of rations to compel concessions, and the American willingness to disregard treaty obligations, which together isolated the free-roaming camps and undermined their support.
Interpretation C — Contingency and Indigenous choice: Still others stress that outcomes were not predetermined, that leaders made different choices, and that different decisions at several points, including the dispersal after Little Bighorn, might have produced different results.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on army reports, Indian Bureau records, agency correspondence, Indigenous testimony, and environmental evidence concerning the buffalo.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns causation and the relative weight of factors, rather than the basic chronology.

Why it matters: How historians weigh these factors shapes whether the war is understood as the product of overwhelming force, of political division, or of contingent decisions, and affects how Indigenous agency is represented.

Student task: Using evidence from this chapter, assess how the interpretations might be combined, and identify evidence that could help determine which factors were most decisive.

Multiple Perspectives

The Great Sioux War as Experienced by Different Participants

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns documented by historians. They are not quotations from any named individual.

Sitting Bull and the political and ceremonial leadership: The war was a defense of a way of life and of treaty rights, requiring the maintenance of a coalition through spiritual and political authority, and later a decision to preserve independence through exile.
Crazy Horse, Gall, and battlefield leaders: The war was a series of immediate decisions about how to protect families and defeat attacking soldiers, made by war leaders acting within their own societies, and later a calculation about the costs of continued resistance.
Northern Cheyenne participants: The war brought the destruction of winter villages, the loss of shelter, and a decision to ally with Lakota relatives, followed by surrender and displacement.
Crow, Shoshone, and Arikara scouts and warriors: The war was an opportunity to confront long-standing rivals and to protect their own territories and communities, through an alliance with a power that they hoped would shield them.
American soldiers and officers: The war was an arduous campaign over difficult country, marked by hardship, uncertainty, and, for many, the shock of defeat, carried out under orders shaped by federal policy.
Families in the camps and at the agencies: The war meant displacement, the loss of food and horses, anxiety for relatives, and the difficult choices of those who stayed near agencies and those who left them.

Note on power: These groups did not hold equal power or freedom of action; the perspectives illustrate complexity rather than equivalence.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Indigenous Political Autonomy and Military Resistance before and after 1876

What Changed

  • Mobile, bison-based life gave way to ration dependency and agency confinement within roughly a year of the great victories
  • Horses and weapons were seized, ending the military independence of most communities
  • The Black Hills were taken and the Great Sioux Reservation reduced, as examined in Chapter 76
  • Leaders were deposed, imprisoned, or killed, and the army claimed the power to choose Lakota leaders

What Stayed the Same

  • Kinship networks, ceremonies, and languages persisted
  • The claim to the Black Hills and to treaty rights continued
  • Distinct Lakota and Cheyenne political identities survived confinement and, for the Cheyenne, removal

Why the pattern matters: The change was rapid and coercive, but continuity of identity and claims shows that the end of armed resistance was not the end of Indigenous political life.

Cause and Effect

From Gold Discovery to Confinement: The Causes and Consequences of the Great Sioux War

Long-term causes

American expansion onto the Plains, the treaty system's contradictions, railroad and mining interests, and the army's institutional approach to Plains warfare.

Short-term causes

The Black Hills expedition and gold rush, the failure of negotiations in 1875, and the decision not to enforce exclusion of miners.

Immediate trigger

The December 1875 ultimatum and the expiry of the January 31 deadline, which transferred the conflict to the army.

Event

Armed resistance by a coalition of Lakota and Cheyenne communities, with victories at the Rosebud and the Little Bighorn.

Immediate effects

Reinforcement of the army, attacks on villages and supplies, disarmament of agency communities, and winter campaigns.

Long-term consequences

Confinement, loss of the Black Hills, the end of Plains military independence, and a long Lakota and Cheyenne struggle for rights.

Historical complexity: The war cannot be reduced to gold, to Custer's decisions, or to any single cause. It resulted from economic interests, federal decisions, treaty failure, military strategy, environmental pressures, and Indigenous choices acting together.

Everyday Life and Social History

Buffalo, Camps, Women, Children, Horses, and Displacement

The war was fought by and against communities whose daily life was organized around the seasonal round. Families moved between winter camps and summer gatherings, following the buffalo, and the work of the camp, including hunting, butchering, tanning hides, drying meat, making and repairing lodges, tending horses, and caring for children and elders, was divided among men, women, and elders in ways that varied among peoples. Women's labor in processing hides and meat was essential to the economy, and in the crises of the war women also fled, protected children, and in some cases took part in the fighting.

Children grew up in camps that were suddenly places of danger. Horses, central to wealth, mobility, and hunting, were prime targets, so that their seizure transformed daily life. After the surrender, families faced ration lines, restrictions on movement, and the loss of the hunt, and the experience of displacement, whether to agencies, to Indian Territory, or to Canada, reshaped households and communities. Historians draw on Indigenous oral history, army reports, and later interviews to reconstruct these experiences, recognizing that many were recorded by outsiders.

Chapter Summary

The Great Sioux War of 1876–1877 arose from the breakdown of treaty protections and the determination of the United States to confine Lakota and Cheyenne communities. The Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868 had recognized the Great Sioux Reservation and unceded territory in the Powder River country, but the Black Hills expedition of 1874, the gold rush, and the failure of negotiations in 1875 led the federal government to abandon enforcement and to pressure the free-roaming camps.

The December 1875 ultimatum, with its impractical deadline, converted people exercising treaty rights into "hostiles" by administrative classification, and the army took over. The communities that resisted were not a single army but a coalition of independent camps joined by kinship and ceremony, led by figures such as Sitting Bull, Crazy Horse, Gall, Two Moons, and others whose roles must be distinguished.

The army's opening campaigns, including the March attack on a Cheyenne village on the Powder River, demonstrated a strategy of striking camps, horses, and supplies. The three-column campaign of summer 1876 relied on Indigenous scouts, including Crow, Shoshone, and Arikara, whose participation reflected their own political interests.

At the Rosebud on June 17, Lakota and Cheyenne warriors halted Crook's advance and removed his column from the decisive days of the campaign. Eight days later, at the Little Bighorn, a very large village of families defended itself against Custer's attack. Custer's immediate command of five companies was destroyed on June 25, while Reno's and Benteen's companies were besieged until June 26.

The evidence for the battle includes Indigenous testimony, army reports and testimony, and archaeology. No member of Custer's immediate battalion survived, so reconstruction depends on combining these sources. Casualty figures must be separated into categories, and estimates of village and warrior numbers remain uncertain.

The victory did not end the war. The United States reinforced its forces, attacked villages at Slim Buttes and on the Red Fork of the Powder, disarmed and dismounted agency communities, and campaigned through the winter at Wolf Mountain, aiming to destroy the means of mobile independence.

Under these pressures, Crazy Horse surrendered in May 1877 and died in September after an attempted arrest, with the broad sequence well supported and many details contested. Sitting Bull chose exile in Canada, returning in 1881. Cheyenne communities were displaced, and the Black Hills were taken by federal legislation in February 1877.

The war's outcome was contradictory: major tactical victories were followed by strategic defeat and confinement. Historians weigh material pressure, political fragmentation, and contingency differently in explaining this outcome.

The end of the war did not end Lakota or Cheyenne sovereignty. These communities preserved their identities, ceremonies, languages, and claims, and the history of the war connects forward to the reservation order, to the violence at Wounded Knee in 1890, and to the long struggle over the Black Hills.

Chapter Reflection

The Great Sioux War is often remembered through a single afternoon, but its significance lies in a longer sequence in which treaty failure, military escalation, Indigenous resistance, and coercive confinement unfolded together. Studying it as a war of communities, not only armies, shows how much depended on the choices of families, leaders, and neighboring nations as well as on the decisions of generals.

The chapter reveals how a powerful state can turn a battlefield defeat into a broader victory by attacking the foundations of its opponents' way of life. The Lakota and Cheyenne won the engagements they chose to fight, and still lost the capacity to remain mobile, armed, and independent. That outcome was not inevitable, and it was not the product of weakness in Indigenous leadership; it reflected the unequal resources, the breakdown of treaty obligations, and the pressure placed on communities that had to feed and protect their people.

The war's consequences shaped the history that follows: the reservation order examined in Chapter 84, the policies of assimilation in later volumes, the violence at Wounded Knee in 1890 treated in Chapter 90, and the continuing dispute over the Black Hills explored in Chapter 119. The same events also seeded continuity. Lakota and Cheyenne communities carried forward their memory of the victories and their claims to land and treaty rights.

For the textbook as a whole, the chapter completes the progression that began with Red Cloud's War and the 1868 treaty, and it demonstrates why Indigenous nations are best understood as continuing political communities whose histories do not end with defeat.
